# Chapter 55: Trading Psychology & the Trade Journal

By now you know the mechanics cold. You can price an option, read the Greeks, structure a spread, size a position, and pick a strike that matches your view of volatility. And yet, if you are like almost every trader who has ever lived, the gap between what you *know* and what you *do* will turn out to be the widest gap in your trading life. You will plan a calm, defined-risk trade and then, in the heat of a fast-moving Bank Nifty expiry, double your size on a hunch, refuse to take a planned loss, and chase a move you swore you would skip. The market did nothing wrong. *You* did — and you knew better the whole time.

This is the uncomfortable truth that this chapter exists to confront: **once you know the mechanics, psychology decides your outcome.** Two traders with identical knowledge and identical edge will diverge wildly, and the difference is almost never analysis — it is discipline under pressure. The good news is that discipline is not a personality trait you are born with or without. It is a *system* you build: written rules, pre-commitments, hard limits, and one habit that is, rupee for rupee, the highest-return thing you will ever do — the **trade journal**. This chapter is about installing that system.

## Core concepts

### Why your brain is wired to lose money in markets

Your brain was tuned over millions of years to keep you alive on a savannah, not to make rational bets on a screen. The instincts that kept your ancestors alive — flee from danger, chase the herd, remember the one time you got hurt — are precisely the instincts that bleed a trading account. Markets are one of the few environments where your gut is reliably, systematically *wrong*, so the first defence is to name the specific ways it betrays you.

### The common destroyers

**Fear and greed.** These are the two engines under every bad decision. Greed makes you oversize, hold a winner past your target hoping for more, and pile into a crowded trade. Fear makes you cut a good trade the instant it dips into the red, refuse to enter a perfectly valid setup, and freeze when your plan says act. The same person swings between both within a single session — greedy at the highs, terrified at the lows — which is exactly backwards from what makes money.

**FOMO and chasing.** Nifty rips 200 points in twenty minutes. You had no setup, no plan, no reason — but everyone on your feed is celebrating and you cannot bear to "miss it," so you buy the call *after* the move, paying inflated premium with inflated implied volatility, right as the move exhausts. The fear of missing out is greed wearing a disguise, and chasing means you systematically buy high. The market rewards the trader who waits for *his* setup and ignores everyone else's.

**Revenge trading.** You take a planned loss. Instead of accepting it as the cost of doing business, you feel *robbed*, and you immediately put on a bigger, sloppier trade to "win it back" right now. This is the single fastest way to turn a small, normal loss into a catastrophic one. The market does not know or care that you are down; it owes you nothing, and trying to extract repayment on your emotional timeline is how a bad morning becomes a blown account.

**Loss aversion — cutting winners early, holding losers late.** Behavioural research (Kahneman and Tversky) showed that a loss hurts roughly twice as much as an equivalent gain feels good. In trading, this asymmetry is poison. It makes you snatch a +₹3,000 profit early because you are terrified of giving it back, while you cling to a −₹3,000 loser because closing it would "make the loss real." The result is a portfolio of small wins and large losses — the exact opposite of the survival math from the sizing chapter. You end up *right* on direction often and still lose money, because your average loss dwarfs your average win.

**Overconfidence after a win streak.** Win five trades in a row and your brain quietly concludes you have "figured it out." Position sizes creep up, the checklist gets skipped, marginal setups start looking like sure things. But a winning streak in a positive-expectancy strategy is statistically *guaranteed* to happen periodically and tells you nothing new about your skill — yet it feels like proof of genius, and that feeling sets up the oversized trade that gives it all back.

**Anchoring to your entry price.** Once you buy a Nifty 24,000 call at ₹150, your brain treats ₹150 as the "true" value and every subsequent decision orbits that number. You refuse to sell at ₹120 because "I'm down," and you refuse to sell at ₹180 because "it'll go higher to justify my genius." But the market has never heard of your entry price — it is irrelevant to what the option is worth now. The only honest question is: *given everything I know this instant, would I put this trade on at the current price?* If no, you should be flat, regardless of what you paid.

### The discipline toolkit

Naming the destroyers is not enough; willpower alone loses to a fast tape every time. You beat psychology with *structure* — decisions made in advance, in calm, that bind your future self in the heat of the moment.

**A written trading plan and rules.** The foundation is a document — yes, actually written down — that specifies, before the session, what you will and will not do. A usable plan answers: Which strategies do I trade and in which volatility regime? What is my max risk per trade and per day? What setups qualify for entry? Where is my exit, both for profit and for loss, *defined before I enter*? What will I never do (e.g. never average down, never trade in the first five minutes, never add size to win back a loss)? Rules that live only in your head are not rules; they are suggestions your emotions are free to overrule.

**Pre-commitment.** This is the deliberate act of removing your future self's discretion. The clearest example is placing your stop-loss order the moment you enter, so that exiting is automatic rather than a decision you must summon the courage to make while losing money. Pre-commitment works precisely because the calm version of you is smarter than the panicked version, and a resting order lets the calm version win.

**Checklists.** Surgeons and airline pilots — experts under pressure with lives at stake — run checklists, not because they forget how to fly, but because pressure makes everyone skip steps. A pre-trade checklist (Is this in my plan? What's the IV regime? Is my size within 1-2%? Where's my exit? Am I chasing?) forces a two-second pause that breaks the impulse loop. The pause is the point.

**A daily loss limit — and walking away.** Decide in advance the rupee loss that ends your trading day, full stop. Many professionals use something like 3-4% of capital, or roughly the equivalent of two maximum losing trades. When you hit it, you are *done* — you close the terminal, no matter how juicy the next setup looks. This single rule is the hard circuit-breaker against revenge trading and tilt, because the danger is not the first loss; it is the spiral of frantic trades that follows it. Walking away costs you nothing on a bad day and saves you from the days that end careers.

**Sizing small enough to think clearly.** This connects straight back to position sizing. When a single trade can seriously hurt you, your reptile brain takes the wheel — every tick feels like an emergency and rational thought shuts down. When you are sized at 1-2% risk, a loss is an annoyance, not a threat, and you can actually follow your plan. **Correct position size is not just risk control; it is the precondition for clear thinking.** The trader risking 20% per trade is not brave — he has guaranteed he will make every decision in a state of panic.

### The trade journal: the single highest-ROI habit

Everything above tells you how to behave *during* a trade. The journal is how you get *better* between trades, and it is the one habit that separates traders who compound from traders who plateau. Here is the core idea: **trading gives you almost no honest feedback in real time.** A good decision can lose money and a terrible decision can win — over a few trades, luck drowns out skill entirely. Your memory, meanwhile, is a liar: it inflates your wins, buries your losses, and quietly rewrites your reasons after the fact so you always look smarter than you were. The journal is the antidote. It is the only mirror that does not flatter you.

A journal converts a fog of half-remembered trades into hard data you can actually learn from. It answers questions you cannot answer from feeling: *What is my real win rate? Which strategy actually makes me money and which one I "love" is secretly bleeding me? Do I lose most when IV is high? Are my losers clustered on Fridays, or right after a winning streak?* You cannot fix what you cannot see, and without a journal you are flying blind while convinced you can see fine.

**What to log for every single trade.** The discipline is to record each trade as you place it — entries written *before* the outcome is known, so you cannot rewrite your thesis afterwards. A complete entry captures:

- **Date / time** — entry date, expiry, and time of day (tilt often has a time signature).
- **Underlying** — Nifty, Bank Nifty, or the stock.
- **Strategy** — long call, bull put spread, iron condor, short straddle, etc.
- **Thesis / setup** — the actual reason, in one honest sentence. "Bullish, support held at 23,900, expecting bounce into expiry." If you cannot write a real reason, that is your answer: do not take the trade.
- **IV regime** — India VIX level and IV Rank/Percentile at entry. This is the field beginners skip and pros obsess over, because it tells you *later* whether you were a buyer of cheap options or expensive ones.
- **Entry** — strikes, premiums paid/received, net debit or credit, number of lots.
- **Planned exit** — your profit target and your stop loss, written *before* entry. This is the field that exposes whether you actually had a plan.
- **Actual exit** — where and when you really got out, and crucially *why* (hit target, hit stop, panicked, got greedy, expired).
- **Greeks at entry** — at minimum position delta and theta; vega if it's a volatility trade. This lets you confirm later whether the trade did what you thought it would.
- **P&L** — the net rupee result after costs (brokerage, STT, exchange charges — they are real and they add up).
- **P&L attribution** — *why* did you make or lose money? Direction (delta), time decay (theta), a change in implied volatility (vega), or just luck? This is the most valuable and most-skipped field. A short straddle that made money because IV collapsed is a *different lesson* from one that made money because the index sat still — and only attribution tells them apart.
- **The lesson** — one honest sentence written after closing. "Exited in panic on a normal dip; the plan was fine, I wasn't." This is where the learning actually compounds.

**How to review it weekly.** Logging is necessary but not sufficient — the payoff is in the review. Once a week, sit down with your closed trades and compute the numbers that tell you the truth about your trading:

- **Win rate** = (number of winning trades) / (total trades). Your *actual* number, not your felt number.
- **Average win and average loss** in rupees. Loss aversion lives or dies here — if your average loss is bigger than your average win, you are leaking money even with a decent win rate.
- **Expectancy** = average rupee profit *per trade*, the bottom-line measure of whether your process makes money:

  `Expectancy = (Win rate * Average win) - (Loss rate * Average loss)`

  A positive expectancy means each trade is, on average, worth taking. A negative one means *stop* — your edge is imaginary and more trades will only lose faster.
- **R-multiples.** Express each trade's result as a multiple of the risk you took on it: `R = (P&L on the trade) / (rupees you risked on the trade)`. A trade where you risked ₹4,000 and made ₹6,000 is a +1.5R; a full stop-out is −1R. R-multiples are powerful because they let you compare trades of different sizes on one honest scale and see your distribution of outcomes — a healthy trader has occasional big +3R winners that pay for many small −1R losers.

Then read the qualitative side: cluster your losers by strategy, by IV regime, by time of day, by emotional note. Patterns you would never feel in the moment jump off the page — "I lose every time I trade in the first ten minutes," or "all my big losses are short-premium in low-VIX," or "my revenge trades are 80% of my red days." That is the gold. You then turn each pattern into a *new rule* in your written plan, and the loop closes: the journal feeds the plan, the plan governs the trading, the trading feeds the journal.

**Make it automatic.** The reason most people never keep a journal is friction — typing twelve fields after every trade is a chore you will abandon by week two. So reduce the friction to near zero. A simple paper-trade journal or logger tool that records the structured fields for you, pulls premiums and Greeks, and computes your weekly stats automatically turns the highest-ROI habit into a thirty-second one. (The companion project for this book includes exactly such a logger — a lightweight journal that timestamps each entry, stores the thesis and IV regime, and rolls up win rate, expectancy, and R-multiples for you.) The format matters far less than the *consistency*: a disciplined spreadsheet beats a fancy tool you don't use. The goal is simply that you never again have to trust your memory about how you actually trade.

## Worked example (₹, Nifty/Bank Nifty)

Let's make this concrete with one journal entry and a weekly review.

**A single journal entry.** It is Monday, India VIX is at 12 (low), and Nifty is at 24,000. You sell a defined-risk bull put spread into weekly expiry. Your logged entry, written *at the moment of entry*:

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Date / expiry | Mon 23-Jun / weekly exp Thu 26-Jun |
| Underlying | Nifty |
| Strategy | Bull put spread (credit) |
| Thesis / setup | Mildly bullish; 23,900 support held twice; want positive theta |
| IV regime | India VIX 12, IV Rank ~20 (low) |
| Entry | Sell 23,900 put ₹70, buy 23,800 put ₹40; net credit ₹30; 1 lot (75) |
| Planned exit | Target: buy back at ₹10 (keep ~₹20). Stop: if loss hits ₹40/share (~1.3x credit) |
| Greeks at entry | Position delta +18 (mildly long), theta +₹450/day, vega negative |
| Max loss | (100 width − 30 credit) * 75 = ₹5,250 (the R for this trade) |

Thursday the index drifts up to 24,050 and the spread decays. You buy it back at ₹10 per the plan. You complete the entry *after* closing:

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Actual exit | Thu, bought back at ₹10 — hit target |
| P&L | (30 − 10) * 75 = ₹1,500 gross; ~₹1,350 net after costs |
| P&L attribution | Mostly theta (time decay) + small delta gain; IV was flat, vega contributed little |
| R-multiple | +1,350 / 5,250 ≈ **+0.26R** |
| Lesson | Plan worked because I let theta do the job and exited at target instead of getting greedy. Low-IV credit spreads are thin — credit was small relative to risk. |

Notice what the attribution field bought you: you now *know* this trade paid because of time decay, not because you were a brilliant forecaster. That is a repeatable, understandable edge — and the note about thin credit in low IV is a real lesson you can act on.

**A weekly stats review.** Over one week you closed ten trades. From the journal:

- 6 winners, 4 losers → **Win rate = 6/10 = 60%**, loss rate 40%.
- Winners (₹): 1,350, 1,500, 900, 2,100, 1,200, 1,950 → total ₹9,000 → **Average win = ₹1,500**.
- Losers (₹): −5,250, −2,000, −4,800, −1,950 → total −₹14,000 → **Average loss = ₹3,500**.

Compute expectancy:

`Expectancy = (0.60 * 1,500) - (0.40 * 3,500) = 900 - 1,400 = -₹500 per trade.`

This is the moment the journal earns its keep. You *felt* like a good week — you won more trades than you lost, 60%! But the cold arithmetic says you **lose ₹500 per trade on average**, and over ten trades you were down ₹5,000. The killer is the ratio: your average loss (₹3,500) is more than twice your average win (₹1,500). You are taking profits too early and letting losers run too far — textbook loss aversion. No amount of "feeling good about the week" would have revealed this; only the numbers did.

Now look at R-multiples and the qualitative notes. Your two worst trades (−5,250 = −1R, and −4,800) were both short-premium trades in low VIX that you let run *past* your stop because "it'll come back." Your winners were all small, disciplined target-exits. The pattern is screaming at you. The corrective rules write themselves and go straight into your plan: **(1) honour stops mechanically — place them as resting orders; (2) let winners run to at least a 1:1 reward-to-risk before taking profit, so average win can rival average loss; (3) be cautious selling thin premium in low IV.** Apply those three rules and the *same* 60% win rate flips expectancy positive. That is the entire game: the journal turns a vague losing week into three specific, fixable rules.

## Common mistakes / risk note

- **"I'll start journaling once I'm profitable."** Backwards. The journal is *how* you become profitable. Skipping it because you're losing is like refusing to see a doctor until you're healthy.
- **Logging only the outcome, not the reasoning.** A list of P&Ls with no thesis, IV regime, or exit plan is a scoreboard, not a teacher. The learning lives in *why*, and the why must be written *before* the result so you can't rewrite it.
- **Reviewing too rarely, or never.** Data you never analyse is just diary entries. The weekly computation of win rate, average win/loss, expectancy, and R-multiples is where behaviour actually changes.
- **Confusing a good outcome with a good decision.** A reckless trade that won is still a bad decision that got lucky — and praising it trains you to repeat it. Grade the *process*, not the result.
- **Ignoring costs.** Brokerage, STT, exchange and SEBI charges, and GST quietly turn marginal winners into losers. Log P&L *net*, or your expectancy is a fantasy.
- **The honest backdrop.** SEBI studies show roughly nine in ten retail F&O traders lose money, and the proximate cause is rarely a lack of knowledge — it is undisciplined behaviour: oversizing, revenge trading, chasing, and refusing to take planned losses. Psychology is not a soft add-on to your edge. For most people it *is* the edge, or the lack of one.

## Key takeaways

- Once you know the mechanics, **psychology decides the outcome** — two traders with equal skill diverge entirely on discipline.
- Know the destroyers by name: fear/greed, FOMO/chasing, revenge trading, loss aversion (cut winners early, hold losers late), overconfidence after wins, and anchoring to your entry price.
- Beat psychology with **structure, not willpower**: a written plan, pre-committed stops, a pre-trade checklist, a hard daily loss limit you actually walk away at, and size small enough to think clearly.
- The **trade journal is the highest-ROI habit in trading** — the only honest mirror, because the market gives no real-time feedback and your memory lies.
- Log every trade *as you place it*: date, underlying, strategy, thesis, IV regime, entry, planned exit, actual exit, Greeks, net P&L, **P&L attribution**, and the lesson.
- Review **weekly** and compute the truth: win rate, average win vs average loss, `Expectancy = WinRate*AvgWin − LossRate*AvgLoss`, and R-multiples — then turn every recurring loss pattern into a new rule.
- Automate the logging (a simple journal/logger tool removes the friction); consistency beats sophistication.

## Practice problems

1. **(Conceptual)** A trader closes the week with a 70% win rate and is thrilled. Explain how he could still be losing money, and which two journal numbers would reveal it.

2. **(Numeric — expectancy)** Over 20 trades a trader wins 8 and loses 12. Average win = ₹4,000; average loss = ₹2,000. Compute his expectancy per trade and his total P&L for the 20 trades. Is the strategy worth keeping?

3. **(Numeric — R-multiples)** On a Bank Nifty trade you risked ₹6,000 (your max loss / 1R). The trade made ₹9,000 net. Express the result in R. Separately, a full stop-out on a ₹4,000-risk trade is how many R? Why are R-multiples more useful than raw rupees for comparing the two?

4. **(Conceptual)** Identify the psychological destroyer in each: (a) Nifty jumps 150 points and you buy a call you had no plan for; (b) after two losses you triple your size to "get it back fast"; (c) you refuse to sell a call at ₹120 because you paid ₹150; (d) after six straight wins you skip your checklist and double up.

5. **(Conceptual)** Your written plan says "daily loss limit = ₹8,000." You hit −₹8,000 by noon, but a "perfect" setup appears. List two concrete reasons the rule says walk away, and what is most likely to happen if you override it.

6. **(Numeric — attribution)** You sold a Nifty straddle when India VIX was 20. You closed it for a ₹12,000 profit. Your journal shows the index barely moved and India VIX fell to 13. Which Greek most likely drove the profit, and why does logging the attribution change what you learn versus just logging "+₹12,000"?

## Solutions

**1.** A high win rate says nothing about *size* of wins versus losses. If his few losers are far larger than his many winners (classic loss aversion — holding losers, cutting winners), expectancy can be negative despite winning most trades. The two revealing numbers are **average win vs average loss** (is the loss bigger?) and **expectancy** = `WinRate*AvgWin − LossRate*AvgLoss`. For example, 70% wins of ₹500 against 30% losses of ₹2,000 gives `0.7*500 − 0.3*2,000 = 350 − 600 = −₹250` per trade — a losing strategy with a "great" win rate.

**2.** Win rate = 8/20 = 0.40; loss rate = 0.60. Expectancy = `(0.40 * 4,000) − (0.60 * 2,000) = 1,600 − 1,200 = +₹400 per trade`. Total over 20 trades = `20 * 400 = +₹8,000` (check: 8*4,000 − 12*2,000 = 32,000 − 24,000 = ₹8,000). A 40% win rate *feels* bad, but because the average win is double the average loss, expectancy is positive — the strategy is **worth keeping**. This is the mirror image of problem 1: win rate alone is meaningless without the win/loss ratio.

**3.** First trade: `R = 9,000 / 6,000 = +1.5R`. A full stop-out is by definition `−1R`. R-multiples are more useful because they normalise for position size: comparing "+₹9,000" against "−₹4,000" in raw rupees conflates the *result* with how *big* the trades were, whereas +1.5R vs −1R compares them on a single risk-adjusted scale. Across many trades, the distribution of R-multiples (lots of small −1R losers paid for by occasional +2R or +3R winners) tells you whether your exits and reward-to-risk are healthy.

**4.** (a) **FOMO / chasing** — entering an unplanned trade because of a move you're afraid to miss. (b) **Revenge trading** — oversizing to recover losses on an emotional timeline (also breaks position sizing). (c) **Anchoring** to the entry price — the ₹150 you paid is irrelevant to what the option is worth now. (d) **Overconfidence after a win streak** — a normal statistical run mistaken for proof of skill, leading to skipped process and oversizing.

**5.** Two reasons: (i) hitting the limit usually means you are *tilted* — you are now likely to trade emotionally, not well, so your judgement of "perfect setup" is itself compromised; (ii) the limit is a hard circuit-breaker against the revenge spiral, and its entire value is that it is non-negotiable — an exception once makes it an exception always. Most likely if you override it: the "perfect" trade is colored by your need to recover, you size it too large, and a ₹8,000 red day becomes the catastrophic day. Walking away costs nothing on a bad day and protects you from the worst ones.

**6.** The profit was most likely driven by **vega** — you were short volatility (short straddle has negative vega), and India VIX fell sharply from 20 to 13, collapsing the options' value in your favour; theta helped, but the index barely moving means delta contributed little and the big mover was the IV crush. Logging the attribution matters because "+₹12,000" alone might fool you into thinking you're a great market-direction forecaster, when in fact you made money *selling expensive volatility that mean-reverted*. That is a completely different, repeatable lesson — it tells you the edge is in selling high-IV premium, not in predicting where Nifty goes — and it warns you that the *same* trade in low IV (VIX 12) would offer far less cushion and far worse odds.
