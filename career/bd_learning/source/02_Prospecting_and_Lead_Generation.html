1. What Prospecting Actually Is (and the Mindset)

Prospecting is the work of finding people and companies who could plausibly buy what you sell, and starting a conversation with them. Lead generation is the broader system — inbound and outbound — that produces those potential buyers in the first place. If you remember one sentence from this whole guide, make it this: prospecting is not about selling; it is about starting the right conversations with the right people at the right time. The sale comes later. Your only job at this stage is to earn a first meeting.

Let us define the vocabulary you will hear every single day, because a beginner who mixes these up looks like a beginner:

  • Suspect — a company that might fit your market but you have not verified anything. Raw name on a list.
  • Lead — a specific person or company that has shown some fit or interest. A lead can be inbound (they raised a hand) or outbound (you identified them).
  • Prospect — a lead you have engaged and lightly qualified: they match your ICP (Ideal Customer Profile) and there is a plausible reason to talk.
  • Marketing Qualified Lead (MQL) — marketing thinks this lead is warm enough (downloaded an e-book, attended a webinar) to hand to sales.
  • Sales Qualified Lead (SQL) — sales has spoken to them and confirmed there is a real opportunity worth pursuing.
  • Opportunity — a qualified deal now tracked in the pipeline with an expected value and close date.
  • SDR / BDR — Sales Development Representative / Business Development Representative. The role you are pivoting into: the person who prospects and books meetings, then hands qualified opportunities to an AE (Account Executive) who closes.
The prospecting funnel (top to bottom)
Suspects → Leads → Contacted → Engaged → SQL / Meeting Booked → Opportunity
Each arrow is a conversion rate. Prospecting lives in the first four boxes. Your north-star output is "meetings booked" (also called SQLs or discovery calls).

1.1 The beginner mindset that actually works

You have no sales experience, so let us replace fear with a few durable beliefs:

  • You are a doctor, not a salesman. A good doctor diagnoses before prescribing. You ask questions, understand the buyer's situation, and only then decide whether you can help. This removes the "pushy" feeling entirely.
  • Rejection is data, not judgment. A "no" usually means wrong person, wrong time, or wrong message — rarely "you are a bad person." Most no's are simply "not now."
  • Prospecting is a numbers game wrapped around a quality game. Volume without relevance is spam. Relevance without volume is a hobby. You need both.
  • Consistency beats intensity. 40 thoughtful touches every day for a month beats 800 touches in one panic-filled week.
  • You are interrupting someone's day — so earn it. The buyer owes you nothing. Every message must give them a reason to spend 30 seconds on you.
Key takeaways: Prospecting = starting conversations, not closing. Learn the funnel vocabulary (suspect → lead → prospect → SQL → opportunity). Your output metric is meetings booked. Adopt the "doctor" mindset: diagnose before you prescribe, and treat every no as "not now / wrong fit," not personal failure.

2. Market Sizing: TAM, SAM, SOM

Before you send a single email you must know who is even worth contacting. Market sizing answers "how big is the opportunity, and which slice can I realistically win?" This is core to business development, and interviewers love it. Three nested circles:

TAM — Total Addressable Market
Total annual revenue available if 100% of every possible customer bought from you.
The whole ocean. Useful for "how big could this ever be?" Rarely reachable in practice.
SAM — Serviceable Addressable Market
The portion of TAM you can actually serve given your product, geography, language, regulation, and pricing.
The part of the ocean your boat can reach — e.g. only India, only regulated NBFCs, only companies above a size threshold.
SOM — Serviceable Obtainable Market
The share of SAM you can realistically capture in a given period, given your team, competition, and sales capacity.
The fish you can actually catch this year. This is your real target list size and revenue goal.

2.1 Two ways to size a market

Top-down starts from a big published number (analyst reports, industry bodies) and narrows down with percentages. Fast but easy to fudge. Bottom-up starts from the unit economics — number of target accounts × average price — and builds up. Slower but far more credible, and the method interviewers respect. Always prefer bottom-up when you can, and sanity-check it against a top-down number.

Worked Example — Sizing the market for a B2B fintech (India)

Suppose you sell a SaaS reconciliation & payments-analytics platform priced at ₹6,00,000 per year (₹6 lakh ARR — Annual Recurring Revenue) to mid-market companies. Let us size it bottom-up.

Step 1 — Define the buyer unit. Our sweet spot: Indian companies with ₹100–1,000 crore revenue that process high volumes of digital payments (e-commerce, D2C brands, NBFCs, insurance aggregators).

Step 2 — Count the accounts (SAM). Say there are ~40,000 such companies in India. Not all are reachable: we sell only in English, only to companies already on modern ERPs, and only those doing enough payment volume to need us. Estimate 35% qualify → SAM ≈ 14,000 companies.

Step 3 — TAM (top-down cross-check). If every one of the 40,000 bought at ₹6L: 40,000 × ₹6,00,000 = ₹2,400 crore TAM. SAM = 14,000 × ₹6,00,000 = ₹840 crore.

Step 4 — SOM (what you can win). You are one vendor among several, and a small team. Assume you can realistically win 4% of SAM over ~2 years: 14,000 × 4% = 560 customers. SOM revenue = 560 × ₹6,00,000 = ₹33.6 crore ARR.

Step 5 — Turn SOM into a prospecting target. If your win rate from meeting to close is 20%, you need 560 ÷ 0.20 = 2,800 meetings over two years, i.e. ~117/month. If it takes ~100 quality outbound touches to book one meeting, that is ~11,700 touches/month across the team. Now you know exactly how much activity the plan demands.

Common trap: Quoting a giant TAM ("the Indian fintech market is $1 trillion!") as if it were reachable. Interviewers and managers immediately discount you. Always walk TAM → SAM → SOM and show the assumptions. A defensible ₹33 crore SOM beats an indefensible ₹2,400 crore TAM every time.
Key takeaways: TAM = whole ocean, SAM = what your boat can reach, SOM = what you can catch this year. Prefer bottom-up (accounts × price), cross-check top-down. SOM converts directly into a meetings target and therefore a daily activity number — that is why sizing matters to a prospector, not just a strategist.

3. Targeting: Ideal Customer Profile (ICP) & Buyer Personas

SOM tells you how many. The ICP tells you which ones. Getting the ICP right is the single highest-leverage thing a prospector does, because a mediocre message to a perfect-fit account beats a brilliant message to a wrong-fit account.

3.1 Ideal Customer Profile (ICP) — the company you should target

The ICP describes the company (the account), not the person. It is built from firmographic, technographic, and trigger attributes:

  • Firmographics — industry/sector, company size (employees, revenue), geography, business model, growth stage.
  • Technographics — the tools they already use (e.g. they run on SAP, use Razorpay, or have no reconciliation tool at all).
  • Triggers / signals — recent events that create a need: new funding round, new CFO/CTO hire, entering a new market, a regulatory change (e.g. new RBI norms), rapid headcount growth, a public complaint about a problem you solve.
  • Pain fit — do they plausibly have the exact problem you solve, and is it painful enough to spend money on?
Example ICP — for the fintech reconciliation platform above

ICP one-liner: "Fast-scaling Indian digital-first companies (₹100–1,000 cr revenue) running high payment volumes across multiple gateways, with a dedicated finance function feeling reconciliation and settlement pain."

3.2 Buyer personas — the humans inside the ICP account

Inside every ICP account are several personas — role-based profiles of the individuals you will actually message. A persona captures their title, goals, pains, what they care about, and objections. You write different messages to different personas even at the same company.

Common trap: Writing one generic message and blasting it to every title. The CFO cares about ROI and risk; the ops manager cares about daily headaches; the engineer cares about integration effort. Same product, three different messages. Speaking to a persona in the language of their pain is 90% of personalization.
Key takeaways: ICP = the ideal company (firmographics + technographics + triggers + pain fit). Persona = the ideal person inside it (goals, pains, objections). Tighten your ICP until you can say it in one sentence, then tailor a distinct hook to each persona. Right account × right persona × right timing = the entire game.

4. The Buying Committee: Economic Buyer, Champion, User, Gatekeeper

In B2B, especially anything above a few lakh rupees, you almost never sell to one person — a group decides. This group is the buying committee (or DMU — Decision-Making Unit). Knowing the roles lets you build a coalition instead of pinning all hope on one contact.

RoleWhat they doWhat they wantHow you work them
Economic BuyerControls the budget; final yes/no on spend (often CFO/CXO)ROI, risk reduction, strategic fitGet here with the champion; speak money and outcomes, not features
ChampionInternal advocate who sells for you when you're not in the roomPersonal win — look good, solve their own pain, career upsideArm them with a business case, ROI numbers, one-pager; make them the hero
User (End User)Uses the product dailyEase, time saved, fewer headachesWin them with demos; their enthusiasm becomes proof for the buyer
Technical / Gatekeeper BuyerEvaluates fit; can veto (IT, security, procurement)Compliance, security, integration, priceSatisfy their checklist early so they don't block later
Gatekeeper (access)Controls access to decision-makers (EA, receptionist)Protect the boss's timeRespect + a legitimate reason; more in Section 12
Blocker / DetractorOpposes the change (fear, competitor loyalty, turf)Status quoUnderstand their objection; neutralize via the champion

Champion vs Economic Buyer — the distinction beginners miss: the champion wants your solution and pushes internally, but usually cannot sign the cheque. The economic buyer can sign but may never touch the product. A deal dies when you have a passionate champion but never reach the economic buyer, or when you pitch the economic buyer with no champion to defend the deal internally. You need both.

Multi-thread in practice

At "ScaleKart" (a D2C brand), the Finance Manager loves your recon tool (champion + user). She cannot approve ₹6 lakh. The CFO (economic buyer) will only meet if there is a clear ROI. IT (technical buyer) must approve the API integration and data security. Your play: help the Finance Manager build a one-page business case ("saves 4 days/month, ₹X in caught leakage"), get her to introduce you to the CFO, and separately answer IT's security questionnaire early. That is multi-threading — engaging several stakeholders in parallel so the deal doesn't hinge on one person.

Key takeaways: Complex B2B deals are decided by a committee. Map it: economic buyer (signs), champion (sells internally), user (validates), technical/procurement (can veto), gatekeeper (controls access). Multi-thread — never bet a deal on a single contact. A champion without an economic buyer, or an economic buyer without a champion, is a stalled deal.

5. Lead Sources: Inbound vs Outbound

Leads come from two directions. Inbound = the prospect comes to you (they raised a hand). Outbound = you go to the prospect (you reach out cold). A healthy pipeline uses both. As an SDR/BDR you will mostly do outbound and receive inbound MQLs to follow up.

DimensionInboundOutbound
DirectionProspect finds youYou find the prospect
ChannelsSEO/blog, webinars, e-books, referrals, reviews, ads, events they attendCold email, cold call, LinkedIn/social selling, targeted events, direct mail
IntentHigher — they already have interestLower — you create the interest
Speed to startSlow to build (content takes months)Fast — you can start today with a list
Control & targetingLow — you get who shows upHigh — you choose exact ICP accounts
Cost patternHigh upfront, low marginalLow upfront, ongoing effort per lead
ScalabilityCompounds over timeScales linearly with reps/tools

5.1 Inbound channels (what they are)

  • Content marketing & SEO — blogs, guides, and pages that rank on Google so buyers searching "payment reconciliation software India" find you. SEO = Search Engine Optimization.
  • Webinars & events — teach something useful; attendees become warm leads.
  • Lead magnets — a downloadable e-book, template, or calculator in exchange for an email.
  • Referrals — an existing happy customer or contact introduces you. The highest-converting source that exists — a warm intro can convert 3–5× better than a cold touch. Always ask, "Who else do you know facing this?"
  • Reviews & word of mouth — G2, Capterra, LinkedIn recommendations, community reputation.

5.2 Outbound channels (what you will actually run)

  • Cold email — scalable, low-cost, asynchronous. Section 7.
  • Cold calling — direct, high-signal, faster feedback. Section 8.
  • LinkedIn / social selling — build familiarity and reach people who ignore email. Section 9.
  • Events & conferences — fintech meetups, industry summits; collect cards, follow up fast.
  • Warm outbound — outbound to accounts showing a signal (visited your pricing page, new funding). Blends the targeting of outbound with the intent of inbound. The best-converting outbound there is.
Common trap ("spray and pray"): Buying a list of 50,000 emails and blasting the same message to all. It destroys your domain reputation, converts near zero, and can violate anti-spam norms. Volume is not a strategy. A tightly targeted list of 300 perfect-fit accounts with tailored messaging beats 50,000 random sends every single time.
Key takeaways: Inbound = they come to you (higher intent, slow to build); outbound = you go to them (lower intent, fast, full control). Referrals are the highest-converting source — always ask for them. As a new BDR you'll live mostly in outbound: email + call + LinkedIn, prioritizing accounts that show a buying signal ("warm outbound").

6. Building a Lead List & Data Tools

A prospecting engine runs on a lead list: a clean, targeted set of accounts and contacts with verified contact details. Quality of list > quantity of list. Here is the build process.

The 5-step list-build
1. Define ICP & personas → 2. Source accounts that match → 3. Find the right people (titles) → 4. Get & verify contact data (email, phone, LinkedIn) → 5. Enrich with a personalization hook per contact.
Step 5 is what separates a pro from a spammer — every row has a reason-to-reach-out note.

6.1 Where accounts and contacts come from

  • LinkedIn Sales Navigator — the workhorse. Filter companies and people by industry, size, geography, title, seniority, and "recently changed jobs / posted." Build lead lists and account lists here. (Concept: it is LinkedIn's premium prospecting search.)
  • Apollo.io — a database of companies + contacts with emails/phones, plus filters and email sequencing built in. Good all-in-one for a startup budget.
  • Lusha — a browser extension that reveals phone numbers and emails on LinkedIn/company sites. Quick contact-data lookups.
  • ZoomInfo — enterprise-grade contact and company database with deep firmographics and intent data; expensive, very comprehensive. Strong in the US, patchier for Indian mobile numbers.
  • Indian-context sources — the MCA (Ministry of Corporate Affairs) portal for company directors/registration, industry association member lists, RBI's lists of licensed NBFCs/banks, startup databases (Tracxn, YourStory), and event attendee lists.

Data hygiene note: emails decay ~2–3% per month as people change jobs. Always run addresses through a verifier (e.g. NeverBounce, ZeroBounce concept) before a big send, and remove bounces immediately. In India, mobile numbers are often more reliable than direct-dial desk lines — and note DND/regulatory rules for calls.

Worked mini-build — 50 target accounts in an afternoon

1. In Sales Navigator, filter: Industry = Internet / Financial Services; Headcount = 201–1,000; Geography = India; keyword "D2C" or "payments". Save 50 companies to an account list.
2. For each, filter people by title: "Finance Head" OR "Controller" OR "VP Finance" OR "Head of Payments". Save 1–2 contacts per account.
3. Use Apollo/Lusha to pull each contact's work email + mobile; verify emails.
4. For each row, add a one-line hook: e.g. "Posted about scaling to 3 new payment gateways last month" or "Company just raised Series B."
5. Export to your CRM / sequencing tool with columns: Company, Contact, Title, Email, Phone, LinkedIn URL, Hook, ICP-fit score (A/B/C).

Common trap: Treating list-building as done once you have emails. A list with no personalization hook column is a spam list waiting to happen. If you cannot write one specific true sentence about why you're contacting this person, they don't belong on the list yet.
Key takeaways: Build lists in 5 steps: ICP → accounts → people → verified data → personalization hook. Tools: Sales Navigator (search), Apollo (all-in-one), Lusha (quick contact reveal), ZoomInfo (enterprise depth). Verify emails before sending; in India, mobiles often beat desk lines and DND rules apply. Every row needs a true reason-to-reach-out.

7. Cold Email: Structure, Personalization, Sequences

Cold email is the backbone of modern outbound: cheap, scalable, and asynchronous (the buyer reads it when they want). But inboxes are war zones. A great cold email is short, relevant, about them, and easy to say yes to. Aim for 50–125 words. If it takes more than 15 seconds to read, it is too long.

7.1 The anatomy of a cold email

Cold email = Subject + Opener + Value + CTA (+ signature)
Subject line — 2–5 words, curiosity or relevance, no hype, no "RE:" tricks. Goal: get it opened.
Opener — one line about them, not you. A specific observation (trigger, post, role). Goal: earn the next line.
Value / body — the problem you solve + a proof point, framed for their persona. 2–3 sentences.
CTA (Call To Action) — one small, specific ask. Prefer "interest CTA" over "calendar CTA" on the first touch.
Signature — name, role, company, one credibility link. Keep it lean; heavy signatures hurt deliverability.

The subject line

  • Do: "quick question," "reconciliation at {Company}," "{Company} + settlements," "idea for month-end close." Lowercase often feels human, not marketing.
  • Don't: "REVOLUTIONARY AI PLATFORM 🚀", "Increase revenue 300%!!!", anything that screams broadcast.

The opener (the make-or-break line)

Never open with "I hope this email finds you well" or "My name is X and I work at Y, we are a leading provider of…" — that is the single most-ignored opening in sales. Open with them: their post, their role, their company's trigger.

The CTA — interest-based beats calendar-based on touch #1

Asking "Do you have 30 minutes Tuesday at 3?" on a cold first email is a big ask from a stranger. An interest CTA is lower-friction: "Worth a quick look?" or "Should I send over how {similar company} did it?" You earn the meeting on touch #2–3 once they've replied.

7.2 Ready-to-use templates

Template 1 — B2B fintech product, to a Finance Head (problem-led)

Subject: reconciliation at {{Company}}

Hi {{FirstName}},

Saw {{Company}} now runs payments across Razorpay, PayU and Cashfree — that usually means month-end reconciliation turns into a multi-day, spreadsheet-heavy grind for the finance team.

We help D2C & NBFC finance teams auto-match settlements across gateways and close the books in ~2 days instead of 6. {{SimilarCompany}} caught ₹40 lakh of settlement leakage in the first quarter.

Worth a quick look, or should I send a 2-min Loom showing how it'd work for {{Company}}?

Best,
{{YourName}} · {{Company}} · [1-line credibility link]

Template 2 — Financial-services offering, to a CFO (ROI / economic-buyer led)

Subject: {{Company}} + cash visibility

Hi {{FirstName}},

Most CFOs scaling past ₹200 cr tell us the same thing: they can't see real-time cash across gateways and banks, so decisions wait on the finance team's manual roll-up.

We give you a live cash position and auto-reconciled books — typically paying for itself within one quarter via caught leakage and freed-up finance hours.

Open to me sharing a one-page ROI view built around {{Company}}'s volumes? No meeting needed to start — just reply "send it."

Best,
{{YourName}}

Template 3 — Trigger-based (they just raised funding / hired)

Subject: congrats on the Series B

Hi {{FirstName}},

Congrats on the raise — scaling usually means transaction volume explodes faster than the finance team can hire.

That's the exact moment reconciliation breaks. We help fast-scaling teams keep books clean and close on time without adding 3 analysts. Happy to share what worked for {{SimilarCompany}} post-raise.

Useful to see? Just say the word and I'll send a short breakdown.

Best,
{{YourName}}

Template 4 — The follow-up ("bump"), sent 2–3 days later

Subject: (reply in the same thread — keep subject)

Hi {{FirstName}}, floating this back to the top — I know month-end is chaos.

Quick yes/no: is reconciliation across gateways currently a pain worth 15 minutes, or should I close the loop and not clutter your inbox?

Either answer is genuinely helpful. Thanks, {{YourName}}

Note the "breakup" style follow-up: giving the prospect an easy out ("should I close the loop?") paradoxically lifts reply rates, because it is low-pressure and respects their time.

7.3 Personalization at scale

Personalization does not mean writing 300 emails by hand. It means every email has a true, specific opener. The trick is tiered personalization:

  • Tier 1 (1:1) — top ~20 dream accounts. Fully hand-written, deep research (their earnings call, a specific LinkedIn post). Highest effort, highest reply rate.
  • Tier 2 (1:few) — segment by trigger or persona. One strong template with 1–2 variable lines ("Saw {{Company}} added {{Gateway}}…"). Most of your volume.
  • Tier 3 (1:many) — same industry, lighter touch, one relevant industry insight. Lowest effort, use sparingly.

The "compliment–context–connect" opener works at scale: a specific observation (context), why it matters, then the bridge to your value. Personalization variables — {{FirstName}}, {{Company}}, {{Trigger}} — get merged from your CRM/sequencer.

7.4 Sequences & cadences (email)

A sequence (or cadence) is a pre-planned series of touches over time. Almost nobody replies to touch #1. A typical email-only sequence is 4–6 emails over ~2–3 weeks, each adding a new angle (problem, proof, different persona pain, case study, breakup). Never just resend the same email.

TouchDayAngle
Email 1Day 1Problem + proof, interest CTA
Email 2Day 3Bump in same thread, easy yes/no
Email 3Day 6New angle: different persona pain or a case study
Email 4Day 10Short insight / relevant resource, no ask

7.5 Deliverability basics (so you actually land in the inbox)

Deliverability = whether your email reaches the inbox vs spam. A beginner who ignores this sends into the void. The essentials:

  • Authenticate your domain — set up SPF, DKIM, and DMARC (DNS records that prove your emails are legitimate). Ask your IT/admin; non-negotiable.
  • Warm up new domains/inboxes — start with low volume and ramp slowly. Don't blast 500 on day one from a fresh domain.
  • Use a separate sending domain — send outbound from a look-alike domain (e.g. getcompany.com) so a spam hit doesn't burn your main domain.
  • Keep volume human — roughly ≤50 cold emails/inbox/day; use multiple inboxes to scale rather than hammering one.
  • Protect reply rate & low bounce — verify emails, remove bounces, avoid spammy words ("free," "guarantee," excessive links/images), and always include a real signature and an unsubscribe path.
  • Plain and personal beats fancy — text-like emails with few links land better than image-heavy marketing templates.
Common trap ("pitching in the first line"): Opening with "We are a leading AI-powered fintech platform that…" The reader's guard slams shut. The first line must be about them. Your company name and pitch come after you've earned the second line. Talk about their world first, yours second.
Common trap (giving up after one follow-up): Most replies come on touches 2–5, yet beginners quit after one nudge. Roughly half of all sales replies arrive after the first follow-up. Persistence — polite, value-adding, spaced out — is where the meetings are.
Key takeaways: Cold email = Subject + Opener (about them) + Value + one small CTA. Keep it under ~125 words. First-touch CTA should be interest-based, not calendar-based. Personalize in tiers, run a 4–6 email sequence with new angles (never resends), and protect deliverability with SPF/DKIM/DMARC, warm-up, low volume, and verified lists. Follow up — the meetings live in touches 2–5.

8. Cold Calling: Openers, Pattern Interrupts, Rebuttals

Cold calling feels terrifying to a beginner and is the fastest way to get real answers. Email is a monologue; a call is a conversation where you learn in 60 seconds whether there is a fit. The goal of a cold call is not to sell — it is to earn 30 more seconds, then a meeting.

8.1 Mindset & preparation

  • Detach from the outcome. Your job is a good process — dials made, conversations had. Meetings are a by-product.
  • Smile and stand up. It genuinely changes your voice — warmer, more confident.
  • Slow down. Nervous reps rush. Lower your pace and pitch slightly; it signals calm authority.
  • Pre-call research (30 seconds). Know the company, their likely trigger, and one reason this person specifically.
  • Best times: generally early morning or late afternoon; avoid Monday 9am and Friday evening. Test and track for your market.

8.2 The opener & the pattern interrupt

A pattern interrupt is an opener that breaks the buyer's autopilot "no." Because most cold callers sound identical, sounding different buys you attention. Two proven structures:

  • Permission-based opener — honest and disarming: "Hi Farhan, this is a cold call — do you want to hang up, or give me 30 seconds and then decide?" The candor is the pattern interrupt; most people give you the 30 seconds because you respected them.
  • Curiosity opener — "Hi Farhan, you weren't expecting my call — mind if I tell you why I picked up the phone?"
Full sample cold-call script — fintech recon platform

1. Pattern-interrupt opener (permission-based):
"Hi Farhan, this is Sai from ReconEdge. Full honesty — this is a cold call. Do you want to hang up, or can I have 30 seconds to tell you why I called, and you decide from there?"
[Pause. Let them answer. Almost always: "Go on."]

2. Reason for the call (the hook, tied to a trigger):
"Appreciate it. I noticed {{Company}} runs payments across three gateways now. Usually when finance teams hit that point, month-end reconciliation turns into a 5–6 day spreadsheet marathon and settlement leakage starts slipping through. Is that anywhere close to reality for your team, or have you already solved it?"

3. Discovery (let them talk — one open question):
"How are you handling settlement matching across gateways today?"
[Listen. This is where you learn fit. Take notes.]

4. Micro-pitch (one sentence, tied to what they said):
"Got it. That's exactly the pain we remove — we auto-match settlements across all your gateways so the team closes in ~2 days and catches leakage automatically. {{SimilarCompany}} caught ₹40 lakh in Q1."

5. The ask (soft, specific):
"I'm not going to sell you anything on this call. Would it make sense to grab 20 minutes later this week so I can show you exactly how it'd map to {{Company}}'s setup? Does Thursday 4pm or Friday 11am work better?"

[Offer two specific times — the "alternative choice" close makes saying yes easy.]

6. Confirm & close:
"Perfect, Thursday 4pm. I'll send a calendar invite and a 2-line agenda. What's the best email? … Thanks Farhan, talk Thursday."

8.3 Handling brush-offs — the rebuttal cheat-sheet

A brush-off is a reflex, not a real objection ("send me an email," "I'm busy," "we already have a vendor"). Acknowledge, reframe, and ask a small question. Never argue.

They sayYou say (rebuttal)
"I'm busy right now.""Totally expected — I'll be quick or we can pick a better time. Is now genuinely bad, or can you give me 30 seconds and then decide?"
"Just send me an email.""Happy to — so I send something useful and not generic, can I ask one quick question: is settlement reconciliation across gateways actually a pain for your team right now?"
"We already use [competitor].""Makes sense, most teams your size have something. Out of curiosity, does it auto-match across all three gateways, or does the team still fix breaks manually? That's usually the gap."
"We don't have budget.""Fair — and I'm not asking you to spend today. Most clients find it pays for itself in a quarter via caught leakage. Worth 20 minutes to see if the math works for you?"
"Not interested.""Fair enough — and I may have caught you at a bad moment. Quick check: is that because reconciliation is genuinely smooth for you, or just not a priority this quarter?"

"Call me next quarter.""Will do — and so it's worth your time then, can I send one short case study now and lock a tentative 15-min slot for the first week of next quarter?"

8.4 Voicemail

Most calls go unanswered — leave a short, curiosity-driven voicemail (under 20 seconds) and follow with an email referencing it ("just left you a voicemail"). Say your name and number slowly, twice.

Voicemail script (≤20 seconds)

"Hi Farhan, it's Sai from ReconEdge — 98xxxxxx12. I called about cutting your month-end reconciliation from ~6 days to 2 across your payment gateways. I'll follow up by email, but if it's easier, I'm on 98xxxxxx12. Thanks."

Common trap: Reading the script robotically and refusing to pause. The most powerful tool on a cold call is silence after a question — let them answer. Talking over your own question, or launching into a 90-second feature monologue, guarantees a hang-up. Ask, then shut up and listen.
Key takeaways: A cold call's goal is 30 more seconds, then a meeting — not a sale. Open with a pattern interrupt (permission-based works best), tie your reason to a trigger, ask one open discovery question, deliver a one-sentence micro-pitch, and close with two specific time options. Treat brush-offs as reflexes: acknowledge, reframe, ask a small question. Pause and listen — silence sells.

9. LinkedIn & Social Selling

Social selling means using LinkedIn to build familiarity, credibility, and relationships before and alongside the ask. Buyers who ignore email and calls often respond on LinkedIn because it feels personal and low-pressure. It also lets them "check you out" — which is why your profile matters.

9.1 Optimize your profile first (you are the landing page)

Before you message anyone, fix your profile — prospects will click it. Optimize it to sell to them, not to recruiters:

  • Photo — professional, friendly, clear headshot. Banner — a simple branded image or your value proposition.
  • Headline — not just "BDR at ReconEdge." Say who you help and how: "Helping Indian finance teams cut month-end close from 6 days to 2 | Payments & reconciliation."
  • About section — buyer-focused: the problems you solve, who you help, a proof point, a soft CTA.
  • Featured — a case study, a helpful post, or a short explainer.
  • Activity — comment thoughtfully and post occasionally so you don't look dormant.

9.2 Connection requests & value-first messaging

Golden rule: never pitch in the connection request. First connect, then build a little rapport, then (maybe) offer value, then ask. The "connect-and-blast" pitch is the LinkedIn equivalent of spam.

Sample connection request (personalized, no pitch)

"Hi Farhan — I work with finance teams at fast-scaling D2C & NBFC companies on payments & reconciliation, and I really liked your post on scaling operations. Would love to connect and follow your work."

(Under 300 characters. Specific, warm, zero ask. Even a blank/no-note request often works — but a specific note lifts acceptance.)

Sample follow-up message (2–3 days after they accept) — value first

"Thanks for connecting, Farhan! Not pitching anything — just saw {{Company}} now runs multiple payment gateways, and month-end reconciliation tends to get painful at that stage. We put together a short checklist finance teams use to cut settlement leakage — happy to send it over if useful. No strings."

Then, only after they engage: "Glad it helped. If it's ever worth a deeper look at how {{Company}} could automate this, I'm happy to walk you through a 15-min example — no pressure either way."

Sample "trigger" outreach message

"Congrats on the Series B, Farhan! Scaling this fast usually means transaction volume outpaces the finance team. If reconciliation ever starts eating your month-end, I've helped a few post-raise teams get ahead of it — happy to share what worked. Either way, genuinely rooting for the ride."

9.3 Content & the "surround sound" effect

Posting useful content (short lessons, mistakes-we-see, mini case studies) means prospects encounter your name repeatedly across email, calls, and their feed — the "surround sound" / omnipresence effect. When they finally reply, you are already familiar, not a stranger. You do not need to be a creator; commenting intelligently on your ICP's posts is enough to build presence.

Common trap: Sending a connection request and immediately following with a hard sales pitch the second it's accepted ("Thanks for connecting! Can we book 30 mins to discuss our platform?"). It reeks of automation and gets you ignored or reported. Lead with genuine relevance and value; the ask comes later, earned.
Key takeaways: Fix your profile first — it's your landing page; headline should say who you help and how. Never pitch in the connection request. Sequence: connect (specific note) → value-first message → soft ask, spaced over days. Add value through comments/content so you become familiar ("surround sound"). LinkedIn reaches people email and calls can't.

10. Multi-Channel Cadence Design

The best prospectors do not pick email or call or LinkedIn — they orchestrate all three into a single multi-channel cadence (also called a sequence). Different people respond on different channels; hitting all three multiplies your odds and creates that "surround sound" familiarity. A cadence spreads ~10–15 touches across ~2 weeks so you're persistent without being annoying.

Example 2-week multi-channel cadence (per prospect)
DayChannelAction / Angle
Day 1LinkedInSend connection request (specific, no pitch)
Day 1EmailEmail #1 — problem + proof, interest CTA
Day 2CallCall #1 — pattern-interrupt opener; if no answer, voicemail #1
Day 3EmailEmail #2 — bump in thread, easy yes/no
Day 4LinkedInIf connected: value-first message (share checklist/resource)
Day 6CallCall #2 — different time of day; voicemail #2 referencing email
Day 7EmailEmail #3 — new angle: case study or different persona pain
Day 9LinkedInEngage with their post / comment thoughtfully (no ask)
Day 10CallCall #3 — final call attempt; voicemail
Day 11EmailEmail #4 — short insight / relevant resource, soft ask
Day 14EmailEmail #5 — breakup email ("closing the loop")
Day 14LinkedInOptional: brief breakup DM mirroring the email

Total: ~12 touches across 3 channels in 14 days. If they reply positively at any point, you exit the cadence and switch to a human 1:1 conversation. After the cadence ends with no response, move them to a "nurture" list and re-approach in ~90 days with a fresh angle or trigger.

Design principles: (1) vary the channel — don't send five emails in a row; (2) vary the angle — each touch adds something new; (3) space it out — daily hammering feels desperate; (4) always have an exit — a positive reply ends the automation and a human takes over.

Key takeaways: Orchestrate email + call + LinkedIn into one ~12-touch, 2-week cadence. Vary channel and angle each touch; space them out; exit to a human conversation the moment someone replies positively. Multi-channel beats single-channel because buyers live on different channels and repetition builds familiarity.

11. Qualification Frameworks

Qualification = deciding whether a lead is worth pursuing, so you spend time on deals that can actually close. Frameworks are just checklists of what to learn. You do not interrogate a prospect — you weave these questions into a natural conversation. Below are the big ones, when to use each, and example questions.

FrameworkStands forBest when…
BANTBudget, Authority, Need, TimelineSimple, transactional sales; fast triage. Classic but can feel seller-centric.
CHAMPChallenges, Authority, Money, PrioritizationModern, buyer-centric; leads with the prospect's challenge, not your budget question.
ANUMAuthority, Need, Urgency, MoneyWhen qualifying authority early matters most; fast-moving inbound.
MEDDIC / MEDDPICCMetrics, Economic buyer, Decision criteria, Decision process, Identify pain, Champion (+ Paper process, Competition)Complex, high-value, multi-stakeholder enterprise deals. Rigorous.
SPINSituation, Problem, Implication, Need-payoff (a questioning method)Consultative selling; discovery calls where you build the buyer's own awareness of pain.
GPCT(BA)Goals, Plans, Challenges, Timeline (+ Budget, Authority)Inbound / consultative; anchors on the prospect's goals first.

11.1 BANT — the classic starter

  • Budget — "Have you set aside budget for solving this, or would this need a business case?"
  • Authority — "Besides yourself, who else would weigh in on a decision like this?"
  • Need — "How much of a problem is reconciliation for the team today?"
  • Timeline — "If you found the right fit, when would you want it live?"

Caution: BANT leads with budget, which can feel pushy and disqualify early-stage prospects who could find budget if the pain is real. Many modern teams prefer CHAMP or GPCT.

11.2 CHAMP — lead with the challenge

  • Challenges — "What's the biggest headache in your month-end process right now?"
  • Authority — "Who owns this decision alongside you?"
  • Money — "What's this problem costing you in time or leakage today?"
  • Prioritization — "Where does fixing this sit against your other priorities this quarter?"

11.3 ANUM — authority-first

Same ingredients, reordered to qualify authority first ("Are you the right person for this, or should we loop someone in?"), then Need, Urgency, Money. Useful when you get lots of inbound and must quickly find who can actually move.

11.4 MEDDIC / MEDDPICC — for big, complex deals

  • Metrics — the quantified outcome ("cut close from 6 to 2 days; recover ₹40L/yr").
  • Economic buyer — who controls the money (find and reach them).
  • Decision criteria — what they'll judge vendors on (security, integration, price).
  • Decision process — the actual steps to a signature (legal, procurement, board?).
  • Identify pain — the concrete pain driving the purchase.
  • Champion — your internal advocate with influence.
  • (MEDDPICC adds) — Paper process (contract/procurement steps) & Competition.

MEDDIC is less "one call checklist" and more a discipline you fill in across a long enterprise cycle. If any letter is blank, you have a risk to work.

11.5 SPIN — the questioning method (deep dive)

SPIN is not a checklist to "qualify in/out"; it is a sequence of questions that makes the buyer articulate their own pain and its cost, so they talk themselves into wanting a solution. This is the most useful framework for a beginner to internalize for discovery calls.

SPIN question set — recon platform discovery call

S — Situation (facts, keep brief):
• "How many payment gateways are you running today?"
• "How does the team handle settlement reconciliation currently — manual, spreadsheets, a tool?"
• "How big is the finance team handling month-end?"

P — Problem (surface the pain):
• "How long does month-end reconciliation take you right now?"
• "How often do settlement mismatches or unmatched entries slip through?"
• "Where does the process break down most?"

I — Implication (make the pain expensive — the crucial step):
• "When close takes 6 days, what does that delay do to your reporting and cash decisions?"
• "If leakage goes uncaught for months, what's that costing over a year?"
• "What's the impact on the team — burnout, errors, audit risk — of doing this manually?"

N — Need-payoff (let them state the value):
• "If you could close in 2 days instead of 6, what would that free the team up to do?"
• "How valuable would it be to catch that leakage automatically every month?"
• "If reconciliation just… ran itself, what would that be worth to you?"

Notice: by the Need-payoff stage, the prospect is describing the benefits back to you. That is the goal — they sell themselves.

11.6 GPCT(BA) — goals-first (HubSpot's framework)

  • Goals — "What are the finance team's key goals this year?"
  • Plans — "How are you planning to hit them? What's in place today?"
  • Challenges — "What's getting in the way?"
  • Timeline — "By when do you need this solved?"
  • (+ Budget, Authority) — added once goals/challenges are clear.

Which should a beginner use? Start with CHAMP or GPCT for discovery (buyer-centric, natural), use SPIN questioning inside those conversations to deepen pain, keep BANT/ANUM as a quick mental triage for whether to invest more time, and graduate to MEDDIC/MEDDPICC when deals get large and multi-stakeholder.

Common trap: Treating qualification as an interrogation — firing "What's your budget? Who's the decision-maker? What's your timeline?" like a form. It kills rapport. Weave the questions into a genuine conversation, lead with their challenges and goals, and always trade insight for information.
Key takeaways: Qualification = investing time only in winnable deals. BANT (fast triage, budget-first), CHAMP/GPCT (buyer-centric, challenge/goal-first — best default), ANUM (authority-first), MEDDIC/MEDDPICC (complex enterprise), SPIN (a questioning method to build pain awareness). Don't interrogate — weave questions naturally. Master SPIN's Implication and Need-payoff questions; they make the buyer sell themselves.

12. Getting Past Gatekeepers

A gatekeeper — receptionist, executive assistant (EA), or an ops person — controls access to the decision-maker. Their job is to filter out sellers. The mistake is treating them as an obstacle to trick. Treat them as an ally to enlist. Respect gets you further than cleverness.

12.1 Principles

  • Be respectful and confident, never sneaky. Tricks ("tell him it's personal") burn trust and travel back to the boss.
  • Be brief and clear about why you're calling — vagueness triggers the block reflex.
  • Enlist them. A good EA knows the org better than anyone. Ask for their help genuinely.
  • Use their name, be human, say thank you. They remember the polite ones.
Gatekeeper scripts

The honest, respectful ask:
"Hi, I'm hoping you can help me — I'm trying to reach whoever owns payment reconciliation on the finance team, and I'm honestly not sure who that is. Would that be Farhan, or someone else? … Any chance you could point me the right way?"

If asked "What's this regarding?":
"Of course — I help finance teams cut month-end reconciliation from ~6 days to 2. I wanted to check if it's relevant for {{Company}}, and figured you'd know who to point me to."

Enlisting the EA as an ally:
"You clearly know how things work there better than I do — what's the best way to get 15 minutes with Farhan? Morning or afternoon usually better for him?"

Leaving a message with them:
"If it's easier, could you let Farhan know Sai from ReconEdge called about reducing month-end close time, and I'll follow up by email today? Thanks so much for your help — really appreciate it."

Other tactics: call slightly before or after standard hours when decision-makers sometimes answer their own phone; combine channels (email/LinkedIn to the decision-maker so your name is familiar when you call); and when you do reach the buyer, reference the helpful EA by name.

Key takeaways: Gatekeepers are allies, not obstacles. Be respectful, brief, and honest about why you're calling; enlist their help and use their name. Never trick them — it backfires. Combine channels so your name is already familiar, and try off-hours when execs answer their own phones.

13. Prospecting Metrics & How Many Touches It Takes

Prospecting is measurable, and what gets measured gets improved. Metrics fall into two buckets: activity metrics (inputs you control) and outcome metrics (results). A beginner should obsess over activity while learning, because outcomes lag — but always watch conversion rates to know if the activity is quality activity.

MetricTypeWhat it tells youRough healthy benchmark*
Dials / calls madeActivityCalling effort40–80/day (SDR)
Connect rateConversion% of dials that reach a live person~5–15% of dials
Emails sentActivityEmail effortVaries; ≤50/inbox/day

Reply rateConversionRelevance of message~5–10% (cold)

LinkedIn connect-accept rateConversionProfile + request quality~30–40%

13.1 How many touches does it really take?

Beginners quit far too early. The reality: it typically takes 8–12 touches across multiple channels to get a response from a cold prospect, and roughly half of all replies come after the first follow-up. One email or one call is not a real attempt — it is barely a hello. A "contacted" prospect who got a single email should be considered untouched.

Worked example — funnel math backwards from a meetings goal

Goal: 10 meetings booked this month. Assume: positive-reply-to-meeting ~50%, so you need ~20 positive replies. If positive reply rate ≈ 2% of prospects touched in a full cadence, you need ~1,000 prospects in cadence. At ~12 touches each, that's ~12,000 touches/month ≈ ~600 touches per working day.

Split across channels: say ~250 emails + ~60 dials + ~40 LinkedIn actions per day (across a fresh + in-flight mix), using multiple inboxes for deliverability. Now "book 10 meetings" is a concrete daily activity plan — and if your conversion rates are better than assumed, you need less volume. This is why you track conversion rates: they let you hit goals with less brute force.

Leading vs lagging indicators: activity metrics are leading (you control them today, they predict tomorrow's results); meetings and revenue are lagging (they arrive weeks later). When results dip, first check whether activity dropped, then whether conversion (message/targeting quality) dropped.

Common trap: Judging yourself only on meetings in week one and panicking. Early on, control the inputs (dials, quality emails, connects) and watch conversion rates. If activity is high but conversion is near zero, the problem is targeting or message, not effort — fix the list and the opener before doing more volume.
Key takeaways: Track activity (dials, emails, connects) and conversion (connect, reply, positive-reply, meeting-held, meeting→SQL). It takes ~8–12 multi-channel touches; ~half of replies come after the first follow-up — one touch is not an attempt. Work backwards from your meetings goal to a daily activity plan, and use conversion rates to hit goals with less brute force. Low conversion = fix targeting/message, not just volume.

14. Cadence Discipline & CRM Hygiene

All the tactics above collapse without discipline and clean data. (The CRM is deep-dived in a separate guide; here are the essentials a prospector must live by.)

  • Time-block prospecting daily. Protect 2–3 hours of "power hours" for outreach; it is the first thing to get crowded out and the last thing you can afford to skip.
  • Work the cadence to completion. Don't abandon prospects at touch 2. Finish the sequence for everyone who doesn't reply.
  • Log everything in the CRM. CRM = Customer Relationship Management system (e.g. Salesforce, HubSpot, Zoho). Every call, email, and note goes in — same day. If it's not in the CRM, it didn't happen.
  • Keep data clean. Update statuses, remove bounced emails, mark bad numbers, correct titles. Dirty data quietly wrecks conversion and deliverability.
  • Set next steps and reminders. Every active prospect must have a scheduled next action with a date. No "floating" leads.
  • Nurture the no's. "Not now" prospects go to a nurture list with a 90-day recall and a fresh trigger to re-approach.
Key takeaways: Discipline beats talent in prospecting. Time-block daily power hours, finish every cadence, log every touch in the CRM the same day, keep data clean, always set a dated next step, and nurture the "not-now" leads for a 90-day re-approach. If it's not in the CRM, it didn't happen.

15. Question Bank — 40 Q&A with Model Answers

Use these to test yourself and to prepare for BDR/SDR interviews. Model answers are concise; expand with your own examples.

Q1: What is the difference between a lead, a prospect, and an opportunity?
A: A lead is a person/company showing some fit or interest. A prospect is a lead you've engaged and lightly qualified against your ICP. An opportunity is a qualified deal in the pipeline with an expected value and close date.
Q2: What is the goal of prospecting?
A: To start relevant conversations with the right buyers and book qualified meetings — not to close the sale. The output metric is meetings booked (SQLs).
Q3: Define TAM, SAM, and SOM.
A: TAM = total revenue if everyone possible bought (whole market). SAM = the slice you can serve given product/geo/regulation. SOM = the share of SAM you can realistically win in a period. SOM is your real target.
Q4: Top-down vs bottom-up market sizing — which is better?
A: Bottom-up (accounts × average price) is more credible and defensible; top-down (big number × %) is faster but easy to fudge. Use bottom-up and cross-check with top-down.
Q5: What is an ICP and what goes into it?
A: Ideal Customer Profile — the ideal company to target. Built from firmographics (industry, size, geo), technographics (tools they use), triggers (funding, new hires, regulation), and pain fit.
Q6: How is a buyer persona different from an ICP?
A: ICP describes the ideal company; a persona describes an ideal person inside it — their role, goals, pains, and objections. You tailor messaging per persona.
Q7: Explain economic buyer vs champion.
A: The economic buyer controls budget and gives the final yes (often the CFO). The champion is an internal advocate who sells for you when you're not in the room but usually can't sign. You need both.
Q8: What is multi-threading and why does it matter?
A: Engaging multiple stakeholders (buyer, champion, user, IT) in parallel so the deal doesn't die if one contact leaves or goes cold. Single-threaded deals are fragile.
Q9: Inbound vs outbound — key trade-offs?
A: Inbound = prospect comes to you: higher intent, slow to build, low targeting control, compounds over time. Outbound = you go to them: lower intent, fast to start, full targeting control, scales with effort.
Q10: Why are referrals so valuable?
A: They arrive with borrowed trust, so they convert far higher than cold touches. Always ask happy contacts, "Who else do you know facing this problem?"
Q11: Name four prospecting data tools and what each is for.
A: LinkedIn Sales Navigator (targeted people/company search), Apollo.io (all-in-one database + sequencing), Lusha (quick contact-data reveal), ZoomInfo (enterprise-grade database + intent). Plus Indian sources like MCA and RBI lists.
Q12: What are the five steps to build a lead list?
A: Define ICP/personas → source matching accounts → find the right people (titles) → get and verify contact data → enrich each row with a personalization hook.
Q13: What are the components of a good cold email?
A: A relevant short subject; an opener about them; a value section (problem + proof for their persona); one small CTA; a lean signature. Under ~125 words.
Q14: Why is an interest-based CTA better than a calendar CTA on the first email?
A: Asking a stranger for a specific 30-minute slot is high-friction. "Worth a quick look?" is low-friction and lifts replies; you earn the meeting on touch 2–3.
Q15: What is deliverability and how do you protect it?
A: Whether your email lands in the inbox vs spam. Protect it with SPF/DKIM/DMARC authentication, domain warm-up, a separate sending domain, low daily volume, verified lists (low bounce), and plain, personal, low-link emails.
Q16: What is personalization at scale?
A: Giving every email a true, specific opener without hand-writing each one — using tiered effort (1:1 for top accounts, 1:few by trigger/persona, 1:many by industry) and merge variables.
Q17: What is a sequence/cadence and how long should an email one be?
A: A pre-planned series of spaced touches. An email cadence is typically 4–6 emails over 2–3 weeks, each adding a new angle — never a resend.
Q18: What is the goal of a cold call?
A: To earn the next 30 seconds and then a meeting — not to sell on the call. Learn fit fast and book the discovery call.
Q19: What is a pattern interrupt?
A: An unexpected opener that breaks the buyer's reflex "no." The permission-based opener ("this is a cold call — 30 seconds and you decide?") works because its honesty is disarming.
Q20: How do you handle "just send me an email"?
A: Agree, then trade for one qualifying question so the email is relevant: "Happy to — so I send something useful, is reconciliation across gateways actually a pain right now?"
Q21: How do you handle "we already use a competitor"?
A: Acknowledge it's normal, then probe the gap: "Most teams your size have something. Does it auto-match across all gateways, or does the team still fix breaks manually?"
Q22: What makes a good voicemail?
A: Under 20 seconds, curiosity-driven, name and number said slowly twice, and paired with a follow-up email referencing it. Its job is to make the callback/email land, not to pitch.
Q23: Why is silence powerful on a cold call?
A: After asking a question, pausing lets the prospect answer and reveals real information. Talking over your own question or monologuing features gets you hung up on.
Q24: What should you fix before doing LinkedIn outreach?
A: Your own profile — prospects will click it. Make the headline say who you help and how, and the About section buyer-focused, because your profile is your landing page.
Q25: What's the golden rule of LinkedIn connection requests?
A: Never pitch in the request. Connect with a specific, warm note; build a little rapport; offer value; then make a soft ask — spaced over days.
Q26: What is the "surround sound" / omnipresence effect?
A: When a prospect sees your name across email, calls, and their LinkedIn feed, you become familiar, so when they finally engage you're not a stranger. Content and thoughtful comments create it.
Q27: Why use a multi-channel cadence instead of just email?
A: Different buyers respond on different channels, and repetition across channels builds familiarity. Combining email + call + LinkedIn multiplies response odds versus any single channel.
Q28: Outline a healthy 2-week cadence.
A: ~12 touches across email, call, and LinkedIn: connect + email #1 day 1; call + voicemail day 2; email bump day 3; LinkedIn value day 4; more calls/emails with new angles through day 11; breakup email day 14. Exit to a human on any positive reply.
Q29: What does BANT stand for and what's its weakness?
A: Budget, Authority, Need, Timeline. Weakness: leading with budget is seller-centric and can prematurely disqualify prospects who'd find budget if the pain were made real.
Q30: How does CHAMP differ from BANT?
A: CHAMP (Challenges, Authority, Money, Prioritization) leads with the prospect's challenge instead of budget — more buyer-centric and natural in a modern discovery call.
Q31: When would you use MEDDIC/MEDDPICC?
A: For complex, high-value, multi-stakeholder enterprise deals. It tracks Metrics, Economic buyer, Decision criteria, Decision process, Identify pain, Champion (+ Paper process, Competition). Any blank letter is a deal risk.
Q32: Explain SPIN and why it's powerful.
A: A questioning sequence — Situation, Problem, Implication, Need-payoff. Implication questions make the pain feel expensive; Need-payoff questions get the buyer to state the value themselves, so they sell themselves on solving it.
Q33: Give an example of an Implication question.
A: "When close takes 6 days instead of 2, what does that delay do to your cash decisions and reporting?" It quantifies the cost of the status quo.
Q34: What is GPCT and when is it useful?
A: Goals, Plans, Challenges, Timeline (+ Budget, Authority). Goals-first and consultative — great for inbound and discovery where you anchor on what the prospect wants to achieve.
Q35: How should you treat a gatekeeper?
A: As an ally, not an obstacle. Be respectful, brief, and honest about why you're calling; enlist their help and use their name. Never use tricks — they backfire.
Q36: Name three activity metrics and three conversion metrics.
A: Activity: dials, emails sent, LinkedIn connects. Conversion: connect rate, reply/positive-reply rate, meeting-held rate (and meeting→SQL rate).
Q37: How many touches does it typically take to get a response?
A: Around 8–12 multi-channel touches, and roughly half of replies come after the first follow-up. A single email or call is not a real attempt.
Q38: Your activity is high but you're booking no meetings. What do you check?
A: Conversion, not effort. The problem is likely targeting (wrong ICP/list) or message (weak opener/value). Fix the list and the opener before adding more volume.
Q39: What's the difference between leading and lagging indicators in prospecting?
A: Leading = activity you control today that predicts results (dials, quality emails). Lagging = downstream outcomes that arrive later (meetings, revenue). Manage the leading to move the lagging.
Q40: Why does CRM hygiene matter to a prospector?
A: Clean, fully logged data drives accurate follow-up, better conversion, and deliverability. Every touch logged same-day, every prospect with a dated next step, bounces removed, "not-now" leads nurtured. If it's not in the CRM, it didn't happen.

16. Final Checklist

  • Targeting: Can I state my ICP in one sentence and name 3 personas with distinct pains?
  • List: Does every row have a verified email/phone and a true personalization hook?
  • Message: Does my email open about them, stay under ~125 words, and end in one small ask?
  • Call: Do I have a pattern-interrupt opener, one discovery question, a one-line pitch, and two time options ready?
  • Channels: Am I running email + call + LinkedIn as one cadence, not in isolation?
  • Persistence: Am I completing 8–12 touches, or quitting after one follow-up?
  • Qualification: Am I weaving CHAMP/SPIN questions naturally, not interrogating?
  • Deliverability: SPF/DKIM/DMARC set, list verified, volume human?
  • Discipline: Daily power hours protected, every touch logged, every prospect has a dated next step?
  • Metrics: Do I know my connect, reply, positive-reply, and meeting-booked numbers — and am I fixing conversion, not just adding volume?
The one-paragraph summary: Prospecting is a system: size the market (TAM/SAM/SOM) → define a razor-sharp ICP and personas → build a clean, hook-rich list from good data tools → reach out across email, phone, and LinkedIn with short, buyer-centric, personalized messages → run a disciplined 8–12 touch multi-channel cadence → qualify naturally with CHAMP/SPIN → get past gatekeepers with respect → measure activity and conversion → keep the CRM immaculate. Relevance plus persistence plus discipline — that is the whole craft. Start conversations, help before you pitch, and the meetings will come.
