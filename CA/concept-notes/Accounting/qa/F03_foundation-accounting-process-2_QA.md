## Section A — Theory

### Q1. Why are subsidiary books needed when the journal already exists? (5 marks)

**Model answer.**
The journal records every transaction as a two-sided entry in date order, but in a real business a *few types* of transaction (credit purchases, credit sales, cash movements) recur thousands of times. Recording them all in one journal is slow, cannot be divided among clerks, and repeats the same account names endlessly. Subsidiary books split the journal into special-purpose books so that:

1. **Division of labour** — several clerks work simultaneously (a cashier on the cash book, a sales clerk on the sales book).
2. **Time saving** — the common account (e.g. Purchases A/c) is posted only once, in total, at period-end.
3. **Specialisation and fewer errors** — each clerk masters one book.
4. **Ready information** — the total of the sales book is instantly the month's credit sales.
5. **Internal check and fraud control**, especially over cash.
6. **A cleaner ledger** — fewer, summarised postings.

*Presentation tip: state the problem in one line, then give the numbered advantages — examiners award marks per distinct, correctly-named advantage.*

### Q2. Distinguish between trade discount and cash discount. (4 marks)

**Model answer.** Present as a table — it earns full marks fastest.

| Basis | Trade discount | Cash discount |
|-------|----------------|---------------|
| Meaning | Reduction in list price for bulk/trade buying | Reduction for prompt payment |
| Time given | At the time of sale/purchase | At the time of payment/receipt |
| Recorded in books? | No — only net amount entered | Yes — Discount Allowed / Received A/c |
| Purpose | Promote larger orders | Encourage early settlement |
| Shown in cash book? | No | Yes, in discount columns |

*Key sentence to include: "Trade discount is merely deducted on the invoice and never appears as a separate account, whereas cash discount is a nominal account affecting the P&L."*

### Q3. What is a contra entry? How is it recorded and indicated? (3 marks)

**Model answer.** A contra entry is a transaction in which **both aspects (debit and credit) relate to items already inside the cash book** — i.e. a movement between cash and bank. Examples: cash deposited into bank, or cash withdrawn from bank for office use.

- Cash deposited into bank → **Bank column debited, Cash column credited.**
- Cash withdrawn for office use → **Cash column debited, Bank column credited.**

Because both accounts (Cash and Bank) are contained in the cash book, **no separate ledger posting is required**, and the letter **"C"** is written in the L.F. column to signal a contra. A withdrawal for the proprietor's personal use is *not* a contra — it is drawings.

### Q4. Explain the imprest system of petty cash. Why is it considered a good control? (4 marks)

**Model answer.** Under the imprest system the head cashier advances the petty cashier a **fixed sum (imprest amount)** for a period. The petty cashier pays small expenses against vouchers, and at period-end the head cashier **reimburses exactly the amount spent**, restoring the float to the original fixed figure for the next period.

It is a strong control because: (i) the petty cashier never holds more than the small fixed float, limiting loss/fraud; (ii) since reimbursement equals expenditure, the system self-checks (opening float = closing cash + vouchers = imprest); (iii) every payment must be voucher-supported before reimbursement; and (iv) it keeps trivial payments out of the main cash book.

### Q6. Why can the bank column of a cash book show a credit balance but the cash column cannot? (3 marks)

**Model answer.** The cash column represents physical cash in hand — an asset that can be *reduced to nil at most*; you cannot physically pay out more cash than you hold, so it can only carry a debit (or nil) balance. The bank column represents the relationship with the bank; when the firm has drawn more than its deposits under an overdraft facility, it **owes** the bank, so the bank account becomes a liability and shows a **credit balance (overdraft)**. Hence a credit bank balance is legitimate, but a credit cash balance signals an arithmetical error.

### Q7. What is the Journal Proper used for? List five items. (5 marks)

**Model answer.** The Journal Proper (general journal) records transactions that do not belong in any special subsidiary book:

1. Opening entries.
2. Closing entries (transfer of nominal accounts to Trading and P&L).
3. Adjustment entries (outstanding/prepaid expenses, depreciation, provisions).
4. Rectification of errors.
5. Credit purchase or sale of **assets** (not goods) — e.g. machinery on credit.
6. Dishonour of bills and other bill entries not covered by the bills books.

*(Any five suffice for full marks.)*

### Q8. Is the discount column of a three-column cash book balanced? Explain. (2 marks)

**Model answer.** No. The two discount columns are **memorandum columns**, not part of the double entry of the cash book. Each side's discount column is merely **totalled**: the debit-side total is posted to the **debit of Discount Allowed A/c** and the credit-side total to the **credit of Discount Received A/c**. They are never balanced against each other, because discount allowed (an expense) and discount received (an income) are two separate accounts.

### Q13. Cash discount computed on net-of-trade-discount value. (5 marks)

A trader sells goods with list price ₹50,000 to a customer, allowing **20% trade discount** and **2% cash discount** if paid within 10 days. The customer pays within the period. Show the amounts and the cash discount.

**Solution.**
- Net invoice value = 50,000 − 20% (10,000) = **₹40,000** (this is what is recorded as the sale).
- Cash discount is computed on the **net invoice value**, not the list price: 2% × 40,000 = **₹800**.
- Cash received = 40,000 − 800 = **₹39,200**.

Journal on receipt:
- Cash/Bank A/c Dr ₹39,200
- Discount Allowed A/c Dr ₹800
- To Customer A/c ₹40,000

**Check:** Dr 39,200 + 800 = 40,000 = Cr ✔. Trade discount ₹10,000 does not appear as an account.

### Q16. Identify and correct the error. (4 marks)

An accountant recorded a **cash purchase of goods ₹5,000** in the **Purchases Book**. What is wrong, and what is the effect?

**Model answer.** The Purchases Book records only **credit** purchases of goods; a **cash** purchase must go to the **Cash Book (credit side)**. Recording it in the Purchases Book:
- overstates the Purchases Book total posted to Purchases A/c only if it is a genuine extra — but here Purchases is correctly debited either way; the real error is that the **supplier is wrongly credited ₹5,000** (creating a fictitious creditor) while **cash is not reduced**. Trial balance still tallies (a compensating personal-vs-cash misstatement), so it is an **error of principle** that the trial balance will not reveal.
Correct treatment: record on the credit (payment) side of the cash book — By Purchases A/c ₹5,000 — and reverse the wrong creditor credit.

### Q22. Why is the cash book both a journal and a ledger? (3 marks)

**Model answer.** The cash book is a **book of original entry (journal)** because cash and bank transactions are recorded in it first, directly from source documents, in date order — they are not journalised elsewhere. It is simultaneously a **ledger** because it is ruled with debit and credit sides and is balanced periodically exactly like the Cash A/c and Bank A/c; indeed no separate Cash A/c or Bank A/c is opened in the ledger because the cash book *serves as* those accounts. This dual character is unique among the subsidiary books.

### Q23. Compute closing cash from scattered data. (4 marks)

During February, **cash receipts** were: opening cash ₹2,500, cash sales ₹40,000, cash from debtor ₹6,500, withdrawal from bank for office ₹5,000 (contra). **Cash payments** were: cash purchases ₹12,000, wages ₹8,000, deposited into bank ₹15,000 (contra), drawings ₹3,000. Find closing cash.

**Solution.**
- Total cash receipts = 2,500 + 40,000 + 6,500 + 5,000 = **₹54,000**.
- Total cash payments = 12,000 + 8,000 + 15,000 + 3,000 = **₹38,000**.
- **Closing cash = 54,000 − 38,000 = ₹16,000** (debit balance). ✔

**Check:** both contras (₹5,000 in, ₹15,000 out) correctly touch the cash column; drawings is a genuine payment (not a contra), correctly deducted.

### Q24. Presentation question — full-marks structure for a cash-book problem. (2 marks)

**Model answer.** To score full marks on a cash-book numerical: (i) rule all three columns on **both** sides and put opening balances as "To/By Balance b/d" on the correct side (bank o/d starts on the credit side); (ii) enter receipts on the debit side ("To …") and payments on the credit side ("By …"); (iii) mark every contra with **"C"** in the L.F. column; (iv) total the **discount columns separately** and post them — do not balance them; (v) balance the cash and bank columns, show "By/To Balance c/d", and carry the balances down; (vi) state the closing balances and the discount-account postings explicitly. Neat ruling and correct sides carry method marks even if an arithmetic slip occurs.
