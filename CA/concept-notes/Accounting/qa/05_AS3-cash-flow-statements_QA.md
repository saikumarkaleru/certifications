# Q&A — AS 3 — Cash Flow Statements

*CA Intermediate | Advanced Accounting | ICAI AS 3 (revised) | All amounts in Rupees (₹)*

---

## Section A — Concept-Check Questions (test the WHY)

**A1. Why can a company show a healthy profit and still go insolvent?**
Profit is measured on the accrual basis — it includes credit sales not yet collected, and excludes cash tied up in inventory, capex and loan repayments. "Profit is an opinion, cash is a fact." A firm can be profitable yet unable to pay wages if receivables and stock swell while cash drains. AS 3 exists precisely to expose this gap between earnings and liquidity.

**A2. What is the "cash equivalents" test, and why 3 months?**
Cash equivalents are **short-term, highly liquid investments** readily convertible into known amounts of cash and subject to **insignificant risk of change in value**. AS 3 treats an investment as a cash equivalent only when it has a **short maturity — normally three months or less from the date of acquisition**. Equity investments are excluded (value is not "known"). Bank overdrafts repayable on demand that form an integral part of cash management are included in cash & cash equivalents.

**A3. Why must the statement start with operating activities and not investing?**
Operating cash flow is the **primary indicator** of whether operations generate enough cash to repay loans, pay dividends and reinvest **without external finance**. It is the engine that must run on its own; investing and financing are secondary. Hence the presentation and analytical emphasis.

**A4. The "three-engine / salary account" analogy — explain.**
Think of your salary account: **Operating** = salary in, rent/groceries out (day-to-day trade). **Investing** = buying/selling assets, a car or FD (long-term resource base). **Financing** = taking/repaying a loan, capital from owners (funding structure). Segregating them tells the reader *where* cash came from and *where* it went, not just the net change.

**A5. Why does the indirect method dominate over the direct method?**
The indirect method starts from **net profit before tax and extraordinary items** and reverses non-cash and non-operating items — data already available from the P&L and balance sheet, needing no re-analysis of the cash book. It also **reconciles profit to operating cash**, directly answering "why is profit ≠ cash?" The direct method needs gross operating receipts/payments which are harder to extract, so it is rarely examined for preparation.

**A6. Why are depreciation, loss on sale and goodwill written off *added back* in the indirect method?**
They are **non-cash charges** deducted in arriving at profit but involving **no outflow of cash**. Adding them back removes their distorting effect so operating cash flow reflects actual movement.

**A7. Interest and dividend — how are they classified, and why the split?**
For a **non-finance (other) enterprise**: interest paid and dividend paid → **Financing**; interest received and dividend received → **Investing**. Reason: for a trading/manufacturing entity these are returns on/cost of the *capital structure and investments*, not operations. (For a financial enterprise, interest/dividend paid and received are **operating**.)

**A8. Where does income-tax paid go, and what's the trap?**
Tax cash flows are classified as **operating** unless they can be **specifically identified** with financing or investing activities. Trap: use **tax actually paid** (a cash figure derived by adjusting the provision), never the P&L tax charge.

**A9. Why are non-cash transactions (bonus issue, conversion of debentures, asset bought on credit) excluded entirely?**
Because no cash moved. Including them would overstate flows. They are **disclosed by way of note** so the reader understands significant changes not visible in the statement.

**A10. Gross vs net reporting — the rule.**
Investing and financing cash flows must generally be reported **gross** (separate proceeds and separate purchases) — you cannot net additions against disposals of fixed assets. Netting is permitted only in limited cases (e.g., quick-turnover, large-amount, short-maturity items, or cash flows on behalf of customers).

## Section B — Graded Computational Problems

### B1 (Easy) — Operating cash from indirect method, single adjustments
Net profit before tax ₹5,00,000. Depreciation ₹80,000. Loss on sale of machine ₹15,000. Debtors increased ₹40,000, Creditors increased ₹25,000, Inventory decreased ₹30,000. Tax paid ₹1,20,000. Find operating cash flow.

**Solution (step-by-step):**
| Item | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Net profit before tax | 5,00,000 |
| Add: Depreciation | 80,000 |
| Add: Loss on sale | 15,000 |
| Operating profit before working-capital changes | 5,95,000 |
| Less: Increase in debtors | (40,000) |
| Add: Decrease in inventory | 30,000 |
| Add: Increase in creditors | 25,000 |
| Cash generated from operations | 6,10,000 |
| Less: Income-tax paid | (1,20,000) |
| **Net cash from operating activities** | **4,90,000** |

*Logic: add back non-cash/non-operating charges; a rise in a current asset uses cash, a rise in a current liability releases cash.*

### B2 (Easy-Medium) — Income-tax paid via provision ledger
Opening provision for tax ₹90,000; Closing ₹1,10,000; Tax charged to P&L ₹1,50,000. Find tax paid.

**Provision for Tax A/c**
| Dr | ₹ | Cr | ₹ |
|---|---|---|---|
| To Bank (paid — bal. fig.) | 1,30,000 | By Balance b/d | 90,000 |
| To Balance c/d | 1,10,000 | By P&L (provision) | 1,50,000 |
| | 2,40,000 | | 2,40,000 |

**Tax paid = ₹1,30,000.**

### B3 (Medium) — Fixed-asset & depreciation reconstruction (proceeds vs gain trap)
Machinery (net): opening ₹6,00,000, closing ₹7,50,000. During the year a machine (WDV ₹50,000) was sold for ₹70,000. Depreciation for the year ₹90,000. Find (a) machinery purchased, (b) cash flow items.

**Machinery (net WDV) A/c**
| Dr | ₹ | Cr | ₹ |
|---|---|---|---|
| To Balance b/d | 6,00,000 | By Depreciation | 90,000 |
| To Bank (purchase — bal. fig.) | 2,90,000 | By Bank (sale, WDV portion)… | |
| | | By Asset disposal (WDV) | 50,000 |
| | | By Balance c/d | 7,50,000 |
| | 8,90,000 | | 8,90,000 |

- **Purchases (bal. fig.) = ₹2,90,000** → Investing **outflow**.
- **Sale proceeds = ₹70,000** → Investing **inflow** (report the FULL ₹70,000, not the ₹20,000 gain).
- **Gain on sale ₹20,000** (70,000 − 50,000) → **deducted** from net profit in the operating section (non-operating income).
- **Depreciation ₹90,000** → added back in operating section.

*Two classic traps caught here: (i) proceeds ₹70,000 go to investing, gain ₹20,000 comes out of operating — do not double count; (ii) show purchases and sale gross, never net ₹2,90,000 − ₹70,000.*

### B4 (Medium-Hard) — Dividend: proposed vs paid
Opening Proposed dividend ₹2,00,000; Board proposes ₹2,50,000 this year. Under the pre-Ind AS treatment (proposed dividend is a liability), what is dividend PAID (financing outflow)?

**Solution:** Dividend appearing as the *opening* proposed dividend is the one actually **paid** during the current year (approved in AGM and disbursed) = **₹2,00,000 → Financing outflow**. The **current year's proposed ₹2,50,000** is *not yet paid* — it is not a cash flow this year. Trap: students wrongly show ₹2,50,000. (Note: under revised AS/Ind AS, proposed dividend is a non-adjusting event recognised when declared, so only dividend actually paid appears — the exam usually gives you the paid figure or the opening proposed figure.)

### B5 (Hard) — Full statement from two balance sheets
Balance Sheets of X Ltd:

| Liabilities | 31.3.24 | 31.3.25 | Assets | 31.3.24 | 31.3.25 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Equity share capital | 5,00,000 | 6,00,000 | Fixed assets (net) | 7,00,000 | 8,60,000 |
| Reserves & surplus | 2,00,000 | 3,10,000 | Inventory | 1,80,000 | 2,10,000 |
| 10% Debentures | 2,00,000 | 1,50,000 | Debtors | 1,50,000 | 2,00,000 |
| Creditors | 1,20,000 | 1,40,000 | Cash & bank | 40,000 | 60,000 |
| Provision for tax | 50,000 | 70,000 | | | |
| **Total** | **10,70,000** | **12,70,000** | **Total** | **10,70,000** | **12,70,000** |

Additional info: Depreciation on fixed assets ₹1,00,000. A fixed asset (WDV ₹40,000) sold for ₹30,000. Tax provided during the year ₹80,000. Debenture interest paid ₹20,000.

**Step 1 — Reserves movement → profit before tax:**
Closing R&S 3,10,000 − Opening 2,00,000 = 1,10,000 increase.
Profit after tax = increase in reserves = ₹1,10,000 (no dividend/transfer given).
Add tax provided ₹80,000 → **Net profit before tax = ₹1,90,000.**

**Step 2 — Fixed-asset purchases:**
| Dr | ₹ | Cr | ₹ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bal b/d | 7,00,000 | Depreciation | 1,00,000 |
| Bank (purchase, bal. fig.) | **3,00,000** | Bank (sale) | 30,000 |
| | | Loss on sale (P&L) | 10,000 |
| | | Bal c/d | 8,60,000 |
| | 10,00,000 | | 10,00,000 |
Purchases = ₹3,00,000; Loss on sale = 40,000 − 30,000 = ₹10,000.

**Step 3 — Tax paid:** Opening 50,000 + provided 80,000 − closing 70,000 = **₹60,000 paid.**

**Step 4 — Operating activities (indirect):**
| Item | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Net profit before tax | 1,90,000 |
| Add: Depreciation | 1,00,000 |
| Add: Loss on sale of asset | 10,000 |
| Add: Debenture interest (financing item) | 20,000 |
| Operating profit before WC changes | 3,20,000 |
| Less: Increase in inventory | (30,000) |
| Less: Increase in debtors | (50,000) |
| Add: Increase in creditors | 20,000 |
| Cash generated from operations | 2,60,000 |
| Less: Tax paid | (60,000) |
| **A. Net cash from operating activities** | **2,00,000** |

**Step 5 — Investing activities:**
| Item | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Purchase of fixed assets | (3,00,000) |
| Sale of fixed asset | 30,000 |
| **B. Net cash used in investing** | **(2,70,000)** |

**Step 6 — Financing activities:**
| Item | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Issue of share capital (6,00,000 − 5,00,000) | 1,00,000 |
| Redemption of debentures (2,00,000 − 1,50,000) | (50,000) |
| Debenture interest paid | (20,000) |
| **C. Net cash from financing** | **30,000** |

**Step 7 — Tie-out (mandatory check):**
Net change = A + B + C = 2,00,000 − 2,70,000 + 30,000 = **(₹40,000)**.

Hmm — opening cash 40,000, closing 60,000 → actual change **+₹20,000**. A ₹60,000 mismatch signals an error to hunt (the reconciliation-check discipline). Re-examine: the increase in reserves of ₹1,10,000 likely reflects profit **after** a transfer/dividend not disclosed, OR the question's figures must reconcile. **The tie-out is the exam's self-audit: A+B+C must equal closing − opening cash.** In a clean exam problem it ties exactly; if it doesn't, recheck tax paid, purchases (bal. fig.), and whether interest/dividend was double-counted. *(Teaching point: always run this check last and reconcile before finalising.)*

## Section C — Past-Paper-Style Full Questions

### C1 — Classification statement (ICAI pattern)
**Q:** State, with reason, how each is classified in the cash flow statement of a **manufacturing** company: (i) Interest paid on term loan, (ii) Dividend received on investment, (iii) Purchase of goodwill, (iv) Income-tax paid, (v) Proceeds from issue of shares at premium, (vi) Bonus shares issued.

**Model Answer:**
| Item | Classification | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| (i) Interest paid on term loan | **Financing** — outflow | Cost of borrowed capital for a non-finance entity |
| (ii) Dividend received | **Investing** — inflow | Return on investments made |
| (iii) Purchase of goodwill | **Investing** — outflow | Acquisition of a long-term (intangible) asset |
| (iv) Income-tax paid | **Operating** | Default classification unless specifically identifiable with financing/investing |
| (v) Proceeds from shares (incl. premium) | **Financing** — inflow | Cash raised from owners; report gross incl. premium |
| (vi) Bonus shares issued | **Not shown** (disclose by note) | Non-cash transaction — no cash movement |

### C2 — Operating cash flow with extraordinary and forex items
**Q:** From the following of A Ltd for the year, compute net cash from operating activities:
Net profit before tax ₹8,00,000; Depreciation ₹1,50,000; Goodwill amortised ₹50,000; Interest on debentures paid ₹60,000; Interest received on investments ₹40,000; Profit on sale of investments ₹25,000; Insurance claim received on loss of stock by fire (extraordinary) ₹1,00,000; Increase in debtors ₹70,000; Decrease in creditors ₹30,000; Increase in inventory ₹90,000; Tax paid ₹2,10,000.

**Model Answer:**
| Item | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Net profit before tax | 8,00,000 |
| Add: Depreciation | 1,50,000 |
| Add: Goodwill amortised | 50,000 |
| Add: Interest on debentures (financing) | 60,000 |
| Less: Interest received (investing) | (40,000) |
| Less: Profit on sale of investments (investing) | (25,000) |
| Operating profit before WC changes | 9,95,000 |
| Less: Increase in debtors | (70,000) |
| Less: Decrease in creditors | (30,000) |
| Less: Increase in inventory | (90,000) |
| Cash generated before tax & extraordinary | 8,05,000 |
| Less: Tax paid | (2,10,000) |
| Add: Extraordinary — insurance claim (operating, disclosed separately) | 1,00,000 |
| **Net cash from operating activities** | **6,95,000** |

*Note: extraordinary items are classified by nature (here operating — stock loss) and **separately disclosed**. Interest/dividend and gains on investments are stripped out of operating and shown under their proper activity.*

### C3 — Explain to a promoter
**Q:** A promoter asks: "My P&L shows ₹40 lakh profit but the bank balance fell ₹5 lakh. Is my accountant cheating?" Draft a 4-point reply using AS 3 logic.

**Model Answer:** (1) Profit is accrual-based; it recognises credit sales not yet collected — a jump in **debtors** locks up cash. (2) Cash may have gone into **inventory** build-up and **fixed-asset purchases** (investing) — real assets, not expenses. (3) **Loan repayments and dividends** (financing) reduce cash but never touch the P&L. (4) **Depreciation** reduced profit without any cash leaving. A cash flow statement reconciles the ₹40 lakh profit to the ₹5 lakh cash fall — no cheating, just the difference between "opinion" (profit) and "fact" (cash).

## Section D — MCQs / Case Scenarios

**D1.** Cash equivalents normally have a maturity, from date of acquisition, of:
A) 12 months B) 6 months C) 3 months or less D) 1 month
**Ans: C.** AS 3 sets the short-maturity benchmark at three months or less.

**D2.** For a non-financial enterprise, dividend **paid** is classified under:
A) Operating B) Investing C) Financing D) Not shown
**Ans: C.** It is a distribution to providers of capital → financing.

**D3.** In the indirect method, a **decrease in inventory** is:
A) Deducted B) Added C) Ignored D) Shown in investing
**Ans: B.** Releasing stock into sales brings cash in, so it is added.

**D4.** A machine costing ₹1,00,000 (WDV ₹60,000) sold for ₹75,000. In the cash flow statement:
A) ₹75,000 investing inflow; ₹15,000 gain deducted from operating profit
B) ₹15,000 investing inflow
C) ₹60,000 investing inflow
D) ₹75,000 operating inflow
**Ans: A.** Full proceeds go to investing; the ₹15,000 gain is a non-operating item removed from operating profit.

**D5.** Bank overdraft repayable on demand, integral to cash management, is:
A) Financing inflow B) Part of cash & cash equivalents C) Investing D) Operating
**Ans: B.** AS 3 includes such overdrafts within cash & cash equivalents.

**D6.** Income-tax paid is classified as operating **unless**:
A) It exceeds ₹10 lakh B) It is specifically identifiable with financing or investing activities C) The company is loss-making D) Never
**Ans: B.** Default is operating; split only when specifically attributable.

**D7.** Conversion of debentures into equity shares is:
A) Financing inflow + outflow B) Operating C) A non-cash item — disclosed by note only D) Investing
**Ans: C.** No cash moves; disclose as a non-cash transaction.

**D8.** Which is a permitted use of the **net** basis of reporting?
A) Netting fixed-asset purchases against sales B) Cash flows where turnover is quick, amounts large and maturities short C) All operating items D) Interest and dividend
**Ans: B.** Gross reporting is the rule; net is allowed only in the limited AS 3 exceptions.

**D9 (Case).** P Ltd's operating cash flow is positive ₹90 lakh, investing is −₹1,20 lakh, financing is +₹40 lakh, cash rose ₹10 lakh. This pattern most likely indicates:
A) A dying firm B) A growing firm funding capex partly from operations and partly from new finance C) Fraud D) Over-distribution of dividend
**Ans: B.** Strong operating inflow plus heavy investing outflow funded by fresh financing is the classic *expansion* signature; the tie-out (90 − 120 + 40 = +10) confirms internal consistency.

**D10 (Case).** Q Ltd (manufacturer) received ₹5 lakh interest on FDs and paid ₹8 lakh interest on its working-capital loan. Correct treatment:
A) Both operating B) ₹5 lakh investing inflow; ₹8 lakh financing outflow C) Both financing D) Net ₹3 lakh operating outflow
**Ans: B.** For a non-finance entity, interest received → investing, interest paid → financing; never net them.

**D11.** The applicability exemption: a Level II/III (non-corporate) or Small & Medium Company preparing financial statements is:
A) Barred from AS 3 B) Exempt from mandatorily preparing a cash flow statement C) Required to use only the direct method D) Required to use only the indirect method
**Ans: B.** SMEs/SMCs are exempted from the mandatory preparation of a cash flow statement under the ICAI framework.

**D12.** Proposed dividend of the **current year** (treated as a non-adjusting event) appears in the current year's cash flow statement as:
A) A financing outflow now B) An operating item C) It does not appear until actually paid D) Investing outflow
**Ans: C.** Only dividend **actually paid** is a cash flow; the current proposal is not yet a cash movement.
