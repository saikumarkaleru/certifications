## Section A — Theory (with model answers)

### Q1. Why are two separate accounts — Trading and Profit & Loss — prepared instead of one combined profit account? *(4 marks)*

**Model answer.**
The two accounts measure profit at two different levels, which is diagnostically useful.
- The **Trading Account** isolates **Gross Profit** — the profit arising *purely from buying and selling goods* (Net Sales − Cost of Goods Sold). It captures only *direct* items: opening/closing stock, purchases, sales, wages, carriage inward and other manufacturing/procurement costs.
- The **Profit & Loss Account** starts from Gross Profit and adjusts for all *indirect* items — administrative, selling and financial expenses, and other incomes — to arrive at **Net Profit**, the amount finally belonging to the proprietor.

*Presentation tip:* state the purpose of each account first, then give one line of what each contains, and close with the diagnostic value — "a good gross margin with a net loss reveals excessive overheads rather than bad buying." Examiners reward the *reasoning*, not just the definition.

---

### Q2. Explain the "double effect" of every adjustment. Illustrate with any three adjustments. *(5 marks)*

**Model answer.**
Because accounting is a **double-entry** system, every adjustment records an economic event that the books missed at year-end, and therefore produces **two effects** — one that affects profit (Trading/P&L) and one that affects the Balance Sheet.

| Adjustment | Effect 1 (profit) | Effect 2 (Balance Sheet) |
|---|---|---|
| Outstanding wages | Added to wages in Trading A/c | Shown as a current liability |
| Prepaid insurance | Deducted from insurance in P&L | Shown as a current asset |
| Depreciation | Charged as expense in P&L | Deducted from the asset |

*Presentation tip:* explicitly name the concept ("double entry") and always present the two effects as a two-column idea — the examiner is checking whether you know *both* landing points, which is exactly what makes the Balance Sheet tally.

---

### Q3. Distinguish between an item appearing in the trial balance and the same item appearing only in the adjustments. *(3 marks)*

**Model answer.**
- An item **in the trial balance** has *already been recorded* by double entry, so it produces only **one effect** in the final accounts — it is taken to its relevant account once. Example: "Bad Debts Rs 3,000" in the TB goes only to the debit of the P&L Account.
- An item given **only in the adjustments** has *not yet been recorded*, so it produces **two effects**. Example: "Further bad debts Rs 5,000" is both charged in the P&L *and* deducted from Sundry Debtors.

*Presentation tip:* the one-liner examiners want — "trial balance item = one effect; adjustment item = two effects."

---

### Q4. What is meant by "marshalling" of assets and liabilities? State the two orders. *(4 marks)*

**Model answer.**
**Marshalling** is the arrangement of assets and liabilities in the Balance Sheet in a definite, meaningful order. Two orders are used:
- **Order of liquidity** — most easily convertible into cash first (Cash → Bank → Debtors → Stock → Fixed Assets → Goodwill); liabilities in order of urgency of payment (outstanding expenses first, capital last).
- **Order of permanence** — the reverse: most permanent/fixed items first (Goodwill → Machinery → Furniture → Stock → Debtors → Cash); liabilities from capital and long-term loans down to current liabilities.

Either order is acceptable provided it is applied **consistently**; sole proprietors most often use the order of permanence.

---

### Q5. Why is interest on capital treated as an expense in the P&L Account, even though it is paid to the owner himself? *(4 marks)*

**Model answer.**
Interest on capital recognises the **opportunity cost** of the proprietor's funds. Had the capital been invested elsewhere (say a bank deposit), it would have earned a return; by locking it into the business the owner forgoes that return. Charging interest on capital in the P&L therefore measures whether the business earns *more than the owner's money would earn elsewhere* — a truer test of business performance.

Its two effects are: (i) an expense in the P&L Account, and (ii) an addition to the Capital Account in the Balance Sheet. It is thus an internal transfer that nets to zero for the owner's total wealth but sharpens the profit measure. Its mirror, **interest on drawings**, charges the owner for withdrawing funds and is credited as an income of the business.

### Q6. State the order in which bad debts and the two provisions on debtors must be computed, and why the order matters. *(4 marks)*

**Model answer.**
The strict order is:
1. **Further bad debts** — written off first, reducing debtors to the collectible base.
2. **Provision for doubtful debts (PDD)** — computed as a % of debtors *after* step 1.
3. **Provision for discount on debtors (PDDr)** — computed as a % of debtors *after* steps 1 and 2 (only good debtors who actually pay can claim a cash discount).

The order matters because each step operates on the balance left by the previous one; applying a later percentage to the gross debtors figure overstates the provision. It also reflects economic logic — you cannot offer a discount to a debtor you have already judged doubtful or bad.

### Q7. Give the two bases for computing a manager's commission and the formula for each. *(3 marks)*

**Model answer.**
Let net profit *before* charging any such commission be **P** and the rate be **r%**.
- **Commission on profit before charging commission** = **P × r/100**.
- **Commission on profit after charging commission** = **P × r/(100 + r)**.

The "after" basis gives a smaller figure because the commission is computed on a base already reduced by the commission itself. *Presentation tip:* always write both formulas, then substitute — examiners award a mark for the correct formula even before the arithmetic.

---

### Q8. What is the accounting treatment of goods withdrawn by the proprietor for personal use? *(3 marks)*

**Model answer.**
Goods taken for personal use are not a business sale; they must be removed from purchases and charged to the owner.
> Entry: `Drawings A/c   Dr` to `Purchases A/c` (at cost).

Two effects: (i) **Purchases are reduced** in the Trading Account (lowering cost of goods sold); (ii) **Drawings are deducted from Capital** in the Balance Sheet. Failing to remove them overstates both COGS and the owner's capital.

---

### Q9. Why must closing stock normally appear in the adjustments (twice) and not in the trial balance? *(3 marks)*

**Model answer.**
Closing stock is valued only *after* a physical count on the last day, so it is not part of the ledger balances that form the trial balance — it is supplied as an adjustment. Being an adjustment, it has **two effects**: (i) credited in the **Trading Account** (removing the cost of unsold goods from COGS, per the matching concept), and (ii) shown as a **current asset** in the Balance Sheet.
If, exceptionally, closing stock *already* appears in the trial balance (because opening stock was adjusted through a Stock Account), it has only **one effect** — shown as an asset only, not in the Trading Account.

---

### Q10. State the accounting concepts underlying (a) closing stock valuation at lower of cost and NRV, and (b) provision for doubtful debts. *(3 marks)*

**Model answer.**
Both flow from the **prudence (conservatism) concept** — anticipate all probable losses but never anticipate gains.
- **Lower of cost and NRV:** if stock's realisable value has fallen below cost, the loss has already become probable and is recognised now by writing the stock down; it is never written up above cost.
- **Provision for doubtful debts:** experience shows a probable proportion of debtors will default; that expected loss is charged to the year that made the sales, rather than waiting for the actual default next year.
Both also honour the **matching concept** by placing the loss in the period that earned the related revenue.

### Q14. Provision *release* (a decrease in required provision). *(4 marks)*

Opening provision for doubtful debts Rs 10,000; bad debts written off during the year Rs 2,000 (already posted); no further bad debts; debtors at year-end Rs 1,00,000; maintain PDD @5%.

**Solution.** New PDD required = 5% of 1,00,000 = Rs 5,000.
P&L amount = Bad debts 2,000 + New PDD 5,000 − Old PDD 10,000 = **− Rs 3,000**.
A negative charge means Rs 3,000 is **credited to the P&L Account** (a gain), because the old provision was more than enough to absorb the bad debts and still leave a surplus over the new requirement.

**Provision for Doubtful Debts A/c**

| Dr | Rs | Cr | Rs |
|---|---|---|---|
| To Bad Debts | 2,000 | By Balance b/d | 10,000 |
| To P&L A/c (excess written back) | 3,000 | | |
| To Balance c/d | 5,000 | | |
| **Total** | **10,000** | **Total** | **10,000** |

Balance Sheet debtors = 1,00,000 − 5,000 = **Rs 95,000**. ✓

### Q15. Manager's commission on both bases. *(4 marks)*

Net profit before charging any commission is Rs 88,000. The manager is entitled to 10% commission.

**Solution.**
- **On profit before charging:** 88,000 × 10/100 = **Rs 8,800**.
- **On profit after charging:** 88,000 × 10/110 = **Rs 8,000**.

*Verification (after basis):* profit after commission = 88,000 − 8,000 = 80,000; 10% of 80,000 = 8,000 = commission. Consistent. ✓ The unpaid commission is shown as **commission outstanding (a current liability)**.

### Q16. Interest on drawings by the average-period method. *(4 marks)*

Mr. B withdrew Rs 2,000 at the **end of each month** throughout the year ended 31 March 2025. Interest on drawings is charged @12% p.a. Compute the interest.

**Solution.**
Total drawings = 2,000 × 12 = Rs 24,000.
For equal amounts drawn at the **end** of each month, the average period = (11 + 0)/2 = **5.5 months**.
Interest = 24,000 × 12% × 5.5/12 = **Rs 1,320**.

*Check: 24,000 × 12% = 2,880 (full-year figure); × 5.5/12 = 2,880 × 0.4583 = 1,320. ✓*
(Had the drawings been at the **beginning** of each month, average period = 6.5 months and interest = 24,000 × 12% × 6.5/12 = Rs 1,560.)
Two effects: **credited to P&L** as income, and **deducted from Capital** in the Balance Sheet.

### Q19. Effect of adjustments on gross and net profit. *(5 marks)*

A trader's draft Gross Profit is Rs 2,00,000 and draft Net Profit is Rs 90,000, before the following are adjusted: (a) closing stock undervalued by Rs 6,000; (b) outstanding wages Rs 4,000; (c) prepaid insurance Rs 1,500 (insurance is in P&L); (d) depreciation Rs 8,000 not yet provided. Compute corrected Gross Profit and Net Profit.

**Solution.**

*Gross Profit* is affected only by direct items — closing stock (raises GP) and wages (lowers GP):
Corrected GP = 2,00,000 + 6,000 (stock up) − 4,000 (wages) = **Rs 2,02,000**.

*Net Profit* starts from corrected GP and absorbs the P&L items:
| Particulars | Rs |
|---|---|
| Draft Net Profit | 90,000 |
| Add: Closing stock undervaluation | 6,000 |
| Less: Outstanding wages | (4,000) |
| Add: Prepaid insurance (reduces expense) | 1,500 |
| Less: Depreciation | (8,000) |
| **Corrected Net Profit** | **85,500** |

*Check via GP route: corrected GP 2,02,000 − (original indirect expenses that reduced draft NP)… draft NP was GP 2,00,000 − indirect 1,10,000 = 90,000, so indirect = 1,10,000. Corrected NP = 2,02,000 − 1,10,000 + 1,500 (insurance) − 8,000 (dep) = 85,500. ✓*

### Q20. Income received in advance and accrued income together. *(4 marks)*

Rent received during the year Rs 60,000. Of this, Rs 5,000 relates to next year (received in advance). In addition, Rs 4,000 of rent has been earned but not yet received. Show the treatment.

**Solution.**
Rent to be credited to the P&L Account = 60,000 − 5,000 (advance) + 4,000 (accrued) = **Rs 59,000**.

| P&L Account (credit side extract) | Rs |
|---|---|
| By Rent Received 60,000 | |
| Less: Received in advance (5,000) | |
| Add: Accrued rent 4,000 | 59,000 |

Balance Sheet:
- **Rent received in advance Rs 5,000** → current liability.
- **Accrued rent Rs 4,000** → current asset.

Both effects are recorded: the income figure is corrected to what was *earned this year*, and the two timing differences appear on the Balance Sheet. ✓

### Q21. Abnormal loss of stock by fire. *(5 marks)*

Goods costing Rs 20,000 were destroyed by fire. The insurer admitted a claim of Rs 14,000. Show the accounting treatment in the final accounts.

**Solution.**
The cost of goods lost is first removed from the Trading Account (it was not sold), and the loss is split between the amount recoverable and the amount borne by the business.

> Entry 1: `Loss by Fire A/c   Dr 20,000` to `Trading A/c 20,000` (removes lost stock from the cost of goods available for sale).
> Entry 2: `Insurance Claim (Debtor) A/c   Dr 14,000` and `Profit & Loss A/c   Dr 6,000` to `Loss by Fire A/c 20,000`.

**Effects in final accounts:**
- **Trading A/c (Cr side):** By Loss of goods by fire Rs 20,000 (or shown as a deduction from purchases).
- **P&L A/c (Dr side):** To Loss by fire (net) Rs 6,000 (uninsured portion).
- **Balance Sheet (asset):** Insurance claim receivable Rs 14,000.

*Check: 14,000 (claim) + 6,000 (P&L loss) = 20,000 (cost of goods lost). ✓*

### Q24. Locate the errors in a student's Balance Sheet treatment. *(5 marks)*

A student made these treatments. Identify whether each is correct; if wrong, give the correction.
(i) Closing stock shown on the debit of the Trading Account.
(ii) Outstanding wages shown only as a liability, not added to wages.
(iii) Provision for discount on debtors computed on gross debtors before deducting bad debts and PDD.
(iv) Interest on drawings deducted from capital only, with no entry in the P&L.
(v) Depreciation added to the asset in the Balance Sheet.

**Solution.**

| # | Verdict | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| (i) | Wrong | Closing stock is a **credit** in the Trading Account (and an asset), not a debit. |
| (ii) | Wrong | Outstanding wages must **also be added to wages** in the Trading Account — the missing second effect distorts gross profit. |
| (iii) | Wrong | PDDr is computed on debtors **after** deducting further bad debts *and* the new PDD, not on gross debtors. |
| (iv) | Wrong | Interest on drawings must also be **credited as income in the P&L Account** — it has two effects. |
| (v) | Wrong | Depreciation is **deducted** from (not added to) the asset in the Balance Sheet, and charged as an expense in the P&L. |

Each error is a broken "double effect," which is why the Balance Sheet would fail to tally — the single most reliable self-check in this chapter. ✓
