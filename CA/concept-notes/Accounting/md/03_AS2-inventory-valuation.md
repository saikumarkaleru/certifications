# Chapter 3 — AS 2: Valuation of Inventories (the clearest example of prudence)

*If Chapter 1 taught you "prudence," this chapter shows prudence doing real work. AS 2 is small, high-scoring, and the perfect proof that a standard is just a principle applied.*

---

## 1. The Problem

At year end a trader has unsold goods sitting in the warehouse — **closing stock.** This stock has to appear on the Balance Sheet (as an asset) and it directly affects profit, because:

> Profit = Sales − Cost of Goods Sold, and **COGS = Opening Stock + Purchases − Closing Stock.**

Look at that formula. **The higher you value closing stock, the lower your COGS, and the higher your profit.** So the value you put on unsold stock is a direct profit lever. That's dangerous — a company could inflate profit simply by valuing leftover stock optimistically.

Two questions must be pinned down or profit becomes fiction:
1. **What costs go *into* the value of stock?** (Just purchase price? Also freight? Also the factory manager's salary?)
2. **At what amount do we carry it** if the goods have lost value (damaged, outdated, price crashed)?

---

## 2. The Core Idea

> Value inventory at **the lower of (a) Cost and (b) Net Realisable Value (NRV)**.

- **Cost** = what you spent to bring the stock to its present location and condition.
- **NRV** = what you can realistically sell it for, *minus* costs still needed to sell/complete it.

Why *lower of the two*? **Prudence.** An asset should never be carried at more than the cash it can actually bring in. If stock cost ₹100 but can now only fetch ₹70, carrying it at ₹100 overstates both the asset and profit — you'd be pretending a ₹30 loss hasn't happened. AS 2 forces you to recognise that loss *now*, the moment it's probable. But if NRV is *above* cost, you do **not** write stock *up* — because that would be booking an unrealised profit on goods you haven't sold yet (prudence again: anticipate losses, never profits).

This single "lower of cost or NRV" rule *is* prudence from Chapter 1, made concrete.

---

## 3. Why it's built this way

### 3a. Why "cost" is defined so carefully
If "cost" were vague, companies would stuff it or starve it to move profit. So AS 2 pins down what's **in** and what's **out**:

**Cost INCLUDES** (everything to get goods *ready and in place*):
- **Purchase cost** — price + duties/taxes *not later recoverable* + freight inward + other directly attributable acquisition costs. (Taxes you can reclaim, like recoverable GST/ITC, are **excluded** — you never really bore them.)
- **Conversion cost** (for manufacturers) — direct labour + a systematic share of **production overheads**.

**Cost EXCLUDES** (things that aren't about getting *this* stock ready to sell):
- Abnormal waste (a burst pipe ruining material isn't a "cost of inventory" — it's a loss of the period). *Why?* Because inventory should carry only *normal, necessary* costs; dumping abnormal losses into stock value would hide them and inflate the asset.
- Storage costs (unless necessary *before* a further production stage).
- Selling and distribution costs (those relate to *selling*, which happens *after* the stock is ready — they belong to the period, not the asset).
- Administrative overheads not related to production.

**The unifying logic:** a cost enters inventory only if it helped bring the goods *to their present location and condition ready for sale.* Ask that one question and you can classify any cost without a list.

### 3b. Why the overhead absorption rule uses "normal capacity"
Fixed production overheads (factory rent, supervisor salary) are spread over units produced. AS 2 says spread them over **normal capacity**, not actual output. *Why?* If a factory that can make 1,000 units only makes 100 in a slump, dividing all the fixed rent over just 100 units would balloon each unit's "cost" — and then "lower of cost/NRV" might value stock too high, hiding the fact that idle capacity is a *loss of the period*, not a cost of the product. Using normal capacity keeps the unused-capacity cost where it belongs: as a current expense.

### 3c. Why cost formulas (FIFO / Weighted Average) exist — and why not LIFO
When identical items were bought at different prices, which cost attaches to the units sold vs the units left? You need a *formula*:
- **FIFO (First-In-First-Out):** assume the oldest stock sells first, so closing stock reflects the *most recent* prices. Mirrors how most goods physically flow.
- **Weighted Average:** blend all costs into one average per unit. Smooths out price swings.
- **LIFO is not permitted** under AS 2. *Why?* LIFO leaves *ancient* prices sitting in the Balance Sheet stock value, so the asset figure drifts far from reality — it fails the "reflect economic reality" test. It was historically used to reduce tax in inflation, i.e., a manipulation motive, so the standard bans it.
- **Specific identification** is used when items are *not* interchangeable (e.g., a car dealer tracks each car's actual cost) — because averaging unique items would be meaningless.

The point: the formula is an *assumption about flow of cost*, chosen for faithfulness, not a random pick.

## 4. The Mechanics (through the RMPD lens from Chapter 2)

- **Recognition:** inventory is recognised as an asset while unsold; when sold, its cost flows to the P&L as an expense (COGS) in the *same* period as the related sale (that's accrual/matching).
- **Measurement:** at **lower of Cost and NRV**, applied normally **item by item** (not on the whole stock lumped together — see Traps).
  - Cost = purchase + conversion + other costs to reach present location/condition (per 3a).
  - NRV = estimated selling price − estimated costs of completion − estimated costs to make the sale.
- **Presentation:** shown under **Current Assets** on the Balance Sheet; the expense side hits COGS in the P&L.
- **Disclosure:** the **accounting policy** used (which cost formula), and the total carrying amount, often by classification (raw material / WIP / finished goods). *Why disclose the formula?* Straight from AS 1 — so profit is interpretable.

## 5. Reasoned example

*A shirt cost ₹500 to buy and get to the shop. At year end, fashion moved on; you can now sell it for ₹450, but you'll spend ₹20 on repackaging to sell it.*

Reason it:
- **NRV** = 450 − 20 = **₹430**.
- **Cost** = ₹500.
- Lower of the two = **₹430.** Carry the shirt at ₹430.
- This forces a **₹70 write-down** to be recognised as a loss *now*, in the year the value fell — not deferred to whenever you finally sell it.
- Prudence in action: you didn't wait for the loss to "become real" on sale; you recognised it the moment it became probable.

*Now flip it:* if that shirt could sell for ₹560 (NRV ₹540) against cost ₹500, you still carry it at **₹500** (cost, the lower). You do **not** write it up to ₹540 — that ₹40 is unrealised profit you haven't earned yet. Same rule, both directions, one principle.

## 7. Traps & confusions

- **Applying "lower of cost or NRV" to the whole inventory in one lump — wrong.** Do it **item by item** (or by similar groups). If you net a written-down item against a profitable one, you'd hide the loss — defeating prudence. This is the single most common AS 2 exam trap.
- **Never write inventory *up* above cost** even if NRV is higher. Losses are anticipated; profits are not.
- **Recoverable taxes are not part of cost.** If GST paid is claimable as input credit, you didn't really bear it — excluding it keeps cost real.
- **Selling costs are not inventory costs.** They relate to *selling*, which is after the goods are ready — they hit the period. (But selling costs *are* subtracted when computing NRV — different role, don't confuse.)
- **Abnormal loss ≠ inventory cost.** A flood ruining stock is a period loss, not something you bury in the value of surviving stock.

---

## 8. First-principles recap

- Closing stock value is a **direct profit lever** (via COGS), so AS 2 exists to pin it down and stop manipulation.
- Rule: **lower of Cost and NRV**, applied **item by item** — pure prudence (recognise probable losses now; never book unrealised gains).
- A cost enters inventory **only if it brought the goods to their present location and condition ready for sale** — that one test classifies any cost (in: purchase + conversion; out: abnormal waste, storage, selling, admin, recoverable taxes).
- Spread fixed production overheads over **normal capacity**, so idle-capacity cost stays a period loss.
- **FIFO / Weighted Average** are assumptions about *cost flow* chosen for faithfulness; **LIFO is banned** because it leaves stale values on the Balance Sheet.
- Disclose the **cost formula used** (AS 1 logic) so the profit is interpretable.
