# Q&A — International Financial Markets

Companion practice bank for Chapter 19. Every question is followed by a full answer. Section A checks concepts, B applies them to numbers and situations, C rehearses interview questions with model answers, and D sharpens judgement on the tricky distinctions through MCQs with reasoning.

## Section A — Concept Check

**A1. What is the single primal need that brought international financial markets into existence?**

**Moving capital across the borders that trap it.** Every economy has a mismatch between how much it saves and how much it can profitably invest — China and Japan have historically saved more than they could use, the US has invested and consumed more than it saved, and fast-growing emerging markets like India have abundant investment opportunities but shallow domestic savings. Left to purely domestic markets, that mismatch is wasted: surplus savings earn low returns at home while high-return projects elsewhere go unfunded. International markets exist to close four specific gaps — geographic (savers and borrowers live in different countries), currency (a borrower wants dollars but investors hold yen or rupees), regulatory (each country wraps its markets in different rules), and information/trust (a Boston investor cannot easily assess an Indian firm). Every instrument in the chapter is an engineered answer to one or more of those gaps.

**A2. Two channels carry almost all cross-border capital. Name and contrast them.**

**Foreign Direct Investment (FDI)** and **Foreign Portfolio Investment (FPI/FII).** FDI buys *control* of a real business or asset — a factory, a controlling stake, a subsidiary — and is "sticky": strategic, long-term, hard to reverse. FPI buys *financial securities* (shares, bonds) for returns without control, and is "hot": liquid, return-seeking, able to exit in a day. The distinction is the single most important conceptual anchor in the field, because it maps directly onto a country's vulnerability — a nation funded by sticky FDI is far safer than one funded by flighty FPI that can reverse on a Fed headline. Confusing the two mis-reads a country's balance-of-payments fragility entirely.

**A3. What is a depositary receipt, and what friction does it remove?**

A **depositary receipt (DR)** is a negotiable certificate issued by a bank in one country representing shares of a company in another, letting the company's equity trade abroad in foreign currency. Its genius is to move the friction *from the investor to a specialised intermediary*. Without it, a US fund wanting Infosys must register as an FPI, convert dollars to rupees, learn Indian settlement, and repatriate dividends through exchange controls. With an ADR, the fund buys "Infosys" on the NYSE in dollars through its ordinary broker with familiar settlement — while a depositary bank (BNY Mellon, Citi, JPMorgan) absorbs the cross-border custody, dividend conversion and corporate actions.

**A4. Distinguish an ADR from a GDR.**

Both represent home-country shares trading abroad; the difference is *where* they trade. An **ADR (American Depositary Receipt)** trades in the US market, is priced in USD, and is regulated by the SEC. A **GDR (Global Depositary Receipt)** trades *outside* the US — typically listed in London or Luxembourg — and is marketed to European and other international investors, usually in USD or EUR. The mirror image is an **IDR (Indian Depositary Receipt)**, a foreign company's shares trading in India (Standard Chartered's was the notable, lonely example).

**A5. Why does the word "Euro" in "Eurobond" have nothing to do with the euro currency?**

Because "Euro" here is a historical accident meaning *offshore*, not *European*. A **Eurocurrency** is any currency deposited or lent outside its home country; a **Eurodollar** is a dollar deposit held in a bank outside the United States (originally London); and a **Eurobond** is a bond issued and sold outside the country of the currency in which it is denominated. So a US-dollar bond issued in Singapore is a "Eurodollar bond" — the prefix signals that it escaped the currency's home jurisdiction, nothing more.

**A6. Distinguish a Eurobond from a foreign bond.**

A **foreign bond** is issued by a *foreign* borrower in the *local* currency of the market where it is sold — a US company issuing a yen bond in Japan (a "Samurai"), or a foreign issuer selling a USD bond in the US (a "Yankee"). A **Eurobond** is denominated in a currency *foreign to* the market where it is sold — an Indian company issuing a USD bond in London. The rule of thumb: a foreign bond matches the *local* currency of its market; a Eurobond deliberately does not.

**A7. What is a Masala bond, and what is clever about it?**

A **Masala bond** is a *rupee-denominated* bond issued *offshore* to foreign investors. The clever part is *who bears the currency risk*. A normal offshore dollar bond leaves the Indian issuer bearing USD/INR risk — it earns rupees but must repay dollars. A Masala bond is repaid in rupees, so if the rupee depreciates, the *foreign investor* absorbs the loss, not the borrower. The company raises foreign capital while keeping its liabilities in its home currency. Masala bonds crystallise the deep truth that international instruments are fundamentally about *who holds the currency risk*.

**A8. Why do FPI flows matter so disproportionately to emerging markets?**

Because they are **large relative to float, procyclical, and driven by global rather than local factors.** Net FPI buying or selling of a few thousand crore in a single day can swing the Nifty. The flows are reflexive — arriving when sentiment is good (pushing prices and the currency up) and fleeing when it sours (pushing both down), amplifying cycles. And they respond to US interest rates, the dollar index and global risk appetite far more than to host-country fundamentals, so an emerging market can be punished by *push factors* entirely outside its control.

**A9. What are "push" versus "pull" factors?**

**Pull** factors are attractive *local* conditions that draw capital in — strong growth, reforms, high yields, stability. **Push** factors are *global* conditions that push capital out of source countries or pull it back — low developed-market rates that send yield-seekers abroad, or Fed tightening that yanks dollars home. The distinction matters because emerging markets are often victims of push factors they cannot influence: the 2013 Taper Tantrum hit India despite no change in Indian fundamentals.

**A10. Integration lowers the cost of capital. What is its dark twin?**

**Contagion** — the disproportionate transmission of a shock from one market to others, often far out of proportion to real economic linkages. Free capital flows lower the cost of capital, improve risk-sharing, deepen liquidity and discipline reckless governments. But the same openness means shocks travel instantly, through the trade channel (partners lose exports), the financial/portfolio channel (a fund losing money in Country A sells Country B to raise cash), the confidence/herding channel (investors flee a whole asset class), and the common-lender channel (the same banks pull credit everywhere at once). There is no version of integration where you get the benefits without the exposure.

## Section B — Applied / Scenario Questions

**B1. A US fund earns a 15% return on Indian equities over a year, but the rupee depreciates 10% against the dollar over the same period. Roughly what did the fund earn in dollars, and what is the lesson?**

Total foreign return ≈ local asset return + currency return ≈ 15% − 10% = **roughly 5% in dollars.** The lesson is the first-order rule of international investing: **currency risk can dominate — even erase — the underlying return.** A great stock in a depreciating currency is a mediocre or bad foreign investment. Always decompose a cross-border return into its local-asset component and its currency component; being right on the stock is not enough if you are wrong on the currency.

**B2. Infosys's ADR trades on NASDAQ at a persistent premium to its Mumbai share price, adjusted for the DR ratio and exchange rate. Normally arbitrage would close such a gap. Why doesn't it here?**

Because **fungibility is restricted.** A DR is normally convertible back into the underlying shares, so if the ADR trades rich, arbitrageurs convert shares into ADRs (or vice versa) until the prices re-tether. But Indian rules cap reconversion beyond the "headroom" of previously converted receipts — you cannot freely create new ADRs from local shares. With the arbitrage link broken, the same economic asset can carry two persistent prices, and strong US demand for a scarce, non-expandable pool of ADRs pushes the receipt to a durable premium.

**B3. An Indian company needs USD 300 million for five years. Compare (a) borrowing from Indian banks, (b) a USD Eurobond in Singapore, and (c) a Masala bond in London — on who bears currency risk and why each might be chosen.**

(a) **Indian bank loan** — rupee funding, no currency risk, but Indian cost of funds is high and banks are wary of concentrated exposure. (b) **USD Eurobond** — taps deep global dollar savings at a potentially lower rate and escapes US domestic registration, but the *company* now bears USD/INR risk (earns rupees, repays dollars) unless it swaps. (c) **Masala bond** — raises foreign capital while keeping liabilities in rupees, so the *foreign investor* bears the currency risk; the trade-off is that investors demand a higher rupee coupon to compensate for that risk. The choice hinges on relative all-in cost and how much currency risk the firm wants to keep.

**B4. In May 2013, Ben Bernanke merely *hinted* the Fed might slow its bond-buying. No rate hike occurred, yet the rupee fell from about 55 to nearly 68 per dollar. Explain the mechanism and name the framework.**

This is the **Taper Tantrum**, the textbook case of **push-factor contagion.** A *signal* of future Fed tightening — not an actual hike — raised the expected return on safe US assets, so FPIs pulled money out of emerging markets to rotate home. The "Fragile Five" (India, Indonesia, Brazil, Turkey, South Africa), all running large current-account deficits reliant on foreign inflows, were hit hardest because they most needed the capital that was leaving. Nothing had changed in Indian fundamentals; a shift in *US* policy expectations triggered the exit. India's defence — Raghuram Rajan's FCNR(B) dollar-deposit swap window — pulled in about USD 26 billion to stem the flight.

**B5. Thailand devalued the baht in July 1997. Within months the Indonesian rupiah collapsed roughly 80% and Korea needed an IMF bailout, though neither had an obvious mechanical link to Thailand. India was barely touched. Explain both facts.**

The transmission was **financial and psychological, not mechanical.** The same international banks that had lent across Asia pulled credit everywhere at once (the **common-lender channel**), and investors treated all "Asian tiger" economies as a single trade to exit (**herding/confidence channel**). So a Thai shock became a regional collapse without any direct trade link. India was spared because it had a largely **closed capital account and a non-convertible rupee** — foreign hot money simply could not flood in or rush out at scale. It is a real-world natural experiment: capital controls buy insulation at the cost of forgoing integration's benefits.

**B6. A US pension fund earns an excellent return on an Indian infrastructure project, but the Indian government imposes temporary capital controls and it cannot convert its rupees to dollars to take the money home. Which specific risk materialised, and how does it differ from default risk?**

**Transfer / convertibility risk** — a form of country risk. The government is perfectly *able* and *willing* to let the investment succeed; the problem is a restriction on moving money across the capital account, which traps otherwise-good returns. This differs from **sovereign default risk**, where the government cannot or will not pay its own obligations. The nightmare here is precisely that the investment *worked* — great returns you cannot repatriate. Country risk includes both the risk of not being paid *and* the risk of being paid but not being able to take it out.

**B7. An investor is choosing between an Indian government bond and a US Treasury. The rupee offers a much higher yield. She hedges the currency exposure with a forward. Why does the hedge eat most of the extra yield, and what principle explains it?**

**Covered Interest Parity (CIP).** The forward exchange rate must adjust so that hedging removes any risk-free advantage from a higher-yielding currency — otherwise arbitrageurs would earn free money. A higher-interest currency like the rupee therefore trades at a forward *discount*, and locking in that forward costs roughly the interest-rate differential. So the hedged return on the Indian bond collapses back toward the US Treasury yield: the extra carry she was chasing is almost exactly the price of the hedge. Hedging a high-yield currency is not costless insurance.

**B8. An index provider raises India's weight in the MSCI Emerging Markets Index. No Indian company's fundamentals have changed. Why does this move markets, and through what channel?**

Because **trillions of dollars in index-tracking passive funds mechanically allocate capital based on these labels.** When India's weight rises, every fund benchmarked to MSCI EM must buy Indian stocks to match the index, regardless of valuation or fundamentals — triggering billions in passive inflows purely from the reclassification. This is why index-provider reviews are market-moving events, and why India's rising weight (as China's has fallen) matters so much for Indian equities independent of any earnings news.

**B9. A firm has a US subsidiary. Classify each of the following: (a) a EUR 10m receivable due in 90 days, (b) restating the subsidiary's assets into rupees for the consolidated balance sheet, (c) a sustained rupee appreciation making the firm's exports uncompetitive over the next decade. Which is hardest to hedge?**

(a) **Transaction exposure** — a known future foreign-currency cash flow whose home-currency value is uncertain; cleanly hedgeable with a forward, future or option. (b) **Translation (accounting) exposure** — a restatement effect on the balance sheet, not a cash-flow event. (c) **Economic (operating) exposure** — the deep, long-run impact of currency moves on competitive position and future cash flows. (c) is by far the **hardest to hedge**, because it is diffuse, long-dated and tied to competitive dynamics rather than a single dated cash flow that a forward can offset.

## Section C — Interview-Style Questions with Model Answers

**C1. "In one sentence, why do international financial markets exist?"**

*Model answer:* "They exist to move capital across the geographic, currency, regulatory and trust barriers that otherwise trap savings inside national borders — so that surplus savings in one country can fund high-return projects in another, lowering the cost of capital for good borrowers everywhere and raising returns for savers everywhere." Lead with the function — closing the gap between where savings sit and where they are best used — and the rest of the chapter becomes the machinery that delivers it.

**C2. "Explain FDI versus FPI, and why the distinction matters for a country's stability."**

*Model answer:* "FDI is buying *control* of a real business or asset abroad — a factory, a controlling stake — and it's sticky: strategic, long-term, hard to reverse. FPI is buying *securities* for returns without control, and it's hot: liquid and able to exit in a day. The distinction matters enormously for stability because a country funded by sticky FDI is far safer than one funded by flighty FPI. When sentiment turns, FDI stays put — you can't sell a half-built factory overnight — but FPI reverses on a Fed headline, dragging the currency and equity market down with it. So when you assess a country's balance-of-payments fragility, you look hard at how much of its foreign funding is the sticky kind versus the hot kind."

**C3. "Walk me through the two ways a foreign investor can own an Indian company, and why the depositary-receipt route exists."**

*Model answer:* "The direct route: the investor registers as an FPI with SEBI through a custodian, converts dollars to rupees, buys shares that the custodian settles through the domestic depository and holds in custody, and repatriates dividends and proceeds back through the currency at exit. That's a lot of friction — registration, currency conversion, unfamiliar settlement, exchange-control rules. The depositary-receipt route removes that friction by shifting it to an intermediary: the Indian shares are deposited with a local custodian, a foreign depositary bank issues receipts against them, and those receipts list on the NYSE or London in dollars or euros. The investor now buys 'Infosys' on a familiar exchange, in a familiar currency, with familiar settlement, and the bank handles the cross-border plumbing. The whole point is to move complexity from the investor to a specialist."

**C4. "Why isn't a 'Eurobond' European, and how is it different from a Yankee or a Samurai bond?"**

*Model answer:* "'Euro' here is a historical accident meaning *offshore*, not European. A Eurobond is a bond denominated in a currency outside that currency's home country — an Indian firm issuing a US-dollar bond in London, say. A Yankee and a Samurai are *foreign* bonds, which is a different animal: a foreign bond is issued in the *local* currency of the market where it's sold — a Yankee is a foreign issuer selling dollars in the US, a Samurai is a foreign issuer selling yen in Japan. So the test is simple: if the bond's currency matches the local market, it's a foreign bond; if the currency is foreign to that market, it's a Eurobond. The reason the Eurobond market thrives is regulatory arbitrage — it escapes the registration and disclosure burden of domestic markets, which lowers cost and speeds issuance while reaching a deep, unified global investor base."

**C5. "A country wants to open its capital account. What macro constraint does it run into?"**

*Model answer:* "The **Impossible Trinity** — a country cannot simultaneously have a fixed exchange rate, free capital movement, and independent monetary policy. It can pick at most two. If you open the capital account and want to keep an independent monetary policy, you have to let the exchange rate float, because free capital flows will otherwise arbitrage away any gap between your interest rate and the world's, breaking the peg. This is exactly why emerging markets manage their capital accounts so carefully: full convertibility buys you integration and a lower cost of capital, but it forces a choice between controlling your currency and controlling your interest rates. India has resolved this by keeping full current-account convertibility but only partial capital-account convertibility — trading some integration for stability, which is why it was relatively insulated in 1997 and 2008."

**C6. "How should I think about the total return on a foreign investment, and what's the mistake people make?"**

*Model answer:* "Total return in your home currency is roughly the local asset return *plus* the currency return — two separable components. The mistake is to focus only on the asset and ignore the currency. You can be completely right on the stock — earn 15% on Indian equities — and still end up with 5% in dollars if the rupee falls 10%, or even a loss if it falls more. Currency risk can dominate or erase the underlying return. On top of that sits country risk — political, sovereign, transfer, regulatory — which is why the same project carries a higher hurdle rate in an emerging market than in a developed one. So the discipline is: decompose every cross-border return into local-asset and currency pieces, and price a country risk premium into the hurdle rate."

**C7. "Is a hedge on a high-yielding foreign bond a free lunch?"**

*Model answer:* "No, and Covered Interest Parity explains why. If you buy a high-yield rupee bond and hedge the currency with a forward, the forward rate is set so the higher-yielding currency trades at a forward *discount* — otherwise there'd be a risk-free arbitrage. Locking in that forward costs roughly the interest-rate differential, so the hedged rupee return collapses back toward the US Treasury yield. The extra carry you were chasing is almost exactly the cost of the hedge. The only way to *keep* the carry is to leave the currency unhedged — which means you're no longer earning a free lunch, you're taking currency risk. When CIP visibly breaks, as it did post-2008 with the cross-currency basis, that itself is a stress signal — a shortage of dollars in offshore funding markets."

**C8. "Close the interview: sum up international financial markets in a couple of sentences."**

*Model answer:* "Capital is trapped inside national borders while its best uses and best sources sit on opposite sides of the world, so humans built bridges — depositary receipts to trade equity abroad in foreign currency, Eurobonds and offshore markets to raise debt outside a currency's home, and a toolkit of forwards, swaps, diversification and risk premia to survive currency and country risk. The master trade-off is that integrating these pools of savings lowers the cost of capital for everyone, but the price of integration is that shocks travel across borders instantly, and currency and country risk sit on top of every cross-border return."

## Section D — MCQs with Reasoning

**D1. The key distinction between FDI and FPI is that FDI:**
(a) Is always larger in rupee value
(b) Buys control of a real business and is sticky, long-term capital
(c) Is only available to sovereign wealth funds
(d) Cannot involve foreign investors

**Answer: (b).** FDI acquires *control* of a real business or asset and is sticky and hard to reverse, whereas FPI buys securities for return without control and is hot, liquid capital. Size (a) is not the defining trait — FPI flows can be huge. FDI is not restricted to sovereign funds (c), and it inherently *does* involve foreign investors (d), which is what makes it "foreign."

**D2. An ADR differs from a GDR principally in that an ADR:**
(a) Represents debt rather than equity
(b) Trades in the US market under SEC regulation, priced in USD
(c) Can never raise fresh capital
(d) Is issued only by US companies

**Answer: (b).** An ADR trades in the US, priced in USD, under SEC oversight; a GDR trades outside the US, typically London or Luxembourg, for international investors. Both represent equity (a). ADRs *can* raise capital — a Level III ADR is a public offering of new shares (c). And ADRs represent *foreign* (non-US) companies' shares trading in the US, not US companies (d).

**D3. A US-dollar bond issued in Singapore by an Indian company is best described as a:**
(a) Foreign bond (Yankee)
(b) Domestic bond
(c) Eurobond (specifically a Eurodollar bond)
(d) Masala bond

**Answer: (c).** The bond is denominated in a currency (USD) *outside* that currency's home country (the US), issued offshore — the definition of a Eurobond, and a Eurodollar bond specifically. A Yankee (a) is a foreign issuer selling USD *in the US*. It is not domestic (b), since it's neither in India's currency nor market in the domestic sense. A Masala bond (d) is *rupee*-denominated, not dollar.

**D4. The distinctive feature of a Masala bond is that:**
(a) It is denominated in US dollars
(b) The Indian issuer bears all the currency risk
(c) It is rupee-denominated and issued offshore, so the foreign investor bears the currency risk
(d) It can only be issued inside India

**Answer: (c).** A Masala bond is rupee-denominated but sold offshore to foreign investors, so if the rupee depreciates the *investor* absorbs the loss — the issuer keeps its liabilities in its home currency. It is not USD-denominated (a); that would be an ordinary offshore dollar bond where the *issuer* bears the risk (b). And being issued *offshore* is precisely what makes it a Masala bond, not an onshore instrument (d).

**D5. The 2013 "Taper Tantrum" is the classic example of:**
(a) A pull factor attracting capital to India
(b) A push factor triggering emerging-market outflows despite unchanged local fundamentals
(c) Foreign direct investment reversing
(d) A sovereign default

**Answer: (b).** A mere *signal* of Fed tapering — a global push factor entirely outside India's control — triggered FPI outflows and hammered the "Fragile Five," even though nothing had changed in their fundamentals. It was not a pull factor (a), which draws capital *in*. The fleeing money was portfolio (FPI), not sticky FDI (c), and no government defaulted (d) — it was a currency and capital-flow shock.

**D6. Covered Interest Parity implies that a currency with a higher interest rate will trade at a:**
(a) Forward premium
(b) Forward discount
(c) Fixed rate to the dollar
(d) Rate unrelated to interest differentials

**Answer: (b).** CIP requires the forward rate to offset the yield advantage, so a higher-yielding currency trades at a forward *discount* — hedging it costs roughly the interest-rate differential, erasing the carry. A forward premium (a) attaches to the *lower*-yielding currency. CIP does not imply a fixed rate (c), and the whole point is that the forward is tightly *linked* to interest differentials, not unrelated (d).

**D7. India was relatively insulated from the 1997 Asian crisis and 2008 GFC primarily because it had:**
(a) A fully convertible capital account
(b) No foreign investors at all
(c) Partial capital-account convertibility limiting hot-money flows
(d) A fixed exchange rate pegged to the dollar

**Answer: (c).** A largely closed capital account and non-convertible rupee meant foreign hot money could not flood in or rush out at scale, buying insulation at the cost of full integration. Full convertibility (a) is the opposite — it would have increased exposure. India did have foreign investors (b), just constrained ones. And India runs a *managed float*, not a hard dollar peg (d).

**D8. Under the "sovereign ceiling" principle, an excellent Indian company's credit rating is:**
(a) Usually higher than India's sovereign rating
(b) Usually capped at or below India's sovereign rating
(c) Completely independent of India's rating
(d) Always exactly equal to a US Treasury rating

**Answer: (b).** A firm's rating rarely exceeds its country's, because the sovereign controls the currency, capital controls and the operating environment the firm depends on — so even a stellar Indian company pays a spread reflecting India's ~BBB- sovereign rating. It is not typically *higher* (a), not independent (c), and certainly not pegged to a US Treasury rating (d), which is a different sovereign entirely.

**D9. Contagion through the "common-lender channel" means that:**
(a) Two countries trade heavily with each other
(b) The same banks lending across many countries pull credit from all of them at once when one gets into trouble
(c) A central bank lends to all commercial banks simultaneously
(d) Investors buy the whole asset class in a boom

**Answer: (b).** The common-lender channel transmits a shock when the same international banks exposed across many markets simultaneously withdraw credit everywhere after losses in one — a purely financial linkage, as in 1997 Asia. It is not the *trade* channel (a), which works through exports. It is not a domestic lender-of-last-resort action (c), and it describes *withdrawal* in a bust, not buying in a boom (d).
