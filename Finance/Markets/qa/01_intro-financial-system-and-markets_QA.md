# Q&A — The Financial System and Markets

Practice question bank for the Financial Markets guide, Chapter 01. Every question is followed by a full answer. Work each one before reading the answer; the goal is to *reason*, not memorise. Indian context (RBI, SEBI, NSE/BSE) with global parallels where useful.

---

## Section A — Concept Check (test understanding)

**A1. What single problem does a financial system exist to solve?**
**Answer.** Savers and users of capital are *different people* with mismatched needs — different amounts, different time horizons, different information, different risk appetites. Left alone they cannot transact. The financial system is society's institutional machine for moving surplus funds from savers (surplus units) to productive users (deficit units), safely, at scale, and across time. When it works, idle savings become investment and investment becomes growth.

**A2. Name the four (or five) frictions that block a direct saver-to-borrower match.**
**Answer.** (1) The **size / denomination** problem — small savings, huge investment needs. (2) The **maturity / duration** problem — savers want liquidity and short horizons; projects are long-lived and illiquid. (3) The **information** problem — asymmetric information causing adverse selection (before the deal) and moral hazard (after). (4) The **risk** problem — any single loan can default. Plus (5) the **trust and enforcement** problem — why would a stranger repay without contracts, courts and records?

**A3. "Every financial instrument is an asset to one party and a liability to another." Explain with an example.**
**Answer.** A financial claim is a promise about future money, so it has two sides. When a saver buys a Government of India bond, the bond is her *asset* (the government owes her interest and principal). The very same bond is the government's *liability*. This double-entry nature is the DNA of all finance — no claim exists without an obligor on the other side.

**A4. Distinguish direct finance from indirect finance. What is the single test?**
**Answer.** In **direct finance**, the borrower sells its own security to the saver through a market — the saver ends up holding *the borrower's* claim (e.g., a mutual fund buys Reliance's bond). In **indirect finance**, an intermediary interposes its own balance sheet — the saver holds a claim on the *intermediary* (a bank deposit), and the intermediary separately holds a claim on the borrower (a loan). The single test: **does the middleman create its own claim in between?** If yes, it is indirect; if it merely facilitates (broker, exchange, underwriter), it is direct.

**A5. List the five transformation functions an intermediary performs.**
**Answer.** Size/denomination transformation (pool small deposits into large loans), maturity transformation (fund long illiquid assets with short liquid liabilities), risk transformation (diversify away individual default risk), information production (screen and monitor borrowers), and liquidity provision (let savers withdraw on demand though assets are locked up). These reduce transaction and information costs enough for the whole economy to save and invest more.

**A6. Money market vs capital market — give the cut-off, purpose and regulator.**
**Answer.** Money market: instruments with maturity **≤ 1 year** (T-bills, commercial paper, CDs, call money, repo); purpose is liquidity and short-term interest-rate management; regulated primarily by the **RBI**. Capital market: instruments with maturity **> 1 year** or perpetual (shares, bonds, debentures); purpose is durable capital formation; regulated primarily by **SEBI**.

**A7. In which market does a company actually receive new money — primary or secondary? Why does the other one still matter?**
**Answer.** Only the **primary market** (IPO, FPO, rights issue, private placement, QIP) — that is where funds actually reach the issuer and capital formation happens. The **secondary market** transfers existing securities between investors, so not a rupee reaches the issuer. It still matters because it provides **liquidity and price discovery** — nobody would buy a new IPO share if they could never sell it, so the secondary market is what makes the primary market possible.

**A8. State the six functions of a well-functioning financial market.**
**Answer.** (1) Mobilising savings / capital formation, (2) price discovery, (3) providing liquidity, (4) reducing transaction and information costs, (5) risk transfer / risk sharing, and (6) efficient allocation of capital to its highest-value uses.

**A9. What is maturity transformation, and why is it both banking's "magic" and its core danger?**
**Answer.** Maturity transformation is funding long-term, illiquid loans with short-term, liquid deposits. It is magic because it lets savers keep on-demand access while their money finances multi-year projects. It is dangerous because if depositors all demand their short-term money at once, the bank cannot instantly recall its long-term loans — a **bank run**. This is why central banks act as lender of last resort.

**A10. Name India's four main sectoral regulators and their US counterparts.**
**Answer.** India: **RBI** (banks, NBFCs, money market, forex, monetary policy), **SEBI** (securities markets), **IRDAI** (insurance), **PFRDA** (pensions). US: **Federal Reserve** (banking, monetary policy), **SEC** (securities), plus **CFTC** (commodity/futures derivatives) and **FDIC** (deposit insurance). One-liner: *SEBI is India's SEC; RBI is India's Fed plus bank regulator.*

## Section B — Applied / Scenario Questions

**B1. Priya deposits ₹1,00,000 in SBI at 3%. SBI lends ₹50 lakh (pooled) to a textile firm at 10% for five years. Identify each transformation at work and where the risk sits.**
**Answer.** This is textbook **indirect finance**. SBI turned Priya's *small, on-demand, safe* deposit into the firm's *large, five-year, riskier* borrowing: **size** transformation (millions of small deposits fund one large loan), **maturity** transformation (on-demand liability funds a 5-year asset), **risk** transformation (Priya bears almost no default risk; SBI does, diversified across many borrowers and cushioned by ₹5 lakh deposit insurance), and **information** production (SBI, not Priya, assesses the firm's creditworthiness). SBI earns the ~7% spread as payment for performing these functions.

**B2. Zomato's 2021 IPO raised ~₹9,375 crore on NSE/BSE. When shares began trading the next day, was that primary or secondary market? Did Zomato receive any of the trading proceeds?**
**Answer.** The IPO itself was the **primary market** — investors paid cash directly for newly issued shares, and that money went to Zomato (and selling shareholders). This is direct finance and capital formation. From the day trading began, those shares changed hands between investors on the exchange — the **secondary market**. Zomato received **nothing** from that trading. Yet the secondary market is essential: it gives every IPO investor an exit and continuously *discovers* Zomato's price, telling the company and the world what its equity is worth.

**B3. TCS expects to receive $10 million in three months and fears the rupee will strengthen. It enters a forward contract with its bank to sell dollars at a fixed rate. Which market function is on display, and who now bears the currency risk?**
**Answer.** This is **risk transfer** via the derivatives market. By locking a rate, TCS removes its currency uncertainty; the risk is transferred to the bank, which is willing to bear (and can further hedge) it for a price. Note the whole system at work: a benchmark price (the forward rate, anchored to spot and interest-rate differentials), liquidity, and risk transfer — the same functions that operate for a farmer hedging crop prices or an importer hedging costs.

**B4. In 2018 IL&FS, an infrastructure-financing NBFC, defaulted on its short-term commercial paper. Explain the mechanism that let a single default freeze credit across the whole NBFC sector.**
**Answer.** IL&FS had funded long-term infrastructure loans with short-term borrowings — aggressive **maturity transformation**. When it defaulted, mutual funds holding its commercial paper took losses. Investors, unable to tell which *other* NBFCs were similarly exposed (information asymmetry), stopped lending short-term to **any** NBFC. Short-term funding dried up, so even solvent NBFCs faced a **liquidity** crunch — a solvency-vs-liquidity problem spreading by contagion. It shows how tightly the money market, mutual funds, NBFCs and confidence are interconnected, and why maturity transformation carries systemic risk.

**B5. A bank's assets exceed its liabilities, yet it collapses in a single week. How is that possible?**
**Answer.** The bank was **solvent but illiquid**. Solvency means assets > liabilities on the books; liquidity means being able to meet cash demands *right now*. Because of maturity transformation, most assets are locked in long-term loans that cannot be recalled instantly. If depositors demand their money faster than the bank can raise cash (a run, or a wholesale-funding freeze), it fails despite positive net worth. This distinction — liquidity ≠ solvency — is precisely why a central bank stands ready as lender of last resort.

**B6. A start-up founder says, "We'll just list on the NSE to raise the ₹100 crore we need to build our factory." What is conceptually confused here?**
**Answer.** Listing/trading on the NSE is the **secondary market**, where money flows between investors, not to the company. To *raise* ₹100 crore the firm must make a **primary** issue — an IPO, or after listing an FPO, rights issue, private placement or QIP. Listing does enable future capital raising and gives shares liquidity, but the act of shares trading on the exchange never puts cash in the company's account. The founder is conflating "being listed" with "raising capital."

**B7. Classify each of the following along the primary/secondary and money/capital axes: (i) an RBI 91-day Treasury bill auction, (ii) buying 100 Infosys shares on NSE, (iii) a company's ₹500 crore rights issue of equity, (iv) two banks trading in the call money market.**
**Answer.** (i) 91-day T-bill auction — **primary** market issuance, **money** market (≤ 1 year). (ii) Buying Infosys on NSE — **secondary** market, **capital** market (equity is perpetual). (iii) Rights issue of equity — **primary** market (new money reaches the issuer), **capital** market. (iv) Call money between banks — **money** market (overnight), and it is a fresh borrowing/lending transaction so effectively primary; it is wholesale, OTC and RBI-influenced.

**B8. A retail investor holds physical share certificates from the 1990s and complains they are hard to sell. Which piece of market infrastructure solved this, and how?**
**Answer.** **Depositories** — NSDL and CDSL — solved it by **dematerialisation**: holding securities as electronic records rather than paper. This removes the risks of forgery, loss, theft and slow physical transfer, and lets ownership change instantly on settlement. Combined with **clearing corporations** (NSE Clearing, CCIL) that guarantee and settle trades and remove counterparty risk, dematerialisation is what makes modern, liquid, T+1 secondary trading possible. Physical certificates are illiquid precisely because they sit outside this infrastructure.

## Section C — Interview-Style Questions (with model answers)

**C1. "Walk me through what a financial system actually does."**
**Model answer.** "A financial system exists because savers and investors are different people with different needs — different sizes, time horizons, information and risk appetites. It moves money from those who have a surplus to those who can use it productively, and it does this through four components: **markets** (where claims are created and traded), **intermediaries** (banks, funds, insurers that stand in between), **instruments** (the claims themselves, each an asset to one side and a liability to the other), and **infrastructure and regulators** (the rules, depositories, clearing houses and referees that supply trust). Funds can travel two roads — directly through markets or indirectly through an intermediary's balance sheet. In doing all this, the system turns idle savings into investment and investment into economic growth."

**C2. "What's the difference between direct and indirect finance, and give me a real example of each?"**
**Model answer.** "The test is whether a middleman creates its own claim. In **direct finance**, the saver ends up holding the borrower's own security — for instance, when a mutual fund buys Reliance's newly issued bonds, the money goes straight to Reliance and the fund holds Reliance's actual bond. Brokers and underwriters assist but don't interpose their own balance sheet. In **indirect finance**, an intermediary does interpose — I deposit ₹1 lakh in HDFC Bank and hold a *deposit* (a claim on the bank); HDFC separately lends to a business and holds a *loan* (a claim on the borrower). Two contracts, the bank's balance sheet in the middle, and I never meet the ultimate borrower."

**C3. "Companies raise money on the stock market — true or false? Defend your answer."**
**Model answer.** "Mostly false, and the distinction matters. Companies raise money only in the **primary market** — an IPO, FPO, rights issue or QIP — where new securities are issued and cash reaches the company. The 'stock market' most people mean is the **secondary market**, where already-issued shares trade between investors on the NSE or BSE; not a rupee of that reaches the company. But the secondary market is not pointless — its liquidity and price discovery are exactly what make investors willing to fund the primary market in the first place. So the accurate statement is: companies raise money *through* listed securities, but only at the moment of issuance, not from day-to-day trading."

**C4. "Why do banks and other intermediaries exist at all? Couldn't savers just lend directly?"**
**Model answer.** "In principle yes, but the frictions make it impractical for most people. A saver lending directly faces mismatched sizes, mismatched maturities, no way to assess or monitor the borrower, and concentrated default risk. Intermediaries exist because they *transform* claims and slash costs: they pool small savings into large loans (size), fund long assets with short deposits (maturity), diversify across hundreds of borrowers so any single default barely dents the depositor (risk), specialise in credit screening and monitoring (information), and promise on-demand withdrawal (liquidity). By doing this at scale they cut transaction and information costs so dramatically that the whole economy can save and invest far more than one-to-one lending ever would allow. That reduction in cost *is* their economic value, and the spread is their pay."

**C5. "Explain price discovery and why it matters beyond the trading floor."**
**Model answer.** "Price discovery is the market setting an asset's price through the continuous interaction of buyers and sellers, so the price reflects the collective judgment of all participants. The Nifty level, a bond's yield, the rupee's exchange rate — each is *discovered*, not decreed. It matters beyond trading because these prices are signals that guide real decisions in the economy. A high share price lowers a firm's cost of equity and encourages it to invest; a rising bond yield tells governments and companies that borrowing has become expensive; the exchange rate steers exporters and importers. Efficient price discovery is how markets allocate capital to its highest-value uses — it links the financial world to the real economy."

**C6. "Who regulates what in Indian financial markets, and how does it compare to the US?"**
**Model answer.** "India uses a **sectoral** model. The **RBI** regulates banks, NBFCs, the money market, forex and payment systems, and runs monetary policy. **SEBI** regulates the securities markets — stocks, bonds, mutual funds, brokers and exchanges. **IRDAI** handles insurance and **PFRDA** handles pensions; **IBBI** oversees insolvency. Government bonds sit at an interesting overlap between RBI and SEBI. In the US it's split differently: the **Fed** for banking and monetary policy, the **SEC** for securities, the **CFTC** for commodity and futures derivatives, and the **FDIC** for deposit insurance. The clean mapping to remember is: SEBI is India's SEC, and RBI is India's Fed plus bank regulator rolled into one."

**C7. "What's the connection between the money market and the equity market — aren't they separate worlds?"**
**Model answer.** "They're tightly linked through interest rates. The RBI sets short-term rates in the money market — the repo rate and call money rate. When those rise, the entire discount rate for future cash flows rises: bond prices fall (yields up), and equities, which are valued as the present value of future earnings, get repriced downward too. Higher short rates also raise firms' borrowing costs and can slow the economy, feeding back into earnings expectations. So a move at the very short end of the money market ripples all the way out to long-dated bonds and equity valuations. No segment is an island — the financial system is a web, and interest rates are one of its main transmission cables."

## Section D — MCQs (with reasoning)

**D1.** Which of the following is *not* a component of the financial system?
(a) Financial markets (b) Financial instruments (c) Financial intermediaries (d) Fiscal deficit
**Answer: (d).** The four components are markets, instruments, intermediaries, and infrastructure/regulators. A fiscal deficit is a government's budget shortfall — it may drive borrowing *in* the system but is not a component of it.

**D2.** A saver who holds a bank fixed deposit that the bank has on-lent to a manufacturer is engaged in:
(a) Direct finance (b) Indirect finance (c) Primary market investment (d) A derivative transaction
**Answer: (b).** The bank interposes its own balance sheet — the saver holds a claim on the *bank*, not the manufacturer. That defining feature makes it indirect finance.

**D3.** In which market does the issuing company actually receive fresh funds?
(a) Secondary market (b) Primary market (c) Both equally (d) Neither
**Answer: (b).** Only the primary market channels money to the issuer; the secondary market merely transfers existing securities between investors.

**D4.** Treasury bills, commercial paper and certificates of deposit are traded in the:
(a) Capital market (b) Equity market (c) Money market (d) Derivatives market
**Answer: (c).** All three have maturities of one year or less, which is the defining feature of money-market instruments regardless of who issues them.

**D5.** "A bank can be solvent yet still fail." The best explanation is:
(a) It has more liabilities than assets (b) It cannot meet immediate cash demands because assets are locked in long-term loans (c) Its shareholders sold their shares (d) SEBI cancelled its licence
**Answer: (b).** This is the liquidity-vs-solvency distinction. Solvent means assets exceed liabilities; a solvent bank still fails if it cannot raise cash fast enough — the risk created by maturity transformation.

**D6.** Which pairing of regulator and domain is correct in India?
(a) SEBI — banks and NBFCs (b) RBI — insurance (c) SEBI — securities markets (d) PFRDA — commodity derivatives
**Answer: (c).** SEBI regulates securities markets. RBI covers banks/NBFCs/money market/forex; IRDAI covers insurance; PFRDA covers pensions — so (a), (b) and (d) are all mismatched.

**D7.** The statement "every financial instrument is an asset to its holder and a liability to its issuer" implies that:
(a) All instruments are risky (b) Financial claims always net to a two-sided obligation (c) Only bonds have this feature (d) Equity has no issuer
**Answer: (b).** A claim is a promise of future money, so it necessarily has an obligor on the other side — an asset for one party, a liability for the other. It applies to equity too (the company's obligation to its residual owners).

**D8.** NSDL and CDSL primarily provide which function to the market?
(a) Setting the repo rate (b) Underwriting IPOs (c) Holding securities electronically (depository function) (d) Insuring deposits
**Answer: (c).** They are depositories that dematerialise and hold securities electronically — the reason paper share certificates are largely gone. Repo rate is RBI; underwriting is merchant bankers; deposit insurance is DICGC in India.

**D9.** Which function of financial markets is most directly at work when a farmer locks in a future crop price using a futures contract?
(a) Price discovery (b) Capital formation (c) Risk transfer (d) Liquidity provision
**Answer: (c).** The farmer transfers price risk to a counterparty willing to bear it. (Price discovery and liquidity are also present in the futures market, but the farmer's *purpose* here is hedging — risk transfer.)

**D10 (Case).** Vaibhav Ltd needs ₹200 crore for a plant that will only earn cash after seven years. Its CFO proposes funding it entirely with 90-day commercial paper "because it's cheaper." Identify the flaw and the sounder approach.
**Answer.** The flaw is aggressive **maturity transformation / a matching-principle violation**: funding a seven-year illiquid asset with 90-day paper forces the firm to *roll over* the borrowing repeatedly. If credit markets tighten at any rollover date — exactly the IL&FS story — the firm faces a liquidity crisis despite a sound project. The sounder approach is to match maturities: fund a long-lived asset with **long-term capital market** funds (equity, or 7–10 year bonds/debentures), accepting a slightly higher cost in exchange for eliminating rollover/liquidity risk.
