# Q&A — The Investment Policy Statement

## The chapter in one line

$$\text{IPS} = \underbrace{\text{Return} + \text{Risk}}_{\text{Objectives}} + \underbrace{\text{Time} + \text{Taxes} + \text{Liquidity} + \text{Legal} + \text{Unique}}_{\text{Constraints (TTLLU)}}, \qquad \text{IPS} + \text{CME} \rightarrow \text{SAA}$$

**One-line statement:** The Investment Policy Statement is the written contract that converts a client's messy human circumstances into a precise return-and-risk specification bounded by five constraints, which — combined with capital-market expectations — produces the strategic asset allocation and defines what "success" even means.

---

## Section A — Concept Check

**A1. Define the Investment Policy Statement and state its single most important output.**
The IPS is a written document that specifies a client's investment objectives (return and risk) and constraints (time horizon, taxes, liquidity, legal/regulatory, unique circumstances), and thereby governs how the portfolio is built, managed, and judged. Its single most important output is the **strategic asset allocation (SAA)** — the long-run policy mix of asset classes that is the portfolio's home base. The IPS describes the *investor*; combined with capital-market expectations about the *market*, it yields the SAA.

**A2. What three failures occur when a portfolio is run with no IPS?**
(1) **Objective drift** — the portfolio reflects the manager's views or market fashions rather than the client's needs. (2) **Panic at the bottom** — with no pre-agreed risk budget, the client discovers their true risk tolerance during a crash and sells. (3) **No accountability standard** — with no benchmark, performance reviews degenerate into "you should have bought Bitcoin." The IPS is the engineering spec that prevents all three.

**A3. Distinguish required return from desired return.**
*Required* return is what the investor *needs* to meet their goals (fund retirement, preserve real capital, meet a spending rule) — it is derived from facts, not preference. *Desired* return is what the investor *wants* — often higher and frequently infeasible given the risk constraint. The manager's job is to derive the required return, check it against the risk limit, and reconcile any gap between what the client wants and what the risk budget allows.

**A4. Along what four dimensions must a return objective be specified, and why?**
Nominal vs real (is inflation included?), pre- vs post-tax, total return vs income only, and gross vs net of fees. Each dimension changes the number materially — a 12% pre-tax nominal target may be only 8.5% post-tax, and an endowment preserving purchasing power thinks in *real* terms while a retiree living on coupons needs *income*. A target stated without these tags ("grow at 8%") is operationally meaningless.

**A5. Distinguish ability and willingness to take risk, and give the reconciliation rule.**
*Ability* is objective capacity — it rises with a longer horizon, greater wealth relative to needs, flexible spending, stable outside income, and low liquidity needs. *Willingness* is psychological comfort with volatility, drawn out through questionnaires and behavioural history. **When they conflict, build to the lower of the two, document the gap, and educate the client.** A portfolio the client abandons in a panic is worthless even if they "could afford" the risk on paper.

**A6. Which conflict is the *dangerous* one — high ability/low willingness, or low ability/high willingness?**
Low ability with high willingness is the dangerous case — e.g., a retiree who "loves stocks." Here willingness would push toward more risk, but ability is the binding real-world limit, so the client must be *restrained*. In the reverse case (young investor, high ability, low willingness), you simply respect the lower willingness; the cost is only a possibly under-aggressive portfolio, not ruin.

**A7. Name the five constraints (TTLLU) and give a one-line role for each.**
**T**ime horizon — the master variable of risk; long horizon permits volatile high-return assets. **T**axes — shape the return target and the choice of instruments/strategies. **L**iquidity — planned cash outflows that force cash and penalise illiquid assets. **L**egal/regulatory — the binding legal walls (trust deeds, prudent-investor rules, ERISA, IRDAI/solvency norms). **U**nique circumstances — idiosyncratic constraints (ESG/Shariah screens, concentrated founder stock, donor restrictions).

**A8. Distinguish a liquidity requirement from liquidity risk.**
A liquidity *requirement* is a predictable, plannable cash outflow (a child's education next year, an endowment's 4.5% annual spend) that the manager funds with cash and marketable securities. Liquidity *risk* is the danger of being *forced* to sell illiquid assets at a discount during a crisis. The first is planned and budgeted; the second is a tail danger. Both must be managed, but they are not the same thing.

**A9. Why is a retiree not automatically a "short-horizon" investor?**
Because a 65-year-old may live 25+ more years, so much of the corpus is genuinely long-term; only the *near-term spending* is short-horizon. Retirees typically have **multi-stage** horizons — a short bucket for imminent spending and a long bucket that can still hold equities. Treating the whole corpus as short-horizon forces excessive de-risking and fails to preserve real value over a long retirement.

**A10. Why must the IPS and capital-market expectations be kept in separate boxes?**
The IPS describes the *investor* and is durable — it changes only on major life events (divorce, windfall, retirement). Capital-market expectations describe the *market* and change constantly. IPS + CME together produce the asset allocation. Blurring them means rewriting policy every time the market moves — which is exactly the panic-driven, buy-high/sell-low behaviour the IPS exists to prevent.

**A11. State the endowment required-return shortcut and why the objective is "demanding."**
Required nominal return ≈ **Spending rate + Inflation + Fees**. It is demanding because the endowment must both fund current spending *and* preserve real purchasing power in perpetuity — so a 4.5% spend, 5% inflation, and 0.5% fees imply ~10% nominal, unreachable with bonds alone. This is why perpetual, low-liquidity endowments tilt toward growth and illiquid assets (the "Yale model").

**A12. Why is a DB pension's risk measured in *surplus* terms rather than against cash?**
Because the plan's true objective is to fund a stream of *liabilities*, not to beat cash. If rates fall, the PV of liabilities rises; an equity portfolio may "make money" yet the funded ratio (assets ÷ liabilities) can still deteriorate. Framing risk as the danger of the surplus (assets − PV of liabilities) eroding, and duration-matching assets to liabilities (LDI), keeps the funded status stable against rate moves.

## Section B — Applied / Numerical Problems

**B1. Endowment required return.** The Meridian University Endowment holds ₹500 crore. Its spending policy funds 4.5% of assets annually; long-run inflation is 5.0%; fees run 0.5%. It is tax-exempt and perpetual. (a) Find the required nominal return using the additive approximation. (b) What is the one annual liquidity requirement in rupees? (c) Comment on feasibility with bonds alone.

**(a)** Required nominal return = Spending + Inflation + Fees = 4.5 + 5.0 + 0.5 = **10.0%**.
**(b)** Liquidity requirement = 4.5% × ₹500 cr = **₹22.5 crore** per year.
**(c)** A ~10% nominal target is far above bond yields, so bonds alone cannot fund spending *and* preserve real value. With an infinite horizon and only 4.5% annual liquidity need, the endowment has very high risk ability and can accept illiquidity — hence a growth/illiquid tilt (equities, private equity, real assets), which the IPS constraints *permit*.

**B2. Reconciling ability and willingness for an individual.** Ravi, 35, earns a secure ₹40 lakh salary, has ₹1 crore invested, and retires at 60. He needs the corpus to grow at inflation + 5% (≈ 11% nominal). He panic-sold in 2020 and says a 20% loss would "keep me awake for months." Ability suggests ~80% equity; willingness suggests ~45% equity. Resolve.

**Step 1 — Assess ability.** 25-year horizon, stable income, no near-term liquidity need → **high ability** (~80% equity capacity).
**Step 2 — Assess willingness.** Panic-selling history, low stated loss tolerance → **low willingness** (~45% equity).
**Step 3 — Apply the rule.** Build to the *lower* (45%). But a 45% equity mix cannot plausibly deliver ~11% nominal — the required return is infeasible at that risk level. This is the conflict the IPS exists to surface.
**Step 4 — Document the resolution.** Set a compromise ~55–60% equity that gives the plan a *chance*, and write the mismatch into the IPS: educate on long-horizon volatility, use rupee-cost averaging and pre-committed rebalancing, and revisit the goal/savings rate/retirement age if willingness cannot rise. **Reconciliation:** the numbers do not both fit, so the honest output is an explicit trade-off — take slightly more risk than willingness alone, *and* adjust the goal or save more.

**B3. DB pension surplus and funded ratio.** The Kaveri Pension Plan has ₹800 crore assets and ₹750 crore liabilities (PV of promised pensions). Liability duration ≈ 12 years. (a) Compute surplus and funded ratio. (b) If interest rates fall and liability PV rises 8% while a 30%-equity/70%-bond portfolio rises only 4%, does the surplus survive? (c) What does this imply for allocation?

**(a)** Surplus = 800 − 750 = **₹50 crore**; funded ratio = 800/750 = **106.7%**.
**(b)** New liabilities = 750 × 1.08 = ₹810 cr. New assets = 800 × 1.04 = ₹832 cr. New surplus = 832 − 810 = **₹22 cr**; new funded ratio = 832/810 = **102.7%**. The surplus *shrank* by ₹28 cr and the funded ratio fell ~4 pp even though the portfolio "made money" — because liabilities grew faster than assets.
**(c)** Risk must be measured against liabilities, not cash. Duration-match a large fixed-income core to the ~12-year liability duration (LDI) so the asset PV moves with the liability PV, and fund a smaller return-seeking equity sleeve from the surplus — e.g., ~65% duration-matched bonds, 30% equities, 5% cash. **Reconciliation:** had the bond core been duration-matched, its PV would have risen alongside liabilities, protecting the funded ratio — which is the whole point of LDI.

**B4. Post-tax return target.** A taxable individual states a desired 12% *pre-tax nominal* return. Interest/short-term gains are taxed at 30% and long-term equity gains at 12.5%. If the portfolio's return splits as 4% (taxed at 30%) and 8% (taxed at 12.5%), what is the post-tax return, and why does this matter for the IPS?

Post-tax = 4%(1 − 0.30) + 8%(1 − 0.125) = 2.80 + 7.00 = **9.80%**.
So the "12%" the client cited is really **9.8% net of tax**. It matters because the IPS return objective must be stated on a consistent basis: if the client actually *needs* 12% to fund goals, the portfolio must earn ~14.5%+ pre-tax — a far more demanding, riskier target. Confusing pre- and post-tax targets is a classic way plans quietly fall short.

**B5. Liquidity-forced allocation floor.** A family portfolio of ₹4 crore must fund ₹60 lakh of school fees in 12 months and hold a ₹40 lakh emergency reserve. What minimum fraction should sit in cash/near-cash, and how does this constrain the equity sleeve?

Planned near-term outflow + reserve = 60 + 40 = ₹100 lakh = **₹1 crore**. As a fraction: 1/4 = **25%** must be in cash and highly marketable short instruments. That caps risky assets at ≤75% of the portfolio *before* any risk-tolerance consideration — the liquidity constraint binds first. Placing this ₹1 crore in equities would risk having to sell into a downturn exactly when the fees fall due (liquidity risk), so the constraint is non-negotiable regardless of the client's risk appetite.

**B6. Same label, opposite portfolios.** Contrast the SAA that the IPS drivers produce for the Meridian endowment (B1) versus a P&C insurer facing short, lumpy catastrophe claims. State each dominant driver and a plausible equity/illiquid weight.

Endowment: infinite horizon, low (4.5%) liquidity need, high risk ability → **dominant driver = perpetuity & spending** → high equity + large illiquid sleeve (e.g., ~50% equity-like + 35% private/real assets). P&C insurer: short, unpredictable liabilities, high liquidity need, heavy solvency regulation → **dominant driver = liquidity & solvency** → short-duration bonds, minimal illiquidity, only surplus capital in equities (e.g., ~15% equity, near-zero illiquid). **Reconciliation:** both are "institutions," yet the IPS drivers (horizon and liquidity) point in opposite directions — proving the *document*, not the label, determines the portfolio.

## Section C — Interview-Style Questions

**C1. "A client says: just grow my money safely. Walk me through how you turn that into a portfolio."**
Model answer: "Grow safely" is two vague words I quantify through the IPS. First I pin the *return objective* to a number — required vs desired, tagged nominal/real, pre/post-tax, net of fees — say "beat inflation by 4% net." Then the *risk objective*: separate ability (horizon, wealth vs needs, income stability) from willingness (temperament and behavioural history), and anchor to the lower, documenting the gap. Next the five constraints — TTLLU — which rule asset classes in or out: a near-term liquidity need forces cash; a legal cap limits equity; an ESG screen removes sectors; a long horizon permits illiquid growth. That IPS, plus capital-market expectations, feeds an optimiser that finds the efficient-frontier point meeting the required return at or below the risk ceiling while respecting every constraint — the SAA. Finally I write in benchmarks and a rebalancing rule so we can judge success and resist panic. "Grow safely" becomes an engineered specification.

**C2. "How do individuals and institutions differ in their IPS, and why?"**
Model answer: The framework is universal — objectives plus TTLLU — but content diverges because horizon, liquidity, taxes, and legal status differ. Individuals have finite, life-cycle horizons, are *taxable* (a first-order concern), have modest ability but variable, emotion-driven willingness; behaviour dominates, so the manager is part coach. Institutions are perpetual or long-horizon, usually tax-exempt, governed by boards and fiduciary rules — but they differ profoundly from *each other*: DB pensions are liability-driven and think in surplus terms (LDI, duration-matching); endowments are perpetual real-return machines with high risk ability and an illiquidity tilt; life insurers hold long, predictable liabilities and run bond-heavy; P&C insurers face short, lumpy claims and demand high liquidity and short duration. The label tells you little — the IPS drivers tell you everything.

**C3. "Your client's ability and willingness disagree. What do you actually do?"**
Model answer: I never split the difference blindly. I build to the *lower* of the two, then document and educate. If ability is high but willingness low — a young, secure earner who fears volatility — I respect the low willingness and use rupee-cost averaging and pre-committed rebalancing to reduce the emotional cost, while showing long-horizon data so willingness can rise. The more dangerous case is low ability, high willingness — a retiree who loves stocks — where I must *restrain* them, because ability is the hard limit and a bad drawdown could be unrecoverable. If the required return simply cannot be met at the lower risk level, I surface that conflict and offer real trade-offs: lower the goal, save more, or extend the horizon. The IPS turns an invisible conflict into an agreed plan before the market forces the issue.

**C4. "In 2022 both stocks and bonds fell and many 60/40 plans dropped double digits. Did the IPS fail?"**
Model answer: No — a bad year is not a failed policy, provided the IPS pre-agreed the risk budget. The IPS is written precisely so that in calm times the client accepts, in advance, that a bad drawdown can happen; that pre-commitment is its whole behavioural value. What 2022 tests is not the IPS concept but the *inputs* — the correlation and capital-market assumptions behind the allocation. The disciplined response is to check whether anything in the client's *life* changed (usually not) — if not, the IPS stands and we rebalance against it rather than tearing it up. If the episode revealed that the client's true willingness is lower than stated, that is genuine new information and we revise the risk objective. The failure mode the IPS guards against is exactly the temptation to rewrite policy mid-panic.

**C5. "Explain liability-driven investing to someone who thinks a pension should just maximise returns."**
Model answer: A DB pension does not exist to beat the market — it exists to pay promised pensions. Those promises are a liability whose present value moves with interest rates: when rates fall, the liability balloons. If the plan chased returns with all equities and rates dropped, liabilities could grow faster than assets, and the plan could become *underfunded* even though the portfolio "made money." LDI reframes the goal as protecting the *surplus* — assets minus the PV of liabilities. You hold a large bond core whose duration matches the liabilities' duration, so their present values move together and the funded ratio is immunised against rate moves; a smaller equity sleeve, funded largely by the surplus, earns extra return. Maximising return in isolation ignores the liability side — how well-funded plans quietly slip into deficit.

## Section D — Multiple Choice (with reasoning)

**D1. The single most important output of the IPS is:**
(a) a list of approved securities · (b) the strategic asset allocation · (c) the fee schedule · (d) the tax return
**Answer: (b).** The IPS distils client circumstances into objectives and constraints that, with capital-market expectations, produce the policy portfolio — the SAA. Allocation dominates return variance, so this is the consequential output.

**D2. "Ability to take risk" rises with all of the following EXCEPT:**
(a) a longer time horizon · (b) greater wealth relative to needs · (c) higher near-term liquidity needs · (d) stable outside income
**Answer: (c).** High near-term liquidity needs *reduce* ability, because money may have to be withdrawn before drawdowns can recover. The other three all raise capacity to absorb loss.

**D3. When ability and willingness to take risk conflict, the standard rule is to build to:**
(a) the higher of the two · (b) the average of the two · (c) the lower of the two, document the gap, and educate · (d) whatever the optimiser outputs
**Answer: (c).** A portfolio the client abandons at the bottom is worthless; anchor to the lower, record the mismatch, and coach the client.

**D4. The mnemonic TTLLU for IPS constraints stands for:**
(a) Total, Tax, Level, Liquidity, Utility · (b) Time horizon, Taxes, Liquidity, Legal/regulatory, Unique circumstances · (c) Timing, Trading, Leverage, Lending, Utility · (d) Term, Tax, Loss, Limit, Underwriting
**Answer: (b).** Time, Taxes, Liquidity, Legal/regulatory, Unique — the five walls of the box inside which the objectives are pursued.

**D5. An endowment spends 5% of assets a year, expects 4% inflation, and pays 0.5% fees. Its approximate required nominal return is:**
(a) 5.0% · (b) 8.5% · (c) 9.5% · (d) 4.5%
**Answer: (c).** Spending + Inflation + Fees = 5 + 4 + 0.5 = 9.5%, the real-preservation-plus-spending target.

**D6. A DB pension frames its risk objective primarily in terms of:**
(a) tracking error vs the S&P 500 · (b) the surplus (assets minus PV of liabilities) · (c) absolute standard deviation of assets · (d) the Sharpe ratio
**Answer: (b).** The goal is funding liabilities; risk is the danger of the funded status/surplus eroding, which drives LDI and duration-matching.

**D7. A predictable ₹50 lakh school-fee payment due next year is a:**
(a) liquidity risk · (b) liquidity requirement · (c) unique circumstance · (d) legal constraint
**Answer: (b).** It is a planned, plannable outflow the portfolio budgets cash for. Liquidity *risk* is the separate danger of forced illiquid sales in a crisis.

**D8. Which pairing of investor and dominant driver is correct?**
(a) P&C insurer → perpetuity and spending · (b) Endowment → duration-matched liabilities · (c) DB pension → liabilities (LDI) · (d) Individual → tax-exempt status
**Answer: (c).** DB pensions are liability-driven; endowments run on perpetuity/spending, P&C insurers on liquidity/solvency, individuals on tax and behaviour.

**D9. Keeping the IPS and capital-market expectations in separate boxes matters because:**
(a) regulators require two documents · (b) the IPS (investor) is durable while CME (market) changes constantly, preventing policy from being rewritten in every panic · (c) it lowers fees · (d) CME is confidential
**Answer: (b).** The asymmetry — stable investor policy vs constantly changing market views — is what anchors behaviour through storms.
