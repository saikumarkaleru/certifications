## Section A — Concept-Check

**A1. State the three-part test a person must satisfy to be counted as "unemployed."**

To be counted unemployed a person must simultaneously be (a) *without a job*, (b) *available* to work, and (c) *actively searching*. Fail any one and the person is *out of the labour force*, not unemployed — e.g., a retiree, a full-time student, or a stay-at-home parent not looking for work.

**A2. Why is the unemployment rate calculated on the labour force rather than the total population, and what distortion does this create?**

Unemployment rate = (Unemployed / Labour Force) × 100 — only the employed plus the actively searching count in the denominator. This creates the **discouraged-worker effect**: when jobs are scarce, some searchers give up and leave the labour force. Both numerator and denominator shrink, so the *measured* rate can fall even though the labour market has deteriorated. A falling rate is good news only if participation is stable or rising.

**A3. Name the four types of unemployment by cause, one line each.**

- **Frictional** — short-term joblessness from search time between jobs; healthy and unavoidable.
- **Structural** — persistent joblessness from a skills/location/industry mismatch; caused by technology, globalisation, immobility.
- **Cyclical** — joblessness from deficient aggregate demand in a recession; the target of stimulus.
- **Seasonal** — predictable joblessness tied to the calendar; stripped out by seasonal adjustment.

**A4. Which types make up the "natural rate," and how does cyclical unemployment relate to it?**

Natural rate = frictional + structural — the joblessness that persists at full capacity because search and mismatch never vanish. Cyclical is the part *on top*: cyclical = actual − natural. Only the cyclical part can and should be eliminated by demand stimulus.

**A5. Explain why "full employment" does not mean zero unemployment.**

Full employment means cyclical unemployment has been eliminated, leaving only frictional and structural unemployment — the economy sits at its natural rate. Zero unemployment is impossible and undesirable: it would require that no one ever searches for a better job. Pushing below the natural rate does not create sustainable jobs; it overheats the economy and accelerates inflation.

**A6. What is the NAIRU, and why can central banks never observe it directly?**

NAIRU is the Non-Accelerating Inflation Rate of Unemployment — the rate at which inflation is stable. Below it, scarce labour drives wages and prices up; above it, slack eases inflation. It cannot be observed directly: it is a theoretical equilibrium that must be *estimated*, it *shifts over time*, and central banks have repeatedly been wrong about it (US unemployment fell well below the presumed NAIRU in the late 2010s without igniting inflation).

**A7. Define hysteresis in the labour market and explain why it worries central banks.**

Hysteresis is the danger that a long, deep recession *raises the natural rate itself*. Workers unemployed for years lose skills and labour-force attachment and become effectively unemployable — so a temporary *cyclical* shock leaves a *permanent structural* scar. It worries central banks because the cost of letting a recession run deep is not fully reversible; the damage persists even after demand recovers.

**A8. Why is the "flow" view of the labour market more insightful than the "stock" view?**

The observed rate is a snapshot of the level in the "Unemployed" box, but that level is set by the *rates of flow* between three states — Employed, Unemployed, Not in the labour force. The same rate can mean different things: a *high-flow* market (US) has many entering but leaving quickly (short spells); a *low-flow* market (Europe historically) has fewer entering but long spells. Same rate, very different reality — and long-term unemployment is far more damaging.

**A9. Why are wages "sticky downward," and what is the consequence for unemployment?**

Firms are reluctant to cut *nominal* wages because pay cuts destroy morale and productivity and workers resist them. So when labour demand falls, firms cut *jobs* rather than *pay* — the market clears through quantity (layoffs) not price (wage cuts), which is the mechanical origin of involuntary unemployment.

## Section B — Applied / Numerical (Full Solutions)

**B1. Working-age population = 250 million; 160 million employed, 15 million unemployed. Calculate the labour force, unemployment rate, and labour force participation rate (LFPR).**

- Labour force = employed + unemployed = 160 + 15 = **175 million**.
- Unemployment rate = (15 / 175) × 100 = **8.57%**.
- LFPR = (175 / 250) × 100 = **70%**.

**B2. (Discouraged-worker trap.) From B1, 5 million unemployed give up searching and leave the labour force; no one gains a job. Recompute the unemployment rate and LFPR, and comment.**

Now unemployed = 15 − 5 = 10 million; employed unchanged at 160 million.

- New labour force = 160 + 10 = **170 million**.
- Unemployment rate = (10 / 170) × 100 = **5.88%** (down from 8.57%).
- LFPR = (170 / 250) × 100 = **68%** (down from 70%).

Comment: the rate *fell* from 8.57% to 5.88% even though nobody found a job — purely because discouraged workers exited. The falling LFPR is the warning sign, and why analysts never read the rate alone.

**B3. Using B1's numbers (160m employed, 250m working-age), compute the employment-to-population ratio, then recompute after the B2 scenario. Why is it more robust?**

- B1: (160 / 250) × 100 = **64%**.
- B2: employment and working-age both unchanged, so ratio = **64%** — unchanged.

The employment-to-population ratio uses the *working-age population* (not the labour force) as its denominator, so it is immune to the discouraged-worker distortion. It correctly signals that job availability did not improve, whereas the headline rate falsely improved.

**B4. (Okun's Law — output to unemployment.) Natural rate = 4.5%; potential GDP = 20,000. Actual GDP is expected 3% below potential. With Okun coefficient c = 2, estimate the actual unemployment rate.**

Okun's gap form: (Actual − Potential)/Potential ≈ −c × (Unemployment − Natural).

- −0.03 = −2 × (u − 0.045) → (u − 0.045) = 0.015 → u = **0.06 = 6.0%**.

A 3% output shortfall corresponds to unemployment rising 1.5 points above the natural rate, to about **6%**.

**B5. (Okun's Law — unemployment to output.) The same economy's unemployment rises from 5% to 7%. With c = 2, estimate the output gap and actual GDP (potential = 20,000).**

- Level of gap = (u − natural) = 7% − 4.5% = 2.5 points.
- Output gap = −2 × 0.025 = **−5%**.
- Actual GDP = 20,000 × (1 − 0.05) = **19,000 (₹ '000 cr)** — a shortfall of 1,000 below potential.

**B6. (Why the Okun coefficient exceeds 1.) Explain the three effects that make a 1-point rise in unemployment cost ~2% of GDP rather than the naive ~1%.**

1. **Hours before heads** — firms cut *hours* of existing staff before laying anyone off, so output falls more than headcount.
2. **Labour hoarding** — firms keep skilled workers idle rather than fire people they will need in the recovery, reducing measured *productivity*.
3. **Discouraged dropouts** — some workers leave the labour force entirely, so the true loss of labour input exceeds the measured rise in unemployment.

Stacked together these roughly double the naive estimate, giving a coefficient near 2.

**B7. (Expectations-augmented Phillips Curve.) Expected inflation = 3%, natural rate = 5%, actual unemployment = 3%, slope b = 0.5, and a supply shock adds 1 point to inflation. Compute actual inflation. Then find inflation if unemployment equals the natural rate with no shock.**

Formula: Inflation = Expected inflation − b × (Unemployment − Natural) + supply shocks.

Case 1 (u = 3%, shock = +1): = 3% − 0.5 × (3% − 5%) + 1% = 3% + 1% + 1% = **5%**. The tight labour market (u below natural) adds 1 point and the supply shock adds another.

Case 2 (u = natural = 5%, no shock): = 3% − 0.5 × 0 + 0 = **3%** = expected inflation. When unemployment sits at the natural rate with no shock, actual inflation equals expected inflation — the long-run outcome, which is why the long-run Phillips Curve is vertical.

**B8. (Broad vs headline slack.) A labour force of 100 million has 5 million officially unemployed (U-3). There are also 2 million discouraged workers (outside the labour force) and 4 million involuntary part-timers. Compute the headline rate and a broad (U-6-style) rate including all three groups.**

- Headline (U-3) = (5 / 100) × 100 = **5.0%**.
- Broad: numerator = 5 + 2 + 4 = 11 million; denominator = labour force + discouraged = 100 + 2 = 102 million.
- Broad rate = (11 / 102) × 100 ≈ **10.8%**.

The broad measure is more than double the headline, capturing the "hidden" slack the U-3 figure misses — the gap is especially large in economies with big informal sectors.

## Section C — Interview-Style (Model Answers)

**C1. "Why should a finance professional care about the monthly jobs report?"**

Because the labour market is where growth and inflation meet, and the central bank reads inflation risk through it. A strong jobs report signals a tight labour market → wage pressure → inflation risk → the central bank stays hawkish → yields up, rate-sensitive equities down, currency stronger. A weak report reverses every link. Since interest rates price every bond and discount every equity, the labour report is effectively a real-time interest-rate forecast. US non-farm payrolls, released the first Friday of each month, can move yields, currencies, and equity futures in milliseconds — a first-order, tradeable event, not a lagging statistic.

**C2. "Walk me through the rise and fall of the Phillips Curve."**

In 1958 A.W. Phillips found an inverse relationship in British data: low unemployment went with high wage inflation. The intuition is labour scarcity — scarce workers bargain hard for higher wages, and firms raise prices to cover the wage bill. This seemed to hand policymakers a menu. Then the 1970s brought *stagflation* — high inflation *and* high unemployment together — which the simple curve said was impossible. Friedman and Phelps explained why: workers care about *real* wages, so holding unemployment below the natural rate works only while inflation is *unexpected*. Once workers see inflation has eaten their raise, they demand higher nominal wages, firms raise prices again, and unemployment drifts back to the natural rate — now at higher inflation. Hence the expectations-augmented curve, and a *vertical* long-run curve at the natural rate: no permanent trade-off. Three lessons follow — no permanent trade-off, expectations and central-bank credibility are everything, and supply shocks can shift the whole curve.

**C3. "The unemployment rate just fell. Is that good news? How would you check?"**

Not necessarily. The rate divides unemployed by the labour force, so it can fall for a bad reason — discouraged workers leaving the labour force, which shrinks both numerator and denominator. I'd check the *participation rate* and the *employment-to-population ratio*. If the rate fell while participation held steady and the employment-to-population ratio improved, it's a genuine recovery. If it fell while participation dropped, that's a sick economy wearing a healthy mask — people gave up, they didn't get hired. In a developing economy I'd also watch underemployment and informality.

**C4. "Explain the 'good news is bad news' dynamic in markets."**

When markets fear inflation and further hikes, a *strong* jobs report — good for the real economy — is *bad* for asset prices, because a hot labour market means rates stay higher for longer: yields up, rate-sensitive stocks down. A weak report rallies stocks and bonds by bringing cuts closer. The sign flips with the regime: when recession is the fear, weak jobs are bad news; when inflation is the fear, strong jobs are bad news. Early 2023 was the textbook case — blowout payrolls with unemployment near 3.4% repeatedly forced markets to price *more* hikes, and each hot print was a loss for leveraged bondholders.

**C5. "India's official unemployment rate is often lower than in rich countries. Does that mean India's labour market is healthier?"**

No — the headline is misleading. India has no meaningful unemployment insurance and a vast informal sector, so most people cannot afford to be *openly* unemployed; they must do *some* work. The real problem shows up as *underemployment* (the qualified engineer driving a taxi), *informality* (most workers lack formal contracts), and a strikingly *low female participation rate*, long below 30%. For sizing India's growth the headline rate is nearly useless; participation, the formalisation trend, and job quality are what matter. The source is the Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS).

**C6. "What is the natural rate, what moves it, and why does it matter for policy?"**

The natural rate is the unemployment that persists at full employment — frictional plus structural — and it is not zero. It matters because it is the (unobservable) target central banks aim at: pushing below it over-stimulates demand and accelerates inflation. It moves with labour-market institutions (generous benefits and strong firing protections raise it), minimum wages and union power, demographics and skills, the pace of structural change (automation and AI), and hysteresis. Because it shifts and can't be observed directly, central banks estimate it, have often been wrong, and therefore watch a whole suite of labour indicators.

## Section D — Multiple-Choice (with Reasoning)

**D1. A full-time student who is not looking for work is best classified as:**
A. Unemployed
B. Employed
C. Out of the labour force
D. Underemployed

**Answer: C.** Being unemployed requires being jobless, available, *and* actively searching. A student not looking fails the "searching" test — out of the labour force, excluded from both numerator and denominator.

**D2. A skilled coal miner remains jobless for three years after the entire mining industry in his region collapses. This is primarily:**
A. Frictional unemployment
B. Structural unemployment
C. Cyclical unemployment
D. Seasonal unemployment

**Answer: B.** The joblessness stems from a mismatch between the worker's skills/location and the industries now hiring — structural unemployment, persistent (years) unlike frictional (weeks), and not tied to the cycle or calendar.

**D3. The natural rate of unemployment equals:**
A. Cyclical + seasonal unemployment
B. Frictional + structural unemployment
C. Frictional + cyclical unemployment
D. Zero at full employment

**Answer: B.** The natural rate is joblessness that persists at full capacity — search (frictional) plus mismatch (structural). Cyclical is the part *above* it that policy fights.

**D4. During a recession, the measured unemployment rate falls even though hiring has not improved. The most likely explanation is:**
A. The participation rate rose sharply
B. The employment-to-population ratio rose
C. Discouraged workers left the labour force
D. Seasonal adjustment errors

**Answer: C.** Discouraged workers who stop searching exit the unemployed count; both numerator and denominator shrink and the rate can fall despite a worsening market.

**D5. Okun's Law with a coefficient of 2 implies that if unemployment rises 1.5 points above the natural rate, real GDP is about:**
A. 1.5% below potential
B. 2% below potential
C. 3% below potential
D. 0.75% below potential

**Answer: C.** Output gap ≈ −c × (unemployment gap) = −2 × 1.5 = −3%. The coefficient of 2 reflects hours cuts, labour hoarding, and dropouts.

**D6. The long-run Phillips Curve is:**
A. Downward-sloping, offering a permanent trade-off
B. Upward-sloping
C. Vertical at the natural rate of unemployment
D. Horizontal at zero inflation

**Answer: C.** Once inflation expectations fully adjust, unemployment returns to the natural rate regardless of the inflation rate, so the long-run curve is vertical at the NAIRU — no permanent trade-off.

**D7. Stagflation — high inflation and high unemployment together — is best explained by:**
A. A demand-side movement along the naive Phillips Curve
B. An adverse supply shock shifting the Phillips Curve
C. A fall in the natural rate
D. The discouraged-worker effect

**Answer: B.** A supply shock (e.g., an oil-price spike) raises inflation and unemployment together — the supply-shock term in the expectations-augmented curve. The naive demand-side curve cannot explain both rising at once; that is what "broke" it in the 1970s.

**D8. In an inflation-fearing market regime, a much stronger-than-expected non-farm payrolls print is most likely to cause:**
A. Bond yields to fall and rate-sensitive stocks to rise
B. Bond yields to rise and rate-sensitive stocks to fall
C. No market reaction, since jobs data is a lagging indicator
D. The currency to weaken

**Answer: B.** Strong jobs signal a tight labour market and wage/inflation pressure, so the central bank stays hawkish — yields up, rate-sensitive equities down. This is "good news is bad news" when inflation is the dominant fear.

**D9. Which measure is immune to the discouraged-worker distortion?**
A. The headline (U-3) unemployment rate
B. The labour force participation rate
C. The employment-to-population ratio
D. The broad (U-6) unemployment rate

**Answer: C.** The employment-to-population ratio uses the working-age population as its denominator, so workers leaving the labour force do not artificially improve it. The headline rate is the one distorted by discouraged workers.

**D10. Hysteresis in the labour market refers to:**
A. Seasonal patterns that repeat each year
B. A long recession permanently raising the natural rate via skill erosion
C. The lag between a rate cut and its effect on hiring
D. The tendency of wages to be sticky downward

**Answer: B.** Hysteresis is the danger that a prolonged downturn erodes skills and attachment so badly that a temporary shock leaves a permanent structural scar, raising the natural rate itself.

**D11. "Full employment" is best defined as:**
A. Zero unemployment
B. Unemployment at the natural rate (only frictional + structural remain)
C. Unemployment below the NAIRU
D. Every working-age person employed

**Answer: B.** Full employment means cyclical unemployment is eliminated, leaving only frictional and structural joblessness — unemployment sits at the natural rate. It is neither zero nor a state where everyone works.
