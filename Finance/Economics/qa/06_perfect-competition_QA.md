# Q&A — Market Structures — Perfect Competition

A companion practice bank for the concept guide *Market Structures: Perfect Competition*. Every question is followed by a full answer: Section A locks the definitions, Section B builds numerical fluency, Section C rehearses finance-interview framing, and Section D pressure-tests your reasoning against distractors.

## Section A — Concept-Check Questions

**A1. What single question does the theory of market structure try to answer, and where does perfect competition sit on that spectrum?**

Market structure asks: *how much power does an individual firm have over its own price?* This governs pricing power, margin durability, and moats — the core drivers of profitability. Perfect competition is the extreme endpoint of **zero pricing power**: the firm cannot influence price at all and can only choose how much to produce. It is deliberately idealised; its value is as a benchmark against which the pricing power of any real firm is measured.

**A2. State the four core assumptions of perfect competition and the analytical job each one does.**

1. **Large number of small buyers and sellers** — each is tiny relative to the market, so no one's output decision moves price; everyone is a price-taker.
2. **Homogeneous (identical) product** — outputs are perfect substitutes, so buyers have no reason to prefer any seller; price is the only variable.
3. **Free entry and exit** — no barriers, patents, or sunk costs; this is the mechanism that erodes profit in the long run.
4. **Perfect information** — everyone knows all prices and qualities, so no one can charge above the market price and get away with it.

(Two often-added assumptions: perfect mobility of resources and no transaction/transport costs.)

**A3. Why does a perfectly competitive firm face a horizontal demand curve when the law of demand says demand slopes downward?**

There are two demand curves at two different levels. The **market (industry) demand curve** slopes downward normally — total quantity and price move inversely. The **individual firm's demand curve** is horizontal (perfectly elastic) because the firm is a negligible speck; its own output changes are invisible to the market, so it can sell any quantity at the going price and nothing above it. No contradiction — different curves, different levels.

**A4. Explain the identity P = AR = MR for a price-taker.**

- **Average Revenue** = TR/Q = (P × Q)/Q = P. AR always equals price for any firm.
- **Marginal Revenue** = extra revenue from one more unit. Because a price-taker sells every extra unit at the same fixed price P, MR = P.

So P = AR = MR, and all three coincide with the firm's horizontal demand curve. This is unique to perfect competition; in every other structure MR lies *below* price because selling more requires cutting price on all units.

**A5. Derive the P = MC profit-maximisation rule from the general MR = MC rule.**

Any firm maximises profit where the last unit's added revenue equals its added cost: **MR = MC** (on the rising portion of MC). For a price-taker, MR = P. Substituting gives **P = MC**. Intuition: if P > MC, the next unit adds more to revenue than to cost, so produce it; if P < MC, it adds more to cost than revenue, so don't; the optimum is where they are equal.

**A6. How do you read profit or loss off the diagram once output is chosen?**

At the profit-maximising quantity, compare price to **average total cost (ATC)**:
- P > ATC → **supernormal (economic) profit**; total profit = (P − ATC) × Q.
- P = ATC → **normal profit only** (zero economic profit, break-even).
- P < ATC → **loss**, which then triggers the shutdown question.

**A7. State the shutdown rule and explain why it hinges on AVC, not ATC.**

Keep producing if **P ≥ minimum AVC**; shut down if **P < minimum AVC**. Fixed costs are sunk in the short run — paid whether or not you produce — so they are irrelevant to the forward-looking decision. As long as price covers average *variable* cost, every unit contributes something toward those unavoidable fixed costs, so producing loses less than shutting down. Below minimum AVC, each unit loses money before touching fixed costs, so producing zero (losing only fixed cost) is better.

**A8. What is the perfectly competitive firm's short-run supply curve?**

Its **marginal cost curve above the minimum of average variable cost**. Below min AVC the firm supplies zero; above it, the firm supplies the quantity where P = MC. This is why supply curves slope upward — they trace rising marginal cost.

**A9. Walk through the mechanism that drives long-run economic profit to zero.**

Free entry and exit act as a thermostat. If firms earn supernormal profit, the visible profit (perfect information) plus no barriers (free entry) attract new firms → market supply shifts right → price falls → profit shrinks; entry continues until economic profit hits zero. If firms make losses, some exit → supply shifts left → price rises → losses shrink until survivors break even. Equilibrium arrives when economic profit = 0 for the marginal firm.

**A10. Write the full chain of equalities at long-run equilibrium and interpret it.**

**P = MR = MC = ATC = minimum of ATC.** Every firm produces at the lowest point of its ATC curve (productive efficiency), charges a price equal to that minimum cost, and earns exactly normal profit. Because P = MC also holds, the outcome is allocatively efficient. This double efficiency is why perfect competition is the gold-standard benchmark.

**A11. Distinguish normal profit, zero economic profit, and zero accounting profit.**

**Normal profit** is the opportunity cost of the owner's capital and effort — the return achievable elsewhere. Economics treats it as a *cost*, so earning exactly normal profit shows up as **zero economic profit**. That is not zero accounting profit: the firm is healthy, its owners earn the market rate of return, accounting profit is positive. In finance terms, the firm earns exactly its cost of capital — ROIC = WACC, zero EVA.

**A12. Define productive and allocative efficiency and tie each to a condition of perfect competition.**

- **Productive efficiency** — producing at minimum ATC; the same output cannot be made more cheaply. Achieved because long-run equilibrium sits at min ATC.
- **Allocative efficiency** — producing where **P = MC**, so the value of the last unit to consumers equals its cost to society; no reallocation could improve welfare. Achieved by the P = MC rule.

## Section B — Applied / Numerical Problems (with full solutions)

**B1. Revenue schedule for a price-taker.** Market price is ₹50. Complete TR, AR, and MR for Q = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 and state what you notice.

**Solution.** TR = P × Q; AR = TR/Q; MR = ΔTR/ΔQ.

| Q | TR (50×Q) | AR | MR |
|---|-----------|----|----|
| 0 | 0 | — | — |
| 1 | 50 | 50 | 50 |
| 2 | 100 | 50 | 50 |
| 3 | 150 | 50 | 50 |
| 4 | 200 | 50 | 50 |

TR rises in a straight line of slope 50; AR and MR are both constant at 50. This confirms **P = AR = MR = 50** and a horizontal demand curve.

**B2. Profit at the optimum.** A firm's MC = ₹50 at Q = 5,000; its ATC at that output is ₹42. Market price is ₹50. Find the profit-maximising output and total economic profit.

**Solution.** Profit-max where P = MC → Q* = 5,000 (P = 50 = MC there).
- Profit per unit = P − ATC = 50 − 42 = ₹8.
- Total economic profit = 8 × 5,000 = **₹40,000** (supernormal).

**B3. Shutdown decision.** A shale well has AVC (wellhead operating cost) of $35/barrel and full ATC of $55/barrel. Decide, at market prices of (a) $45 and (b) $30, whether to keep pumping.

**Solution.**
(a) At $45: P < ATC ($55) so an accounting loss, but P > AVC ($35). Each barrel contributes $45 − $35 = $10 toward sunk drilling cost. **Keep pumping** — shutting down would forfeit the entire fixed cost.
(b) At $30: P < AVC ($35). Each barrel loses money before fixed costs. **Shut the well in** — produce zero and lose only the sunk cost.

**B4. Break-even versus shutdown prices.** A firm's short-run costs give minimum AVC = ₹18 and minimum ATC = ₹25. Identify (i) the shutdown price, (ii) the break-even price, and (iii) what happens in the range 18 ≤ P < 25.

**Solution.**
(i) Shutdown price = minimum AVC = **₹18** (below it, supply zero).
(ii) Break-even price = minimum ATC = **₹25** (P = min ATC → zero economic profit).
(iii) For 18 ≤ P < 25 the firm makes a **loss but keeps operating** in the short run, because price covers all variable cost and contributes toward fixed cost; it produces where P = MC.

**B5. Total-cost approach to profit.** At Q = 800, a firm's TC = ₹60,000 and market price is ₹90. Compute TR, profit, and ATC, and confirm whether this is supernormal, normal, or loss.

**Solution.**
- TR = 90 × 800 = ₹72,000.
- Profit = TR − TC = 72,000 − 60,000 = **₹12,000** (positive → supernormal).
- ATC = TC/Q = 60,000/800 = ₹75. Since P (90) > ATC (75), profit per unit = ₹15; ×800 = ₹12,000, matching.

**B6. Long-run price adjustment.** In an increasing… no — assume a **constant-cost** industry with minimum ATC = ₹17. Firms currently earn ₹3/unit of supernormal profit at a market price of ₹20. Where does price settle in the long run, and why?

**Solution.** The ₹3 margin attracts entry (free entry, perfect information). New firms raise market supply, shifting it right and pushing price down. Entry stops only when economic profit = 0, i.e., when **P = minimum ATC = ₹17**. Long-run price = **₹17**; each firm earns only normal profit. In a constant-cost industry, input prices don't rise with expansion, so long-run supply is horizontal at ₹17.

**B7. Marginal reasoning trap.** A firm currently produces where MC = ₹40 and P = ₹40, earning ₹12,000 profit. A manager argues, "If we push output higher, total revenue keeps rising at ₹40 per unit, so profit must rise too." Evaluate.

**Solution.** Wrong. Beyond the P = MC point, MC *rises above* ₹40 (MC slopes upward through the optimum). Each extra unit then adds ₹40 of revenue but more than ₹40 of cost, so profit *falls*. MR = MC gives the **profit-maximising** output, not the maximum output. The current ₹12,000 is already the peak; expanding erodes it.

**B8. Contribution-margin framing.** A product line sells at ₹200/unit, has variable cost of ₹150/unit, and carries ₹5,00,000 of allocated fixed (largely sunk) cost. Monthly volume is 8,000 units. Accounting shows a loss. Should management close the line in the short run?

**Solution.**
- Contribution per unit = 200 − 150 = ₹50.
- Total contribution = 50 × 8,000 = ₹4,00,000/month.
- This is below fixed cost (₹5,00,000), so the line loses ₹1,00,000 — hence the accounting loss.
- But P (₹200) > AVC (₹150), so the line covers all variable cost and contributes ₹4,00,000 toward otherwise-unavoidable fixed cost. Closing it would forfeit that ₹4,00,000 and worsen the loss to the full ₹5,00,000. **Keep it running short run** (the perfectly competitive shutdown rule = contribution-margin logic). Reassess in the long run when fixed costs become avoidable.

## Section C — Interview-Style Questions (with model answers)

**C1. "In plain English, what is perfect competition and why should an investor care?"**

Model answer: "Perfect competition is the benchmark of *zero pricing power*. Many firms sell an identical product with perfect information and free entry, so each firm is a price-taker — it accepts the market price and only chooses quantity, producing where price equals marginal cost. Free entry then competes long-run economic profit down to zero at minimum average cost. Investors care because it defines the *absence of a moat*. It's the state Buffett avoids. The whole job of finding durable competitive advantage is finding businesses that escape this benchmark and sustain returns above their cost of capital."

**C2. "Why do DCF and residual-income models fade excess returns toward zero in the terminal period?"**

Model answer: "Because of the long-run zero-economic-profit result. In a competitive market, any firm earning above its cost of capital signals an opportunity; entry and imitation compete that excess away. So assuming a firm earns above its WACC *forever* implicitly assumes it isn't in a competitive market — that it has a durable barrier: a patent, brand, network effect, scale advantage, or regulation. Absent a defensible moat, credible models fade ROIC toward WACC and economic profit toward zero. The length and slope of that fade period is really a judgement about how strong and durable the moat is."

**C3. "An oil company is losing money at current prices but keeps pumping. Is management irrational?"**

Model answer: "Not necessarily — it's the shutdown rule. The relevant line isn't full cost, it's *average variable cost*. If price is below full ATC but above wellhead/operating cost, every barrel still contributes toward the sunk drilling cost, so producing loses less than shutting in. Keep pumping already-drilled wells while cutting *new* drilling; only shut in when price falls below variable cost. That's what we saw in 2015–16 and 2020. Distinguish variable cost, which drives the operate/shut decision, from full cost, which drives long-run entry/exit."

**C4. "Give me a real-world market that behaves close to perfect competition and explain why."**

Model answer: "Plain-vanilla index funds — say S&P 500 trackers. The product is nearly homogeneous (same 500 stocks, near-identical returns), information is transparent (fees published to the basis point), and entry is easy for large asset managers. Theory predicts price driven to marginal cost and profit to zero — and that's exactly what happened: expense ratios collapsed from around 1% to 0.03% or even 0% when Fidelity launched zero-fee funds in 2018. Providers earn essentially normal profit on the product itself and monetise elsewhere — scale, securities lending, ancillary services. It's textbook perfect competition playing out in modern finance, and it's why commoditised beta races to zero while genuinely differentiated active managers can still charge 1–2%."

**C5. "What's the difference between zero economic profit and a failing business?"**

Model answer: "They're opposites in a sense. Zero *economic* profit means the firm earns exactly its opportunity cost of capital — a normal profit. Economics counts that opportunity cost as a cost, so 'zero economic profit' still means positive accounting profit and owners earning the market rate of return. The business is perfectly healthy and viable; it just has no *excess* return, no EVA, ROIC equal to WACC. A failing business earns *below* its cost of capital and destroys value. Confusing the two is the single most common error with this concept."

**C6. "How does perfect competition connect to the search for moats?"**

Model answer: "Perfect competition is the formal definition of *no moat*: identical products, free entry, and perfect information grind economic profit to zero. So a moat is precisely whatever defends against that mechanism — the thing that stops entry from competing your profit away. Brands and switching costs blunt homogeneity; patents and regulation block entry; scale and network effects raise costs for entrants. Understanding perfect competition tells you exactly what a moat must defend against, which is why it's the right mental starting point before you assess any competitive advantage."

**C7. "Why is P = MC called 'efficient,' and efficient for whom?"**

Model answer: "P = MC delivers *allocative* efficiency: the price consumers pay for the last unit equals the marginal cost society incurs to make it, so the last unit is worth exactly what it costs — no more should be made, no less. Combined with production at minimum ATC (*productive* efficiency), society gets the good at the lowest possible cost and in the right quantity, with consumer and producer surplus maximised and zero deadweight loss. It's efficient for society as a whole. That's why it's the welfare benchmark — the formal statement of Adam Smith's invisible hand and the First Fundamental Theorem of Welfare Economics — and why monopoly, which sets P > MC, is judged against it as creating deadweight loss."

## Section D — Multiple-Choice Questions (with reasoning)

**D1.** A perfectly competitive firm's demand curve is:
A. Downward-sloping B. Horizontal (perfectly elastic) C. Vertical D. Upward-sloping

**Answer: B.** The firm is a negligible price-taker, so it can sell any quantity at the market price and nothing above it — perfectly elastic demand. (The *market* demand curve is downward-sloping, but that's a different curve.)

**D2.** For a price-taker, which equality always holds?
A. P = AR = MR B. MR < P C. MR = 0 D. P = ATC

**Answer: A.** AR = P by definition, and each extra unit sells at the fixed price so MR = P too. MR < P (B) is true for imperfect competition, not perfect competition. P = ATC (D) holds only at break-even/long-run equilibrium, not always.

**D3.** A firm should shut down in the short run when:
A. P < ATC B. P < AVC C. P < MC D. It earns zero economic profit

**Answer: B.** Shut down only when price fails to cover average variable cost, because fixed costs are sunk. P < ATC (A) means a loss but the firm keeps operating if P ≥ AVC. Zero economic profit (D) is a healthy break-even, not a reason to close.

**D4.** The short-run supply curve of a perfectly competitive firm is:
A. Its entire MC curve B. Its MC curve above minimum AVC C. Its ATC curve D. Horizontal at market price

**Answer: B.** The firm supplies where P = MC, but only above minimum AVC; below that it supplies zero. So supply is the MC curve above min AVC, not the entire MC curve (A).

**D5.** At long-run equilibrium in perfect competition, which chain is correct?
A. P = MR = MC = ATC = min ATC B. P > MC = ATC C. P = MC > ATC D. MR > MC = min ATC

**Answer: A.** Free entry/exit drives economic profit to zero at the minimum of ATC, where all these magnitudes coincide. Options with P > MC or P > ATC describe supernormal profit or non-competitive structures.

**D6.** "Zero economic profit" means the firm:
A. Is about to go bankrupt B. Earns exactly its opportunity cost of capital (normal profit) C. Has zero accounting profit D. Charges a price of zero

**Answer: B.** Economic profit nets out the opportunity cost of capital, so zero economic profit = normal profit = ROIC equal to WACC. Accounting profit is positive and the firm is healthy — ruling out A, C, and D.

**D7.** Which is the *best* real-world approximation to perfect competition?
A. Branded soft drinks B. A regulated water utility C. A wheat/commodity market D. A smartphone operating system

**Answer: C.** Commodity markets come closest — many small sellers, a homogeneous product, transparent prices, and relatively free entry. Branded goods have differentiation (A), utilities are near-monopolies (B), and OS platforms have huge network-effect barriers (D).

**D8.** MR = MC identifies the output that:
A. Maximises revenue B. Maximises output C. Maximises profit D. Minimises average cost

**Answer: C.** MR = MC is the profit-maximising condition (on rising MC). It is not the maximum output — producing beyond it raises MC above MR and reduces profit — and it does not by itself minimise average cost.

**D9.** In a constant-cost industry, the long-run supply curve is:
A. Upward-sloping B. Horizontal at minimum ATC C. Downward-sloping D. Vertical

**Answer: B.** With input prices unchanged as the industry expands, entry restores price to minimum ATC at any scale, giving a horizontal long-run supply curve. Upward-sloping (A) fits an increasing-cost industry; downward-sloping (C) a decreasing-cost industry.

**D10.** A firm produces where P = MC and finds P > ATC. This means:
A. It is making a loss and should exit B. It earns supernormal profit that will attract entry C. It should shut down D. It is at long-run equilibrium

**Answer: B.** P > ATC means economic profit is positive (supernormal). Under free entry and perfect information, that profit attracts new firms, raising supply and eventually pushing price down to min ATC. It is therefore a *short-run* position, not long-run equilibrium (D).
