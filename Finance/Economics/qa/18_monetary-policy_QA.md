# Q&A — Monetary Policy

A practice bank for the Economics for Finance chapter "Monetary Policy." Work each question before reading the answer. Sections: A (concept-check), B (applied/numerical), C (interview-style), D (MCQs with reasoning).

---

## Section A — Concept Check

**A1. In one sentence, what problem does monetary policy exist to solve?**

It is the deliberate management of the total quantity and cost of money and credit so that aggregate spending grows fast enough to keep people employed, but not so fast that prices spiral into inflation. A market economy left to itself lurches between credit-fuelled booms and busts, and monetary policy is society's hand on the tiller.

**A2. Why is the central bank able to set the overnight interest rate almost perfectly?**

Because it is the monopoly supplier of reserves (central-bank money) that every commercial bank needs to settle daily payments. Whoever controls the supply of a good sets its price. By adding or draining reserves the central bank pegs the overnight interbank rate wherever it chooses.

**A3. Describe India's liquidity corridor and name the three rates that define it.**

The overnight market rate is trapped in a band. The **SDF (Standing Deposit Facility)** rate is the floor — banks park surplus reserves there, so no bank lends to a peer for less. The **MSF (Marginal Standing Facility)** rate is the ceiling — banks can borrow reserves there, so no bank pays a peer more. The **repo rate** is the policy anchor sitting in the middle, and daily operations keep the market rate hugging it.

**A4. Distinguish the repo rate from the reverse repo / SDF.**

They are opposite sides of the same window. The **repo rate** is what banks *pay to borrow* reserves from the RBI (against government collateral). The **reverse repo / SDF** is what banks *earn to park* surplus reserves at the RBI. Which one is the effective operating rate depends on whether the system is short of liquidity (repo bites) or flush with it (the SDF floor bites).

**A5. What is the single deepest idea of the chapter — the gap the whole subject is built around?**

The central bank has one small, precisely controlled lever (a short-term interest rate) but must use it to reach a distant, sluggish, imperfectly controllable goal (inflation one to two years ahead). The gap between the precise lever and the fuzzy target is bridged by the **transmission mechanism**, and almost every macro-finance debate and market opportunity lives somewhere along that chain.

**A6. List the five channels of monetary transmission.**

1. **Interest-rate channel** — cheaper/costlier loans and yields change investment and durable spending.
2. **Credit / bank-lending channel** — policy changes the *availability* of loans, not just their price.
3. **Asset-price / wealth channel** — lower rates lift stock, bond and property values; richer households spend more.
4. **Exchange-rate channel** — lower domestic rates weaken the currency, boosting net exports (and imported inflation).
5. **Expectations channel** — credible policy anchors inflation psychology, moderating wage- and price-setting.

**A7. What did Milton Friedman mean by "long and variable lags," and what does it force the central bank to do?**

A rate change today affects inflation only 12–24 months later, and the delay is not constant. So the central bank must act on a *forecast* — steering toward where the economy will be, not where it is — like turning a supertanker. This is also why a bank can overtighten into recession or ease too long into a bubble.

**A8. Distinguish CRR from SLR.**

**CRR (Cash Reserve Ratio)** is the fraction of deposits a bank must hold as *cash reserves with the RBI*, earning no interest — raising it locks up funds and drains liquidity. **SLR (Statutory Liquidity Ratio)** is the fraction of deposits a bank must hold in *safe liquid assets it keeps itself*, chiefly government securities, which earn interest. Different assets, different holders, different purposes (SLR is primarily prudential).

**A9. Why did central banks converge on inflation targeting rather than targeting money supply?**

The link between money supply and inflation (the monetarist target) proved unstable once financial innovation made "money" hard to define. An inflation target is simple, transparent, and directly anchors the one variable that most drives actual inflation — *inflation expectations*. If everyone believes the RBI will deliver 4%, they set wages and prices around 4%, and the belief becomes self-fulfilling.

**A10. What does the word "flexible" add in "flexible inflation targeting"?**

It means the RBI targets inflation over the **medium term**, gives real weight to growth, and looks through temporary supply shocks. It does not slam the brakes because onion prices spiked one month. Inflation has primacy, but growth and employment remain genuine considerations.

**A11. Explain "pushing on a string."**

In a slump the central bank can make credit cheap and abundant, but it cannot *force* pessimistic firms to invest or frightened banks to lend. Easing is far more effective at cooling an overheated economy than at heating a demoralised one — monetary policy works better as a brake than as an accelerator.

**A12. Why is monetary policy nearly powerless against a pure supply shock?**

Monetary policy works on the *demand* side. Inflation from an oil embargo, war, or crop failure is a supply-side price rise. The central bank can only offset it by crushing demand hard enough to pull the overall price level back — which is painful and does nothing to cure the underlying shortage.

## Section B — Applied / Numerical (Full Solutions)

**B1. The RBI cuts the repo rate from 6.50% to 6.25%. Express the change in basis points and state its likely direction of effect on (a) G-Sec yields, (b) the rupee, (c) rate-sensitive equities.**

*Solution.* A basis point is one-hundredth of a percentage point. 6.50% − 6.25% = 0.25% = **25 bps** cut. This is expansionary easing.
- (a) G-Sec yields: **fall** (lower policy anchor pulls the curve down).
- (b) Rupee: tends to **weaken** — a lower domestic rate narrows the interest differential, making the currency less attractive to hold.
- (c) Rate-sensitive equities (real estate, autos, long-duration growth/tech): **generally supported**, because a lower discount rate raises the present value of future cash flows and cheaper credit aids demand.

**B2. A firm is weighing a project that returns 7% annually. At a borrowing cost of 9% it is rejected. The central bank eases and the firm's borrowing cost falls to 6%. Show why the decision flips, and name the transmission channel.**

*Solution.* Investment proceeds when the project return exceeds the cost of funds.
- At 9%: return 7% < cost 9% → net −2% → **reject**.
- At 6%: return 7% > cost 6% → net +1% → **accept**.
The rate cut turned a loss-making project into a profitable one, so investment rises and aggregate demand increases. This is the **interest-rate channel** of transmission.

**B3. CPI inflation prints at 6.4% in Q1, 6.7% in Q2, and 6.2% in Q3, all above the tolerance band. What is the band, has it been breached in the accountability sense, and what must the RBI do?**

*Solution.* India's target is **4% CPI with a ±2% tolerance band**, i.e. **2%–6%**. All three quarterly readings (6.4, 6.7, 6.2) exceed 6% — inflation has averaged above the upper bound for **three consecutive quarters**. This triggers the statutory accountability clause: the RBI must **write a public letter to the government** explaining why the target was missed and what remedial action it will take (as it did in 2022).

**B4. A bank holds ₹100 in an overnight surplus. The SDF rate is 6.25%, the repo rate 6.50%, and the MSF rate 6.75%. In a normal interbank market, roughly what rate should it be able to lend that surplus at, and why can it never accept much below 6.25%?**

*Solution.* The overnight market rate is confined to the corridor **SDF (6.25%) ≤ market ≤ MSF (6.75%)**, normally hugging the repo anchor of ~6.50%. The bank will not lend a peer below **6.25%**, because it can always park the ₹100 risk-free at the RBI's SDF and earn 6.25% with no counterparty risk. The SDF is therefore the effective floor; the MSF (6.75%) is the ceiling, because no bank pays a peer more than it can borrow from the RBI itself.

**B5. During COVID the RBI cut the repo rate from 5.15% to 4.00%. (a) How many basis points was the total cut? (b) Classify the stance. (c) Name two additional liquidity tools used alongside the rate cut.**

*Solution.*
- (a) 5.15% − 4.00% = 1.15% = **115 bps** of cuts.
- (b) **Expansionary / accommodative (dovish)** — cutting rates and injecting liquidity to fight a demand collapse.
- (c) The RBI cut the **CRR** (releasing bank funds) and deployed **LTROs (long-term repo operations)** and large **OMO purchases**; it also used **Operation Twist** to flatten the curve. Any two qualify.

**B6. Between May 2022 and February 2023 the MPC raised the repo rate from 4.00% to 6.50%. (a) Total hike in bps. (b) Which banking-sector equities typically benefit and why? (c) What happens to the 10-year G-Sec yield?**

*Solution.*
- (a) 6.50% − 4.00% = 2.50% = **250 bps**.
- (b) **Banks** tend to outperform: as rates rise their net interest margins can widen (loan rates reprice faster than deposit costs), so they earn more.
- (c) The **10-year G-Sec yield climbs** — a higher policy anchor and tighter liquidity push yields up across the curve, and bond prices fall.

**B7. Under the Taylor-Rule logic, in which direction should the policy rate move if (a) inflation is above target and output is above potential, and (b) inflation is below target and output is below potential? What real-world situation defeats this clean prescription?**

*Solution.*
- (a) Both signals point the same way → **raise** the rate (lean against both the inflation overshoot and the positive output gap).
- (b) Both point the same way → **cut** the rate (support demand and lift inflation back to target).
The clean prescription breaks down under **stagflation** — high inflation *and* weak growth together (e.g. the 1970s oil shocks). Inflation says hike, the output gap says cut; the two arms of the rule conflict and the central bank must "pick its poison."

**B8. A bank's retail loans are linked to the repo rate under the 2019 EBLR rule. The RBI cuts the repo by 50 bps. Contrast the pass-through with the pre-2019 regime.**

*Solution.* Under **EBLR (External Benchmark Lending Rate)**, retail and small-business loan rates are contractually tied to the repo rate, so a 50 bps repo cut flows through to those borrowers' rates **quickly and near-fully** (at the next reset). Pre-2019, lending rates were set off internal benchmarks funded largely by fixed-rate deposits, so pass-through was **slow and incomplete** — banks delayed and diluted cuts. EBLR was the RBI's fix for India's historically leaky transmission.

## Section C — Interview-Style (Model Answers)

**C1. "Walk me through exactly how a 25 bps repo cut ends up moving the stock market."**

*Model answer.* Start at the plumbing: the RBI lowers the rate at which banks borrow overnight reserves. Because every other rate is priced relative to the risk-free overnight rate, short-term market rates and bond yields drift down, and bank lending and deposit rates follow (fast now, thanks to EBLR). That feeds equities through two forces. First, the **discount-rate effect**: a lower risk-free rate raises the present value of future corporate cash flows, so valuations rise — most powerfully for long-duration growth and tech names. Second, the **liquidity and demand effect**: cheaper credit supports consumption and investment, lifting expected earnings, while surplus liquidity often rotates into risk assets. The standard result is supportive equities — "don't fight the Fed" — with the caveat that if the cut is a response to a severe recession crushing earnings, the market can fall anyway.

**C2. "Why does the RBI target inflation over the medium term rather than reacting to every monthly print?"**

*Model answer.* Two reasons. First, **lags**: a rate change affects inflation only 12–24 months out, so reacting to a one-month spike is steering by the rear-view mirror. Second, **the nature of shocks**: a lot of monthly volatility is temporary supply noise (a vegetable-price spike, a fuel move) that monetary policy cannot fix and will have reversed by the time a hike bites. Flexible inflation targeting lets the RBI look through such shocks, keep growth in view, and anchor the medium-term trend. What it must protect at all costs is **inflation expectations** — if those stay anchored at 4%, actual inflation is far easier to control.

**C3. "The Fed hiked from roughly 0% to 5.5% in 2022–23. Trace the consequences for an emerging market like India."**

*Model answer.* Higher US rates widen the interest differential in America's favour, so global capital chases US yields. Through the **exchange-rate channel** this strengthens the dollar and pressures the rupee — it drifted toward ₹83/$. That imports inflation (costlier oil and imports in rupee terms) and can prompt the RBI to tighten in sympathy to defend the currency and its own inflation target. Simultaneously, surging US bond yields dragged global yields up and hit long-duration equities hardest worldwide. So a single foreign central bank's cycle exports tighter financial conditions everywhere — the clearest recent illustration that relative monetary policy, via rate differentials, moves currencies and capital flows more than almost anything else.

**C4. "Draghi said 'whatever it takes' and spent no money that day, yet yields collapsed. How is that possible?"**

*Model answer.* Because **expectations are a monetary tool**. Long-term rates depend on the expected future path of policy plus a risk premium. In 2012 Italian and Spanish yields carried a huge premium for the perceived risk the euro would break up. When a credible central banker committed to do "whatever it takes," he removed that tail risk from investors' minds, so the premium collapsed and yields fell — no purchases required. It is the purest example of **forward guidance**: words, backed by credibility, re-priced an entire continent's sovereign debt. Credibility is a central bank's most valuable asset.

**C5. "When is monetary policy the wrong tool, and what do central banks reach for instead?"**

*Model answer.* It struggles in four situations. (1) At the **zero lower bound**, conventional rate cuts run out of room. (2) In a demoralised slump — "**pushing on a string**" — cheap credit cannot force reluctant firms and banks to act. (3) Against **pure supply shocks** (oil, war, harvest failure), it can only crush demand, not cure the shortage. (4) When **long lags and forecast error** make timing treacherous. At the ZLB especially, central banks reach for **unconventional tools**: **Quantitative Easing** (large-scale bond purchases that lower long yields and push investors out the risk curve), aggressive **forward guidance**, and in some cases **negative rates (NIRP)**. India used variants — LTROs, Operation Twist, and large OMOs — rather than full-scale QE.

**C6. "Tell me the three market linkages every finance professional must know cold about monetary policy."**

*Model answer.* Policy moves three things. **Yields**: hikes push yields up across the curve (short end most directly), cuts push them down, and QE specifically lowers *long* yields; the yield curve itself is a forecast of the future policy path. **Currencies**: higher relative rates attract capital and strengthen the currency via interest differentials — the engine of the carry trade — while cuts weaken it. **Equities**: easing lowers the discount rate and adds liquidity (bullish, especially for long-duration growth stocks), while tightening is a headwind and rotates leadership toward banks and value. The Fed's 2022–23 cycle showed all three at once — yields up, dollar up, growth stocks down.

## Section D — MCQs (With Reasoning)

**D1. India's flexible inflation-targeting mandate specifies a CPI target of:**
A) 2% with a band of ±1%
B) 4% with a band of ±2%
C) 6% with a band of ±2%
D) 5% with a band of ±3%

**Answer: B.** The RBI Act amendment (2016) sets a 4% headline CPI target with a ±2% tolerance band, i.e. 2%–6%. C confuses the upper bound with the target.

**D2. Which rate forms the *floor* of India's liquidity corridor?**
A) MSF rate
B) Repo rate
C) SDF rate
D) Bank rate

**Answer: C.** Banks can always park surplus reserves at the SDF, so no bank lends below it — it is the floor. MSF is the ceiling; the repo rate is the anchor in the middle.

**D3. An Open Market Operation in which the RBI *buys* government bonds is:**
A) Contractionary — it drains reserves
B) Expansionary — it injects reserves
C) Neutral for liquidity
D) A change in the CRR

**Answer: B.** Buying bonds pays sellers with newly created reserves, injecting liquidity — expansionary. Selling bonds (A's description) drains reserves.

**D4. "Long and variable lags" implies that a central bank must:**
A) React to the latest monthly inflation print
B) Set policy on a forecast of future conditions
C) Never change rates more than once a year
D) Target money supply instead of inflation

**Answer: B.** Because effects arrive 12–24 months later, the bank must steer toward where the economy will be — acting on a forecast, not current data.

**D5. Which tool is primarily a *communications* instrument rather than a mechanical one?**
A) CRR
B) OMO
C) Forward guidance
D) MSF

**Answer: C.** Forward guidance shapes the expected future path of rates through words, moving long-term rates today without any actual rate change.

**D6. Under EBLR (2019), monetary transmission in India became:**
A) Slower and less complete
B) Faster and more complete
C) Irrelevant to retail borrowers
D) Dependent only on the SLR

**Answer: B.** Linking retail and small-business loan rates directly to the repo rate forced faster, fuller pass-through of policy changes — fixing India's historically leaky transmission.

**D7. Quantitative Easing works mainly by:**
A) Directly financing government spending
B) Raising the CRR
C) Buying long-term bonds to lower long yields and push investors out the risk curve
D) Cutting the repo rate below zero

**Answer: C.** QE is large-scale secondary-market bond buying that lowers long-term yields, floods the system with reserves, and drives the portfolio-balance effect. It is distinct from directly monetising deficits (A).

**D8. Stagflation is uniquely difficult for monetary policy because:**
A) Both inflation and growth are strong, so no action is needed
B) Inflation is high while growth is weak, so easing and tightening each fix one problem and worsen the other
C) It only occurs at the zero lower bound
D) It removes the central bank's control over reserves

**Answer: B.** High inflation argues for tightening; weak growth argues for easing. The two goals conflict, so the bank must accept pain on one front — "pick its poison."

**D9. A rate cut may *fail* to lift equities when:**
A) Inflation expectations are anchored
B) The cut is a response to a severe recession that is crushing corporate earnings
C) The currency weakens
D) Bond yields fall

**Answer: B.** The *reason* for a cut matters as much as the cut. If it signals a deep recession hitting earnings, "bad news is good news" flips to "bad news is bad news" and stocks can fall despite easier policy.

**D10. Higher domestic policy rates, all else equal, tend to make the currency:**
A) Weaker, because exports fall
B) Stronger, because higher relative yields attract capital
C) Unaffected, because FX is set by trade only
D) Weaker, because imports become cheaper

**Answer: B.** Via the exchange-rate channel and interest-rate differentials, higher relative rates draw foreign capital seeking yield, appreciating the currency — the logic behind the carry trade and the 2022 dollar surge.

**D11. CRR differs from SLR in that CRR is held as:**
A) Government securities kept by the bank, earning interest
B) Cash with the RBI, earning no interest
C) Corporate bonds held offshore
D) Equity in the RBI

**Answer: B.** CRR is non-interest-bearing cash parked at the RBI; SLR (A's description) is interest-earning safe assets, chiefly G-Secs, held by the bank itself.

**D12. The Volcker episode (US, 1979–82) is chiefly cited to show that:**
A) Rate cuts always end recessions instantly
B) Beating entrenched inflation requires credibility and the willingness to accept real economic pain
C) Money supply targeting is superior to inflation targeting
D) Supply shocks can be cured by monetary policy alone

**Answer: B.** Volcker raised the funds rate to ~19–20%, caused a deep recession, but broke inflation and restored the Fed's credibility — proving credibility is a central bank's most valuable asset.
