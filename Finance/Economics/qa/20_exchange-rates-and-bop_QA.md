# Q&A — Exchange Rates and the Balance of Payments

A practice bank built to test whether you *understand* the mechanics, not just memorise definitions. Work each part before reading the answer.

## Section A — Concept Check (short answer)

**A1. What is an exchange rate, and what is the difference between the nominal and the real exchange rate?**

The nominal exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another (e.g. Rs 83 per USD). The real exchange rate adjusts this for relative price levels between the two countries: Real rate = Nominal rate x (Foreign price level / Domestic price level), or in the more common form Real = e x (P*/P). The nominal rate tells you what you pay at the bank; the real rate tells you whether your goods are actually cheaper or dearer than foreign goods after accounting for inflation. Two countries can hold a fixed nominal rate while the real rate drifts sharply if their inflation rates differ.

**A2. Distinguish between a direct quote and an indirect quote.**

A direct quote states the number of units of *domestic* currency per one unit of *foreign* currency (Rs 83/USD, from the Indian viewpoint). An indirect quote states units of *foreign* currency per one unit of domestic currency (USD 0.012/INR). One is simply the reciprocal of the other. India conventionally uses direct quotes.

**A3. Under a floating exchange rate, what does "depreciation" mean, and how does it differ from "devaluation"?**

Depreciation is a market-driven fall in a currency's value under a floating regime — supply and demand move the rate. Devaluation is a deliberate, official lowering of the currency's pegged value by the central bank/government under a fixed regime. The economic effect on trade is similar (exports cheaper, imports dearer); the difference is the *cause* — market forces versus policy decision.

**A4. State the two main accounts of the Balance of Payments and what each records.**

The Current Account records trade in goods and services, primary income (investment income, compensation of employees) and secondary income (unilateral transfers such as remittances and grants). The Capital and Financial Account records cross-border transactions in assets and liabilities — FDI, portfolio investment, loans, and changes in official reserves. Broadly, the current account tracks flows of goods/income; the capital and financial account tracks flows of ownership/claims.

**A5. Why must the Balance of Payments always "balance" in accounting terms, yet be described as being in "surplus" or "deficit"?**

The BoP balances by construction because it uses double-entry bookkeeping — every transaction has an offsetting entry, so the overall account sums to zero (with errors and omissions as the balancing residual). When people say "BoP surplus/deficit" they mean a surplus or deficit on a *sub-account* — typically the current account, or the balance excluding accommodating reserve transactions. The imbalance in the autonomous transactions is financed by accommodating transactions (reserve movements).

**A6. Distinguish autonomous from accommodating transactions.**

Autonomous transactions are undertaken for their own sake — for profit, trade or return — independent of the BoP position (exports, imports, FDI). Accommodating transactions are undertaken *to finance* the imbalance created by autonomous flows — chiefly the central bank running down or building up foreign reserves. A BoP "deficit" is really an excess of autonomous outflows requiring accommodating financing.

**A7. What is the Marshall-Lerner condition?**

It states that a currency depreciation will improve the trade balance only if the sum of the absolute values of the price elasticities of demand for exports and imports exceeds one (|Ex| + |Em| > 1). If demand is too inelastic, the higher price of imports and cheaper exports do not shift enough quantity to improve the balance — the value effect can even worsen it initially.

**A8. Explain the J-curve effect in one line each for cause and shape.**

Cause: immediately after a depreciation, quantities are sticky (contracts and habits fix trade volumes), so the higher import bill worsens the trade balance first; over time volumes adjust and the balance improves. Shape: the trade balance dips below its starting point, then rises above it, tracing a "J".

**A9. Name and describe the two purchasing power parity (PPP) concepts.**

Absolute PPP: the exchange rate equals the ratio of the two countries' price levels (law of one price applied to a basket) — e = P/P*. Relative PPP: the *rate of change* of the exchange rate equals the *difference* in inflation rates between the two countries. Relative PPP is empirically more defensible because it does not require identical baskets or the absence of transport costs and tariffs.

## Section B — Applied / Numerical (full solutions)

**B1. Cross rate.** If USD/INR = 83.00 and USD/EUR = 0.92, find the INR/EUR rate (rupees per euro).

Solution: 1 USD = Rs 83.00 and 1 USD = EUR 0.92, so EUR 0.92 = Rs 83.00. Therefore 1 EUR = 83.00 / 0.92 = **Rs 90.22 per euro**. (Check: a euro is worth more than a dollar, and 90.22 > 83, consistent.)

**B2. Real exchange rate.** Nominal rate e = Rs 80/USD. US price index (P*) = 110, Indian price index (P) = 132. Compute the real exchange rate and interpret.

Solution: Real rate = e x (P*/P) = 80 x (110/132) = 80 x 0.8333 = **66.67**. Because India's price level rose faster, the real rate (66.67) is below the nominal rate (80): Indian goods have become relatively more expensive, eroding competitiveness despite the nominal number. A real appreciation has occurred even though the nominal rate is unchanged from a benchmark of 80.

**B3. Percentage depreciation.** The rupee moves from Rs 75/USD to Rs 83/USD. By what percentage has the rupee depreciated, and by what percentage has the dollar appreciated (from the rupee's viewpoint)? Explain why the two numbers differ.

Solution:
Rupee depreciation is measured in the currency it takes to buy a dollar. The dollar's rupee price rose from 75 to 83: change = (83 - 75)/75 = 8/75 = **10.67% dollar appreciation**.
The rupee's value in dollars fell from 1/75 = 0.013333 to 1/83 = 0.012048. Rupee depreciation = (0.012048 - 0.013333)/0.013333 = -0.09639 = **-9.64%**.
They differ because percentage change is not symmetric under reciprocals: a +10.67% move in one direction corresponds to a -9.64% move in the inverse. The base of the fraction differs.

**B4. Interest rate parity (covered).** Spot USD/INR = 83.00. Indian one-year rate = 7%, US one-year rate = 4%. Using covered interest rate parity, find the one-year forward rate. Is the rupee at a forward premium or discount?

Solution: CIP forward = Spot x (1 + i_domestic)/(1 + i_foreign) = 83.00 x (1.07/1.04) = 83.00 x 1.028846 = **Rs 85.39/USD**. The dollar buys more rupees forward than spot, so the rupee is at a **forward discount** (the higher-interest currency trades at a forward discount — this is exactly what prevents arbitrage). Approximate check: forward premium on dollar ~ i_INR - i_USD = 3%, and 83 x 1.03 = 85.49, close to the exact figure.

**B5. Relative PPP forecast.** Expected Indian inflation = 6%, expected US inflation = 2%, current spot Rs 83/USD. What does relative PPP predict for the rupee over one year?

Solution: Expected depreciation of the rupee ~ inflation differential = 6% - 2% = 4%. Predicted rate = 83 x (1.06/1.02) = 83 x 1.039216 = **Rs 86.25/USD** (or approximately 83 x 1.04 = 86.32 by the linear rule). The higher-inflation currency is expected to depreciate to keep relative purchasing power constant.

**B6. Construct a BoP statement (identify the balances).** Given (USD bn): Exports of goods 320; Imports of goods 480; Services net +90; Primary income net -40; Secondary income (remittances) net +100; FDI net +50; Portfolio net +30; Loans/other net +20. Find the trade balance, current account balance, and the change in reserves needed to balance (ignore errors and omissions).

Solution:
- Merchandise trade balance = 320 - 480 = **-160** (deficit).
- Current account = -160 + 90 - 40 + 100 = **-10** (a small current account deficit).
- Capital & financial account (excluding reserves) = 50 + 30 + 20 = **+100**.
- Overall autonomous balance = -10 + 100 = **+90 surplus**.
- To balance, reserves must *rise* by 90, recorded as a -90 entry (accumulation of reserve assets is a debit/outflow in the BoP). So **reserves increase by USD 90 bn**. Note how remittances (+100) nearly rescued a large merchandise deficit — typical of India's structure.

**B7. Marshall-Lerner check.** Export price elasticity = 0.7, import price elasticity = 0.5. Will a depreciation improve the trade balance? What if import elasticity were 0.6?

Solution: Sum = 0.7 + 0.5 = 1.2 > 1, so the Marshall-Lerner condition is **satisfied** — depreciation improves the trade balance (after any J-curve lag). If import elasticity were 0.6, sum = 0.7 + 0.6 = 1.3 > 1 — still satisfied, and by a wider margin, so the improvement would be larger/faster. Only if the sum fell to 1 or below would depreciation fail to help.

**B8. Triangular arbitrage.** Quotes: USD/INR = 83.00, GBP/USD = 1.25, GBP/INR = 105.00. Is there an arbitrage opportunity? Show the profit on GBP 1,000,000.

Solution: The no-arbitrage GBP/INR implied by the first two = 1.25 x 83.00 = 103.75. The market quotes 105.00 — pounds are *overpriced* in rupees. Arbitrage: start with GBP, sell them for rupees at 105.00: GBP 1,000,000 -> Rs 105,000,000. Convert rupees to dollars at 83.00: 105,000,000 / 83.00 = USD 1,265,060. Convert dollars back to pounds at 1.25: 1,265,060 / 1.25 = GBP 1,012,048. Profit = **GBP 12,048** (about 1.2%), before transaction costs. The trades would push the rates back toward 103.75.

## Section C — Interview-Style (model answers)

**C1. "India runs a persistent current account deficit. Is that a problem?"**

Not necessarily. A current account deficit means the country is absorbing more than it produces and importing capital to fund the gap — the mirror image of a capital account surplus. For a fast-growing economy that is a rational stage: you import machinery and technology now, build capacity, and export later. The deficit is sustainable if it is (a) moderate as a share of GDP (roughly under 2.5-3%), (b) financed by *stable* flows like FDI rather than volatile "hot" portfolio money, and (c) matched by productive investment rather than consumption. It becomes dangerous when it is large, financed by short-term debt, and coincides with falling reserves — the 1991 and 2013 episodes are the cautionary cases. So the honest answer is: the size, the financing quality, and the use of the funds matter more than the sign.

**C2. "Walk me through how a rupee depreciation transmits through the economy."**

Start with trade: imports become dearer in rupee terms and exports cheaper for foreign buyers, so, subject to the Marshall-Lerner condition and after a J-curve lag, net exports improve. Second, inflation: India imports crude oil, so a weaker rupee raises the domestic fuel bill and feeds imported inflation, which can force the RBI to keep rates higher. Third, external debt: firms and the government with dollar-denominated liabilities see their rupee repayment burden rise. Fourth, capital flows: depreciation can trigger further outflows if investors expect more weakness (self-reinforcing), or attract them if they think the currency is now cheap. Fifth, remittances and IT exporters gain in rupee terms. The net effect depends on India's structure — a large oil-importing, remittance-receiving, services-exporting economy — so the competitiveness gain is real but partly offset by imported inflation.

**C3. "Fixed versus floating exchange rates — which is better?"**

Neither dominates; it is a trade-off framed by the "impossible trinity": a country cannot simultaneously have a fixed exchange rate, free capital movement, and an independent monetary policy — it must give up one. A fixed rate delivers stability and low transaction risk for traders and anchors inflation expectations, but it surrenders monetary autonomy and demands large reserves to defend the peg; if the peg is misaligned, speculators attack it. A floating rate lets the currency absorb external shocks automatically and frees monetary policy, but brings volatility and uncertainty for business. Most large economies, including India, run a *managed float* — market-determined most of the time, with central bank intervention to smooth excessive volatility rather than to hit a target. That captures much of the flexibility while limiting disorderly swings.

**C4. "If PPP holds in the long run, why do exchange rates deviate from it for years?"**

PPP is a long-run anchor, not a short-run predictor. Short-run rates are driven by capital flows, interest differentials, and expectations, which dominate the slow-moving goods-price arbitrage that PPP relies on. Deviations persist because many goods and services are non-tradable (haircuts, housing), transport costs and tariffs break the law of one price, baskets differ across countries, and productivity differences (the Balassa-Samuelson effect) mean richer, high-productivity countries sustain genuinely higher price levels. So PPP tells you the direction real rates should drift over a decade, but interest rate parity and market sentiment tell you where the rate is this quarter.

## Section D — MCQs (with reasoning)

**D1.** Under a direct quote in India, a rise in the numerical exchange rate (Rs 80 -> Rs 84 per USD) means the rupee has:
(a) appreciated (b) depreciated (c) stayed constant (d) been revalued

Answer: **(b) depreciated.** In a direct quote more rupees are now needed per dollar, so each rupee buys fewer dollars — a depreciation. "Revalued" (d) would apply only under a fixed regime and in the appreciation direction.

**D2.** The Marshall-Lerner condition is satisfied when:
(a) |Ex| + |Em| < 1 (b) |Ex| + |Em| = 0 (c) |Ex| + |Em| > 1 (d) Ex = Em

Answer: **(c).** Only when the summed elasticities exceed one does the quantity response outweigh the adverse value effect, so depreciation improves the trade balance.

**D3.** Workers' remittances sent home by Indians abroad are recorded in the BoP under:
(a) merchandise trade (b) financial account, FDI (c) secondary income in the current account (d) official reserves

Answer: **(c).** Remittances are unilateral transfers with no quid pro quo, classified as secondary income within the current account — not capital flows.

**D4.** The J-curve effect describes:
(a) a permanent worsening of the trade balance after depreciation
(b) an initial worsening then improvement of the trade balance after depreciation
(c) the shape of the demand curve for foreign exchange
(d) the relationship between interest rates and bond prices

Answer: **(b).** Sticky quantities cause a short-term deterioration before volume adjustments turn the balance positive, tracing a J.

**D5.** According to covered interest rate parity, the currency of the country with the *higher* interest rate will trade:
(a) at a forward premium (b) at a forward discount (c) at the same forward and spot rate (d) cannot be determined

Answer: **(b) at a forward discount.** The forward discount must exactly offset the interest advantage; otherwise riskless arbitrage would exist. In B4 the higher-rate rupee traded at a forward discount to the dollar.

**D6.** The "impossible trinity" says a country cannot simultaneously have:
(a) low inflation, low unemployment, and growth
(b) a fixed exchange rate, free capital mobility, and independent monetary policy
(c) exports, imports, and reserves
(d) FDI, FII, and remittances

Answer: **(b).** The Mundell-Fleming trilemma forces a choice of two out of three; India broadly keeps monetary independence and managed capital mobility while accepting a flexible (managed-float) rate.

**D7.** In the BoP, a central bank *buying* foreign currency to build up reserves is:
(a) an autonomous credit (b) an accommodating transaction recorded as a debit (c) a current account item (d) errors and omissions

Answer: **(b).** Reserve accumulation is an accommodating (below-the-line) transaction; acquiring foreign assets is recorded as a debit/outflow that offsets an autonomous surplus.

**D8.** If Indian inflation is 6% and US inflation is 2%, relative PPP predicts the rupee will, over the year:
(a) appreciate ~4% (b) depreciate ~4% (c) stay unchanged (d) depreciate ~8%

Answer: **(b) depreciate about 4%.** The higher-inflation currency depreciates by roughly the inflation differential (6% - 2%) to preserve relative purchasing power — consistent with B5.
