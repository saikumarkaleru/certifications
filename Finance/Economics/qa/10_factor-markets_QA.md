# Q&A — Factor Markets and Income Distribution

A companion practice bank for the concept guide *Factor Markets and Income Distribution*. Every question is followed by a full answer: Section A locks the definitions, Section B builds numerical fluency, Section C rehearses finance-interview framing, and Section D pressure-tests your reasoning against distractors.

---

## Section A — Concept-Check Questions

**A1. What is a factor market, and how do the roles of firms and households differ from the product market?**

A factor market is where the *services* of the factors of production — labour, capital, and land — are bought and sold. The roles flip relative to the product market. In the product market, firms are sellers and households are buyers. In the factor market, **households (factor owners) are the sellers** of labour, savings, and land, and **firms are the buyers**. The prices paid — wages, interest, and rent — flow back to households as income. This closes the circular flow: households sell factors to earn income and spend that income buying goods.

**A2. Define "derived demand" and explain why it is the single most important idea in this chapter.**

Derived demand means the demand for a factor is *derived from* the demand for the goods it helps produce. No one wants a welder's labour for its own sake — they want the cars and ships welding creates. It matters because it makes factor prices ultimately a *consequence* of product markets: when demand for the final good rises or falls, demand for every factor used to make it moves with it. It is the causal engine behind wage cycles, land values, and the whole marginal-productivity apparatus.

**A3. Distinguish MPP, MRP, and VMP.**

- **Marginal Physical Product (MPP)** — the extra *physical* output from one more unit of a factor (e.g. one more worker adds 20 units). Measured in units.
- **Marginal Revenue Product (MRP)** — the extra *revenue* from that extra output: MRP = MPP × Marginal Revenue. Measured in money. This is the firm's factor demand curve.
- **Value of Marginal Product (VMP)** — the special case of MRP under perfect competition in the product market, where price is fixed: VMP = MPP × Price.

The key jump is MPP (units) → MRP (rupees). Factor demand tracks MRP, not physical output alone.

**A4. State the profit-maximising hiring rule and give its intuition.**

Hire more of a factor while its **MRP exceeds its price**; stop when **MRP = factor price**. Intuition: if the next worker's MRP is ₹700 and the wage is ₹500, hiring them adds ₹200 to profit — do it. Keep going until the marginal worker's MRP falls (via diminishing returns) to ₹500. Beyond that, the worker costs more than they bring in. It is the exact analogue of "invest while the return exceeds the hurdle rate."

**A5. Why does the MRP (factor demand) curve slope downward?**

Two forces. First, the **law of diminishing marginal returns**: as you add more of a variable factor to fixed factors, MPP falls — the tenth worker in a small kitchen adds less than the third. Second, under imperfect competition in the product market, selling extra output requires cutting price, so marginal revenue also falls. Both pull MRP = MPP × MR downward as more of the factor is hired.

**A6. Explain the backward-bending supply curve of labour.**

At low wages, a wage rise makes people work more: leisure now costs more in foregone earnings, so they "buy" less of it — the **substitution effect** dominates. Past some income threshold, workers are rich enough that a further wage rise makes them choose *more* leisure — the **income effect** dominates — and the individual supply curve bends backward. Note this is the *individual* curve; the *market* labour supply curve usually stays upward-sloping because higher wages pull new entrants into the workforce.

**A7. What is a monopsony, and why does it depress wages?**

A monopsony is a single dominant *buyer* of a factor — a mining town's only employer, say. Because it faces the entire upward-sloping supply curve, hiring one more worker forces it to raise the wage for *everyone*, so its marginal cost of labour exceeds the wage. To maximise profit it restricts hiring and sets the wage **below the competitive level and below MRP**. This is the textbook case where a well-set minimum wage can raise *both* wages and employment.

**A8. Define economic rent and state Ricardo's insight about causation.**

Economic rent is the payment to a factor **in excess of its transfer earnings** — the surplus over the minimum needed to keep it in its current use (its opportunity cost). Because land's supply is fixed and vertical, its price is entirely demand-determined, so rent is *price-determined, not price-determining*. Ricardo: **"Corn is not high because rent is paid; rent is paid because corn is high."** Rent is a consequence of derived demand, not a cost that pushes up output prices.

**A9. Give the two lenses on interest-rate determination.**

(a) The **real / productivity-and-thrift** view: demand for capital comes from its MRP (rate of return); supply comes from saving, which requires compensation for deferring consumption (time preference). Equilibrium is where marginal productivity of capital meets marginal willingness to save. (b) The **loanable funds** view: the interest rate equates the supply of loanable funds (saving, retained earnings, foreign inflows) with demand (investment, government borrowing, consumer credit). Government deficits can *crowd out* private investment by shifting demand right and raising rates.

**A10. State the product-exhaustion (adding-up) theorem and why it closes the model.**

If every factor is paid its marginal product, does that exactly use up total output? Under **constant returns to scale** and competitive markets, **Euler's theorem** guarantees yes: the sum of (each factor × its marginal product) equals total output — no surplus, no shortfall. This is the product-exhaustion theorem, the theoretical proof that competitive marginal-product payments distribute exactly 100% of output.

**A11. Distinguish functional and personal income distribution.**

**Functional distribution** splits national income by *factor type* — wages, interest, rent, profit. **Personal distribution** splits it by *household* — rich versus poor (measured by the Gini coefficient, Lorenz curve). They are related but not identical: a single person can earn from several factors (a salary plus dividends plus rent), so the functional split does not map one-to-one onto who ends up rich.

## Section B — Applied / Numerical Problems (with full solutions)

**B1. Building an MRP schedule.** A firm sells output at a fixed ₹50/unit. The MPP of the 1st through 5th worker is 12, 10, 8, 6, 4 units. Compute VMP for each worker. If the wage is ₹300/day, how many workers are hired?

**Solution.** VMP = MPP × Price (₹50):

| Worker | MPP | VMP (₹) |
|--------|-----|---------|
| 1 | 12 | 600 |
| 2 | 10 | 500 |
| 3 | 8 | 400 |
| 4 | 6 | 300 |
| 5 | 4 | 200 |

Hire while VMP ≥ wage (₹300). The 4th worker's VMP is exactly 300 (hire — non-negative contribution); the 5th's is 200 < 300 (don't). **Hire 4 workers.**

**B2. Effect of a product-price change.** Using B1's MPP figures, the product price rises to ₹75. Recompute the hiring decision at the same ₹300 wage.

**Solution.** New VMP = MPP × 75: 900, 750, 600, 450, 300. Now even the 5th worker's VMP (300) equals the wage, so **hire 5 workers**. This is derived demand in action: nothing about the workers changed — a higher *product* price raised their MRP and pulled up factor demand.

**B3. The hiring rule under imperfect competition.** A worker's MPP is 10 units. The firm must cut price to sell more, so marginal revenue is ₹40 (below the ₹50 average price). What is this worker's MRP, and would you hire at a ₹450 wage?

**Solution.** MRP = MPP × MR = 10 × 40 = **₹400**. Note MRP (₹400) is below VMP (10 × 50 = ₹500) because MR < price under imperfect competition. At a ₹450 wage, MRP (₹400) < wage — **do not hire** this worker. Using VMP here would have wrongly said hire; the correct demand curve is MRP.

**B4. Economic rent versus transfer earnings.** A footballer earns ₹5 crore/year. Her best alternative career (as a coach) would pay ₹40 lakh. Split her pay into transfer earnings and economic rent.

**Solution.** Transfer earnings = the minimum to keep her in football = her opportunity cost = **₹40 lakh**. Economic rent = the surplus above that = 5 crore − 40 lakh = **₹4.6 crore**. Because her talent is in near-fixed supply, almost all her pay is pure economic rent — she would still play for far less.

**B5. Loanable funds and crowding out.** Private saving supply and investment demand clear at an interest rate of 6%. The government runs a large deficit, adding to loanable-funds demand. Qualitatively, what happens to the rate and to private investment?

**Solution.** Extra government borrowing shifts the demand for loanable funds **right**. Along an upward-sloping supply curve, the equilibrium interest rate **rises above 6%**. At the higher rate, some private projects no longer clear the hurdle, so **private investment falls** — this is crowding out. The rate rise rations funds between the new public demand and existing private demand.

**B6. Fisher decomposition.** A bond yields a nominal 9%. Expected inflation is 5%. Estimate the real rate. If inflation expectations fall to 3% with the real rate unchanged, what nominal yield would you expect?

**Solution.** Fisher: nominal ≈ real + expected inflation. Real ≈ 9% − 5% = **4%**. If expected inflation drops to 3% and the real rate holds at 4%, nominal ≈ 4% + 3% = **7%**. This is why falling inflation expectations pull nominal bond yields down even without any change in the underlying real return demanded.

**B7. Land supply and demand-determined rent.** The supply of prime CBD land is fixed at 100 units (perfectly inelastic). Demand gives rent = 500 − 3Q (₹lakh per unit). Find equilibrium rent. If a business boom shifts demand to rent = 800 − 3Q, find the new rent. Comment.

**Solution.** With fixed supply Q = 100, rent = whatever demand gives at Q = 100.
- Original: rent = 500 − 3(100) = **₹200 lakh**.
- After boom: rent = 800 − 3(100) = **₹500 lakh**.
Quantity never changes (supply is vertical); rent is set entirely by demand. The boom raised the value of activity on the land, and rent absorbed all of it — textbook price-determined, demand-driven economic rent.

**B8. Interest rate and asset duration.** Two assets each pay ₹100. Asset A pays in 1 year; Asset B pays in 10 years. The discount rate rises from 5% to 8%. Compute the percentage fall in each value and state the lesson.

**Solution.** PV = 100 / (1+r)^n.
- Asset A: 100/1.05 = 95.24 → 100/1.08 = 92.59. Fall ≈ **2.8%**.
- Asset B: 100/1.05^10 = 61.39 → 100/1.08^10 = 46.32. Fall ≈ **24.6%**.
The long-duration asset falls far more. Because the interest rate is the price of capital / discount rate, a rate rise hits assets whose value sits far in the future hardest — exactly why long-dated bonds and long-duration growth stocks fell most in 2022–23.

## Section C — Interview-Style Questions (with model answers)

**C1. "Explain why a software engineer's salary can double and then stall without her skills changing at all."**

Model answer: "That's derived demand. A factor's demand comes from demand for the *products* it builds, not from the factor itself. When demand for AI products exploded, the marginal revenue product of the engineers who build them jumped, and wages followed. When the sector cooled and product demand fell in 2022–23, the derived demand for those same engineers contracted and wage growth stalled — even though nothing about the engineers changed. Factor prices track the value of the marginal output, and that value is set downstream in the product market."

**C2. "How is the macroeconomic interest rate the same thing as the discount rate on my DCF?"**

Model answer: "They're two faces of one equilibrium. In factor-market theory the interest rate is the price of capital, set where the marginal productivity of capital meets the willingness to save. That equilibrium rate *is* the economy's cost of capital — the risk-free anchor beneath every discount rate. Firms invest while the marginal return on a project exceeds that rate, which is identical to saying 'take positive-NPV projects at the market discount rate.' So when a central bank raises the policy rate, it raises the whole economy's hurdle rate: fewer real projects clear, and every future cash flow is discounted harder — which is precisely what a DCF does. The economics and the spreadsheet are the same equation."

**C3. "Walk me through why prime real estate rents are so high — and be careful about causation."**

Model answer: "The trap is to say rent is high because the land is expensive, or that high rent causes high prices. It's the reverse. Land supply is fixed and perfectly inelastic — you can't manufacture more of a CBD — so rent is entirely demand-determined. Rent is high because the activity clustered on that land is enormously valuable; the derived demand for the location bids the rent up. Ricardo put it as 'rent is paid because corn is high, not the other way round.' Rent is price-determined, not price-determining. For a REIT investor that means you're valuing a stream of pure economic rent, and because you discount it at the cost of capital, cap rates move inversely with bond yields."

**C4. "Does marginal productivity theory prove people are paid what they deserve?"**

Model answer: "No — and conflating the two is the classic error. Marginal productivity theory is a *positive* theory of what competitive markets *do* pay: in equilibrium each factor earns the value of its marginal product. It is not a *normative* claim that this outcome is fair. It rests on strong assumptions — competitive markets, full information, no market power. The moment you add monopsony, discrimination, unions, or bargaining asymmetries, actual pay diverges from marginal product. So 'paid your marginal product' describes a competitive benchmark, not moral desert. That distinction matters enormously in the minimum-wage and inequality debates."

**C5. "What does 'economic rent' mean to a finance professional, versus a Ricardian land economist?"**

Model answer: "Same core idea, different setting. Classically, economic rent is the payment to a factor above its transfer earnings — the surplus over opportunity cost, most vividly the rent on fixed-supply land. In finance we use 'economic rent' for the excess returns a firm earns from a durable competitive moat — the wedge that keeps ROIC above WACC. Both are surpluses that exist because something is in scarce or protected supply: fixed land in one case, a defensible advantage in the other. When I underwrite a business, sustainable economic rent is exactly what I'm trying to identify and value — and quasi-rent is its short-run cousin, the return to a factor that's fixed today but replicable in the long run."

**C6. "Why should an equity investor care about labour's falling share of national income?"**

Model answer: "Because the labour-versus-capital split is, mechanically, the flip side of corporate margins. Since around 1980, labour's share of national income has fallen in many advanced economies while capital's share rose — driven by automation, globalisation, weaker union bargaining power, and superstar firms with market power. A rising capital share means fatter profit margins and stronger equity returns, so it's been a multi-decade tailwind for stocks. But it cuts both ways: the same shift fuels inequality, political backlash, and redistribution pressure, which markets increasingly price as tax and policy risk. So the functional distribution of income is simultaneously a margin story and a risk-premium story."

**C7. "When can raising the minimum wage increase employment? Most people assume it always cuts jobs."**

Model answer: "In a monopsony. If a single dominant employer is the only real buyer of labour, it faces the whole upward-sloping supply curve, so hiring one more worker raises the wage bill for everyone — its marginal cost of labour exceeds the wage it pays. To maximise profit it under-hires and holds the wage below MRP. A minimum wage set between the monopsony wage and the competitive wage removes the employer's incentive to suppress the wage — it can no longer gain by hiring fewer people — so both the wage *and* employment can rise. In a competitive labour market the textbook result flips and a binding minimum wage reduces employment, so the answer genuinely depends on market structure. That nuance is what interviewers are testing."

## Section D — Multiple-Choice Questions (with reasoning)

**D1.** In the factor market, the buyers and sellers are:
A. Firms sell, households buy B. Firms buy, households sell C. Both are firms D. Both are households

**Answer: B.** The roles flip from the product market. Households own and sell factor services (labour, capital, land); firms buy them and pay wages, interest, and rent. A describes the product market.

**D2.** The demand for factors of production is best described as:
A. Autonomous demand B. Derived demand C. Perfectly inelastic D. Independent of product markets

**Answer: B.** Factor demand is derived from demand for the goods the factor produces. It is neither autonomous (A) nor independent of product markets (D); only land *supply* (not factor demand generally) is perfectly inelastic (C).

**D3.** A firm hiring a variable factor maximises profit where:
A. MPP = 0 B. MRP = factor price C. Price = AVC D. MPP is maximised

**Answer: B.** Hire until the marginal revenue product equals the factor's price. MPP = 0 (A) is technical-maximum output, not profit-max; C is the product-market shutdown rule.

**D4.** The MRP curve slopes downward mainly because of:
A. Rising product prices B. Diminishing marginal returns (and falling MR under imperfect competition) C. Fixed factor supply D. Government regulation

**Answer: B.** As more of a variable factor is added, MPP falls (diminishing returns), and under imperfect competition MR falls too — both drag MRP down.

**D5.** The individual labour supply curve bends backward because, at high wages:
A. The substitution effect dominates B. The income effect dominates, so workers choose more leisure C. Firms demand less labour D. Taxes rise

**Answer: B.** Past a threshold, the income effect outweighs the substitution effect: workers are rich enough to "buy" more leisure, so hours supplied fall as wages rise.

**D6.** Economic rent is best defined as:
A. Any payment for land B. Payment to a factor above its transfer earnings C. Total revenue minus total cost D. The interest on capital

**Answer: B.** Rent is the surplus over the minimum needed to keep a factor in its current use (its opportunity cost). It generalises well beyond land (A) and is distinct from profit (C) and interest (D).

**D7.** "Rent is high because the product is valuable, not the reverse." This captures that rent is:
A. Price-determining B. Price-determined C. A cost that raises output prices D. Perfectly elastic

**Answer: B.** With fixed land supply, rent is set by demand for what the land produces — a consequence of value, not a cause of it. Ricardo's corn insight; the opposite (A/C) is the causation trap.

**D8.** In the loanable funds market, a rise in government borrowing tends to:
A. Lower interest rates and raise private investment B. Raise interest rates and crowd out private investment C. Leave rates unchanged D. Increase the supply of loanable funds

**Answer: B.** Extra borrowing shifts loanable-funds *demand* right, raising the equilibrium rate and squeezing out private investment that no longer clears the higher hurdle.

**D9.** Under constant returns to scale and competition, paying every factor its marginal product:
A. Leaves a surplus for the entrepreneur B. Falls short of total output C. Exactly exhausts total output (Euler's theorem) D. Is mathematically impossible

**Answer: C.** The product-exhaustion (adding-up) theorem: marginal-product payments sum to exactly 100% of output — no surplus, no shortfall.

**D10.** A minimum wage can raise *both* wages and employment when the labour market is:
A. Perfectly competitive B. A monopsony C. In long-run equilibrium D. Subject to a backward-bending supply curve

**Answer: B.** A monopsonist under-hires and holds wages below MRP; a well-set minimum wage removes that incentive, allowing both wage and employment to rise. In a competitive market (A) a binding minimum wage reduces employment.

**D11.** The MRP of ₹400 versus a VMP of ₹500 for the same worker indicates the firm operates in:
A. A perfectly competitive product market B. An imperfectly competitive product market (MR < price) C. A monopsonistic labour market D. A market with fixed land

**Answer: B.** VMP = MPP × price and MRP = MPP × MR. VMP > MRP means MR < price, which happens only when the firm must cut price to sell more — imperfect competition in the *product* market.

**D12.** Functional distribution of income refers to the split of national income by:
A. Household (rich vs poor) B. Factor type (wages, interest, rent, profit) C. Region D. Industry

**Answer: B.** Functional = by factor type. The split by household (rich vs poor) is the *personal* distribution (A), measured by the Gini/Lorenz apparatus.
