## Section A — Concept-Check Questions

**A1. Define scarcity, and explain why it — not money — is the true starting point of economics.**

Scarcity is the condition in which human wants are effectively unlimited while the resources to satisfy them (money, time, labour, materials, capital) are finite. It is the starting point because without scarcity there would be nothing to economize: no need to choose, no prices, no interest rates, no risk premia. Money is merely a claim on scarce resources; the scarcity of the underlying resources is what gives money value. If resources were infinite, economics and finance would not exist as disciplines.

**A2. Distinguish scarcity from rarity with an example.**

Rarity is about physical quantity; scarcity is about competing uses at a given price. Clean drinking water is abundant on Earth yet economically scarce wherever using it for one purpose (irrigation) prevents another (drinking). A thing is economically scarce whenever devoting it to one use denies it to another — regardless of how physically common it is.

**A3. State the three central problems every economy must solve, and how a market economy solves them.**

The three central problems are **what** to produce, **how** to produce it (labour- vs capital-intensive), and **for whom** to produce it (distribution). A market economy solves all three through prices and profit: high prices signal what to produce, relative input costs decide how, and purchasing power decides for whom. A command economy solves them by central planning; India, a mixed economy, blends markets, regulation, and state provision.

**A4. Define opportunity cost and explain why it need not involve any cash outflow.**

Opportunity cost is the value of the next-best alternative foregone when a choice is made. It need not involve cash because the alternative given up may be a non-cash benefit — foregone interest on idle cash, foregone rent on an owner-occupied building, foregone salary while studying. The cost is what you gave up, not what you paid.

**A5. Differentiate accounting profit from economic profit.**

Accounting profit = revenue − explicit (money) costs. Economic profit = revenue − explicit costs − implicit (opportunity) costs. A firm can be accounting-profitable yet economically loss-making if the capital employed could earn more in its next-best use. Economic profit is the truer test of whether value is being created.

**A6. State the marginal decision rule and explain why totals are the wrong basis for decisions.**

The rule: continue an activity while marginal benefit (MB) exceeds marginal cost (MC), and stop where MB = MC. Totals mislead because a decision is always about the *next* unit, not the whole stock. Water has enormous total value but its marginal litre is near-worthless because it is abundant; diamonds have small total value but high marginal value because they are scarce. Price and rational choice both hinge on the margin.

**A7. Why are sunk costs irrelevant to a rational decision? Name the fallacy of ignoring this.**

A sunk cost is money already spent and unrecoverable; it does not change with any future choice, so it cannot alter the marginal comparison of future benefits and costs. Letting it influence you is the **sunk-cost fallacy** — e.g., holding a losing stock because "I've already put so much in." The disciplined question is "would I buy it today?" not "how much have I sunk?"

**A8. Define an incentive and give one financial example of moral hazard, adverse selection, and a principal-agent problem.**

An incentive is anything that changes the costs or benefits of an action and thus alters behaviour. **Moral hazard:** a "too big to fail" bank takes excess risk expecting a bailout. **Adverse selection:** at a high loan rate, only the riskiest borrowers accept, worsening the pool. **Principal-agent problem:** a fund manager (agent) chases fees or short-term marks rather than the investor's (principal's) long-run interest.

**A9. Distinguish positive from normative economics, and state why the distinction matters for investing.**

Positive economics describes *what is* — objective, testable ("rate hikes reduce inflation with a lag"). Normative economics prescribes *what ought to be* — value-laden ("the central bank should prioritize inflation over jobs"). It matters because markets pay you for the positive question (what *will* happen) and punish you for trading your normative wishes (what you want to happen).

**A10. Contrast microeconomics and macroeconomics, and give the finance application of each.**

Microeconomics studies individual agents and single markets (a firm's pricing, one stock's supply and demand); its finance use is security-level work — equity valuation, credit analysis, bond pricing. Macroeconomics studies whole-economy aggregates (GDP, inflation, rates, exchange rates); its finance use is top-down — asset allocation, rate and currency strategy, cycle timing. They are two zoom levels on one system.

## Section B — Applied / Numerical Problems (Full Solutions)

**B1. Opportunity cost of idle cash.** A treasurer keeps ₹10,00,000 in a zero-interest current account for a year. A comparable-risk fixed deposit yields 7%. What is the opportunity cost, and what does this imply about "free" cash balances?

*Solution.* Opportunity cost = foregone return = 10,00,000 × 7% = **₹70,000** for the year. Although no cash left the account, the treasurer sacrificed ₹70,000 of interest. This shows that holding cash is never truly free — the cost is the best return foregone at equal risk. Idle balances should be justified by a liquidity or optionality benefit worth at least ₹70,000.

**B2. Economic profit and the reject decision.** A firm has ₹100 crore of surplus cash. A proposed plant earns an accounting return of 9%. Investors can earn 11% on comparable-risk assets. Compute the economic return and state the correct decision.

*Solution.* The 11% comparable return is the firm's cost of capital — an opportunity cost. Economic return = accounting return − cost of capital = 9% − 11% = **−2%**. In rupees, economic loss ≈ 100 × (−2%) = **−₹2 crore per year** relative to the alternative. Despite being accounting-profitable, the plant destroys value. Correct decision: **reject the plant** and return the cash (buyback/dividend) so shareholders can capture the 11%. This is the logic of the hurdle rate and NPV.

**B3. Marginal analysis — when to stop.** A vendor's marginal benefit (₹) from successive units is 100, 80, 60, 40, 20; marginal cost is a constant ₹45 per unit. How many units should be produced, and why not more?

*Solution.* Produce a unit while MB ≥ MC (45).
- Unit 1: MB 100 > 45 → produce
- Unit 2: MB 80 > 45 → produce
- Unit 3: MB 60 > 45 → produce
- Unit 4: MB 40 < 45 → stop

Optimal output = **3 units**. The 4th unit adds ₹40 of benefit for ₹45 of cost, a ₹5 loss on that unit. Producing where MB just covers MC maximizes total net benefit.

**B4. Sunk cost trap.** A fund bought a stock at ₹500; it now trades at ₹300, and the fund has ₹20 lakh of unrealized loss. Fresh analysis says fair value is ₹280. Should the ₹500 cost or the ₹20 lakh loss influence the hold/sell decision?

*Solution.* Neither. The ₹500 purchase price and the ₹20 lakh loss are **sunk** — unrecoverable and unchanged by any future action. The only relevant comparison is current price (₹300) versus forward fair value (₹280). Since fair value (₹280) is below market price (₹300), the marginal decision is to **sell** (or at minimum not add). Anchoring to the ₹500 cost is the sunk-cost fallacy.

**B5. PPF and opportunity cost of growth.** An economy on its PPF can make consumer goods or capital goods. Moving from producing 100 to 120 capital goods forces consumer goods down from 400 to 360. What is the opportunity cost of the extra 20 capital goods, and why does the PPF bow outward?

*Solution.* Opportunity cost of 20 more capital goods = consumer goods foregone = 400 − 360 = **40 consumer goods** (2 consumer goods per capital good). The PPF is bowed outward (concave) because resources are not equally suited to both goods; as you specialize further into capital goods, you pull in resources increasingly ill-suited to them, so each extra capital good costs progressively more consumer goods — the law of increasing opportunity cost. Choosing more capital goods today shifts the future PPF outward faster (higher growth) at the cost of current consumption — the growth-versus-consumption trade-off behind long-run interest rates.

**B6. Incentive design in a fee structure.** A fund charges "2 and 20" (2% of assets plus 20% of profits). Identify which behaviours each component incentivizes and one misalignment it can create.

*Solution.* The **2% management fee** is paid on assets regardless of performance — it incentivizes asset gathering (growing AUM) and provides income stability, but is weakly linked to investor returns. The **20% performance fee** is a call-option-like payoff on profits — it aligns the manager with gains but incentivizes excess risk-taking, because the manager shares the upside without sharing the downside symmetrically (a form of moral hazard). Misalignment: in a poor year the manager may "swing for the fences" to reach the profit hurdle, taking risk the investor would not choose. Hurdle rates, high-water marks, and clawbacks are incentive fixes.

## Section C — Interview-Style Questions (Model Answers)

**C1. "What is your discount rate and why?"**

The discount rate is an opportunity cost — the return available elsewhere at equal risk. For a firm it is the WACC, the blended cost of equity and debt; for a project it is the hurdle rate reflecting the risk of that project's cash flows. Conceptually it answers "what else could this capital earn at comparable risk?" We reject anything that returns less, because funding it destroys value relative to the next-best use. In one line: WACC is opportunity cost wearing a suit.

**C2. "Why did government bonds have one of their worst years ever in 2022 — weren't they 'safe'?"**

"Safe" means low credit risk, not zero price risk. In 2021, supply-chain scarcity, large fiscal transfers, and rapid money-supply growth set up inflation. When inflation arrived in 2022, central banks raised rates sharply. Bond prices move inversely to yields, and long-duration government bonds are highly rate-sensitive, so their prices fell 15–20%. The economics — reading the inflation set-up early — was the warning system. It shows credit-safe assets can still carry large interest-rate risk.

**C3. "Explain the diamond-water paradox and why it matters for pricing anything."**

Water is far more useful in total than diamonds, yet diamonds are dearer. Price reflects *marginal* value, not total value. Water is abundant, so the marginal litre is nearly worthless; diamonds are scarce, so the marginal stone is precious. This matters because every price — of a good, a stock, a bond — is set at the margin by the last willing buyer and seller. Value the marginal unit, not the aggregate usefulness.

**C4. "Walk me through moral hazard using the 2008 crisis."**

Mortgage originators earned fees for writing loans but sold the credit risk onward via securitization, so their incentive was volume, not quality. Rating agencies were paid by the issuers they rated, incentivizing generous ratings. Banks believed they were too big to fail, encouraging excess leverage. Each actor responded rationally to misaligned incentives; the aggregate was systemic collapse. The lesson: before trusting any product or counterparty, map the incentives — ask "who benefits and what are they incentivized to do?"

**C5. "A strategist says the Fed *should* cut rates. How do you use that in a trade?"**

I separate the normative from the positive. "Should cut" is a value judgment — irrelevant to my P&L. The tradeable question is positive: given the data (especially inflation stickiness and the labour market), what *will* the Fed do, and how are my assets priced for it? In late 2023 markets priced six or seven 2024 cuts on the hope of easing; the positive read of sticky inflation pointed to far fewer, and rate-sensitive longs were repriced painfully. Trade what will happen, not what you wish would.

**C6. "Why should a bottom-up stock picker care about macro?"**

Because an asset's price is the present value of scarce future cash flows discounted at a risk-adjusted rate, and both inputs are partly macro. Cash flows depend on the earnings cycle — a recession compresses earnings across the board. The discount rate is built on the macro risk-free rate (central-bank policy plus inflation expectations) and cycle-dependent risk premia. Even a perfect micro model is valuing a company in a vacuum if it ignores the rate regime and the cycle. Micro values the security; macro sets the environment.

**C7. "Give me one economic principle that has saved you from a bad decision."**

Ignoring sunk costs. The temptation is to average down or hold a loser to "make back" what's already lost. But the past outlay is unrecoverable and irrelevant; the only question is whether I'd buy the position today at the current price given current fair value. Reframing every held position as a fresh buy decision each morning removes the sunk-cost anchor and stops good money chasing bad.

## Section D — Multiple-Choice Questions (with Reasoning)

**D1.** Opportunity cost is best defined as:
a) The money paid for a good
b) The value of the next-best alternative foregone
c) The total of all alternatives foregone
d) The accounting cost of production

**Answer: (b).** Opportunity cost is the single next-best alternative given up, not the sum of all alternatives (c) and not necessarily any money paid (a, d). It can be purely implicit, such as foregone interest.

**D2.** A firm earns 8% accounting return; its cost of capital is 10%. Economically, the firm is:
a) Creating value
b) Breaking even
c) Destroying value
d) Cannot be determined

**Answer: (c).** Economic return = 8% − 10% = −2%. Earning below the opportunity cost of capital destroys value even though accounting profit is positive.

**D3.** "Raising interest rates reduces inflation with a lag of 12–18 months" is an example of:
a) A normative statement
b) A positive statement
c) A value judgment
d) A policy recommendation

**Answer: (b).** It describes what *is* and is testable against data. Normative statements (a, c, d) prescribe what *ought* to be and cannot be settled by evidence alone.

**D4.** Which is a macroeconomic variable?
a) The price of a single stock
b) A firm's output level
c) The national inflation rate (CPI)
d) A consumer's utility from one good

**Answer: (c).** CPI is an economy-wide aggregate. The others (a, b, d) are individual agents or single markets — microeconomic.

**D5.** The diamond-water paradox is resolved by recognizing that price reflects:
a) Total utility
b) Marginal utility
c) Cost of extraction only
d) Government regulation

**Answer: (b).** Price is set by the value of the marginal (next) unit. Abundant water has low marginal utility; scarce diamonds have high marginal utility.

**D6.** A sunk cost should be:
a) Recovered before deciding
b) Added to future costs
c) Ignored in the marginal decision
d) Split across future units

**Answer: (c).** Sunk costs are unrecoverable and unchanged by future action, so they are irrelevant to the MB-versus-MC comparison. Treating them otherwise is the sunk-cost fallacy.

**D7.** "Too big to fail" banks taking excess risk because they expect a bailout is an example of:
a) Adverse selection
b) Moral hazard
c) A principal-agent problem
d) Positive economics

**Answer: (b).** Moral hazard is excess risk-taking when one is insulated from the downside. Adverse selection (a) concerns hidden information at the point of selection; the agency problem (c) concerns a decision-maker acting for a principal.

**D8.** The Production Possibility Frontier bows outward (is concave) because:
a) Resources are equally productive in all uses
b) Opportunity cost falls as you specialize
c) Opportunity cost rises as you specialize (resources are not equally suited)
d) Technology is fixed forever

**Answer: (c).** Because resources are not equally good at everything, shifting more into one good pulls in ever-less-suited resources, so each extra unit costs more of the other good — increasing opportunity cost, which makes the curve concave.

**D9.** In finance, the risk-free rate is best interpreted as:
a) A return with no economic meaning
b) The opportunity cost of holding cash instead of a T-bill
c) A normative benchmark set by regulators
d) An accounting convention

**Answer: (b).** The risk-free rate is the return foregone by holding non-interest-bearing cash rather than a short-dated government security — an opportunity cost, the base layer of every discount rate.

**D10.** Which pair correctly matches an economic idea to its finance counterpart?
a) Opportunity cost → sunk cost
b) Marginal analysis → cost of capital
c) Opportunity cost → cost of capital / hurdle rate
d) Normative economics → NPV

**Answer: (c).** The cost of capital and the hurdle rate are opportunity-cost reasoning applied to funds. Marginal analysis maps instead to position sizing and portfolio optimization, and NPV is a positive (not normative) tool.
