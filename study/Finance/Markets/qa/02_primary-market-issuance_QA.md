# Q&A — The Primary Market and Securities Issuance

A companion practice bank for the chapter "The Primary Market and Securities Issuance." Work each question before reading the answer. Sections: **A. Concept-check**, **B. Applied / scenario**, **C. Interview-style**, **D. MCQs with reasoning.**

## Section A — Concept-Check Questions

**A1. In one sentence, what is the defining function of the primary market, and how does the flow of money differ from the secondary market?**

The primary market is where new securities are created and sold for the first time, so money flows *from investors to the issuer* — the company or government receives fresh cash and capital is formed. In the secondary market, existing securities change hands between investors, so money flows *from one investor to another* and the original issuer receives nothing.

**A2. Why is information asymmetry called the "core tension" the primary market must solve?**

The issuer knows everything about its own business; the investor knows almost nothing, yet the investor must part with money *before* being able to verify whether the business is sound. This gap breeds fraud, mispricing and mistrust. Almost every feature of the primary market — the prospectus, the merchant banker, SEBI vetting, underwriting, book-building — exists to bridge that trust gap.

**A3. Distinguish a Fresh Issue from an Offer for Sale (OFS). Which one performs capital formation?**

In a Fresh Issue, brand-new shares are created and the *company* receives the money — this is genuine capital formation. In an OFS, existing shareholders (founders, PE/VC funds) sell their *old* shares to the public, so the *selling shareholders* receive the money and the company's bank balance is unchanged. Only the Fresh Issue forms new capital; an OFS is essentially a secondary sale wrapped inside a primary offer.

**A4. What is the essential difference between an IPO, an FPO, and a rights issue?**

An **IPO** is the first-ever public offer of shares by a hitherto unlisted company. An **FPO** is a *further* offer to the general public by a company that is *already listed*. A **rights issue** is an offer of new shares only to *existing shareholders*, pro rata to their holdings, usually at a discount. So IPO and FPO both target the general public (unlisted vs. listed issuer), whereas rights go only to current owners.

**A5. What does SEBI actually do when it reviews a DRHP — and what does it deliberately NOT do?**

SEBI checks that the disclosure is adequate and truthful and issues *observations* on the draft. It does **not** approve the investment merits, recommend the stock, or guarantee returns. "Buyer beware" survives; SEBI merely ensures the buyer has the facts. The same is true of the SEC in the US.

**A6. Explain ASBA and why it was an investor-protection landmark.**

ASBA (Application Supported by Blocked Amount) means the application money is *blocked* in the investor's own bank account rather than debited and handed to the company. The investor keeps earning interest, and the money is debited only if and to the extent shares are allotted; any unallotted portion is simply unblocked. Before ASBA, application money sat idle with the issuer for weeks, so this was a major reform.

**A7. What is the cut-off price in a book-built issue, and who pays it?**

The cut-off price is the single price at which the *entire* issue can be sold — the clearing price discovered from the demand book. All successful allottees pay this one price, even those who bid higher. It is *not* the lowest price; it is the highest price at which the whole issue clears.

**A8. What is a QIP and why did SEBI create it?**

A Qualified Institutions Placement is a route (introduced 2006) that lets an *already-listed* Indian company raise equity quickly by selling shares only to Qualified Institutional Buyers (QIBs) — mutual funds, banks, insurers, FPIs, pension funds. SEBI created it so listed firms could raise capital fast, at low cost and with formula-bound pricing, instead of enduring a long public offer or listing abroad. It is now the dominant follow-on equity route.

**A9. Name the three tiered disclosure documents in an Indian public issue and what distinguishes each.**

The **DRHP** (Draft Red Herring Prospectus) is the first draft filed with SEBI; it omits the final price and size. The **RHP** (Red Herring Prospectus) is the near-final version filed with the Registrar of Companies before the issue opens, now carrying the price band. The **Prospectus** is the final document containing the fixed price or discovered cut-off, filed after pricing.

**A10. Why does the merchant banker's reputation matter so much?**

Investors cannot verify the issuer directly, but they *can* observe that a reputable banker has done due diligence and attached its name to the issue. The banker effectively lends its credibility to an unknown issuer — the classic "certification" role in finance. Because that reputation is a valuable, repeat-game asset, bankers guard it fiercely.

## Section B — Applied / Scenario Questions

**B1. A company runs an IPO that is 60% Fresh Issue and 40% OFS, raising ₹1,000 crore in total. How much cash does the company actually receive, and where does the rest go?**

The company receives only the Fresh Issue portion: 60% of ₹1,000 crore = **₹600 crore**. The remaining ₹400 crore from the OFS goes to the *selling shareholders* (e.g. the PE fund or promoters exiting), not to the company. This is the classic trap: total IPO size is not the same as capital raised by the firm.

**B2. A stock trades at ₹1,000. The company announces a 1:4 rights issue at ₹600. A shareholder holds 400 shares. How many rights shares can she buy, what will she pay, and what are her three options?**

At 1:4 she is entitled to 1 new share for every 4 held = 400 / 4 = **100 rights shares**, costing 100 × ₹600 = **₹60,000**. Her three options: (1) **subscribe** and pay ₹60,000 to maintain her proportional ownership; (2) **renounce** — sell the rights entitlement to someone else in the market for value, since rights are renounceable; or (3) let the rights **lapse** and forgo them (the worst option, as she loses both the value and gets diluted).

**B3. In a book-built IPO the price band is ₹450–₹475. Retail is subscribed 8× and QIB 40×, and the cut-off is set at ₹475. The stock lists at ₹560. What is the underpricing, and what does it mean for the company?**

Underpricing (the listing gain) = (560 − 475) / 475 ≈ **17.9%**. Day-one investors who received an allotment enjoy this ~18% pop. For the *company*, however, it means it sold shares at ₹475 that the market was willing to pay ₹560 for — roughly ₹85 per share of value "left on the table." Heavy oversubscription plus a large pop signals the issue could arguably have been priced higher.

**B4. Retail is oversubscribed 8× in the above IPO. How does the registrar allot shares, and what happens to the money of unsuccessful applicants?**

Because retail demand exceeds supply, shares cannot be allotted pro rata down to fractions of a lot; instead allotment is done by **lottery** in minimum lot sizes, so each successful retail applicant gets at least one full lot and many get nothing. For unsuccessful applicants, the blocked ASBA funds are simply **unblocked** in their bank accounts — no refund cheque is needed because the money was never debited.

**B5. A listed bank needs ₹20,000 crore quickly to shore up its capital adequacy ratio. Compare doing this via a fresh IPO-style public offer versus a QIP. Which fits, and why?**

An IPO route is impossible — the bank is already listed — and even an FPO to the public would take weeks of SEBI vetting, a full prospectus, marketing and retail logistics. A **QIP** fits: the bank sells directly to ~30 QIBs at a SEBI-formula floor price (two-week average), with no lengthy prospectus vetting, and can close in *days* at minimal cost. Speed and low friction make QIP the workhorse for exactly this situation.

**B6. An issue is only 80% subscribed by the public but is fully underwritten. What happens, and what is this event called?**

The underwriter is contractually bound to buy the unsold 20% shortfall, so the issuer still receives its full intended capital. The event of the unsold shares landing on the underwriter is called **devolvement**. This is precisely the risk the underwriter is paid an underwriting commission to bear, and it lets the issuer know it will get its money regardless of market mood.

**B7. Airbnb priced its US IPO at $68 and opened at $146. Explain what happened and the tension it illustrates.**

The ~115% first-day "pop" is a dramatic case of **IPO underpricing**. The company raised capital at $68 while the market valued the shares at $146 — an enormous amount left on the table. It illustrates the tension between issuers (who want a high price to maximise proceeds) and underwriters (who prefer a modestly underpriced, comfortably oversubscribed deal that rewards their institutional clients and guarantees a "successful" launch). Underpricing is systematic, partly explained by information asymmetry and the winner's-curse compensation to uninformed investors.

**B8. A company wants to sell shares to 250 specific investors in one financial year and thinks it can call this a "private placement." What is the problem?**

In India, a private placement stays "private" only if the offer is to **up to 200 persons** in a financial year (excluding QIBs and ESOP employees). Crossing 200 persons legally converts it into a **public issue**, triggering the full obligations — prospectus, SEBI process, disclosure and listing requirements. Selling to 250 investors as a "private placement" would be a deemed public offer and non-compliant.

**B9. A retail investor wants to apply for ₹4 lakh in an IPO but doesn't want to guess the exact price. What two mechanisms let her do this cleanly in India?**

First, she can bid **"at cut-off,"** agreeing to pay whatever final price the book discovers, sparing her from guessing a number within the band. Second, since her application is up to ₹5 lakh, she can apply through the **UPI-linked ASBA** mechanism, where the ₹4 lakh is blocked in her account via a UPI mandate and debited only on allotment. Both remove friction from retail participation.

**B10. Anchor investors are allotted shares the day before an IPO opens. Where does their allocation come from, and what is the purpose?**

Anchor allocation is carved out of the **QIB portion** (anchors are a subset of QIBs). Large institutions commit a day before the issue opens, subject to a **lock-in**, which signals confidence and anchors demand so that when the issue opens to everyone else, the presence of credible marquee investors builds momentum and reduces the risk of a weak launch.

## Section C — Interview-Style Questions

**C1. "Walk me through what happens, step by step, in an Indian book-built IPO."**

The issuer appoints Book Running Lead Managers (merchant bankers), who conduct due diligence and file a **DRHP** with SEBI. SEBI reviews and issues *observations* (not approval). The bankers then file the **RHP** with a **price band**. The issue opens for about three days; investors apply via **ASBA**, with funds blocked in their bank accounts. Demand builds in the book across three buckets — QIB (up to 50%), NII/HNI (at least 15%), Retail (at least 35%). After bidding closes, the bankers set the **cut-off price**, the registrar runs **allotment** (lottery for oversubscribed retail), unallotted funds are unblocked, shares are credited to demat accounts, and the stock **lists** on the exchange (T+3 timeline), at which point secondary trading begins.

**C2. "Why do so many IPOs 'pop' on the first day? Is that a good thing?"**

The pop is **IPO underpricing** — issuing below the price the market will pay. It persists systematically for a few reasons: information asymmetry means issuers underprice to compensate uninformed investors for the "winner's curse"; bankers want an oversubscribed, reputation-enhancing launch and to reward their institutional clients; and underpricing reduces the risk of a failed issue. For **day-one investors** it is great. For the **company** it is a real cost — capital raised below fair value, money left on the table. So a huge pop is a mixed signal: exciting for flippers, but it means the firm could have raised more.

**C3. "A client is a listed company that needs to raise equity fast. What are their options and how would you choose between them?"**

Since they are already listed, the main routes are an **FPO** (to the general public), a **rights issue** (to existing shareholders), a **preferential allotment** (to identified specific investors), or a **QIP** (to QIBs only). I would choose based on speed, cost, dilution and who the buyers are. For the fastest, cheapest institutional raise, a **QIP** wins — no lengthy prospectus, formula-bound floor price, days not weeks. If they want to reward and tap existing owners cheaply, a **rights issue** (often at a discount, renounceable) is ideal. If a strategic partner is infusing capital, a **preferential allotment** under the ICDR pricing formula fits. An FPO to the public is the slowest and most expensive and is used mainly to meet minimum public shareholding or raise large sums broadly.

**C4. "What is underwriting, and what risk does the underwriter take on?"**

Underwriting is a guarantee: the underwriter contractually agrees to subscribe to any portion of the issue the public does not take up, in exchange for a commission. The risk it bears is **under-subscription** — if demand is weak, the shortfall *devolves* onto the underwriter, which must buy those shares itself and hold inventory it may have to sell at a loss. In **firm-commitment** underwriting (common in the US), the underwriter buys the *entire* issue from the company upfront and resells it, bearing full inventory and pricing risk; in **best-efforts,** it only tries to sell with no guarantee. Underwriting exists so the issuer knows it will get its capital regardless of market sentiment.

**C5. "Explain fixed-price versus book-building. Which is better and why?"**

In a **fixed-price** issue the issuer sets one price upfront in the prospectus and investors take it or leave it — simple and transparent, but the price is a guess made *before* any demand is observed, so it is easily mispriced. In **book-building**, the issuer announces a price *band* and investors bid quantity and price over a few days; demand is recorded in a book and the merchant banker sets the market-clearing **cut-off**. Book-building is superior for large or uncertain issues because it delivers genuine **price discovery** — the market, not the issuer's guess, sets the price — and gives live visibility into demand during bidding. That is why it dominates all large modern IPOs. Fixed price survives mainly for smaller issues.

**C6. "Why is a healthy secondary market a precondition for a healthy primary market?"**

Investors will only subscribe to newly issued securities if they believe they can *sell* them later at a fair price. A liquid, transparent secondary market provides that exit and continuous price signal, which lowers the liquidity premium investors demand and makes them willing to fund new issues. Without credible secondary liquidity, primary issuance would dry up — no one buys what they cannot resell. The two markets are symbiotic: the primary market *creates* securities, the secondary market *sustains their value*, and **listing** is the handoff point between them.

**C7. "How does the primary market connect to private equity and venture capital?"**

VC and PE funds finance companies *before* they reach the public market, taking illiquid private stakes. The IPO is typically their **exit** — the moment they convert those stakes into cash, often through an **Offer for Sale** component in the IPO. So the primary market sits at the drain end of the private-capital pipeline: private money funds a company through its risky early life, and the public primary market provides the liquidity event that recycles that capital back to the funds so they can invest again. This is why many PE-backed IPOs are OFS-heavy rather than fresh-issue-heavy.

**C8. "What is a green-shoe option and what misconception do people have about it?"**

A green-shoe (over-allotment) option lets underwriters allot *more* shares than the base issue and then **buy shares back in the market** if the price falls after listing — a **price-stabilisation** tool that cushions the stock in its fragile early days. The common misconception is that a green-shoe signals oversubscription or extra demand. It does not; it is a stabilisation mechanism, not a demand indicator. By buying back on weakness (and covering the over-allotment on strength), the underwriter smooths post-listing volatility.

## Section D — Multiple-Choice Questions (with reasoning)

**D1. Which of the following raises fresh capital for the issuing company?**
A. An investor buying shares on NSE the day after listing
B. A pure Offer for Sale by a PE fund in an IPO
C. A Fresh Issue of shares in an IPO
D. A shareholder renouncing rights to another investor

**Answer: C.** Only a Fresh Issue creates new shares whose proceeds go to the company. Secondary trades (A), an OFS (B, money goes to the selling fund), and renunciation of rights (D, money goes to the renouncing shareholder) do not add capital to the company.

**D2. Under ASBA, application money is:**
A. Debited immediately and held by the company
B. Blocked in the investor's bank account until allotment
C. Paid to the merchant banker in escrow
D. Refunded only by cheque if shares are not allotted

**Answer: B.** ASBA blocks (does not debit) funds in the investor's own account; the money earns interest and is debited only to the extent of allotment. Unallotted amounts are simply unblocked — no cheque refund and no idle money with the company.

**D3. The cut-off price in a book-built issue is best described as:**
A. The lowest price in the band
B. The average of all bids
C. The single price at which the entire issue clears, paid by all allottees
D. The floor price set by SEBI's formula

**Answer: C.** The cut-off is the market-clearing price discovered from the demand book; every successful allottee pays it, even those who bid higher. It is not the lowest price (A), not an average (B), and the SEBI formula floor (D) applies to QIPs/preferential pricing, not the IPO cut-off.

**D4. A QIP can be used by:**
A. Any unlisted company for its first public offer
B. A listed company raising from Qualified Institutional Buyers
C. A listed company offering shares pro rata to existing shareholders
D. A government raising debt

**Answer: B.** A QIP is available only to *already-listed* companies and only to QIBs. An unlisted first offer is an IPO (A); a pro-rata offer to existing holders is a rights issue (C); government debt is raised via G-Sec auctions (D).

**D5. Which statement about SEBI's role in an IPO is correct?**
A. SEBI guarantees the investment will not lose money
B. SEBI recommends whether investors should buy
C. SEBI vets the adequacy and truthfulness of disclosure, not investment merit
D. SEBI sets the final issue price

**Answer: C.** SEBI ensures disclosure is adequate and issues observations; it never guarantees returns (A), never recommends the stock (B), and does not set the price (D) — pricing is done by the issuer and merchant banker, discovered via book-building.

**D6. A 1:5 rights issue means:**
A. One new share is offered for every five shares held
B. Five new shares for every one held
C. Shares are offered to the public at a 5% discount
D. One share for every five investors

**Answer: A.** The ratio X:Y in a rights issue means X new shares offered for every Y shares already held; 1:5 = one new share per five held. It concerns existing shareholders' proportional entitlement, not the public (C) or a per-investor count (D).

**D7. Devolvement in the context of a public issue refers to:**
A. Shares being oversubscribed by retail investors
B. The unsold portion of an issue landing on the underwriter
C. The company delaying its listing
D. Anchor investors withdrawing their bids

**Answer: B.** Devolvement is when an under-subscribed issue's shortfall must be bought by the underwriter under its guarantee. It is the crystallisation of under-subscription risk — the opposite of oversubscription (A).

**D8. Which route is limited, in India, to an offer to up to 200 persons in a financial year (excluding QIBs and ESOP employees) before it becomes a deemed public issue?**
A. Rights issue
B. IPO
C. Private placement
D. FPO

**Answer: C.** The 200-person threshold defines a private placement; crossing it converts the offer into a public issue with full obligations. Rights, IPO and FPO are governed by their own separate rules.

**D9. The order of tiered disclosure documents in an Indian public issue is:**
A. Prospectus → RHP → DRHP
B. DRHP → RHP → Prospectus
C. RHP → DRHP → Prospectus
D. Abridged Prospectus → DRHP → RHP

**Answer: B.** The draft filed with SEBI (DRHP, no price) comes first, then the near-final RHP with the price band filed with the RoC, then the final Prospectus with the discovered price. The abridged prospectus is a summary accompanying the application form, not a stage in this sequence.

**D10. A green-shoe option primarily serves to:**
A. Prove the issue is oversubscribed
B. Stabilise the share price after listing via over-allotment and buy-back
C. Give existing shareholders anti-dilution protection
D. Set the IPO price band

**Answer: B.** The green-shoe is an over-allotment mechanism that lets underwriters buy shares back in the market to cushion the price after listing. It is a stabilisation tool, not a demand signal (A), not anti-dilution protection (C), and unrelated to setting the band (D).

**D11. In a standard Indian book-built IPO, the maximum share of the issue reserved for QIBs and the minimum for retail are, respectively:**
A. Up to 50% and at least 35%
B. Up to 35% and at least 50%
C. Up to 75% and at least 10%
D. Exactly 50% and exactly 25%

**Answer: A.** The buckets are QIB up to 50%, NII/HNI at least 15%, and Retail at least 35%. Option A matches; the others misstate the standard allocation.

**D12. Which pair correctly matches the regulator and its primary IPO disclosure document?**
A. India — S-1; US — DRHP
B. India — DRHP (SEBI); US — S-1 (SEC)
C. India — RHP (RBI); US — Prospectus (Fed)
D. India — S-1 (SEBI); US — DRHP (SEC)

**Answer: B.** In India the issue is regulated by SEBI with the DRHP/RHP/Prospectus chain; in the US the SEC uses the S-1 registration statement. The RBI and Fed are monetary authorities, not the IPO disclosure regulators here.
