# Q&A — Equity Instruments and Shares

Companion practice bank for Chapter 06. Every question is followed by a full answer. Section A checks concepts, B applies them to numbers and situations, C rehearses interview questions, and D sharpens judgement on the tricky distinctions.

---

## Section A — Concept Check

**A1. What single principle defines equity, and why does everything else follow from it?**

The defining principle is the **residual claim**. Equity holders are paid last — after suppliers, employees, lenders, taxes, and preference shareholders. From this everything follows: being last means they can be wiped out in bankruptcy (high risk), but they own everything *above* the fixed claims, so their upside is unlimited (a bondholder is capped at coupon plus principal). The discretionary dividend, the volatility, and the voting rights all trace back to being the residual claimant. Equity trades priority for upside and safety for control.

**A2. Distinguish authorised, issued, subscribed, and paid-up capital.**

Nested legal layers, each smaller than the last. **Authorised** is the ceiling in the constitution — the maximum it may ever issue. **Issued** is the portion actually offered. **Subscribed** is what investors agreed to buy. **Paid-up** is what they have actually paid in (the rest callable later on partly-paid shares). The chain is authorised ≥ issued ≥ subscribed ≥ paid-up. The trap is treating "authorised capital" as money the company possesses — it is only legal headroom.

**A3. Why are preference shares called a "hybrid," and what do they give up?**

They sit between debt and equity. Like debt, they carry a **fixed dividend rate** and rank ahead of ordinary shares for dividends and liquidation ("preference"). Like equity, they are share capital, not a loan. In exchange for priority and a fixed return, they give up **voting rights** (except on matters affecting them or when dividends are in arrears) and **upside** (no share of surplus profits, unless "participating"). This makes preferred stock economically closer to a bond.

**A4. In India, name one legal constraint on preference shares that does not apply in every jurisdiction.**

Indian preference shares **must be redeemable**, with a maximum tenor of **20 years** (30 years for certain infrastructure companies). Perpetual (irredeemable) preference shares, common in some other markets, are not permitted for ordinary companies in India.

**A5. What does a DVR or dual-class share decouple, and who benefits?**

It decouples **cash-flow rights from voting rights**. A holder owns the economics while holding a different — usually lower — number of votes (often compensated by a higher dividend or lower price). **Founders** benefit: they raise equity without diluting voting control. Tata Motors' DVR (1 vote per 10 shares) is the Indian example; Alphabet's Class A/B/C and Meta are US examples where founders keep control while owning a minority of the economics.

**A6. Par value, book value, market value — give a one-line definition of each and say what drives it.**

- **Par (face) value:** an arbitrary nominal legal figure (₹1–₹10), set by convention. Not a price.
- **Book value per share:** (shareholders' equity − preference capital) ÷ shares — the accounting record of net assets, backward-looking.
- **Market value:** the traded price, driven by supply, demand, and expected future earnings — forward-looking.

They are three lenses (legal, accounting, expectational) on the same share, and can differ by orders of magnitude.

**A7. What is the difference between the record date and the ex-dividend date?**

The **record date** is the cut-off: whoever is on the register of members that day receives the dividend. The **ex-dividend date** is when the share starts trading *without* the right to the upcoming dividend. To be on the register by the record date you must buy *before* the ex-date. Buy on or after it and you get nothing — and the price mechanically falls by roughly the dividend on the ex-date.

## Section B — Applied / Scenario Questions

**B1. A company has shareholders' equity of ₹90,000 crore, no preference capital, and 415 crore shares outstanding at ₹5 par. The market price is ₹1,500. Compute book value per share and the P/B ratio, and interpret.**

Book value per share = ₹90,000 cr ÷ 415 cr ≈ **₹217**. P/B = ₹1,500 ÷ ₹217 ≈ **6.9×** — the market values the company at nearly seven times recorded net assets. That premium reflects expected future profits, a high return on equity, and intangibles (brand, people, contracts) not on the balance sheet. Par value (₹5) is irrelevant here. A high P/B is not automatically "expensive" — it signals the market expects the assets to earn well above book cost.

**B2. Continuing B1, the company announces a 1:1 bonus. What happens to share count, price, book value per share, net worth, and your holding's value?**

Shares double to **830 crore**; price adjusts to ~**₹750**; book value per share halves to ~**₹108** (same equity over twice the shares). Net worth is **unchanged** at ₹90,000 crore — a bonus just capitalises reserves into share capital, moving money between equity line items, not into or out of the company. If you held 100 shares worth ₹1,50,000, you now hold 200 worth ~₹1,50,000. Wealth is unchanged; only per-share figures and share count moved.

**B3. An IT firm has ₹10,000 crore surplus cash, 400 crore shares at ₹500, net profit ₹8,000 crore (EPS ₹20). Compare returning the cash as a ₹25/share dividend versus a buyback at ₹550. What happens to EPS?**

- **Dividend route:** Pay ₹25 × 400 cr = ₹10,000 cr. Share count and EPS unchanged (EPS stays ₹20). Shareholders get cash, taxed at slab rate under post-2020 rules; price drops ~₹25 on the ex-date.
- **Buyback route:** ₹10,000 cr ÷ ₹550 ≈ **18.2 crore shares** repurchased. Shares fall to ~381.8 crore. EPS rises to ₹8,000 cr ÷ 381.8 cr ≈ **₹20.95** — a mechanical increase with no change in the business.

The buyback flatters EPS arithmetically, which is not the same as creating value; overpaying destroys it. Buybacks were more tax-efficient in India until the October 2024 change taxed proceeds in the shareholder's hands.

**B4. Infosys trades at ₹1,600 on the NSE; its ADR represents one share and trades on the NYSE with USD/INR at 83. What is the fair ADR price, and what would an arbitrageur do if the ADR were quoted at $20.00?**

Fair ADR price ≈ ₹1,600 ÷ 83 ≈ **$19.28**. At $20.00 the ADR is expensive relative to the local share. An arbitrageur buys the cheaper local share on the NSE, converts it into an ADR (they are convertible to/from the underlying), and sells the ADR in New York, pocketing roughly $0.72 per share before costs. Buying pressure on the local share and selling pressure on the ADR push the prices back into line. This convertibility is what tethers ADR and local prices — though the ADR holder still bears rupee currency risk.

**B5. You own 100 shares that go ex-dividend tomorrow for a ₹40 dividend, at a price of ₹1,000. A friend says "buy 100 more tomorrow morning to grab the dividend." What is wrong with the plan, and what actually happens to the price?**

Too late. To receive the dividend you must own the shares *before* the ex-date; buying on the ex-date does not entitle you to it. And on the ex-date the price mechanically drops by roughly the dividend (₹1,000 → ~₹960), so even a holder who does get the dividend is just receiving their own capital back — taxable at that. Chasing the dividend this way captures nothing; it is not free money.

**B6. A small-cap stock shows a bid of ₹96 and an ask of ₹100, with tiny volume; a large-cap shows a bid of ₹1,499.95 and ask ₹1,500.00 with huge volume. What does this tell you, and what is the cost implication?**

The small-cap has a **wide spread** (₹4, ~4%) and thin volume — it is **illiquid**. Buying then immediately selling costs ~4% in spread alone, and large orders move the price. The large-cap's spread is a paisa on ₹1,500 with heavy volume — liquid, cheap, and its moves are meaningful because volume backs them. Spread is a direct liquidity indicator; volume confirms whether a move is conviction or noise.

**B7. Company X's shares trade at ₹1,00,000 each; Company Y's at ₹50. A colleague concludes X is a bigger, more expensive company. Where is the error, and what should you compare instead?**

Two errors. (1) **Size** is not per-share price — it is **market cap** = price × shares. A ₹1,00,000 share (e.g., MRF) can belong to a company smaller than one with a ₹50 share but billions of shares. (2) **"Expensive"** means valuation, not sticker price — compare **P/E or P/B**, not the rupee price. A ₹50 stock can be far more richly valued than a ₹1,00,000 one.

**B8. A VC fund invests in a startup using Compulsorily Convertible Preference Shares (CCPS). Why this instrument rather than plain equity or a loan?**

CCPS gives the fund the best of both worlds. Before conversion it behaves like **preferred/debt**: a fixed return, priority over founders' ordinary shares, and downside protection (liquidation preference). At conversion it flips to **ordinary equity**, capturing the upside. It avoids the rigidity of a loan (no repayment schedule to strangle a young company) while still ranking ahead of founders. Hence CCPS is the standard vehicle for PE/VC investment in Indian startups.

## Section C — Interview-Style Questions

**C1. "Walk me through the difference between equity and debt in a company's capital structure."**

Debt is a **fixed, senior claim**: lenders are owed interest and principal on a schedule and rank ahead of equity in liquidation — but their return is capped and they get no control. Equity is a **residual, junior claim**: shareholders are paid last, dividends are discretionary, and they can be wiped out — but they own all the upside above the fixed claims and hold voting control. Debt trades upside for priority and safety; equity trades the reverse. The mix is the firm's leverage decision; preference shares and convertibles bridge the gap.

**C2. "Par value, book value, market value — what's the difference, and which one matters?"**

Par value is a nominal legal figure (say ₹10), mattering only for legal capital and preference dividend rates. Book value per share is net assets ÷ shares — backward-looking, anchoring asset-heavy businesses and driving P/B. Market value is the traded price: forward-looking, reflecting expected future earnings discounted to today. **For an investor, market value matters most**, read *relative to* book and earnings via P/B and P/E. The market-to-book gap is itself information — high P/B says the market expects the assets to earn well; P/B below 1 can signal distress or a bargain.

**C3. "A company can return cash via dividends or buybacks. How would you decide, and what are the trade-offs?"**

Both return surplus cash but differ in mechanics, signalling, and tax. Dividends deliver steady income and cutting one is punished severely — so they signal durable confidence. Buybacks shrink the share count (raising EPS mechanically), signal that management thinks the stock is undervalued, and are flexible. The decision hinges on whether the shares look cheap (favour buyback) or fairly valued (favour dividend), the investor base's income needs, and the tax regime. In India buybacks were long more tax-efficient — until October 2024 taxed them in the shareholder's hands. Crucially, a buyback that overpays destroys value even as it raises EPS.

**C4. "What is an ADR, why would an Indian company issue one, and does it remove currency risk?"**

An ADR is a negotiable certificate issued by a US bank representing shares of a foreign company held in custody at home; it trades on US exchanges in dollars, settled US-style. An Indian company issues ADRs to **access a deeper pool of US capital**, gain global visibility, and sometimes achieve a better valuation — Infosys, Wipro, ICICI Bank, HDFC Bank and Dr. Reddy's are examples. It does **not** remove currency risk: the underlying value is in rupees, so a falling rupee hurts the ADR holder. Arbitrage via convertibility keeps ADR and local prices aligned after adjusting for the ratio and FX.

**C5. "A company announces a 1:1 bonus and the stock jumps. Did shareholders get richer?"**

No — not from the bonus itself. It issues free shares by capitalising reserves; share count rises, price falls proportionally, and total value is unchanged (100 shares at ₹2,000 becomes 200 at ~₹1,000). The mechanical wealth effect is nil. Any price jump reflects **signalling** (management often issues a bonus when confident about earnings) plus improved **affordability and liquidity**. That is the market re-rating expectations, not the arithmetic of the bonus.

**C6. "Why do preference shares usually carry no voting rights, and when do they get a vote?"**

Voting rights compensate for bearing residual risk and a discretionary return. Preference shareholders take *less* risk — a fixed dividend and priority — so control stays with the residual owners. They **regain the vote** in two cases: on resolutions affecting their class rights, and when their dividend has been unpaid for a set period (in India, broadly two years), when they can vote on all resolutions. The vote returns precisely when their preferred position is compromised.

**C7. "What rights does an ordinary shareholder actually have?"**

A bundle: **vote** on directors, auditors, and resolutions; **dividends** when declared, pro-rata; a **residual claim on assets** in winding up; **information** (annual report, accounts, notices); **pre-emptive/rights-issue** rights against dilution; free **transfer** of shares; the right to **attend and speak** at general meetings and to **requisition meetings** with sufficient holding; and **minority-protection** remedies against oppression and mismanagement (Sections 241–242, Companies Act).

## Section D — Multiple-Choice Questions (with reasoning)

**D1. A share has a par value of ₹1, book value of ₹250, and market price of ₹3,600. Which figure best reflects what investors expect the company to earn in the future?**
A. Par value  B. Book value  C. Market price  D. The average of the three

**Answer: C.** Market price is forward-looking — the discounted value of expected future earnings. Par value is a trivial legal artefact; book value is a backward-looking record of net assets. The gap between book (₹250) and market (₹3,600) is the market pricing in future growth and intangibles.

**D2. Which of the following is TRUE of a cumulative preference share?**
A. Missed dividends are lost permanently
B. Unpaid dividends accumulate as arrears that must be cleared before ordinary dividends
C. It always shares in surplus profits
D. It carries full voting rights like ordinary shares

**Answer: B.** "Cumulative" means unpaid dividends build up as arrears payable before ordinary shareholders get anything. A describes *non-cumulative*; C a *participating* share; D is false — preference shares generally lack ordinary voting rights. In India, cumulative is the default.

**D3. On the ex-dividend date, a stock that pays a ₹30 dividend will, all else equal:**
A. Rise by about ₹30  B. Fall by about ₹30  C. Be unchanged  D. Be suspended from trading

**Answer: B.** From the ex-date the share trades without the right to the dividend, so its price mechanically drops by roughly the dividend amount that buyers no longer receive.

**D4. A company does a 2:1 stock split (each share becomes two). Which statement is correct?**
A. Par value is unchanged and reserves are capitalised
B. Par value is halved and total wealth is unchanged
C. The company raises new cash
D. Book value per share is unchanged

**Answer: B.** In a split, par is halved, share count doubles, and price roughly halves — wealth unchanged. A describes a *bonus* (par unchanged, reserves capitalised). C is false — a split raises no cash (a rights issue does). D is false — book value per share falls proportionally.

**D5. Which of the following returns cash to shareholders while mechanically increasing earnings per share?**
A. A rights issue  B. A bonus issue  C. A share buyback  D. A stock split

**Answer: C.** A buyback repurchases and cancels shares, so the same profit is divided among fewer shares and EPS rises. A rights issue raises cash (does not return it). A bonus and a split both increase share count — the opposite effect.

**D6. An American investor buys an ADR of an Indian company. Which risk does the ADR NOT eliminate?**
A. The need to open an Indian demat account
B. US-dollar settlement
C. Currency (rupee) risk
D. Trading during US market hours

**Answer: C.** The ADR removes operational friction — the investor trades in dollars, on a US exchange, during US hours, with no Indian demat account. But the underlying value is in rupees, so a depreciating rupee reduces the return regardless of the dollar quote. Currency risk remains.

**D7. Company A trades at ₹80,000 per share; Company B at ₹40. Which conclusion is valid?**
A. Company A is larger than Company B
B. Company A is more expensively valued than Company B
C. Nothing about size or valuation can be concluded from price alone
D. Company B is a penny stock and therefore risky

**Answer: C.** Per-share price alone tells you nothing. Size is market cap (price × shares); valuation is a multiple like P/E or P/B. A high sticker price can belong to a small company with few shares, a low one to a giant with billions. A, B, and D are all unwarranted inferences from price.

**D8. Under a dual-class structure like Alphabet's, what is the primary purpose of the high-vote founder class?**
A. To pay founders a higher dividend
B. To let founders raise equity capital while retaining voting control
C. To comply with SEC listing rules
D. To increase the company's book value

**Answer: B.** Dual-class/DVR structures decouple economics from control, letting founders raise equity without surrendering voting control — Alphabet's founder-held Class B carries 10 votes each. It is not about dividends (A), not a compliance rule (C), nor book value (D). The trade-off is weaker external accountability, which some investors and index providers resist.
