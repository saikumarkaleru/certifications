# Q&A — Alternative Investments

Companion practice bank for Chapter 17. Every question is followed by a full answer. Section A checks the core concepts, B puts them to work on numbers and scenarios, C rehearses interview questions with model answers, and D sharpens judgement on the distinctions people get wrong.

---

## Section A — Concept Check

**A1. What is an alternative investment, and why is the definition "negative"?**

An alternative investment is **anything that is not a conventional long-only holding of publicly listed stocks, bonds, or cash.** The definition is negative — defined by what it excludes — because the category has no single unifying asset type. It sweeps in private equity, venture capital, hedge funds, real estate, infrastructure, commodities, private credit, farmland and art. What unites this varied universe is not an asset but a shared **economic bargain**: you give up liquidity, accept complexity and higher fees, and in return gain differentiated, less-correlated return streams and access to private value creation.

**A2. What three problems of the traditional stock-bond-cash toolkit do alternatives try to solve?**

(1) **Everything moves together** — in a crisis, "diversified" listed portfolios fall in lockstep, and in 2022 even stocks and bonds fell together, so investors need return streams with different risk drivers. (2) **The best growth happens in private** — companies like Flipkart or Google created most of their explosive value while private, so an investor limited to listed securities is structurally locked out. (3) **Public markets are crowded and efficient** — thousands of analysts price every large-cap, so the mispricings and outsized returns live in harder-to-reach places like distressed debt, pre-IPO startups and physical assets.

**A3. Explain the GP–LP structure.**

An alternatives fund is usually a partnership. The **General Partner (GP)** is the professional manager who sources deals, makes decisions and runs the fund. The **Limited Partners (LPs)** are the passive investors — pension funds, insurers, family offices, sovereign wealth funds, endowments and wealthy individuals — who supply capital, enjoy limited liability and stay hands-off. In India's AIF trust structure the *Sponsor/Manager* plays the GP role and *contributors* are the LPs.

**A4. Distinguish a commitment, a capital call, and dry powder.**

A **commitment** is the total capital an LP pledges (say ₹50 crore). A **capital call** (drawdown) is the GP asking for a slice of that pledge as deals actually arise — perhaps ₹10 crore this year, ₹15 crore next. **Dry powder** is the undrawn balance still committed but not yet called. This is why private funds have long lives: capital is deployed over a 3–5 year investment period, then harvested over the remaining term.

**A5. What is the J-curve and why does it happen?**

The J-curve is the shape of a private fund's reported return over time: **negative early, then rising and turning positive.** Early on the fund charges fees while its young investments are still held at cost, so the LP's paper return is negative. Later, as investments mature and are sold at a profit, the curve rises and turns positive — tracing a "J." It explains why judging a private fund by its first two or three years is misleading.

**A6. Explain the "2 and 20" fee model, including the hurdle rate and high-water mark.**

The classic compensation is a **2% management fee** on committed or invested capital, plus **20% carried interest** ("carry") — a share of profits. Carry is often paid only above a **hurdle rate** (say 8%), so the LP earns a minimum return before the GP shares in gains. A **high-water mark** ensures a hedge fund manager cannot charge a performance fee twice for merely recovering earlier losses — fees apply only on new highs. These fees dwarf a mutual fund's 0.5–1.5% and are the price of accessing skill and private deals.

**A7. Why are alternatives measured by IRR, MOIC, DPI and TVPI instead of simple NAV growth?**

Because cash flows in and out at irregular times (staged calls, staggered exits), so a single start-to-end NAV comparison is meaningless. **IRR** is the cash-flow-timed annualised return. **MOIC / multiple** is total value returned ÷ capital invested ("we made 3x"). **DPI (Distributions to Paid-In)** is cash *actually returned* so far, while **TVPI** is total value including *unrealised* holdings. DPI is real money in hand; TVPI includes the manager's own marks.

**A8. How do private equity and venture capital differ?**

PE buys **mature, profitable** companies, takes **control** stakes, and uses **leverage** (the LBO), creating value by fixing operations. VC funds **young, unprofitable** startups, takes **minority** stakes (founders must stay motivated), uses **no leverage**, and invests in stages (Seed → Series A/B/C). In one line: **PE fixes established businesses; VC funds growth in new ones.**

**A9. Why does the "power law" govern venture capital?**

Because startup outcomes are radically skewed. In a fund of 25 investments, perhaps 15 fail, 8 roughly break even, and 2 become breakout "unicorns" that return the entire fund many times over. Nearly all the profit comes from a tiny handful of winners, so VCs optimise for a few enormous successes in vast addressable markets rather than for avoiding losers.

**A10. Why is a hedge fund a "strategy," not an asset class, and what makes it "alternative"?**

A hedge fund is a flexible, actively managed pool pursuing **absolute returns** — making money whether markets rise or fall — using tools ordinary funds cannot: **short selling, leverage and derivatives.** Unlike PE it usually trades *liquid, listed* instruments. It is "alternative" because of its *strategy and structure* (private, lightly regulated, performance-fee driven), not because of what it holds.

**A11. What are the three sources of return that justify any alternative?**

An **illiquidity premium** (compensation for locking up money for years), a **skill/complexity premium** (paid for expertise the crowd lacks), and a **differentiated risk driver** (a return stream driven by different forces — private company performance, toll traffic, gold, merger outcomes — which is precisely why it diversifies).

**A12. Compare a REIT and an InvIT.**

Both are listed trusts that pool income-producing assets and pass cash flows to unitholders, and both must **distribute at least 90% of net distributable cash flow.** The difference is the underlying asset: a **REIT** holds **real estate** (offices, malls), while an **InvIT** holds **infrastructure** (highways, power lines, pipelines, towers). They democratise assets that would otherwise need ₹1 crore and a 10-year lock-up.

## Section B — Applied / Scenario Questions

**B1. A PE fund buys a company for ₹1,000 crore using ₹350 crore equity and ₹650 crore debt. Over five years it pays down debt to ₹400 crore and sells for ₹1,600 crore. Compute the equity value and MOIC.**

At exit, equity value = sale price − remaining debt = 1,600 − 400 = **₹1,200 crore**. MOIC = 1,200 ÷ 350 = **~3.4x**. Note the enterprise value rose only 60% (1,000 → 1,600), yet the equity multiplied 3.4x — that amplification is leverage plus operational improvement plus (potentially) a higher exit multiple.

**B2. Same deal, but the exit price is only ₹700 crore. What is the equity worth, and what lesson does this illustrate?**

Equity = 700 − 400 = **₹300 crore**, a **loss** on the ₹350 crore invested (~0.86x). The debt must be repaid in full regardless of sale price, so a modest fall in enterprise value wipes out a large chunk of equity. Lesson: **leverage cuts both ways** — it magnifies gains and losses alike, which is why LBO returns are so sensitive to the exit.

**B3. A ₹500 crore VC fund makes 25 investments of ₹20 crore each. 15 return ₹0, 8 return their ₹20 crore, one returns 30x and one returns 15x. Compute the fund's gross multiple and identify where the profit came from.**

Winners: 30 × 20 = ₹600 crore, and 15 × 20 = ₹300 crore. Break-evens: 8 × 20 = ₹160 crore. Failures: ₹0. Total returned = 600 + 300 + 160 = **₹1,060 crore** on a ₹500 crore fund = **~2.1x gross.** The *entire* profit came from just two deals; the median investment lost money. This is the power law in action.

**B4. An LP commits ₹50 crore to a fund. In year 1 the GP calls ₹8 crore and the investments are held at cost while a 2% fee is charged on committed capital. Why is the LP's reported return negative, and what is this called?**

The ₹8 crore of investments sit at cost (no gain yet), but the fund charges 2% of the ₹50 crore commitment = ₹1 crore in fees, dragging reported value below cost. So the LP's early return is **negative** even though nothing has gone wrong — fees are real and gains are unrealised. This early dip is the **J-curve**, and it shows why "committed ≠ invested" and why early returns mislead.

**B5. A REIT reports ₹1,000 crore of net distributable cash flow. What is the minimum it must distribute, and how does an investor with ₹3 lakh benefit versus buying a shop?**

It must distribute at least **90% = ₹900 crore** to unitholders. The ₹3 lakh investor cannot buy a Grade-A building, but by buying REIT units she owns a **fractional, diversified** slice of millions of square feet leased to blue-chip tenants, receives roughly quarterly distributions (perhaps 6–7% yield), and can **sell any trading day** on the exchange. Illiquid, lumpy, management-heavy property becomes a liquid, tradable security.

**B6. A hedge fund charges "2 and 20" with an 8% hurdle and a high-water mark. It falls 10% in year 1, then rises 25% in year 2. On what gain is carry charged in year 2?**

The high-water mark blocks any performance fee until the fund recovers its year-1 loss. Starting at 100, it falls to 90, then rises 25% to 112.5. Carry applies only to the gain **above the old high of 100** (and above the hurdle), i.e. on the 12.5 of new profit — not on the 22.5 rise from 90. The high-water mark prevents the manager from being paid twice for the same recovery.

**B7. An Indian AIF is being marketed to your uncle who wants to invest ₹40 lakh. Can he invest, and what category would a private-credit-to-real-estate fund fall under?**

No — SEBI mandates a **minimum investment of ₹1 crore** per investor in an AIF, so ₹40 lakh is below the threshold. A fund doing private credit / debt into real estate is a **Category II AIF** (everything not in I or III — PE, private credit, real estate funds), which is close-ended and cannot use leverage except short-term.

**B8. An infrastructure fund and a VC fund both promise "high returns." A pension fund needs predictable income to match 30-year liabilities. Which fits better and why?**

**Infrastructure.** It offers monopoly-like positions, regulated or contracted cash flows (a toll road collects fees for decades), inflation-linked revenues and enormous stability — a natural match for long-dated pension liabilities. VC returns are binary and back-loaded (power law, most fail), unsuitable for matching steady liabilities. The pension fund wants *predictability*, not lottery-ticket upside.

**B9. Gold and a corporate bond both cost ₹1 lakh today. In a year of surging inflation and falling equities, why might an investor prefer gold despite it paying no income?**

Gold is a classic **inflation hedge and crisis store of value** whose price tends to rise when inflation surges and when investors flee risk, and it is **poorly correlated** with stocks and bonds, so it diversifies exactly when correlations elsewhere spike to 1. The bond pays a coupon but its fixed cash flows lose real value to inflation and it carries the issuer's credit risk. Gold's cost — **no income** and high volatility — is offset by its behaviour precisely in the scenario described.

**B10. A fund reports TVPI of 1.8x but DPI of only 0.4x after seven years. What does this tell an LP?**

Total value (realised + unrealised) is 1.8x paid-in capital, but only **0.4x has actually been returned as cash** — most of the "gain" is still the manager's unrealised marks on holdings not yet sold. The LP should be cautious: paper marks can compress at exit, and until DPI rises the return is unproven. It signals a fund still mid-harvest, and invites scrutiny of how the remaining 1.4x is valued.

## Section C — Interview-Style Questions with Model Answers

**C1. "Why would a sophisticated investor accept illiquidity and pay 2-and-20 when a cheap index fund exists?"**

*Model answer:* Because the index fund only buys what is public, liquid and already efficiently priced — a crowded arena where beating the market is genuinely hard. Alternatives buy access to what the index cannot reach: whole private companies you can restructure, startups before the world notices, physical assets that throw off inflation-protected cash, and strategies that profit in any direction. The investor is deliberately harvesting three premia — an **illiquidity premium** for locking up capital, a **skill premium** for genuine expertise, and a **diversification benefit** from a different risk driver. The lock-up is a feature, not a bug: it is what lets a manager pursue multi-year value creation without redemption pressure. The caveat is that this only pays if you pick a top-quartile manager — dispersion between best and worst is enormous.

**C2. "Walk me through how an LBO creates value."**

*Model answer:* A GP buys a company using a small slice of its own equity plus a large amount of debt secured on the target's own cash flows. Value then comes from three levers. First, **operational improvement** — better management, cost cuts and growth lift EBITDA. Second, **financial engineering** — the company's cash flow pays down debt over the hold, so an ever-larger share of enterprise value accrues to equity, and cheap leverage amplifies returns. Third, **multiple expansion** — buying at a low valuation multiple and selling at a higher one. Exit is the whole game, via IPO, strategic sale or secondary sale to another fund in four to seven years. The risk is that leverage is symmetric: if the exit disappoints, the fixed debt is repaid first and equity takes the concentrated hit.

**C3. "The name says 'hedge fund' — does that mean it's low-risk? Convince me either way."**

*Model answer:* No. The word "hedge" is historical — it referred to the original idea of hedging market risk by pairing long and short positions — but it does not mean safe. Many hedge funds use substantial leverage and complex derivatives to amplify small edges, which magnifies losses as well as gains. The canonical warning is **LTCM**, a hedge fund run by Nobel laureates that used enormous leverage and nearly took down the financial system in 1998. Some strategies (market-neutral, relative-value) genuinely reduce directional risk; others (levered global macro) are extremely aggressive. So "hedge fund" describes a *structure and toolkit* — shorting, leverage, derivatives, absolute-return goal, 2-and-20, restricted to sophisticated investors — not a risk level. You must look at the specific strategy and leverage, not the label.

**C4. "How does the Indian regulatory framework package alternatives, and what's the logic behind the thresholds?"**

*Model answer:* SEBI wraps privately pooled alternatives in **Alternative Investment Funds** under the 2012 regulations, sorted into three categories: **Category I** for socially or economically desirable investing (VC, SME, infrastructure, angel funds), **Category II** as the catch-all (PE, private credit, real estate — the largest bucket), and **Category III** for complex, leverage-using strategies (hedge, long-short, PIPE). Categories I and II are close-ended and cannot use leverage except short-term; III may. The access thresholds — a **₹1 crore minimum** per investor, a **₹20 crore minimum corpus**, and a **1,000-investor cap** — exist because these products are illiquid, opaque and risky, so SEBI restricts them to investors who can bear the risk and afford advice. Separately, **REITs (2014)** and **InvITs** democratise real estate and infrastructure by listing them, requiring ≥80% in completed rent-yielding assets (REITs) and ≥90% cash-flow distribution — turning ₹1-crore-plus alternatives into units a retail investor can buy.

**C5. "A client says 'alternatives always beat the market.' How do you respond?"**

*Model answer:* I'd correct the premise gently. Alternatives are justified as much by **diversification and access** as by raw return — and after their high fees, many funds actually *underperform* a simple index. The decisive fact is **dispersion**: the gap between top-quartile and bottom-quartile managers is far wider than in public markets, so manager selection matters enormously — picking the wrong GP can turn the whole thesis negative. I'd also flag the J-curve (early returns look bad by design), that "committed ≠ invested," and that reported TVPI leans on unrealised marks. So the honest pitch is: alternatives can improve a portfolio's *risk-adjusted* return through low correlation and access to private value — the Yale endowment model — but only with careful manager selection, a long horizon, and eyes open to fees and illiquidity.

## Section D — Multiple-Choice Questions with Reasoning

**D1. Which best defines an alternative investment?**
A) Any high-risk stock  B) Anything outside conventional listed stocks, bonds, and cash  C) Only hedge funds  D) Foreign equities

**Answer: B.** Alternatives are defined negatively — everything outside the traditional trio of listed equities, bonds and cash, spanning PE, VC, hedge funds, real estate, infrastructure and commodities. A and D are still conventional listed securities; C is only one slice of the universe.

**D2. In a private fund, "dry powder" refers to:**
A) Cash reserves for fees  B) Committed but not yet called capital  C) Distributed profits  D) The management fee

**Answer: B.** Dry powder is capital LPs have committed but the GP has not yet drawn down (called). It is the fund's undeployed firepower, distinct from fees (D) or realised distributions (C).

**D3. The J-curve describes:**
A) A stock's price chart  B) The early negative then rising return path of a private fund  C) A commodity's roll yield  D) The shape of a yield curve

**Answer: B.** Fees and at-cost holdings depress a private fund's return early; maturing exits lift it later, tracing a "J." It is a private-fund phenomenon, unrelated to yield curves or commodity roll.

**D4. The single biggest structural difference between private equity and venture capital is:**
A) PE takes control stakes and uses leverage; VC takes minority stakes and none  B) PE invests abroad, VC domestically  C) VC is regulated, PE is not  D) PE targets startups, VC targets mature firms

**Answer: A.** PE buys mature, profitable firms with controlling stakes financed by debt (LBOs); VC funds young, unprofitable startups with non-leveraged minority stakes. D reverses the two; B and C are not defining distinctions.

**D5. A high-water mark exists to:**
A) Guarantee a minimum return to LPs  B) Prevent a manager charging performance fees twice for recovering the same losses  C) Cap the management fee  D) Set the hurdle rate

**Answer: B.** It ensures performance fees are charged only on new profit above the previous peak, so a manager isn't paid twice for the same recovery. The hurdle rate (D) is a separate minimum-return gate; the two often work together.

**D6. Which metric represents cash actually returned to LPs so far?**
A) TVPI  B) IRR  C) DPI  D) MOIC

**Answer: C.** DPI (Distributions to Paid-In) measures real cash returned relative to capital paid in. TVPI includes unrealised marks, IRR is a time-weighted rate, and MOIC is a total multiple — none isolate realised cash the way DPI does.

**D7. Under SEBI rules, a hedge-fund-style long-short fund using leverage would be registered as:**
A) Category I AIF  B) Category II AIF  C) Category III AIF  D) A REIT

**Answer: C.** Category III covers complex, leverage-using strategies (hedge, long-short, PIPE). Category I is socially desirable (VC/infra/SME), Category II is the PE/private-credit/real-estate catch-all with no leverage, and a REIT is a listed property trust.

**D8. Which is the correct minimum distribution requirement for an Indian REIT or InvIT?**
A) 50% of net income  B) 80% of assets  C) 90% of net distributable cash flow  D) 100% of profits

**Answer: C.** Both REITs and InvITs must distribute at least 90% of net distributable cash flow. The 80% figure (B) is a different rule — the share of a REIT's *assets* that must be in completed, rent-generating property.

**D9. Commodities are attractive to a diversified portfolio mainly because they:**
A) Pay high dividends  B) Are an inflation hedge with low correlation to stocks and bonds  C) Have guaranteed returns  D) Are highly liquid and stable

**Answer: B.** Commodities tend to rise with inflation and move independently of equities and bonds, diversifying the portfolio. They pay **no income** (ruling out A), returns are volatile and price-driven (ruling out C and "stable" in D).

**D10. The primary appeal of private credit to an investor is:**
A) Guaranteed principal  B) Equity-like returns with debt-like seniority, often floating-rate  C) Daily liquidity  D) No credit risk

**Answer: B.** Private credit lenders earn higher, frequently floating-rate yields (protecting against rising rates) while ranking ahead of shareholders in the capital stack. It carries real illiquidity and credit risk, so A, C and D are false.

**D11. A pension fund matching 30-year liabilities would most naturally favour which alternative?**
A) Venture capital  B) Infrastructure  C) Distressed equity  D) Managed futures

**Answer: B.** Infrastructure offers long-life assets with regulated or contracted, inflation-linked cash flows — predictable income that matches long-dated liabilities. VC and distressed equity are back-loaded and binary; managed futures are volatile trend strategies.

**D12. "Committed ≠ invested" matters because:**
A) LPs never pay their full commitment  B) Capital is called over years, so early reported returns understate the eventual outcome  C) The GP keeps the difference  D) It doubles the fee

**Answer: B.** An LP's pledge is drawn down gradually as deals arise, so in the early years little is invested while fees are charged — producing the misleadingly negative J-curve. The commitment is real and payable on call; the point is *timing*, not that LPs escape paying (A) or that the GP pockets it (C).
