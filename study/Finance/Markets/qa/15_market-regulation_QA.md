# Q&A — Market Regulation and Investor Protection

Companion practice bank for Chapter 15. Every question is followed by a full answer. Section A checks concepts, B applies them to situations and cases, C rehearses interview questions with model answers, and D sharpens judgement on the distinctions examiners love to test — insider trading versus manipulation, disclosure versus merit review, and broker-failure funds versus market-loss protection.

---

## Section A — Concept Check

**A1. In one sentence, what is the core purpose of market regulation, and what problem does it exist to solve?**

Market regulation exists **to substitute a credible public referee for the trust that strangers cannot extend to one another directly.** The underlying problem is **asymmetric information and asymmetric power**: the company knows its true condition and you do not; the insider knows tomorrow's news and you do not; the large operator can move a price and you cannot; the intermediary holds your money and you must trust him. Regulation manufactures trust on everyone's behalf so that capital keeps flowing to strangers across distance and time.

**A2. What is the "market for lemons" dynamic, and why does it make regulation necessary rather than optional?**

If investors cannot tell honest issuers from crooks, they price *every* security as if it might be a fraud — demanding sky-high returns or simply staying away. Honest companies, unable to get a fair price, exit; the market fills with lemons and then collapses entirely. The tragedy is that dishonesty by a *few* poisons the well for the honest *majority*. Regulation breaks this dynamic by making honesty **credible**, so that good issuers can be distinguished from bad and capital does not flee. It is not a luxury tax on markets; it is what keeps a market from destroying itself.

**A3. State the four objectives of regulation. Almost every rule serves one of them.**

(1) **Investor protection** — shield savers, especially small retail investors, from fraud, mis-selling, and abuse of their money. (2) **Fairness** — everyone plays by the same rules and has access to the same material information, so no privileged few can systematically extract wealth from the many. (3) **Market integrity** — keep prices *honest*, formed by genuine supply and demand rather than manipulation, so prices carry true information and capital is allocated efficiently. (4) **Systemic stability** — prevent the failure of one institution or one bubble from cascading into a collapse of the whole system.

**A4. What is the dominant *method* of modern securities regulation, and what famous phrase captures its philosophy?**

The dominant method is **mandatory disclosure** rather than merit review. The state does not tell you whether Reliance is a safe bet; it forces Reliance to *tell you everything material* and then punishes it savagely if it lies. Justice Louis Brandeis captured the philosophy in 1914: **"Sunlight is said to be the best of disinfectants."** The regulator guarantees full, timely, truthful sunlight — and separately polices the conduct that sunlight alone cannot prevent (insider trading, manipulation, theft of client money).

**A5. Describe the four-layer architecture of oversight in India, from statute to appeal.**

**Layer 1 — the enabling statute:** Parliament passes the **SEBI Act, 1992**, supported by the SCRA 1956, Depositories Act 1996, and Companies Act 2013. **Layer 2 — the regulator:** the SEBI Act creates SEBI, which combines quasi-legislative (writes regulations), quasi-executive (registers and inspects), and quasi-judicial (holds hearings, imposes penalties) powers. **Layer 3 — SROs and gatekeepers:** SEBI delegates front-line surveillance to exchanges, clearing corporations, depositories, and bodies like AMFI, and leans on gatekeepers (auditors, merchant bankers, rating agencies, independent directors). **Layer 4 — appeal:** because the regulator combines powers, its orders are appealable to the **Securities Appellate Tribunal (SAT)** and thence on questions of law to the **Supreme Court**.

**A6. Define insider trading precisely. What is the fuel, and what exactly is the crime?**

**Insider trading** is dealing in a security while in possession of **Unpublished Price Sensitive Information (UPSI)** — material information not yet public that, once released, would move the price. UPSI is the fuel: financial results, dividends, mergers, buybacks, capital-structure changes, major expansions. The crime is **the informational unfairness** — trading against a counterparty who is structurally blind — not the identity of the trader. India codifies this in the **SEBI (Prohibition of Insider Trading) Regulations, 2015 ("PIT")**.

**A7. What are the two "rhythms" of disclosure, and name the Indian and US instruments for each?**

**Initial / one-time disclosure** happens when a company first raises money: in India a **Draft Red Herring Prospectus (DRHP)** filed under the **ICDR Regulations, 2018**; in the US a **Form S-1** under the 1933 Act. **Continuous / ongoing disclosure** applies once listed: in India the **LODR Regulations, 2015** require quarterly results, immediate disclosure of material events, and shareholding/governance reports; the US analogues are the annual **10-K**, quarterly **10-Q**, and the current-report **8-K**. The governing test throughout is **materiality**, and information must reach *everyone at once* (Reg FD in the US, LODR in India).

**A8. How do insider trading and market manipulation differ at their root?**

They corrupt different things. **Insider trading** exploits *true* private information without artificially moving the price — it corrupts *who knows*. **Manipulation** *creates false* information or prices — it corrupts *the price itself*. Insider trading is the fairness violation of trading on a hidden truth; manipulation is the integrity violation of fabricating a lie in the tape. India polices manipulation chiefly through the **PFUTP Regulations, 2003**.

**A9. What is front-running, and why is it distinct from insider trading?**

**Front-running** is an intermediary who *knows a large impending client order* trading ahead of it for his own benefit, profiting from the price impact the client's order will cause — e.g. a dealer who learns a mutual fund is about to buy 10 lakh shares buys for himself first, rides the move up, and sells into the client's order. It is distinct because it abuses knowledge of a *client's pending order* — a conflict-of-interest and fiduciary breach — not price-sensitive information about the *company*. It is prosecuted under PFUTP.

## Section B — Applied / Scenario Questions

**B1. A promoter prints a glossy prospectus claiming profits the company never earned, raises ₹500 crore from the public, and diverts the money to his private account. Identify every layer of regulation designed to stop this and where each would bite.**

**Disclosure layer:** the prospectus is filed as a DRHP under **ICDR** — the false financials are a disclosure fraud, and the auditors and merchant bankers (gatekeepers) are legally obliged to have caught them. **Conduct layer:** diverting proceeds is fraud under **PFUTP** and a breach of the "use of proceeds" disclosure. **Regulator layer:** SEBI can **investigate** (summon records, attach bank accounts and property since 2014), pass an **interim cease-and-desist** to freeze the ongoing fraud, and finally **disgorge** the gains and **debar** the promoter. **Redress layer:** defrauded investors complain via **SCORES**. The design is layered precisely so that if one gate fails, another catches the fraud.

**B2. Harshad Mehta ramped ACC from ₹200 to nearly ₹9,000 using roughly ₹4,000 crore siphoned from banks. What regulatory changes did this scam directly produce?**

The scam's direct legislative response was to give SEBI **full statutory teeth via the SEBI Act, 1992** — it had existed only as a non-statutory body since 1988. Because Mehta exploited the settlement system in government-securities and bank-receipt markets, the crisis was a major reason India **built electronic depositories (NSDL, CDSL)** and moved to **dematerialised, delivery-versus-payment settlement**, removing the paper-and-trust loopholes he had abused. The general lesson: regulatory architecture is usually born from the ashes of a scam.

**B3. Rajat Gupta phoned a hedge-fund manager within minutes of a Goldman Sachs board call, tipping the ₹/$5bn Buffett injection. Gupta did not personally trade a single share. Under what theory was he convicted, and why does not-trading not save him?**

He was convicted under the **classical / tipper-tippee theory** of Rule 10b-5. The doctrine punishes the **breach of duty and the tip**, not merely personal trading: a "tipper" who passes UPSI in breach of a duty of confidence is liable for the trades his "tippee" then makes. Gupta breached his fiduciary duty to Goldman's shareholders by leaking boardroom UPSI; that Rajaratnam, not Gupta, executed the trades is irrelevant. In India the same act is caught because PIT prohibits **communicating (tipping) UPSI** as an independent offence. The lesson: no reputation is immunity, and you need not trade to be guilty.

**B4. A Telegram channel accumulates a small-cap stock, blasts "target ₹X, buy now" to lakhs of followers, and sells into the buying frenzy its own messages create. Classify the abuse and state SEBI's likely remedies.**

This is a **pump-and-dump**, a species of manipulation prosecuted under **PFUTP** — a modern, digital version of the classic operator-driven ramp. SEBI's remedies, as in its 2023–24 orders against stock-tip channels, are to pass **interim orders** freezing the scheme, **impound the illegal gains (disgorgement)**, **debar** the operators from the market, and levy monetary penalties. The case shows the anti-fraud regulations are deliberately broad enough to catch manipulation as it migrates from broker rings to Telegram groups and finfluencers.

**B5. On 6 May 2010 the Dow fell nearly 1,000 points in minutes. Investigators traced part of it to a trader placing and rapidly cancelling huge S&P 500 futures sell orders he never intended to fill. Name the technique, and explain why placing orders you never execute is still fraud.**

The technique is **spoofing** (with **layering** when stacked at multiple price levels), and the event is the **2010 "Flash Crash"**; the trader was **Navinder Sarao**. It is fraud on the market's **price-discovery mechanism**: the order book is supposed to reflect genuine intended supply and demand, so posting large orders you plan to cancel manufactures a *false impression of selling pressure*, tricking others into reacting to demand that does not exist. The harm is not a completed bad trade but the corruption of the signal that every other participant relies on. It is prosecuted as manipulation even though the spoof orders are cancelled.

**B6. A broker takes client money to buy shares, buys nothing, and gambles it in his own trading account, then goes insolvent. Which specific safeguards were meant to prevent this, and what compensates the client afterward?**

**Prevention:** the **segregation of client funds and securities** rule requires client money to sit in a separate client bank account, never mixed with the broker's own. Since 2022 India enforces **daily/weekly upstreaming** of client funds to the clearing corporation and **direct pay-out** of securities to the client's demat account — so the broker never has idle client cash to gamble. **Compensation:** if the broker is declared a **defaulting member**, the exchange's **Investor Protection Fund (IPF)** compensates the client up to a specified cap — the market's equivalent of deposit insurance. In the US the parallel is **SIPC** (up to $500,000, including $250,000 cash) on broker failure.

**B7. A company's CEO resigns and a plant catches fire on the same morning. What must the company do, under which regulation, and by when?**

Both are **material events** — facts a reasonable investor would consider important — so under the **LODR Regulations, 2015** the company must make an **immediate disclosure** to the stock exchanges (India's rules require prompt disclosure of material events, typically within a short prescribed window such as hours, not days). Critically, it must disclose to **everyone at once** and not selectively tip favoured analysts first. The US analogue would be an **8-K** current report. Until the news is public, it is **UPSI**, and any insider trading on it would separately violate PIT.

**B8. A friend at a printing press sets the type for a company's results and buys the stock before they are announced. Is he an "insider"? Explain using the PIT definition.**

Yes. Under **PIT 2015**, an "insider" is *either* a **connected person** *or* **anyone in possession of UPSI, however obtained.** The printer need not be an employee or director of the company — by handling the unpublished results he came into possession of UPSI, which makes him an insider and his trade illegal. The regulation is deliberately drafted this way so that tippees, printers, professionals, and anyone who *happens* to hold UPSI are all caught; the crime attaches to the **information**, not the job title.

## Section C — Interview-Style Questions

**C1. "Why do we regulate securities markets at all? Couldn't investors just do their own due diligence?"**

Individually, no — the cost and information gaps are insurmountable. A saver in Pune cannot personally verify the accounts of a company in Chennai run by people she will never meet, on documents she cannot audit. Markets ask strangers to trust strangers with money across distance and time, and the resulting **information and power asymmetries** breed fraud. Left alone, this produces a **"market for lemons"**: unable to tell honest issuers from crooks, investors distrust everyone, honest firms exit, and the market collapses. Regulation solves this collectively and cheaply by installing a **credible public referee** who forces disclosure and punishes misconduct — so each investor can rely on the *system* rather than her own impossible investigation. The four goals are investor protection, fairness, market integrity, and systemic stability.

**C2. "What's the philosophy behind disclosure-based regulation? Does SEBI tell me whether a stock is a good buy?"**

No — and that distinction is the heart of it. SEBI and the SEC operate on **mandatory disclosure, not merit review**: they force issuers to reveal everything material — business, financials, promoters, use of proceeds, and a candid *Risk Factors* section — and then punish lies severely, but they explicitly do **not** vouch for whether the investment is good. Every prospectus carries a disclaimer to exactly this effect. The philosophy is Brandeis's **"sunlight is the best of disinfectants"**: an informed market prices securities correctly on its own, so the regulator's job is to guarantee the sunlight is full, timely, and truthful, plus to police the three abuses sunlight cannot stop by itself — insider trading, manipulation, and theft of client money.

**C3. "Explain the difference between insider trading, manipulation, and front-running."**

They corrupt three different things. **Insider trading** corrupts *who trades*: dealing while in possession of **UPSI** — true, material, non-public information — exploiting a counterparty who is structurally blind. **Manipulation** corrupts *the price itself*: deliberately creating a false or misleading appearance of trading or price, through pump-and-dump, wash trading, spoofing, cornering, or marking the close. **Front-running** *steals the client's order*: an intermediary trades ahead of a large client order he knows is coming, profiting from the price impact that rightfully belongs to the client — a fiduciary and conflict-of-interest breach, not company UPSI. One-liner: *insider trading corrupts who knows, manipulation corrupts the price, front-running steals the order.*

**C4. "SEBI combines rule-making, investigation, and judging in one body. Isn't that a dangerous concentration of power? How is it checked?"**

It is a deliberate concentration — SEBI is **quasi-legislative** (writes binding regulations), **quasi-executive** (registers, inspects, investigates, can attach property and accounts), and **quasi-judicial** (adjudicates and penalises). That concentration is *necessary* because markets move too fast for the ordinary three-branch separation; a regulator must be able to freeze an ongoing fraud in hours, not after years of litigation. But it is checked by **appeal**: any SEBI order can be challenged before the **Securities Appellate Tribunal (SAT)**, and from SAT on questions of law to the **Supreme Court**. This appeal layer keeps the referee itself honest, so the speed does not come at the cost of accountability.

**C5. "How does India's insider-trading regime compare with the US approach?"**

The conduct targeted is identical; the legal architecture differs. India **codified** insider trading into explicit rules — the **PIT Regulations, 2015** — with clear definitions of insider, UPSI, and prohibited acts, plus structural safeguards like trading windows, a structured digital database of who received UPSI, and pre-disclosed trading plans. The US relies on **common law** built by courts interpreting the general anti-fraud provision **Rule 10b-5**, through the **classical theory** (insider breaches a duty to his own shareholders) and the **misappropriation theory** (an outsider breaches a duty to the *source* of the information, as in *O'Hagan*). India's codified route gives clearer, more predictable definitions; the US case-law route is more flexible but less certain. The US also pays large **Dodd-Frank whistle-blower bounties** (10–30% of sanctions).

**C6. "A broker of mine went bankrupt. Does SEBI's Investor Protection Fund make me whole?"**

Only for **broker failure**, not for market losses — and that is the single most misunderstood point. The exchange's **Investor Protection Fund (IPF)** compensates you, up to a specified cap, when the broker is declared a **defaulting member** and cannot return the money or securities he was holding for you — it is the market's version of deposit insurance. It does **not** cover a fall in the value of your investments or a bad stock pick; those risks are yours. The US equivalent, **SIPC**, is identical in spirit: up to $500,000 on broker insolvency, explicitly *not* protection against investment losses. Prevention comes first, though — segregation of client money and mandatory upstreaming are designed so the broker never has your cash to lose.

**C7. "Walk me through what happens, end to end, when SEBI catches a manipulation ring."**

First, **detection**: exchanges act as front-line SROs running real-time surveillance that flags abnormal price/volume patterns, which they escalate to SEBI. Second, **investigation**: SEBI uses civil-court-like powers to summon persons and records, and since 2014 can conduct search-and-seizure, attach bank accounts and property, and seek call-data records to prove the collusion. Third, **interim action**: to stop an ongoing fraud, SEBI passes an **ex-parte cease-and-desist order** freezing the operators before final adjudication. Fourth, **adjudication and penalty**: after a hearing it can impose monetary penalties (up to ₹25 crore or three times the illegal gains, whichever is higher), order **disgorgement** of wrongful gains, and **debar** the participants. Finally, **appeal**: the accused may challenge the order at **SAT**. Alternatively the matter may close via a **consent order** — settlement by payment without admitting guilt.

## Section D — Multiple-Choice Questions (with reasoning)

**D1. The dominant method of modern securities regulation is:**
A. Merit review — the regulator judges whether each investment is sound
B. Mandatory disclosure — the regulator forces truthful information and punishes lies
C. Price controls on securities
D. Government ownership of exchanges

**Answer: B.** Modern regulation is built on **mandatory disclosure** — "sunlight is the best of disinfectants" — not on the state judging investment merit. A is precisely what SEBI/SEC do **not** do; every prospectus disclaims any regulatory endorsement of merit. C and D are not features of disclosure-based systems.

**D2. Insider trading is illegal specifically when a person trades:**
A. While being a director or employee of the company
B. While in possession of Unpublished Price Sensitive Information (UPSI)
C. In large quantities
D. Without paying brokerage

**Answer: B.** The crime is trading **while in possession of UPSI** — the informational unfairness — not the trader's identity. Insiders trade legally all the time (disclosed, in trading windows, via pre-cleared plans), so A is wrong; the defining element is the UPSI. Size (C) and brokerage (D) are irrelevant.

**D3. Which pair correctly matches the abuse to what it corrupts?**
A. Insider trading — corrupts the price itself
B. Manipulation — corrupts who has information
C. Manipulation — creates a false or misleading price/activity
D. Front-running — trades on the company's UPSI

**Answer: C.** Manipulation *creates false* prices or activity — it corrupts the price itself. A and B are reversed: insider trading corrupts *who knows* (exploiting true private info), while manipulation corrupts the *price*. D is wrong because front-running abuses a *client's pending order*, not company UPSI.

**D4. In India, an appeal against a SEBI order goes first to:**
A. The High Court
B. The Supreme Court directly
C. The Securities Appellate Tribunal (SAT)
D. The Reserve Bank of India

**Answer: C.** SEBI orders are appealed to the **Securities Appellate Tribunal (SAT)**, and only from SAT, on questions of law, to the **Supreme Court** — so B is premature and A is not the route. The RBI (D) is a co-regulator of the financial system, not an appeal body for SEBI. SAT is the check on SEBI's combined powers.

**D5. India's four "workhorse" SEBI regulation sets are:**
A. ICDR, LODR, PIT, PFUTP
B. SCRA, RBI Act, FEMA, PMLA
C. 10-K, 10-Q, 8-K, S-1
D. NSDL, CDSL, NSE, BSE

**Answer: A.** **ICDR** (IPO/issue disclosure), **LODR** (continuous listing disclosure), **PIT** (insider trading), and **PFUTP** (fraud and manipulation) are SEBI's four core regulation sets. B lists statutes (some not even SEBI's); C lists **US** disclosure forms; D lists depositories and exchanges, not regulations.

**D6. The Investor Protection Fund (IPF) maintained by a stock exchange compensates an investor when:**
A. The stock market falls sharply
B. A stock the investor bought turns out to be a bad investment
C. A broker (defaulting member) becomes insolvent and cannot return client money/securities
D. A company's dividend is lower than expected

**Answer: C.** The IPF is compensation for **broker failure** — a defaulting member who cannot return client assets — the market's equivalent of deposit insurance (US: SIPC). It never covers market losses or bad investment outcomes (A, B) or disappointing corporate results (D); those risks remain the investor's.

**D7. A consent (settlement) order from SEBI means:**
A. SEBI has formally found the party guilty
B. The matter is resolved by payment without admitting or denying guilt
C. The party has been permanently debarred
D. The case has been referred to the Supreme Court

**Answer: B.** A **consent order** settles a case by payment **without any admission or finding of guilt**, saving years of litigation — a pragmatic closure, not a conviction. A is exactly what it is *not*; C (debarment) and D (Supreme Court referral) are separate outcomes that a consent settlement avoids.

**D8. Placing large orders you never intend to execute, then cancelling them to create a false impression of demand or supply, is called:**
A. Front-running
B. Insider trading
C. Spoofing (a form of manipulation)
D. Disgorgement

**Answer: C.** **Spoofing** (with layering) is manipulation of the order book — the technique behind Navinder Sarao and the 2010 Flash Crash. A is trading ahead of a client's order; B is trading on UPSI; D is the *remedy* of surrendering illegal gains, not an abuse. Spoofing is fraud on price discovery even though the orders are cancelled unfilled.
