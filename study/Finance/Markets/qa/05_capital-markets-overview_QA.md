# Q&A — Capital Markets Overview

A companion practice bank to Chapter 05. Every question is followed by a full answer.

## Section A — Concept Check (test your understanding)

**A1. What single feature separates the capital market from the money market?**
Maturity. Capital markets deal in long-term funds — instruments with a life of **more than one year**, often 5, 10, or 30 years, and for equity, perpetual. Money markets deal in instruments of **under one year**. The consequence flows from this: capital markets fund **fixed capital** (plants, infrastructure, R&D, acquisitions) that pays back slowly, while money markets fund **working capital and liquidity**. Everything else — higher risk, share/bond instruments versus T-bills/CPs/CDs — is downstream of that maturity line.

**A2. In one sentence, what is the "core idea" of the capital market?**
Channelling long-term savings into long-term productive investment through **tradeable financial instruments** — by aggregating scattered small savings into large long-horizon capital (size and maturity transformation), securitising claims so they become transferable, and continuously discovering prices that discipline capital allocation.

**A3. Explain "liquidity without recall." Why is it called the genius of capital markets?**
The claim is embodied in a **standardised, transferable security**. The issuer keeps the money permanently (equity) or until maturity (debt) — the investor cannot demand it back. Yet the investor is not trapped: she achieves liquidity by **selling the security to another investor** in the secondary market. So the firm enjoys committed capital while the saver enjoys the option to exit. Reconciling these two opposite needs is the innovation that makes large-scale, long-term finance possible.

**A4. Name the two "great halves" of the capital market and state what flows in each.**
The **primary market**, where securities are created and sold for the first time and **fresh capital actually flows from savers to the issuer**; and the **secondary market**, where already-issued securities are traded among investors and **no new money reaches the issuer** — ownership simply changes hands.

**A5. Distinguish equity from debt on four dimensions.**
(1) *Relationship:* equity holder is an owner; debt holder is a lender. (2) *Return:* equity is residual and uncapped but risky; debt is fixed/capped and contractual. (3) *Seniority:* debt ranks ahead of equity in liquidation. (4) *Tenure and tax:* equity is perpetual with no tax shield; debt is dated and its interest is tax-deductible.

**A6. Why is the secondary market essential to the primary market — even though it gives the issuer no money?**
Because investors will only buy a *new* issue if they believe they can *sell it later*; secondary-market liquidity is the standing promise that makes subscription rational. It also **prices** the primary market — a firm's existing share value benchmarks what it can charge in a follow-on. No exchange, no IPO. The two are symbiotic: primary creates the claim, secondary makes it liquid and priced, and that liquidity loops back to enable more issuance.

**A7. What is book-building and why is it superior to a fixed-price issue for price discovery?**
In a fixed-price issue the price is set in advance by the issuer and its bankers. In **book-building**, a price *band* is announced, investors submit bids across it, and the final "cut-off" price is derived from the actual pattern of demand. It is genuine **price discovery at the point of issue** rather than a guess, so it reduces the risk of grossly mispricing the issue.

**A8. What does T+1 settlement mean, and why does it matter?**
A trade settles — securities delivered, cash paid — **one business day after** the trade date. It matters because faster settlement reduces counterparty and market risk (less time for a party to default or for prices to swing before the exchange of value) and frees up capital sooner. India's move to T+1 across equities is among the fastest cycles in the world.

**A9. What role does a clearing corporation play, and what is "novation"?**
It guarantees settlement. Through **novation** it interposes itself as **counterparty to both sides** of every trade — buyer to every seller and seller to every buyer. So neither side bears the other's default risk; they face the highly creditworthy, margined clearing corporation instead. This guarantee lets strangers trade anonymously with confidence.

**A10. SEBI has a three-fold mandate. What is it, and what historical event gave it statutory teeth?**
To **protect investors, develop the market, and regulate it**. Established in 1988, it gained statutory powers in **1992** after the **Harshad Mehta securities scam**. It vets offer documents, frames disclosure norms, licenses intermediaries, and polices insider trading and fraud.

## Section B — Applied / Scenario Questions (full answers)

**B1. A profitable, cash-generating company with a rock-solid credit rating needs ₹2,000 crore for a new plant. It has no wish to dilute ownership and pays a high tax rate. Which instrument should it lean toward, and why?**
**Debt** (bonds/debentures). Three reasons align: strong credit lets it borrow cheaply; interest is **tax-deductible**, so a high tax rate makes the after-tax cost of debt especially attractive (the tax shield); and debt does **not dilute ownership**. Because it earns more than the interest rate, the surplus accrues to shareholders via **financial leverage**. The trade-off — a fixed obligation payable in good times and bad — is manageable given stable cash flows.

**B2. A loss-making, high-growth startup needs permanent capital and cannot afford fixed interest outflows. What should it issue, and what real example mirrors this?**
**Equity.** It needs *permanent* capital it never has to repay and cannot bear a fixed interest burden while loss-making — exactly what equity provides, at the cost of sharing ownership and future profits. This mirrors **Zomato's 2021 IPO**, where a loss-making food-delivery firm raised ₹9,000 crore of fresh equity precisely because equity imposes no interest burden and never has to be repaid.

**B3. In Zomato's IPO, part of the deal was a "fresh issue" and part was an "Offer for Sale." An investor asks: does all the money I pay reach Zomato? Answer precisely.**
No. Only the **fresh issue** portion reaches Zomato's bank account — primary-market capital funding growth. The **Offer for Sale (OFS)** portion goes to the **early investors selling their existing shares**, not the company. So one IPO can mix issuer-cash (fresh issue) and seller-cash (OFS) — the common trap being that buying shares only funds the company when they are newly issued.

**B4. You buy 100 shares of a listed company on the NSE today. Trace where your money goes and what the company receives.**
Your money goes to the **previous shareholder** who sold you the shares — via the broker and clearing system — not to the company. This is a **secondary-market** transaction: ownership changes hands, no new capital is created. The company receives **nothing directly**. It benefits only indirectly, because a healthy, liquid, well-priced share supports its ability to raise capital cheaply in any *future* issue.

**B5. The 10-year G-Sec yields 7.1%. A company wants to issue a 10-year bond. How will the market price its coupon, and what determines the spread?**
Priced as **"G-Sec yield + a spread"** — roughly 7.1% plus a margin for the extra risk over the sovereign. The spread widens with **lower credit quality** (weaker CRISIL/ICRA/CARE rating), weaker security/covenants, and lower liquidity; it narrows for strong, highly-rated, liquid issuers. The G-Sec is the **benchmark risk-free rate** because sovereign default risk is near zero, so it anchors the pricing of all long-term credit.

**B6. Foreign Portfolio Investors suddenly pull large sums out of Indian equities. Trace two knock-on effects beyond falling share prices.**
(1) **Currency:** to repatriate, FPIs sell rupees to buy dollars, so heavy outflows **depreciate the rupee** — linking capital markets to forex. (2) **Primary market:** falling valuations and risk-off sentiment raise the cost of equity and **freeze or shrink new issuance** (IPOs get postponed), since issuers cannot get attractive prices. The episode shows how tightly secondary, forex, and primary issuance are coupled.

**B7. Two global giants make opposite capital-structure choices: Apple repeatedly issues bonds while Saudi Aramco floated a giant IPO. Explain each choice.**
**Apple** raised **debt** despite huge cash piles because its top-tier credit let it borrow at very low coupons, **interest is tax-deductible**, and it **avoided diluting shareholders**. **Saudi Aramco's 2019 IPO** sold a sliver of **equity** on the Tadawul exchange to raise permanent capital and diversify the state's assets — a pure ownership sale with no repayment obligation. Same decision (debt-vs-equity), opposite answers driven by each issuer's tax position, credit, dilution appetite, and strategic goal.

**B8. A rights issue and a bonus issue both give shares to existing shareholders. How do they differ in what the company receives?**
A **rights issue** offers *new* shares to existing holders pro-rata, who **pay for them** — so the company **raises fresh capital**, while shareholders' proportional ownership is protected. A **bonus issue** gives **free** shares out of accumulated reserves; **no new capital comes in** — it merely capitalises reserves and increases the share count. So rights = money in; bonus = accounting reshuffle with no cash raised.

## Section C — Interview-Style Questions (model answers)

**C1. "Walk me through what happens from a company deciding to raise equity to its shares trading on an exchange."**
The company makes the **capital-structure decision** and chooses equity for permanent, interest-free capital. It appoints **merchant bankers** to structure and underwrite the issue, who draft an offer document (the DRHP); **SEBI reviews** it for disclosure. The issue is marketed via **book-building** — a price band is announced, investors bid, and the **cut-off price is discovered** from demand. Shares are **allotted** to investors' **demat accounts** at NSDL/CDSL. From listing day they trade on the **NSE/BSE**, with **clearing corporations** guaranteeing settlement on a **T+1** cycle — completing the journey from boardroom decision to a live, priced security.

**C2. "Is the stock market the same as the capital market? Impress me with the distinction."**
No — the stock (equity) market is only *half*. The other half is the **debt capital market**: corporate bonds, debentures, and government securities — globally **larger by value** than equity. A capital market is defined by **maturity (>1 year)**, not by whether the claim is ownership or lending. A complete answer names both segments — equity and debt — and both venues — primary and secondary.

**C3. "For whom is debt risky, and for whom is equity risky?"**
It cuts opposite ways. For the **investor**, **equity** is riskier: a residual claim, last in line in liquidation, with no guaranteed return. For the **company (issuer)**, **debt** is riskier: fixed interest and principal must be paid in good times and bad, and excessive leverage can push the firm into **insolvency**. The reflex is always to ask, "risky *for whom*?"

**C4. "Why do we even need a secondary market if it never funds companies?"**
Because its liquidity is what makes the primary market possible: investors subscribe to a new issue only because they trust they can **sell later** — remove that exit and issuance collapses. It also performs **continuous price discovery**, which benchmarks the price a firm can command in future issues, **disciplines management** (a falling share price punishes poor performance and invites takeovers), and steers economy-wide capital toward productive uses. It funds no company directly yet is the load-bearing wall of the whole system.

**C5. "Difference between the primary and secondary markets in one crisp answer, then the killer insight?"**
Primary: securities are **created and issued for the first time** and **capital reaches the issuer**. Secondary: **existing securities trade among investors**, providing **liquidity and price discovery**, with **no money to the issuer**. Killer insight: they are **symbiotic, not separate** — secondary liquidity is what makes anyone willing to buy in the primary, and secondary prices set the terms for future issuance. Primary creates; secondary makes liquid and priced; the loop feeds itself.

**C6. "Why do economists link deep capital markets to faster economic growth?"**
Because they improve both the **quantity and quality** of investment: they **mobilise** idle savings, **allocate** capital efficiently via price signals, enable **capital formation**, provide **liquidity** so savers will commit long-term, **share risk** across many investors, and **discipline management** through disclosure and share-price feedback. Net effect: the **cost of capital falls** and the pool of financeable projects widens, so more good projects get funded — compounding into faster, more stable growth.

**C7. "What is capital structure, and why do you call it a central question of corporate finance?"**
Capital structure is a firm's **mix of debt and equity**. It is central because the mix determines the **cost of capital**, the **risk of insolvency**, the **tax bill** (interest is deductible), **control dilution**, and the **financial leverage** amplifying shareholder returns. Every financing decision trades off the tax shield and non-dilution of debt against its fixed-obligation and insolvency risk — which is why choosing the debt-equity mix is one of the defining problems of corporate finance.

## Section D — Multiple-Choice Questions (with reasoning)

**D1. Which transaction routes money directly to the issuing company?**
A. Buying listed shares on the NSE
B. Subscribing to the fresh-issue portion of an IPO
C. Buying shares sold under an Offer for Sale
D. Buying a bond from another investor in the secondary market
**Answer: B.** Only a **primary-market fresh issue** channels capital to the issuer. A and D are secondary-market trades (money to the other investor); an OFS (C) pays the **selling shareholder**, not the company.

**D2. The defining characteristic of a capital-market instrument is:**
A. It is always equity
B. It has a maturity of more than one year
C. It is always traded on a stock exchange
D. It is issued only by companies
**Answer: B.** The dividing line from the money market is **maturity (>1 year)**. Capital-market instruments include both equity and debt (so A is wrong), some trade over-the-counter rather than on exchanges (C wrong), and issuers include governments and PSUs, not just companies (D wrong).

**D3. In a clearing corporation's process of "novation," the clearing corporation:**
A. Cancels trades that fail to settle
B. Becomes the counterparty to both the buyer and the seller
C. Holds securities in dematerialised form
D. Sets the IPO price band
**Answer: B.** Novation interposes the clearing corporation as **counterparty to both sides**, guaranteeing settlement and removing counterparty risk. Holding demat securities (C) is a **depository's** job; the price band (D) is set in book-building.

**D4. Which statement about equity versus debt is correct?**
A. Debt holders vote; equity holders do not
B. Interest on debt is tax-deductible; dividends on equity are not
C. Equity ranks ahead of debt in liquidation
D. Debt is perpetual; equity is dated
**Answer: B.** Interest is a tax-deductible expense (the tax shield); dividends are not. Equity holders vote, not debt holders (A wrong); **debt ranks ahead** of equity in liquidation (C wrong); **equity is perpetual**, debt is dated (D wrong).

**D5. An Offer for Sale (OFS) in an IPO means:**
A. The company issues brand-new shares and keeps the cash
B. Existing shareholders sell their stakes and receive the cash themselves
C. Shares are given free from reserves
D. Bonds are converted into equity
**Answer: B.** In an OFS, **existing shareholders** (e.g., promoters or early investors) sell, and the proceeds go to **them**, not the company. New-share issuance keeping the cash is a fresh issue (A); free shares from reserves is a bonus issue (C); D describes convertible debentures.

**D6. Book-building is best described as:**
A. Setting the issue price in advance of any bids
B. Discovering the issue price from investor demand within a price band
C. Guaranteeing to buy the unsold portion of an issue
D. Recording securities in electronic form
**Answer: B.** Book-building announces a **band** and derives the cut-off price from actual bids — demand-driven price discovery. A is a fixed-price issue; C is **underwriting**; D is **dematerialisation**.

**D7. Which pair correctly matches institution to role in India?**
A. NSDL — stock exchange
B. SEBI — depository
C. NSE Clearing — settlement guarantee
D. CRISIL — regulator
**Answer: C.** NSE Clearing guarantees **settlement**. NSDL is a **depository**, not an exchange (A wrong); SEBI is the **regulator**, not a depository (B wrong); CRISIL is a **credit rating agency**, not a regulator (D wrong).

**D8. A large IPO "pops" 65% on listing day. From the issuing company's perspective, this most likely indicates:**
A. Unambiguous success — the company gained the most money
B. The issue may have been underpriced, leaving money on the table
C. The company received the higher listing-day price
D. The bond market rallied
**Answer: B.** A big pop suggests the shares were sold **too cheaply** in the IPO, so the company **could have raised more** for the same dilution. The company receives only the **issue price**, not the higher secondary price (C wrong), so "success" is a matter of perspective (A overstated); the pop is an equity event, unrelated to bonds (D wrong).

**D9. Preference shares are best described as:**
A. Pure equity with full voting rights
B. Pure debt secured on assets
C. A hybrid — priority fixed dividend, usually no vote, ranking between debt and equity
D. Government securities
**Answer: C.** Preference shares are a **hybrid**: they carry a priority fixed dividend, usually no vote, and rank **above equity but below debt** — behaving closer to debt. They are neither pure equity (A) nor pure debt (B) nor sovereign paper (D).

**D10. The yield on the 10-year G-Sec is important because it:**
A. Sets the maximum price of every share
B. Serves as the benchmark risk-free rate for pricing other long-term debt
C. Is the interest rate banks charge on working-capital loans
D. Determines the IPO cut-off price
**Answer: B.** The sovereign G-Sec yield is the near-risk-free **benchmark**; corporate bonds are priced as "G-Sec yield + spread." It does not cap share prices (A), is a long-term rate rather than a working-capital (money-market) rate (C), and does not set IPO cut-offs, which come from book-building (D).
