# Q&A — Demand, Supply and Market Equilibrium

Work each question before reading the answer.

## Section A — Concept Check

**A1. State the Law of Demand and the single most important qualifier attached to it.**
The Law of Demand says that, *ceteris paribus*, as the price of a good rises the quantity demanded falls, and as price falls quantity demanded rises — so the demand curve slopes downward. The critical qualifier is *ceteris paribus* ("other things equal"). The law describes the relationship between a good's **own price** and quantity demanded only when every other determinant (income, related-good prices, tastes, expectations, number of buyers) is held constant. Drop that qualifier and the relationship no longer holds, because those other factors shift the whole curve.

**A2. Distinguish "quantity demanded" from "demand."**
*Quantity demanded* is a single number — the amount buyers want at one specific price. *Demand* is the entire relationship (the whole curve/schedule) across all possible prices. A change in the good's own price changes *quantity demanded* (you move along the curve). A change in a non-price determinant changes *demand* (the whole curve shifts).

**A3. Why does the demand curve slope downward? Give the three underlying reasons.**
(1) **Substitution effect** — as a good gets dearer relative to alternatives, buyers switch to now-cheaper substitutes. (2) **Income effect** — a higher price makes buyers effectively poorer, so they buy less of most goods. (3) **Diminishing marginal utility** — each extra unit yields less additional satisfaction, so buyers take more units only at a lower price. The third is the deepest reason.

**A4. Why does the supply curve slope upward?**
Two reasons. (a) Higher prices make production more profitable, drawing existing firms to produce more and new firms to enter. (b) Producing extra units usually raises marginal cost (capacity gets strained), and producers will only incur that higher marginal cost if the price justifies it. So higher price → higher quantity supplied.

**A5. What causes a movement ALONG a curve versus a SHIFT OF a curve?**
A change in the good's **own price** causes a movement *along* the curve (a change in quantity demanded/supplied). A change in **any other determinant** causes a *shift* of the whole curve (a change in demand/supply). The decision rule: ask "what changed?" — if it is the own price, you move along; if anything else, the curve shifts.

**A6. List the determinants of demand (the factors that shift it).**
Income (normal vs inferior goods), prices of related goods (substitutes and complements), tastes and preferences, expectations of future prices, and the number of buyers.

**A7. List the determinants of supply.**
Input/factor prices, technology, prices of related goods in production, expectations, number of sellers, and government policy (taxes, subsidies, regulation).

**A8. Define market equilibrium and the term "market clears."**
Equilibrium is the price–quantity pair (P*, Q*) at which quantity demanded exactly equals quantity supplied, so there is no residual pressure for price to change. The market "clears" because every buyer willing to pay P* finds a seller and every seller willing to sell at P* finds a buyer — there is no excess demand or supply.

**A9. What is the difference between a surplus and a shortage, and how does each self-correct?**
A **surplus** (excess supply) occurs when price is *above* P*: sellers offer more than buyers want, inventories pile up, and sellers cut price toward P*. A **shortage** (excess demand) occurs when price is *below* P*: buyers want more than is offered, goods sell out, and buyers bid price up toward P*. In both cases the price itself moves to erase the imbalance — the price mechanism.

**A10. Distinguish a price ceiling from a price floor and give the equilibrium consequence of each when binding.**
A **price ceiling** is a legal maximum price; when set *below* equilibrium it binds and causes a persistent **shortage** (e.g., rent control). A **price floor** is a legal minimum price; when set *above* equilibrium it binds and causes a persistent **surplus** (e.g., minimum wage → unemployment; agricultural support price → grain gluts). A ceiling above equilibrium or a floor below it is non-binding and has no effect.

**A11. Name three exceptions to the law of demand.**
(1) **Giffen goods** — extreme inferior staples where the income effect overwhelms the substitution effect, so a price rise raises quantity demanded. (2) **Veblen/conspicuous goods** — luxuries bought *because* they are expensive (status signalling). (3) **Speculative assets** — a rising price attracts momentum buyers expecting further rises. The third is the finance-relevant exception and the seed of bubbles.

**A12. Distinguish normal, inferior, substitute, and complement goods.**
A **normal good** sees demand rise with income; an **inferior good** sees demand fall as income rises (buyers upgrade away). **Substitutes** are used instead of each other (tea/coffee): a rise in one's price raises demand for the other. **Complements** are used together (cars/petrol): a rise in one's price lowers demand for the other.

## Section B — Applied / Numerical Problems

**B1. Given demand Qd = 200 − 4P and supply Qs = 20 + 2P, find equilibrium price and quantity.**
Set Qd = Qs: 200 − 4P = 20 + 2P → 180 = 6P → **P\* = 30**. Substitute back: Qd = 200 − 4(30) = 200 − 120 = **80**. Check with supply: Qs = 20 + 2(30) = 80. ✔ So P\* = 30 and Q\* = 80.

**B2. Using the same curves, the government sets a price ceiling of ₹20. Find the resulting shortage.**
At P = 20: Qd = 200 − 4(20) = 200 − 80 = 120; Qs = 20 + 2(20) = 60. Since the ceiling (20) is below equilibrium (30) it binds. **Shortage = Qd − Qs = 120 − 60 = 60 units.** Buyers want 120 but only 60 is offered, and the price is legally barred from rising to clear it — a persistent shortage.

**B3. Demand shifts: incomes rise so the new demand is Qd = 260 − 4P (supply unchanged at 20 + 2P). Find the new equilibrium and describe the movement.**
260 − 4P = 20 + 2P → 240 = 6P → **P\* = 40**, and Q\* = 20 + 2(40) = **100**. Compared with B1 (P\* = 30, Q\* = 80), both price and quantity rose — the textbook result of a rightward demand shift (D increases → P↑, Q↑). The good is a normal good (demand rose with income).

**B4. From a linear demand curve Qd = a − bP, identify what the intercept and slope terms represent, and state the general equilibrium-price formula against supply Qs = c + dP.**
`a` is the autonomous (choke-related) level of demand and `b` is the sensitivity of quantity to price; `c` and `d` are the analogous supply intercept and slope. Setting a − bP = c + dP gives **P\* = (a − c) / (b + d)**. Equilibrium price depends on the intercepts (autonomous levels) and the slopes (price sensitivities); changing any one of them is what a "shift" does algebraically.

**B5. A bond pays a fixed ₹100 coupon. When its market price rises from ₹1,000 to ₹1,250, what happens to its current yield, and how does this illustrate the demand–supply logic of rates?**
Current yield = coupon ÷ price. Before: 100 / 1,000 = **10%**. After: 100 / 1,250 = **8%**. The price rose so the yield fell — price and yield move inversely. In demand-supply terms, stronger **demand for bonds** (e.g., a central bank buying via QE) shifts bond demand right → prices up → yields down. Every interest-rate story is a bond demand-supply story.

**B6. Both curves shift: demand rises to Qd = 260 − 4P and supply rises to Qs = 50 + 2P. Find the new equilibrium and compare with B1.**
260 − 4P = 50 + 2P → 210 = 6P → **P\* = 35**, Q\* = 50 + 2(35) = **120**. Versus B1 (30, 80): quantity clearly rose (both shifts push Q up), but price rose only modestly. Had the supply shift been larger, price could have *fallen*. This is the "both curves shift → one variable is ambiguous" case: with both increasing, Q\* definitely rises but P\* is ambiguous and depends on the relative sizes of the two shifts.

**B7. A per-unit tax of ₹15 is imposed on sellers, so supply becomes Qs = 20 + 2(P − 15). With demand Qd = 200 − 4P, find the new equilibrium price paid by buyers.**
Qs = 20 + 2P − 30 = 2P − 10. Set equal to demand: 200 − 4P = 2P − 10 → 210 = 6P → **P\* = 35** (price buyers pay). Sellers net 35 − 15 = ₹20. Quantity Q\* = 200 − 4(35) = 60. Compared with the no-tax equilibrium (P = 30, Q = 80), the buyer price rose by ₹5 and seller price fell by ₹10 — the ₹15 tax is split, and quantity traded fell. The tax shifted supply up/left.

**B8. Interpret a 2021-style chip market: demand surges and new fabs add capacity, yet prices spiked. Reconcile with theory.**
Both demand (right) and supply (right) shifted. Quantity traded rose unambiguously. Price was ambiguous: in 2021 the demand shift *outran* the supply expansion, so excess demand at the old price pushed prices **up** and created shortages. When capacity later caught up (supply shift dominating), prices fell. Nothing contradicts the model — the ambiguous-price case simply resolved in demand's favour first.

## Section C — Interview-Style Questions

**C1. "Walk me through what happens to a stock's price when a company beats earnings."**
An earnings beat improves investors' expectations of future cash flows, so more participants want to hold the stock and fewer want to sell at the old price. The **demand curve for the stock shifts right** (and sell-side supply may pull back). In the limit-order book, buy orders overwhelm resting sell orders at the prior price, creating temporary excess demand that lifts the clearing price until a new equilibrium forms at a higher level. The price move *is* a re-solved equilibrium. I'd add that the *magnitude* depends on how surprising the beat was relative to what was already priced in — direction from demand-supply, size from the surprise and liquidity.

**C2. "Bond prices and yields — explain the relationship and what QE does to it."**
A bond's yield is its return relative to its price for a fixed set of coupons, so price and yield move inversely: pay more for the same cash flows and your return falls. When a central bank runs **quantitative easing**, it buys bonds in the open market, shifting **bond demand right**. Higher demand raises bond prices and therefore lowers yields — the central bank's tool for pushing long rates down. Conversely, heavy government issuance shifts bond **supply right**, depressing prices and lifting yields. So rate moves are always a bond demand-supply picture.

**C3. "Why does a currency appreciate when a country raises interest rates?"**
Higher domestic interest rates make that country's assets more attractive to global investors. To buy those assets they must first buy the local currency, so **demand for the currency shifts right**, and its price (the exchange rate) rises — appreciation. An exchange rate is simply the equilibrium price of one currency in terms of another; capital flows chasing yield are demand shifts. Central-bank intervention works the same way — it directly shifts the supply of, or demand for, the home currency.

**C4. "Can a demand curve slope upward? When would that matter in markets?"**
For ordinary goods, no — higher price lowers quantity demanded. But in three cases demand can slope up: Giffen goods, Veblen/status goods, and — most relevant to finance — **speculative assets**. When a price rises, momentum buyers may raise their *expected* future price and pile in, so higher price attracts *more* demand. This self-reinforcing loop explains bubbles (upward-sloping demand shifts feeding on themselves) and, in reverse, crashes. Fundamentals eventually reassert intrinsic value, but the path overshoots. The lesson: don't mechanically apply goods-market intuition to a rallying asset.

**C5. "A government caps interest rates on loans below the market level. What happens?"**
That's a price ceiling on credit. Below the market-clearing rate it binds, so the quantity of loanable funds demanded exceeds the quantity supplied — a **credit shortage**. Lenders ration credit (favouring the safest borrowers), informal/black-market lending appears, and marginal borrowers are shut out. The scarcity isn't cured; the price mechanism is just switched off. This is the same logic as rent control or a currency peg — binding price controls create persistent imbalances and eventually break.

**C6. "Direction versus magnitude — why do you keep separating them?"**
The demand-supply model tells me the *direction* of a price/quantity move: which curve a shock hits and which way it slides the equilibrium. But it doesn't tell me *how big* the move is — that depends on **elasticity**, the steepness of the curves. A supply shock on inelastic demand (like oil or onions) causes a large price jump; the same shock on elastic demand barely moves price. A strong interview answer always pairs the two: "demand shifts right, so price rises (direction), and because demand is fairly inelastic the rise is large (magnitude)."

**C7. "OPEC announces a production cut. Trace the effect on oil prices and on airline stocks."**
The cut shifts the **oil supply curve left**; at the old price there's excess demand, so oil rises to a higher equilibrium — and because oil demand is inelastic short-term, the rise is sharp. Jet fuel is a major airline input, so higher oil raises airline costs, cutting expected profits and shifting **demand for airline stocks left**, pushing those shares down. One supply shock propagates through two linked markets via the same curve logic.

## Section D — Multiple Choice Questions (with reasoning)

**D1. A fall in the price of coffee, with everything else constant, will most directly cause:**
(a) a rightward shift of the coffee demand curve
(b) a movement down along the coffee demand curve
(c) a leftward shift of the coffee supply curve
(d) a rightward shift of the tea demand curve
**Answer: (b).** A change in the good's *own* price is a movement *along* its demand curve, not a shift. (d) would be wrong direction anyway — cheaper coffee lowers tea demand (substitute), shifting it left, not right.

**D2. Which of the following shifts the demand curve for cars to the right?**
(a) a rise in the price of cars
(b) a fall in the price of petrol (a complement)
(c) an increase in car excise duty
(d) a fall in household incomes (cars normal)
**Answer: (b).** Cheaper petrol makes car ownership cheaper overall, raising car demand — a rightward shift. (a) is a movement along; (c) raises car prices/costs (supply-side, or a movement along demand); (d) shifts demand *left* for a normal good.

**D3. If a binding price floor is imposed in a competitive market, the result is:**
(a) a shortage
(b) a surplus
(c) equilibrium is unchanged
(d) the demand curve shifts left
**Answer: (b).** A binding price floor sits *above* equilibrium, so quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded — a persistent surplus (e.g., unsold grain, unemployment). It does not shift any curve; it holds price off P*.

**D4. Demand and supply both increase (shift right) at the same time. Which is certain?**
(a) Price rises
(b) Price falls
(c) Quantity rises
(d) Quantity falls
**Answer: (c).** When both curves shift right, equilibrium quantity unambiguously rises, but the price change is ambiguous — it depends on the relative sizes of the two shifts. Hence (a) and (b) are both uncertain.

**D5. For an inferior good, a rise in consumer income will:**
(a) shift its demand curve right
(b) shift its demand curve left
(c) move buyers up along the demand curve
(d) leave demand unchanged
**Answer: (b).** By definition, demand for an inferior good falls as income rises (buyers switch to superior alternatives), so its demand curve shifts left. Income is a non-price determinant, so it shifts rather than moves along.

**D6. A bond's market price falls. Its yield therefore:**
(a) rises
(b) falls
(c) stays the same
(d) cannot be determined
**Answer: (a).** Price and yield move inversely for a fixed-coupon bond — a lower price means the fixed coupons represent a higher return. A falling price (e.g., from heavy issuance shifting bond supply right) raises the yield.

**D7. Which situation best illustrates the speculative exception to the law of demand?**
(a) Onion prices rise after a poor monsoon and people buy fewer onions
(b) A stock rallies and momentum traders buy more as the price climbs
(c) Petrol demand falls when petrol prices rise
(d) People buy more instant noodles when their income falls
**Answer: (b).** Expectation-driven momentum buying means a *higher* price attracts *more* demand — an upward-sloping demand range, the seed of bubbles. (a) and (c) are normal downward-sloping demand; (d) is the inferior-good income effect, not the demand-law exception.

**D8. In the market model, equilibrium is best described as the price at which:**
(a) buyers pay the fairest possible price
(b) quantity demanded equals quantity supplied
(c) producers maximise total revenue
(d) the government sets the clearing price
**Answer: (b).** Equilibrium is where Qd = Qs and the market clears — plans of buyers and sellers are mutually consistent. It is not a normative "fair" price (a common confusion), not a revenue-max point, and it emerges from the market, not from government.

**D9. A per-unit tax on sellers in a competitive market will, other things equal:**
(a) shift the supply curve up/left and reduce quantity traded
(b) shift the demand curve left
(c) leave the equilibrium quantity unchanged
(d) always be paid entirely by sellers
**Answer: (a).** A seller-side tax raises effective costs, shifting supply up/left; the new equilibrium has a higher buyer price and lower quantity. The burden is *split* between buyers and sellers depending on relative elasticities — never automatically all on sellers.
