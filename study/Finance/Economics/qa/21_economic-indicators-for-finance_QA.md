# Q&A — Economic Indicators for Finance

A practice bank for the Economics for Finance chapter "Economic Indicators for Finance." Work each question before reading the answer. Sections: A (concept-check), B (applied/numerical), C (interview-style), D (MCQs with reasoning).

---

## Section A — Concept Check

**A1. In one sentence, why do economic indicators move markets at all?**

Because asset prices are forecasts of future growth, inflation and rates, and an indicator reveals whether reality is tracking ahead of, behind, or in line with that forecast — when reality diverges from the priced-in expectation, prices *must* re-adjust.

**A2. What is the single deepest idea of the chapter?**

Markets price *expectations*; indicators reveal *reality*; the difference between them — the **surprise** — is what actually drives data-day price moves. The absolute level of a number matters far less at the moment of release than how it lands relative to consensus.

**A3. Why does a number that exactly meets consensus usually produce no price move?**

Because it is already "in the price." If everyone expected CPI at 5.0% and it prints 5.0%, nothing new has been learned, so there is nothing to re-price. Only the *gap* between actual and expected forces a re-pricing.

**A4. Define leading, coincident, and lagging indicators with one example each.**

- **Leading** — turns *before* the cycle; e.g. PMI new orders (also yield curve, building permits, jobless claims).
- **Coincident** — turns *with* the cycle; e.g. IIP (also GDP near-real-time, retail sales, employment level).
- **Lagging** — turns *after* the cycle; e.g. the unemployment rate (also core CPI, corporate profits).

**A5. State the professional rule that ties the lead-lag framework together.**

**Lead for entry, lag for confirmation.** Leading signals get you positioned early but are noisy and give false alarms; you confirm with coincident and lagging data before committing fully. Trading purely off lagging data means you are always late.

**A6. What is a diffusion index, and what does the "50 line" mean for PMI?**

A diffusion index measures the *breadth* of change across respondents rather than magnitude. For the PMI, a reading **above 50 signals expansion, below 50 contraction** — it captures the direction and momentum of activity rather than its level.

**A7. Why do central banks care more about core inflation than headline inflation?**

Core inflation strips out volatile food and fuel to reveal the "sticky," demand-driven pressure that policy can actually influence with rates. A food-driven headline spike may be transitory supply noise the bank looks through; core reflects persistent pressure that requires a policy response.

**A8. Distinguish CPI from WPI.**

**CPI** measures a *consumer* basket at retail level — in India roughly 46% food and beverages, and it is the RBI's official target. **WPI** measures *wholesale/producer* prices — no services, heavy manufacturing and fuel weight. WPI leads CPI at the margin (input costs feed consumer prices) and reads producer pricing power; the two can diverge sharply.

**A9. What is a base effect?**

A distortion in a year-on-year figure caused by an unusual value in the *year-ago* comparison period rather than by anything happening now. A very low reading a year ago can make current YoY inflation look artificially high, and vice versa — which is why professionals read both YoY and MoM.

**A10. Why is GDP described as the "master scorecard" yet a poor guide to the future?**

It is the broadest measure of growth and the denominator for ratios like debt-to-GDP, but it is *lagging* — reported roughly two months after the quarter ends and heavily revised. It tells you where the economy *was*, not where it is going; for the future you watch leading indicators.

**A11. What is a reaction function?**

The mapping from a data surprise to an asset-class response through the channels of growth, inflation and interest rates. It is why the same number is not universally "good" or "bad" — there is only a number, an asset, and the reaction function connecting them.

**A12. Why is the yield curve considered the premier leading indicator?**

Because it aggregates the market's *collective* forecast of the future rate path rather than any single data point. An inversion (short rates above long rates) has preceded every US recession since the 1960s, usually by 12–18 months, because it signals the market expects the central bank to cut rates as growth slows.

## Section B — Applied / Numerical (Full Solutions)

**B1. Consensus for US non-farm payrolls was 180,000; the actual print was 350,000. (a) Compute the surprise. (b) State the likely direction of move in 10-year Treasury yields and the dollar, with reasoning.**

*Solution.*
- (a) Surprise = actual − expected = 350,000 − 180,000 = **+170,000 (a large upside beat).**
- (b) A hot labour market implies persistent wage pressure and sticky inflation, forcing the central bank to hold rates "higher for longer." So **10-year yields rise** (bond prices fall) and the **dollar strengthens** (higher expected rates attract capital via the rate-differential channel). The level of employment was healthy either way — it was the *surprise* that moved prices.

**B2. Indian CPI prints at 5.8% against a consensus of 5.0%. The RBI's target is 4% ±2%. (a) Is the print inside the tolerance band? (b) What is the surprise, and how should G-Sec yields react?**

*Solution.*
- (a) The band is **2%–6%**. 5.8% is inside the band but near the upper edge.
- (b) Surprise = 5.8% − 5.0% = **+0.8 percentage points (an upside miss).** Even though it stays within the band, it exceeds what was priced, so the market must raise the odds of the RBI holding rates higher for longer. **G-Sec yields rise, bond prices fall.** The level relative to *consensus* drives the move, not the level relative to the band.

**B3. A PMI reads 48.5 this month, down from 51.2 last month. (a) What does the level signal? (b) What does the change add? (c) Why might a copper trader act on this before GDP is released?**

*Solution.*
- (a) 48.5 is **below 50 → contraction**; the manufacturing sector is shrinking.
- (b) The drop from 51.2 to 48.5 signals a **loss of momentum and a crossing of the expansion/contraction line** — a turning point, not just a weak level.
- (c) PMI arrives on the first business day of the following month, *weeks before* hard IIP and GDP data, and its new-orders component is forward-looking. A copper trader positions for weaker industrial demand off the PMI signal alone, gaining a timing edge over anyone waiting for GDP to confirm.

**B4. Headline CPI is 6.5% while core CPI is 4.2%, with the gap driven by a monsoon-led food spike. Should the central bank aggressively hike? Explain using the composition.**

*Solution.* The 2.3-point gap between headline (6.5%) and core (4.2%) shows the overshoot is **supply-driven food noise, not broad demand pressure.** Because core — the sticky, demand-driven component policy can influence — remains contained, the bank can reasonably treat the food spike as transitory and **hold rather than hike aggressively.** Reading only the headline would produce a wrong, over-tightening call; the *quality* of the inflation matters more than the headline over the following days.

**B5. The 10-year yield is 6.9% and the 2-year yield is 7.2%. (a) Compute the 10y–2y slope. (b) Is the curve normal or inverted? (c) What does this classically imply, and what is the key caveat?**

*Solution.*
- (a) Slope = 10y − 2y = 6.9% − 7.2% = **−0.3% (−30 bps).**
- (b) A negative slope means short rates exceed long rates — the curve is **inverted.**
- (c) Inversion classically signals **elevated recession risk over the next 12–18 months**, because the market expects the central bank to cut rates as growth slows. The caveat: it is a probability shift, not a deterministic trigger — inversion warns of risk and can occasionally give false or badly-timed signals, so it is confirmed or refuted by later coincident data.

**B6. GDP grows 7% but the composition shows most of the gain came from government spending and an inventory build, with private consumption and investment flat. Is this high-quality growth? Explain.**

*Solution.* No. GDP = Consumption + Investment + Government + Net Exports. Growth propped up by **government spending and inventory swings is lower-quality and less durable** than growth driven by private consumption and investment, which are self-sustaining. A 7% headline is impressive, but a professional discounts it because the *engine* is temporary — inventories reverse and government stimulus is finite. Composition, not just the headline number, determines how the market should read the print.

**B7. WPI is running at −1.0% (deflation) while CPI is at 5.5%. Explain how both can be true simultaneously.**

*Solution.* They measure **different baskets at different points in the supply chain.** WPI is wholesale/producer-level, heavily weighted to manufacturing and fuel and containing *no services*; falling global commodity and input prices can push it negative. CPI is consumer-level with a large food weight and a big services component; sticky services costs and food prices can keep it elevated even as wholesale goods prices fall. They are not interchangeable and can diverge sharply — WPI negative while CPI runs hot is a common real-world pattern.

**B8. You are told a strong retail-sales beat sent equities *down*, not up. Under what regime does this "good news is bad news" reaction occur, and why?**

*Solution.* It occurs in a **high-inflation, hawkish regime.** Strong data has two opposing effects on equities: it lifts *earnings* expectations (bullish) but, by raising *expected policy rates*, it lifts the discount rate and compresses valuation *multiples* (bearish). When the central bank is hawkish and inflation is the dominant worry, the discount-rate/multiple effect dominates the earnings effect, so strong data raises rate-hike fears and stocks fall despite the better earnings outlook.

## Section C — Interview-Style (Model Answers)

**C1. "Walk me through exactly what happens in the seconds and days after a major data release."**

*Model answer.* At the scheduled time the number drops and algorithms parse it in milliseconds, computing the **surprise versus consensus** — the gap between actual and the median forecast that was already priced. Prices adjust almost instantly along the reaction function: an upside inflation surprise pushes yields up, the currency stronger, equity multiples down. Then *humans* take over and dig into **composition** — was strong GDP durable private demand or one-off government spending? Was CPI hot because of volatile food or sticky core services? That quality assessment often drives the following days more than the headline did. Finally, most indicators get **revised**, so a strong first print can quietly weaken two months later — which is why I track revision trends, not just first prints.

**C2. "There is no such thing as a universally good or bad economic number. Defend that claim."**

*Model answer.* Correct — because each asset has its own reaction function. A hot jobs report is good for corporate *earnings*, so equities can like it, but it is bad for *bonds* because growth stokes inflation and rate hikes. Strong growth lifts a *currency* through higher expected rates but can hurt *gold*, which pays no yield and suffers when real yields rise. So the same release simultaneously helps one asset and hurts another. There is only a number, an asset, and the growth/inflation/rate channels connecting them. Anyone who says "the number was good" without naming the asset and the regime does not understand what moves prices.

**C3. "How would you build an economic-indicator dashboard that actually produces investable views?"**

*Model answer.* I structure it in four layers. **Layer 1 — Growth**: PMI (leading), IIP/core sector (coincident), GDP nowcast, retail sales, GST collections — *is the economy accelerating or decelerating?* **Layer 2 — Inflation**: headline and *core* CPI, WPI, wage growth, inflation expectations — *is pressure rising, and is it demand- or supply-driven?* **Layer 3 — Policy and rates**: policy rate, forward guidance, yield-curve slope, real yields, credit spreads — *what will the central bank do?* **Layer 4 — Labour and financial conditions**: payrolls/claims/unemployment (or PLFS/EPFO in India), credit growth, confidence surveys. For every indicator I track four fields — actual, consensus, trend, and revision — not just the headline. Then I synthesise a **cycle regime** — early expansion, mid-cycle, late-cycle/overheating, or slowdown/recession — read leading indicators to anticipate the *next* regime, confirm with coincident data, and set asset tilts accordingly.

**C4. "Which indicators would you weight most heavily for timing a cycle turn, and why?"**

*Model answer.* The leading set, because they turn before the economy does. Top of my list is the **yield curve** — its slope aggregates the market's collective rate forecast and has led every US recession by 12–18 months. Then **PMI new orders**, because it arrives first (first business day of the month) and is genuinely forward-looking. **Jobless claims** are my leading labour signal — a rising trend flags a turning labour market long before the lagging unemployment rate moves. **Building permits** and **credit spreads** round it out; widening spreads lead stress. I weight these for *positioning*, but I never treat them as deterministic — they are probability shifts confirmed or refuted by coincident data like GDP and IIP.

**C5. "Give me a real example where reading only the headline would have produced the wrong trade."**

*Model answer.* Through 2023–24 Indian headline CPI repeatedly spiked above the RBI's 6% upper tolerance, driven by food — tomatoes, onions, cereals — after erratic monsoons. A trader reading only the headline would have expected aggressive hikes and sold bonds hard on every spike. But **core CPI**, ex food and fuel, stayed contained and even eased, so the RBI treated the food spike as transitory supply noise and held the repo rate steady. Bond markets that dissected headline-versus-core did *not* aggressively sell off, and were right. The composition of a number often matters more than the headline.

**C6. "The US 10y–2y curve inverted deeply in 2022–23 but no immediate recession followed. What does that teach you about leading indicators?"**

*Model answer.* It teaches the central caveat: **leading indicators warn of risk, they do not guarantee outcomes or timing.** The inversion — the deepest in four decades — correctly flagged elevated recession risk and justified defensive tilts (overweight quality bonds, underweight cyclicals, raise cash). But the recession it "predicted" did not arrive on schedule. The right way to use such a signal is as a *probability shift* that adjusts positioning, confirmed or refuted by later coincident data — never as a deterministic trigger you bet the book on. Good macro is Bayesian: leading signals move the odds, coincident data updates them.

## Section D — MCQs (With Reasoning)

**D1. At the moment of a data release, the primary driver of the price move is:**
A) The absolute level of the number
B) The surprise versus consensus
C) The revision to the prior print
D) The seasonal adjustment factor

**Answer: B.** Markets price expectations, so only the gap between actual and the priced-in consensus forces a re-pricing. A 5% CPI is bullish for bonds if consensus was 5.5% and bearish if consensus was 4.5% — the level alone tells you nothing.

**D2. A PMI reading of 47 indicates:**
A) Expansion at a slowing pace
B) Contraction
C) Exactly stable activity
D) 47% capacity utilisation

**Answer: B.** PMI is a diffusion index where 50 is the dividing line; below 50 signals contraction. It is not a capacity figure (D) and not stability (C, which would be exactly 50).

**D3. Which of the following is a *leading* indicator?**
A) Unemployment rate
B) Core CPI
C) Building permits
D) Corporate profits

**Answer: C.** Building permits turn before construction activity and the cycle. The other three (unemployment rate, core CPI, corporate profits) are classic *lagging* indicators that turn after the cycle.

**D4. Central banks focus on core rather than headline inflation because core:**
A) Is always higher than headline
B) Includes food and fuel
C) Captures sticky, demand-driven pressure that policy can influence
D) Is released earlier than headline

**Answer: C.** Core strips out volatile food and fuel to reveal persistent, demand-driven inflation the central bank can actually affect with rates. It *excludes* food and fuel (so B is wrong) and is not systematically higher (A).

**D5. An inverted yield curve most reliably signals:**
A) An immediate recession this quarter
B) Elevated recession risk over roughly the next 12–18 months
C) That inflation is about to accelerate
D) That equities will rise

**Answer: B.** Inversion reflects market expectations of future rate cuts as growth slows, historically leading recessions by 12–18 months — a probability shift, not an immediate or guaranteed trigger (A).

**D6. WPI differs from CPI chiefly in that WPI:**
A) Has a heavier services weight
B) Is measured at the wholesale/producer level with no services
C) Is the RBI's official inflation target
D) Always moves in lockstep with CPI

**Answer: B.** WPI is wholesale/producer-level, manufacturing- and fuel-heavy, and contains no services. CPI (not WPI) is the RBI's target (C), and the two can diverge sharply (D).

**D7. Real GDP differs from nominal GDP in that real GDP:**
A) Includes inflation
B) Strips out inflation to measure true volume growth
C) Is always larger than nominal GDP
D) Excludes government spending

**Answer: B.** Real GDP removes price changes to measure genuine volume growth; nominal GDP (real plus inflation) drives tax revenue and corporate sales. When inflation is positive, nominal exceeds real, so real is not larger (C).

**D8. A hawkish inflation surprise would typically cause:**
A) Yields down, currency weaker, gold stronger
B) Yields up, currency stronger, equity multiples compressed
C) Yields unchanged, equities up sharply
D) Bond prices up, currency weaker

**Answer: B.** Higher expected policy rates push bond yields up (prices down), strengthen the currency via the rate differential, compress equity valuation multiples through a higher discount rate, and weigh on gold as real yields rise.

**D9. "Good news is bad news" for equities occurs when:**
A) Inflation is low and the central bank is dovish
B) Strong data raises rate-hike fears in a high-inflation, hawkish regime
C) The currency weakens
D) Bond yields fall

**Answer: B.** In a hawkish regime the discount-rate/multiple effect of stronger data dominates the earnings effect, so strong data lifts rate expectations and hurts stocks despite better earnings.

**D10. Initial jobless claims are classified as a *leading* labour indicator, whereas the unemployment rate is *lagging*, because:**
A) Claims are released annually
B) Firms cut jobs only after demand has already fallen, so the unemployment rate turns late, while a rising claims trend flags the turn early
C) Claims measure only government workers
D) The unemployment rate is forward-looking

**Answer: B.** A rising trend in weekly claims signals a turning labour market early; the unemployment rate lags because layoffs follow, not precede, falling demand.

**D11. A YoY inflation figure that looks unusually high purely because the year-ago month was abnormally low is an example of:**
A) Seasonal adjustment
B) A base effect
C) Core inflation
D) Nowcasting

**Answer: B.** A base effect is a distortion in a year-on-year figure caused by an unusual value in the comparison (base) period, not by current conditions — which is why professionals also read month-on-month.

**D12. The professional rule "lead for entry, lag for confirmation" means:**
A) Ignore leading indicators entirely
B) Use noisy leading signals to position early, then confirm with coincident/lagging data before committing fully
C) Trade only off the unemployment rate
D) Wait for GDP before any positioning

**Answer: B.** Leading indicators get you positioned ahead of the turn but give false alarms, so you confirm with coincident and lagging data. Trading purely off lagging data (D) means you are always late.
