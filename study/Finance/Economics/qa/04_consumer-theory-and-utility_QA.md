# Q&A — Consumer Theory and Utility

A companion practice bank for Chapter 04. Every question is followed by a full answer. Attempt your own answer first, then compare. The four sections build from recall (A) to calculation (B) to spoken fluency (C) to exam technique (D). The through-line to hold onto: diminishing marginal utility does almost all the work — first for goods, then for wealth.

---

## Section A — Concept Check (test your understanding)

**A1. In one sentence, what is the central problem consumer theory solves?**

How a rational agent allocates a limited income across competing wants — priced goods — so as to make themselves as well off as possible; the answer, worked out, *derives* the demand curve rather than assuming it.

**A2. Distinguish total utility from marginal utility, and state the mechanical link between them.**

Total utility (TU) is the aggregate satisfaction from consuming a given quantity; marginal utility (MU) is the change in TU from one more unit (MU_n = TU_n − TU_(n−1)). The link: TU rises while MU is positive, is at a maximum when MU = 0 (saturation), and falls when MU turns negative.

**A3. State the Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility and give the reason behind it.**

As a consumer consumes more units of a good, other things equal, the MU from each successive unit declines. The reason is psychological and physiological: wants are satiable, so each extra unit is applied to a less urgent use — the first glass of water quenches thirst, the fourth is a struggle.

**A4. Why does diminishing marginal utility make the demand curve slope downward (in the cardinal framework)?**

Price measures marginal utility in money terms; a consumer buys until MU = P. Because each extra unit is worth less (DMU), the buyer will only take more units if the price is also lower. Lower willingness to pay at higher quantities is exactly a downward-sloping demand curve.

**A5. State the equi-marginal (cardinal) equilibrium condition and give the arbitrage intuition.**

Equilibrium is where marginal utility per rupee is equal across all goods: MU_x/P_x = MU_y/P_y = … = MU of money. Intuition: if coffee gives 8 utils/₹ and tea only 5, shift a rupee from tea to coffee for a net +3 utils; keep reallocating until DMU pushes the ratios into equality, at which point no reshuffle helps.

**A6. What is an indifference curve, and what are its two most important properties?**

An indifference curve joins all bundles of two goods that give the same satisfaction. It is (1) downward-sloping — to hold utility constant, more of one good requires less of the other — and (2) convex to the origin, which is the geometric expression of a diminishing marginal rate of substitution.

**A7. Why can two indifference curves never intersect?**

Because the intersection point would lie on both curves and therefore represent two different utility levels simultaneously — a logical contradiction that violates transitivity of preferences.

**A8. Define the marginal rate of substitution and state why it diminishes.**

MRS_xy is the amount of Y a consumer will give up for one more unit of X while staying equally satisfied; it equals MU_x/MU_y, the slope of the IC. It diminishes because as you accumulate X its marginal utility falls and the Y you are surrendering becomes scarcer and more valuable — so you sacrifice less Y for each additional X.

**A9. What does the budget line show, and what is its slope?**

It shows every bundle a consumer can buy by spending their entire income at given prices: P_x·Q_x + P_y·Q_y = M. Its slope is −P_x/P_y, the market's rate for swapping Y into X — distinct from the MRS, which is the consumer's willingness to swap.

**A10. How does the budget line respond to (a) a rise in income and (b) a fall in the price of good X?**

(a) A rise in income shifts the line outward parallel to itself — relative prices, hence slope, are unchanged. (b) A fall in P_x pivots the line outward around the unchanged Y-intercept, flattening it because the X-intercept (M/P_x) moves out.

**A11. State the ordinal condition for consumer equilibrium and show it matches the cardinal one.**

Equilibrium is the tangency of the budget line with the highest attainable indifference curve, where MRS_xy = P_x/P_y. Since MRS = MU_x/MU_y, this rearranges to MU_x/P_x = MU_y/P_y — identical to the cardinal equi-marginal condition.

**A12. Name the two effects a price change is decomposed into, and give the sign of each.**

The substitution effect (X is now relatively cheaper, so buy more X) is *always* negative — price down, quantity up. The income effect (real purchasing power rises) is positive for a normal good but negative for an inferior good. Total effect = substitution + income.

**A13. What is a Giffen good, and why does its demand curve slope upward?**

A strongly inferior good on which a poor household spends a large income share. Its income effect is so large and negative that it overwhelms the always-negative substitution effect, so a price fall *reduces* quantity demanded — an upward-sloping demand curve. It is the limiting case proving the curve's slope is derived, not axiomatic.

**A14. What is consumer surplus?**

The difference between what a consumer would have been willing to pay (their marginal utility, traced by the demand curve) and what they actually pay (market price) — geometrically the area below the demand curve and above the price line. It measures the net welfare gain from buying at a single price when earlier units were worth more.

**A15. State the one-line bridge from consumer theory to finance.**

Diminishing marginal utility of wealth makes the utility-of-wealth function U(W) concave, and concavity of U(W) *is* the mathematical definition of risk aversion. The same idea that flattens the value of the fifth pizza slice makes an investor demand an equity risk premium.

**A16. Why does concave U(W) imply a person rejects a fair bet?**

With a concave curve the utility gained from winning ₹1,000 is smaller than the utility lost from losing ₹1,000 (the upside is flatter). So the expected utility of a 50/50 fair gamble is below the utility of the certain starting wealth — the risk-averse agent declines and will even pay a premium (insurance) to avoid the risk.

## Section B — Applied / Numerical Problems (full solutions)

**B1. From the TU schedule below, compute MU for each unit and identify the saturation point.**

| Q | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| TU | 20 | 35 | 45 | 50 | 50 | 45 |

MU = TU_n − TU_(n−1): unit 1 = 20, unit 2 = 15, unit 3 = 10, unit 4 = 5, unit 5 = **0**, unit 6 = −5. Saturation is at **unit 5**, where MU = 0 and TU is at its maximum (50). The sixth unit has negative MU, so consuming it actually lowers total satisfaction (disutility). Note TU keeps rising through units 1–4 even as MU falls — falling MU is fully compatible with rising TU.

**B2. Equi-marginal allocation. A student has ₹100 for coffee (₹20 each) and samosas (₹10 each). At the current bundle MU_coffee = 40 utils and MU_samosa = 30 utils. Is she in equilibrium? If not, which way should she reallocate?**

Compute utility per rupee: coffee = 40/20 = **2 utils/₹**; samosa = 30/10 = **3 utils/₹**. They are unequal, so she is *not* in equilibrium. Samosas deliver more satisfaction per rupee, so she should **shift spending toward samosas**. As she buys more samosas, MU_samosa falls (DMU); as she buys fewer coffees, MU_coffee rises. She stops when MU_coffee/20 = MU_samosa/10, i.e. when the per-rupee utilities are equal.

**B3. A consumer's equilibrium bundle satisfies MU_x/P_x = MU_y/P_y. If P_x = ₹4, P_y = ₹2, and MU_y = 6 utils, what must MU_x be at equilibrium?**

Set the ratios equal: MU_x/4 = 6/2 = 3 utils/₹. So MU_x = 3 × 4 = **12 utils**. The pricier good must deliver proportionally more marginal utility at the optimum — here twice the MU because it costs twice as much per unit.

**B4. Price-cut decomposition. A commuter buys 8 cups of tea a week at ₹15. The price falls to ₹10 and she now buys 12. An analyst attributes +2 cups to the pure relative-price change. Split the total into substitution and income effects and classify the good.**

Total effect = 12 − 8 = **+4 cups**. Substitution effect (given) = **+2 cups** — tea is now cheaper relative to coffee, so she substitutes toward it even at constant satisfaction. Income effect = total − substitution = 4 − 2 = **+2 cups** — the lower price raised her real income and she spent part of it on more tea. Because the income effect is positive (moves the same way as substitution), tea is a **normal good** for her. Plotting the ₹15→₹10 change against 8→12 cups gives one segment of her demand curve — demand derived, not assumed.

**B5. Budget-line arithmetic. Income M = ₹240, P_x = ₹12, P_y = ₹8. Find both intercepts and the slope. Then P_x falls to ₹8 — describe the new line.**

X-intercept = M/P_x = 240/12 = **20 units** of X. Y-intercept = M/P_y = 240/8 = **30 units** of Y. Slope = −P_x/P_y = −12/8 = **−1.5**. When P_x falls to ₹8, the Y-intercept stays at 30 (P_y unchanged), the X-intercept moves out to 240/8 = **30**, and the new slope is −8/8 = **−1.0** — the line pivots outward on the X-axis and flattens, expanding the feasible set. This pivot is exactly the mechanism that, re-optimized at each price, traces the demand curve.

**B6. Certainty equivalent and risk premium. An investor with concave utility faces a project: 50% chance of ₹0 and 50% chance of ₹1,00,000. Her certainty equivalent for it is ₹42,000. Compute the expected value and the risk premium.**

Expected value = 0.5 × 0 + 0.5 × 1,00,000 = **₹50,000**. Risk premium = expected value − certainty equivalent = 50,000 − 42,000 = **₹8,000**. She would swap the gamble for a guaranteed ₹42,000, i.e. she sacrifices ₹8,000 of expected value to shed the risk. This is precisely why risky cash flows are discounted at higher rates than safe ones — the aggregate of such premia is CAPM's market risk premium.

**B7. Insurance as utility maximization. A homeowner faces a 1% chance of a ₹10,00,000 fire loss. An insurer offers cover for a ₹13,000 premium. Compute the expected loss and explain why a risk-averse owner buys while a risk-neutral one refuses.**

Expected loss = 0.01 × 10,00,000 = **₹10,000**. The premium (₹13,000) exceeds the expected loss by ₹3,000 — the insurer's risk-loading plus margin. A **risk-neutral** person (linear utility) values money at face value and refuses, overpaying ₹3,000 on average. A **risk-averse** owner has concave U(W): the utility loss from a ₹10,00,000 hit is enormous relative to the small premium, so paying ₹3,000 above the actuarial cost to remove a catastrophic downside raises her expected utility. The concavity of U(W) is the entire reason an insurance industry exists.

**B8. MRS check. At a candidate bundle a consumer's MRS_xy = 3 (willing to give up 3 units of Y for 1 more X) while the price ratio P_x/P_y = 1.5. Is she optimizing? Which good should she buy more of?**

Not optimizing: MRS (3) > price ratio (1.5). She values an extra X at 3 Y but the market only charges her 1.5 Y for it — a bargain. She should **buy more X** (and less Y), climbing to a higher indifference curve. As she does, diminishing MRS lowers her willingness to trade until MRS falls to 1.5 = P_x/P_y, the tangency equilibrium.

## Section C — Interview-Style Questions (economics asked in finance interviews)

**C1. "Why does a demand curve slope downward? Give me the real derivation, not just 'law of demand.'"**

Start from utility maximization. At the optimum the consumer sets marginal utility per rupee equal across goods, so for a single good MU = price. Now cut the good's price. Two things happen: the substitution effect makes it relatively cheaper so the consumer swaps toward it (always positive), and the income effect raises real purchasing power. For a normal good both push quantity up; joining the re-optimized quantities at each price traces a downward-sloping demand curve. The deeper cause is diminishing marginal utility: each extra unit is worth less, so buyers only take more at lower prices. That the slope is *derived* is proven by the Giffen exception, where a dominant negative income effect flips it upward.

**C2. "Connect diminishing marginal utility to risk aversion — why should I care as an investor?"**

Replace 'quantity of a good' with 'wealth' and define U(W). Diminishing marginal utility of wealth means U is concave: U' > 0 (more is better) but U'' < 0 (each extra rupee adds less). Concavity mechanically produces risk aversion — the utility lost from a loss exceeds the utility gained from an equal win, so a fair gamble has expected utility below the certain wealth. From that single fact flow expected-utility maximization, the certainty equivalent, the risk premium (expected value minus certainty equivalent), and the Arrow–Pratt coefficients. It is the microfoundation of the equity risk premium and every risk-adjusted discount rate. In one line: the demand curve slopes down and investors demand a risk premium for the *same* reason.

**C3. "What is a risk premium in utility terms, and how does it end up in a discount rate?"**

The risk premium is the gap between a gamble's expected value and its certainty equivalent — the guaranteed sum that gives the same utility as the risky prospect. Because U(W) is concave, the certainty equivalent sits below the expected value, so a risk-averse investor demands compensation to hold risk. Aggregated across the market, that compensation is the equity risk premium. When you value a risky project you discount at the risk-free rate *plus* this premium, which lowers present value — CAPM's required return is exactly risk-free plus beta times the market risk premium. So a discount rate is a utility statement dressed in finance notation.

**C4. "How would you use the normal/inferior distinction to think about a business through the cycle?"**

The distinction lives in the sign of the income effect. For a normal good, rising real income raises demand; for an inferior good it lowers demand. In portfolio terms, normal and especially luxury (income-elastic) goods — autos, travel, premium retail — are cyclicals I'd lean into during an expansion. Inferior goods — discount groceries, budget transport — actually gain as incomes fall, so they're defensive, even counter-cyclical. Consumer theory tells me *why*: the income effect of a real-income change points in opposite directions for the two categories, which is precisely what sector rotation is betting on.

**C5. "Why do people buy lottery tickets and insurance at the same time if they're rational and risk-averse?"**

Under the classical concave-U(W) model they shouldn't — that's the puzzle prospect theory resolves. Kahneman and Tversky show the value function is defined over gains and losses relative to a reference point, is steeper for losses (loss aversion, roughly 2–2.5x), and that people *overweight small probabilities*. Overweighting a tiny chance makes a lottery's slim jackpot feel attractive and a rare catastrophe feel worth insuring against — so the same person does both. It doesn't overturn utility theory; it reshapes the utility and probability-weighting functions. Being able to say this signals you know both the baseline model and its documented failures.

**C6. "What's the single most important equilibrium condition in this chapter, and what does it really mean?"**

MRS_xy = P_x/P_y, equivalently MU_x/P_x = MU_y/P_y. It says that at the optimum the rate at which you're *willing* to trade two goods equals the rate at which the *market* forces you to trade them. If they differ, you can trade at market prices and reach a higher indifference curve — an unexploited gain. The same logic reappears everywhere in finance: at an optimal portfolio the marginal return per unit of risk is equalized across assets, which is the direct analogue of equalizing marginal utility per rupee across goods.

**C7. "Explain consumer surplus and why it matters to a company's pricing strategy."**

Consumer surplus is the value buyers capture: willingness to pay minus the price actually charged, the area under the demand curve above price. A single market price leaves surplus on the table because early units were worth far more to the buyer than the price. Pricing strategy is largely about capturing that surplus — tiered subscriptions, versioning, and price discrimination all aim to charge each segment closer to its willingness to pay. When I assess a firm's pricing power, I'm really asking how much of the consumer surplus it can convert into producer surplus without driving customers away.

## Section D — Multiple-Choice Questions (with reasoning)

**D1. Total utility is at its maximum when:**
(a) marginal utility is at its maximum  (b) marginal utility equals zero  (c) marginal utility is negative  (d) marginal utility equals price

**Answer: (b).** TU rises while MU > 0 and falls once MU < 0, so TU peaks exactly where MU = 0 (saturation). (a) confuses TU's peak with MU's peak; (c) describes the region past saturation where TU is already falling; (d) is the single-good purchase condition, not the TU maximum.

**D2. The Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility states that as consumption of a good increases, other things equal:**
(a) total utility falls  (b) marginal utility of each extra unit falls  (c) marginal utility becomes negative immediately  (d) price falls

**Answer: (b).** DMU is about the *marginal* unit adding progressively less. (a) is false over the range where MU is still positive; (b) does not require MU to be negative — (c) overstates it; (d) confuses the consumer's valuation with market price.

**D3. At consumer equilibrium under the ordinal approach:**
(a) MRS_xy > P_x/P_y  (b) the budget line cuts the indifference curve  (c) MRS_xy = P_x/P_y  (d) MU_x = MU_y

**Answer: (c).** Equilibrium is the tangency where the IC's slope (MRS) equals the budget line's slope (price ratio). (a) is a disequilibrium signalling "buy more X"; (b) means you can still reach a higher IC; (d) is wrong because equal marginal utilities aren't required — equal MU *per rupee* is.

**D4. A fall in the price of good X causes the budget line to:**
(a) shift outward parallel  (b) shift inward parallel  (c) pivot outward around the Y-intercept  (d) stay unchanged

**Answer: (c).** Only P_x changed, so the Y-intercept (M/P_y) is fixed while the X-intercept (M/P_x) moves out — the line pivots and flattens. Parallel shifts (a, b) come from *income* changes, which leave relative prices unchanged.

**D5. The substitution effect of a price fall on quantity demanded is:**
(a) always positive (buy more)  (b) always negative (buy less)  (c) positive only for normal goods  (d) zero

**Answer: (a).** Holding satisfaction constant, a cheaper relative price always induces the consumer to substitute toward the good, so quantity rises — regardless of normal/inferior status. The normal/inferior distinction only affects the *income* effect, not the substitution effect.

**D6. A Giffen good must be:**
(a) a luxury  (b) a normal good  (c) an inferior good with a dominant income effect  (d) any good with a horizontal demand curve

**Answer: (c).** A Giffen good is a strongly inferior good whose negative income effect outweighs the positive substitution effect, producing an upward-sloping demand curve. All Giffen goods are inferior, but almost no inferior goods are Giffen — the dominance condition is extreme and rare.

**D7. A concave utility-of-wealth function U(W) implies the investor is:**
(a) risk-neutral  (b) risk-averse  (c) risk-seeking  (d) indifferent to risk

**Answer: (b).** Concavity (U'' < 0) means the marginal utility of wealth diminishes, so a loss hurts more than an equal gain pleases; the investor rejects fair bets and demands a premium to bear risk — the definition of risk aversion. A linear U(W) would be risk-neutral (a); a convex one risk-seeking (c).

**D8. The risk premium of a gamble equals:**
(a) certainty equivalent − expected value  (b) expected value − certainty equivalent  (c) expected value + variance  (d) the probability of loss

**Answer: (b).** For a risk-averse investor the certainty equivalent lies *below* the expected value, and the gap — expected value minus certainty equivalent — is the compensation demanded for bearing the risk. (a) reverses the sign and would be negative for a risk-averse agent.

**D9. Two indifference curves cannot intersect because intersection would violate:**
(a) completeness  (b) transitivity  (c) non-satiation  (d) continuity

**Answer: (b).** The crossing point would belong to two curves and thus carry two different utility rankings at once, contradicting the internal consistency of preferences — transitivity. The other axioms (completeness, non-satiation, continuity) are not what the non-intersection property rests on.

**D10. Consumer surplus is best described as:**
(a) price paid minus willingness to pay  (b) the firm's profit margin  (c) willingness to pay minus price paid  (d) the area above the demand curve and below price

**Answer: (c).** Consumer surplus is the buyer's net gain — the excess of what they'd have paid (marginal utility along the demand curve) over what they actually paid — geometrically the area *below* the demand curve and *above* the price line. (a) reverses the subtraction; (d) reverses the geometry.
