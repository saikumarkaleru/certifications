# Q&A — Elasticity

A companion practice bank for Chapter 03. Every question is followed by a full answer. Attempt your own answer first, then compare. The four sections build from recall (A) to calculation (B) to spoken fluency (C) to exam technique (D).

## Section A — Concept Check (test your understanding)

**A1. In one sentence, what is elasticity?**

Elasticity is the percentage change in one variable divided by the percentage change in another — a unit-free number that measures how strongly an "effect" responds to a "cause."

**A2. Why does economics use percentage changes rather than absolute unit changes to measure responsiveness?**

Because absolute changes depend on the units of measurement (barrels vs litres, rupees vs dollars) and cannot be compared across goods. Percentages strip out units, so elasticity is comparable across products, countries, and time — the same reason finance prefers returns (%) over absolute price moves.

**A3. What is the benchmark value against which every elasticity is judged, and what do the three regions mean?**

The benchmark is 1. If the response percentage exceeds the cause percentage the ratio is above 1 (**elastic**, very responsive); below 1 it is **inelastic** (sluggish); exactly 1 it is **unit elastic**. Most important results — like the revenue rule — flip at 1.0.

**A4. Why is elasticity described as a "local" measurement?**

Because it generally differs at every point on the same curve. Elasticity equals slope times P/Q, and P/Q changes as you move along the curve, so a single demand curve can be elastic in one region and inelastic in another. This mirrors bond duration, option delta, and stock beta, which are all local sensitivities.

**A5. Distinguish the point (base) method from the midpoint (arc) method.**

The point method measures percentage change against the *starting* value, so you get different answers moving A→B versus B→A (100→120 is +20%, 120→100 is −16.7%). The midpoint method uses the *average* of the two values as the base, giving a direction-independent answer, which is why exams favour it between two points.

**A6. For price elasticity of demand, why is the sign negative, and what do economists do with it?**

The law of demand guarantees price and quantity move in opposite directions, so the ratio is negative. By convention economists drop the minus sign and quote the absolute value — "elasticity of 1.5" means |E| = 1.5.

**A7. For income and cross elasticity, why must you keep the sign?**

Because the sign carries the entire message. A negative income elasticity signals an inferior good; positive signals normal. A positive cross elasticity signals substitutes; negative signals complements. Dropping the sign throws away the classification.

**A8. List the five determinants of price elasticity of demand.**

(1) Availability of close substitutes — the biggest factor; (2) necessity vs luxury; (3) proportion of income spent on the good; (4) time horizon (longer = more elastic); (5) durability/postponability of the purchase.

**A9. Why is demand for petrol inelastic in the short run but more elastic in the long run?**

In the short run drivers cannot change their commute, car, or home, so they absorb the price. Over years they buy fuel-efficient or electric cars, move closer to work, and switch modes — giving them room to adjust, which raises elasticity. Time creates alternatives.

**A10. What are the three time periods for supply, and how elastic is supply in each?**

Market period (very short run): output is fixed, supply is vertical/perfectly inelastic (fresh fish must sell today). Short run: firms vary output with existing plant, supply is somewhat elastic. Long run: firms build capacity and new firms enter, supply is most elastic.

**A11. Why are commodity and property prices so volatile in the short run?**

Because their short-run supply is highly inelastic — mines, plantations, and buildings take years to create. A demand surge hits a wall of fixed supply, so price does all the adjusting, producing large swings.

**A12. State the revenue rule linking elasticity and total revenue.**

If demand is inelastic, price and total revenue move in the same direction (raise price, revenue rises). If elastic, they move in opposite directions (raise price, revenue falls). If unit elastic, revenue is unchanged and at its maximum.

**A13. Why does a rational monopolist never operate on the inelastic part of its demand curve?**

On the inelastic part it could raise price, sell fewer units, and still increase total revenue while cutting costs — so it is leaving money on the table. It keeps raising price until it reaches the elastic region (profit maximisation stops slightly before revenue maximisation because costs matter too).

**A14. What is Engel's Law, and which elasticity does it describe?**

Engel's Law states that the share of income spent on food falls as income rises. It is simply the statement that food has an income elasticity below 1 (a normal necessity) — demand grows slower than income.

**A15. What is the Marshall–Lerner condition?**

A currency devaluation improves a country's trade balance only if the sum of the price elasticities of exports and imports exceeds 1. If both are too inelastic, a cheaper currency worsens the balance in the short run (the "J-curve").

## Section B — Applied / Numerical Problems (full solutions)

**B1. A coffee shop raises the price of a latte from ₹200 to ₹240 and daily sales fall from 300 to 260 cups. Compute PED using the midpoint method and classify it.**

%ΔQ = (260 − 300) / [(260+300)/2] = −40 / 280 = −14.29%.
%ΔP = (240 − 200) / [(240+200)/2] = 40 / 220 = +18.18%.
PED = −14.29% / +18.18% = **−0.79**, so |E| ≈ 0.79 < 1 → **inelastic**.
Revenue check: old TR = 300 × 200 = ₹60,000; new TR = 260 × 240 = ₹62,400 → revenue rose, consistent with the inelastic rule (price and revenue move together).

**B2. A streaming service cuts its monthly price 20% and subscriptions rise 35%. Find PED, classify it, and state what happens to revenue.**

PED = +35% / −20% = **−1.75**, |E| = 1.75 > 1 → **elastic**. Because demand is elastic, a price cut raises revenue (the quantity gain outweighs the lower price). Approximate revenue change ≈ (1 − 0.20)(1 + 0.35) − 1 = 1.08 − 1 = **+8%**.

**B3. Point-elasticity practice. A linear demand curve is Q = 120 − 2P. Find PED at P = 20 and at P = 40, and identify which point is elastic.**

Slope dQ/dP = −2. Point elasticity = (dQ/dP)(P/Q).
At P = 20: Q = 120 − 40 = 80, E = −2 × (20/80) = **−0.5** → inelastic.
At P = 40: Q = 120 − 80 = 40, E = −2 × (40/40) = **−2.0** → elastic.
Same straight line, constant slope, but elastic at the high-price/low-quantity end and inelastic at the low-price/high-quantity end — because P/Q rises as you move up the curve. (The midpoint P = 30, Q = 60 gives E = −1, unit elastic, where revenue peaks.)

**B4. Income elasticity. When average income rises 6%, demand for domestic air travel rises 15% and demand for public bus tickets falls 3%. Classify each good.**

Air travel: YED = +15% / +6% = **+2.5** → normal luxury, income-elastic (a cyclical). Bus tickets: YED = −3% / +6% = **−0.5** → inferior good (demand falls as income rises). A portfolio manager expecting rising incomes would favour the airline and be wary of the bus operator; the reverse in a downturn.

**B5. Cross elasticity. Tea's price rises 12% and, in response, coffee demand rises 9%. Later, the price of milk (a complement to coffee) rises 10% and coffee demand falls 4%. Compute both cross elasticities and interpret.**

Coffee–tea: XED = +9% / +12% = **+0.75** (positive → substitutes; buyers switch from dearer tea to coffee). Coffee–milk: XED = −4% / +10% = **−0.40** (negative → complements; dearer milk depresses coffee consumption). The magnitudes say the substitute link is stronger than the complement link.

**B6. Tax incidence. Demand for cigarettes is inelastic (|E| ≈ 0.4) and supply is fairly elastic. A ₹20 per-pack excise duty is imposed. Who bears most of the tax and why, and what happens to government revenue?**

The burden falls on whichever side is *less* elastic. Here demand (0.4) is far less elastic than supply, so **consumers bear most of the ₹20** — they keep buying and absorb the higher price. Because quantity barely falls, the tax base holds up and **government revenue is large and reliable**. This is exactly why "sin taxes" target inelastic goods.

**B7. Revenue optimisation. A toll road currently charges ₹100 and carries 50,000 cars/day; a consultant estimates |PED| ≈ 0.6 around this price. Should the operator raise or lower the toll to increase revenue?**

|PED| = 0.6 < 1 → demand is inelastic, so price and revenue move together. The operator should **raise the toll**: a price increase loses proportionally fewer cars than the price gain, lifting total revenue. It should keep raising until demand approaches unit elasticity (|E| = 1), where revenue peaks. (Congestion or policy caps aside, this is the pure revenue logic.)

**B8. Supply elasticity across time. After a frost destroys part of the coffee crop, prices spike 40% within a month but only 8% a year later. Explain using supply elasticity.**

In the market/short-run period, coffee output is fixed (trees cannot regrow quickly), so supply is near-vertical; the demand that survives collides with fixed supply and price spikes 40%. Over a year, growers harvest more, draw down stocks, and other regions expand — supply becomes more elastic, absorbing the shock, so the price rise shrinks to 8%. Elasticity of supply rises with time.

## Section C — Interview-Style Questions (economics asked in finance interviews)

**C1. "What does elasticity have to do with a company's pricing power or 'moat'?"**

A company's price elasticity of demand *is* its pricing power. If demand is inelastic — strong brand, few substitutes, high switching costs — the firm can raise prices without losing many customers, protecting margins through inflation and competition. That is the Buffett-style quality test: "Can it raise prices without losing customers?" A low PED signals a durable moat; a high PED signals a price-taker with thin, fragile margins.

**C2. "How would you use income elasticity to position a portfolio for the business cycle?"**

Income elasticity separates cyclicals from defensives. High-YED sectors — autos, airlines, luxury, discretionary retail — have earnings that swing more than income, so I overweight them heading into an expansion and underweight them into a recession. Low-YED sectors — food, utilities, healthcare, discount retail — hold up across the cycle, so I rotate into these defensives when I expect incomes to fall. Some discount grocers are even inferior goods (YED < 0) that gain in downturns. Sector rotation is, at bottom, a bet on income elasticities.

**C3. "A competitor just cut prices 10%. How do you estimate the hit to our revenue?"**

I use cross elasticity of demand. If our product and theirs are close substitutes with, say, XED = +1.8, a 10% competitor price cut implies roughly an 18% fall in our quantity demanded (0.10 × 1.8). That converts a vague worry into a quantified revenue threat and tells me how aggressively to respond. The magnitude of XED tells me how substitutable we really are — a low XED means the cut barely touches us; a high XED means urgent margin pressure.

**C4. "Why do governments tax fuel, tobacco, and alcohol, and who really pays those taxes?"**

Because their demand is inelastic. A tax that raises prices sharply cuts consumption only slightly, so the tax base holds and revenue is stable and predictable — governments want reliable receipts. On incidence, the burden falls on the less-elastic side of the market: since buyers of these goods can't easily walk away, consumers bear most of the tax. The same incidence logic generalises to corporate and capital taxes — whoever is less able to escape pays more.

**C5. "Connect elasticity to a concept from finance you already know."**

Every sensitivity in finance is an elasticity in disguise. Bond duration is the price elasticity of a bond to its yield. An option's delta and vega are sensitivities of option price to the underlying and to volatility. A stock's beta is the sensitivity of its return to the market. Each is a percentage response to a percentage stimulus, measured locally at a point — exactly the elasticity idea, which is why the whole risk toolkit becomes intuitive once you think this way.

**C6. "Explain why commodity prices are so volatile using elasticity."**

Both sides of a commodity market are inelastic in the short run. Supply is inelastic because mines, wells, and plantations take years to expand; demand is inelastic because industrial users can't switch inputs overnight. When a shock hits, neither quantity can adjust much, so the entire adjustment falls on price — producing sharp spikes and crashes. This short-run inelasticity is the root cause of energy and materials equity volatility.

**C7. "Walk me through the single most useful result in this chapter for a business."**

The revenue rule. Total revenue is price times quantity, and a price change pulls those two in opposite directions. Elasticity decides which wins. If demand is inelastic, raising price raises revenue because you lose few customers; if elastic, raising price destroys revenue because you lose too many. Revenue is maximised exactly at unit elasticity. The practical takeaway: never assume a price hike raises revenue — first find out where you sit on the demand curve. Misjudging this is the number-one pricing mistake.

## Section D — Multiple-Choice Questions (with reasoning)

**D1. A good has PED = −0.3. A firm raises its price. Total revenue will:**
(a) fall  (b) rise  (c) stay the same  (d) cannot tell

**Answer: (b) rise.** |E| = 0.3 < 1 → inelastic. With inelastic demand, price and revenue move together, so a price increase raises total revenue. (a) and (c) describe the elastic and unit-elastic cases respectively.

**D2. Along a straight-line (linear) demand curve, elasticity is:**
(a) constant and equal to the slope  (b) elastic at the top, unit elastic at the midpoint, inelastic at the bottom  (c) always unit elastic  (d) inelastic at the top, elastic at the bottom

**Answer: (b).** Slope is constant but elasticity = slope × (P/Q), and P/Q falls as you move down the curve. So the high-price/low-quantity top is elastic, the midpoint is unit elastic, and the low-price/high-quantity bottom is inelastic. (a) confuses slope with elasticity; (d) reverses the pattern.

**D3. A good with income elasticity of −0.6 is best described as:**
(a) a normal luxury  (b) a normal necessity  (c) an inferior good  (d) a complement

**Answer: (c) an inferior good.** A negative income elasticity means demand falls as income rises — the definition of an inferior good (e.g., bus travel, instant noodles). (a) needs YED > 1; (b) needs 0 < YED < 1; (d) is a cross-elasticity, not an income-elasticity, concept.

**D4. Two goods have a cross elasticity of demand of +2.5. They are:**
(a) strong complements  (b) unrelated  (c) weak substitutes  (d) strong substitutes

**Answer: (d) strong substitutes.** A positive XED means substitutes (a rise in one's price lifts the other's demand), and the large magnitude (2.5) means the substitution is strong — fierce competitors. Negative would be complements; near zero, unrelated.

**D5. Supply in the "market period" (very short run) is:**
(a) perfectly elastic  (b) perfectly inelastic  (c) unit elastic  (d) elastic

**Answer: (b) perfectly inelastic.** In the market period output is already produced and fixed (e.g., today's catch of fish), so quantity cannot respond to price at all — a vertical supply curve. Supply becomes more elastic as the time horizon lengthens.

**D6. Which good is most likely to have the most elastic demand?**
(a) salt  (b) insulin for a diabetic  (c) a particular brand of bottled water  (d) electricity

**Answer: (c) a particular brand of bottled water.** It has many close substitutes (other brands, tap water), the biggest driver of high elasticity. Salt, insulin, and electricity are necessities with few substitutes and thus inelastic — insulin is close to perfectly inelastic.

**D7. A perfectly elastic demand curve is:**
(a) vertical, with E = 0  (b) horizontal, with E = ∞  (c) a rectangular hyperbola, E = 1  (d) upward-sloping

**Answer: (b) horizontal, with E = ∞.** At the going price buyers take any quantity, but a tiny price rise wipes out all demand — the situation of a single seller in perfect competition (a price-taker). A vertical curve (a) is perfectly *inelastic*; a rectangular hyperbola (c) is unit elastic everywhere.

**D8. A tax is placed on a good whose demand is much less elastic than its supply. The burden falls mainly on:**
(a) sellers  (b) buyers  (c) split equally  (d) the government

**Answer: (b) buyers.** Tax incidence falls on the less-elastic side of the market. Buyers here are relatively inelastic — they can't easily stop buying — so they absorb most of the tax, while more-elastic sellers pass it through.

**D9. A firm finds it is currently operating where demand is unit elastic. To increase total revenue it should:**
(a) raise price  (b) cut price  (c) neither — it is already at maximum revenue  (d) exit the market

**Answer: (c).** At unit elasticity (|E| = 1) the price effect and quantity effect exactly cancel, so total revenue is at its peak. Any move — up or down — reduces revenue. This is the summit of the revenue "hill."

**D10. The Marshall–Lerner condition says a currency devaluation improves the trade balance only if:**
(a) exports are inelastic  (b) the sum of export and import price elasticities exceeds 1  (c) imports exceed exports  (d) the currency is fully floating

**Answer: (b).** Devaluation makes exports cheaper and imports dearer, but it only improves the trade balance if quantities respond enough — specifically if the sum of the two price elasticities exceeds 1. If demand is too inelastic, the balance can worsen first (the J-curve).
