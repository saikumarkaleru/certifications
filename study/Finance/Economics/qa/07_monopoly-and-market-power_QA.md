# Q&A — Monopoly and Market Power

A companion practice bank for the concept guide *Monopoly and Market Power*. Every question is followed by a full answer: Section A locks the definitions, Section B builds numerical fluency, Section C rehearses finance-interview framing, and Section D pressure-tests your reasoning against distractors.

## Section A — Concept-Check Questions

**A1. Define a monopoly precisely. Which words in the definition are load-bearing?**

A monopoly is a market with a **single seller** of a product that has **no close substitutes**, protected by **barriers to entry**. All three phrases matter. "Single seller" means the firm *is* the industry, so the firm's demand curve is the whole market demand curve. "No close substitutes" is what gives it real pricing power — if buyers could easily switch, its demand would be elastic and the power would evaporate. "Barriers to entry" are what make any profit *durable*; without them, entry would compete profit away exactly as in perfect competition.

**A2. Why does marginal revenue lie below price for a single-price monopolist?**

Because the monopolist faces a downward-sloping demand curve, selling one more unit requires cutting the price — and the cut applies to *every* unit sold, not just the extra one. So MR = (price of the new unit) − (revenue lost on all the previous units now sold at the lower price). That second term is always positive on a downward-sloping curve, so **MR < P always**. This single wedge is the engine behind every monopoly result.

**A3. State the MR–elasticity relationship and the "golden rule" it produces.**

**MR = P × (1 − 1/|Ed|).** If demand is elastic (|Ed| > 1), MR is positive; if unit-elastic, MR = 0 (total revenue peaks); if inelastic, MR is negative. The golden rule: **a rational monopolist never operates in the inelastic region of demand.** In the inelastic region it could raise price, sell less, earn *more* revenue *and* cut costs — an unambiguous win — so the profit-maximising point is always where demand is elastic and MR is positive.

**A4. Walk through the monopolist's two-step profit-maximisation procedure.**

Step 1: find the **quantity** where MR = MC — call it Q_m. Step 2: go *up* to the demand curve at Q_m and read off the **price** consumers will pay — call it P_m. Because MR < P, the price P_m sits above MC. The universal rule (MR = MC) is unchanged from competition; what differs is that MR ≠ P, so the outcome is restricted output and a price above marginal cost. The classic exam error is thinking MR = MC gives the price — it gives the quantity.

**A5. "A monopoly always makes a profit." True or false, and why?**

False. Profit still depends on **P_m versus ATC**. If demand is weak or costs are high — the only cinema in a dying town, a patented drug for a rare disease — the monopolist may earn only normal profit or even a loss. Monopoly guarantees the *best available* outcome given demand and costs, and barriers block the entry that would erode any profit that *does* exist. Barriers protect profit; they do not manufacture demand.

**A6. Distinguish the transfer from the deadweight loss in the welfare analysis of monopoly.**

Two separate effects. The **transfer** is the rectangle of surplus that moves from consumers to the monopolist because the price on units still sold rises above the competitive level — this is a *distributional* change, not a loss to society. The **deadweight loss** is the triangle of surplus that simply vanishes: units between Q_m and the competitive Q_c are worth more to consumers than they cost to produce, yet go unproduced. That destroyed surplus benefits nobody and is the true *efficiency* case against monopoly.

**A7. List the main families of barrier to entry and rank them loosely by durability.**

Legal/government-granted (patents, licences, franchises); control of a key resource; natural monopoly (huge economies of scale); network effects; high sunk/capital costs; and brand/switching costs. Durability hierarchy: **legal barriers expire** (a patent cliff is a scheduled moat-demolition); **resource control can be undermined** by new discoveries or synthetics; **network effects and scale-based natural monopolies tend to be the most durable and self-reinforcing** — which is why the most valuable modern monopolies (search, social, marketplaces, payments) are built on them.

**A8. What are X-inefficiency and rent-seeking, and how do they add to the pure deadweight loss?**

Beyond the allocative deadweight loss, monopoly carries two extra costs. **X-inefficiency**: shielded from competition, the firm faces weak pressure to control costs, so costs drift above the minimum — slack, bloat, the "quiet life." **Rent-seeking**: firms spend real resources (lobbying, litigation, defensive patents) to acquire and defend monopoly status; that spending produces nothing and merely protects a position, so it is socially wasteful.

**A9. State the three conditions required for price discrimination to work.**

(1) **Market power** — a downward-sloping demand curve. (2) The ability to **segment buyers** by willingness to pay. (3) The ability to **prevent resale/arbitrage** — otherwise low-price buyers resell to high-price buyers and the scheme collapses. Miss any one and uniform pricing returns.

**A10. Why does perfect (first-degree) price discrimination eliminate deadweight loss, and what is the catch?**

Under perfect discrimination the monopolist charges each buyer exactly their maximum willingness to pay, so every unit is priced at its own value. It then remains profitable to keep selling right up to the point where P = MC — the *efficient* output. Deadweight loss disappears. The catch: **all** the surplus is captured by the producer; consumers keep none. So the outcome is allocatively efficient but maximally unequal — a clean demonstration that efficiency and fairness are different axes.

**A11. What is a natural monopoly, and why can't the regulator simply force marginal-cost pricing?**

A natural monopoly arises when economies of scale are so large that one firm can supply the whole market at a lower average cost than two or more could — huge fixed cost, low marginal cost, so average cost keeps falling across the relevant range. Because AC is still falling, **MC lies below AC**. Forcing P = MC would set price below average cost, so the firm makes a permanent loss and goes bankrupt without a subsidy. That is the regulatory dilemma; regulators fall back on average-cost, price-cap, or rate-of-return rules instead.

**A12. Define the Lerner Index and explain what it reveals about market power and elasticity.**

**Lerner Index L = (P − MC)/P**, running from 0 (perfect competition, P = MC) toward 1 (pure monopoly). At the profit-maximising point it equals **1/|Ed|**, so market power is *inversely* related to the elasticity of demand the firm faces. More inelastic demand — fewer substitutes, a stronger brand, higher switching costs — means a higher markup and more power. This is the formal link between "moat" and "pricing power."

## Section B — Applied / Numerical Problems (with full solutions)

**B1. Building the MR schedule.** A monopolist faces the demand schedule below. Complete total revenue and marginal revenue, and identify where total revenue is maximised.

| Price (₹) | Q |
|---|---|
| 10 | 1 |
| 9 | 2 |
| 8 | 3 |
| 7 | 4 |
| 6 | 5 |
| 5 | 6 |

**Solution.** TR = P × Q; MR = ΔTR/ΔQ.

| P | Q | TR | MR |
|---|---|----|----|
| 10 | 1 | 10 | — |
| 9 | 2 | 18 | 8 |
| 8 | 3 | 24 | 6 |
| 7 | 4 | 28 | 4 |
| 6 | 5 | 30 | 2 |
| 5 | 6 | 30 | 0 |

MR is below price at every step (e.g. at Q = 3, price is ₹8 but MR is only ₹6). **Total revenue is maximised at ₹30 (Q = 5–6), where MR = 0.** Beyond Q = 6, MR turns negative and TR falls — the inelastic region a rational monopolist avoids.

**B2. Profit maximisation from equations.** Demand is P = 100 − 2Q. Marginal cost is constant at MC = 20. Find the profit-maximising quantity, price, and the Lerner Index.

**Solution.** With linear demand P = 100 − 2Q, marginal revenue has the same intercept and twice the slope: **MR = 100 − 4Q.** Set MR = MC: 100 − 4Q = 20 → 4Q = 80 → **Q_m = 20.** Price from demand: P = 100 − 2(20) = **₹60.** Lerner Index = (P − MC)/P = (60 − 20)/60 = **0.67** — a substantial markup, confirming strong market power.

**B3. Deadweight loss.** Using B2 (demand P = 100 − 2Q, MC = 20), compare the monopoly outcome with the competitive (P = MC) outcome and compute the deadweight loss.

**Solution.** Competitive outcome: P = MC → 100 − 2Q = 20 → **Q_c = 40**, price = ₹20. Monopoly restricts output from 40 to 20 and raises price from ₹20 to ₹60. The deadweight loss is the triangle between the demand and MC curves over the lost output (Q_c − Q_m = 20 units): area = ½ × base × height = ½ × (40 − 20) × (60 − 20) = ½ × 20 × 40 = **₹400.** That surplus existed under competition and is destroyed under monopoly.

**B4. Does the monopoly actually profit?** Continue B2 (Q_m = 20, P_m = ₹60). The firm's average total cost at Q = 20 is (a) ₹45, (b) ₹60, (c) ₹72. Classify each case.

**Solution.** Profit = (P − ATC) × Q.
(a) (60 − 45) × 20 = **+₹300 → supernormal profit.**
(b) (60 − 60) × 20 = **₹0 → normal profit only** (P just covers ATC).
(c) (60 − 72) × 20 = **−₹240 → a loss.** The monopolist is already at its best point (MR = MC); it cannot do better. This is the numerical proof that **monopoly does not guarantee profit** — it depends entirely on P versus ATC.

**B5. MR and elasticity cross-check.** At its chosen output a monopolist charges ₹80 and estimates the price elasticity of demand there as |Ed| = 2. Find its marginal revenue and marginal cost.

**Solution.** MR = P × (1 − 1/|Ed|) = 80 × (1 − 1/2) = 80 × 0.5 = **₹40.** At the optimum MR = MC, so **MC = ₹40** as well. Note the firm operates where demand is elastic (|Ed| = 2 > 1), exactly as the golden rule requires; if elasticity had been below 1, MR would be negative and the firm would be irrationally placed.

**B6. Lerner Index from elasticity.** Two firms both price above marginal cost. Firm A faces demand elasticity |Ed| = 5; Firm B faces |Ed| = 1.25. Compute each Lerner Index and interpret.

**Solution.** At the optimum L = 1/|Ed|. Firm A: L = 1/5 = **0.20**; Firm B: L = 1/1.25 = **0.80.** Firm B, with much less elastic demand (fewer substitutes, stronger lock-in), commands a far larger price-cost markup — 80% of price is margin over MC — while Firm A, facing many substitutes, can mark up only 20%. **Market power is inversely proportional to elasticity.**

**B7. Natural monopoly pricing dilemma.** A water utility has ATC = 8 and MC = 3 (₹ per unit) at the market quantity, with average cost still falling. Compare (i) unregulated monopoly pricing at, say, ₹12, (ii) marginal-cost pricing, and (iii) average-cost pricing.

**Solution.**
(i) **Unregulated (₹12):** price well above both MC and ATC → large monopoly profit, restricted output, deadweight loss — the harm regulators want to prevent.
(ii) **Marginal-cost pricing (P = ₹3):** allocatively efficient, but price (₹3) is below ATC (₹8), so the firm loses ₹5 per unit → bankruptcy without a subsidy. Efficient but unfundable.
(iii) **Average-cost pricing (P = ₹8):** the firm breaks even and earns a normal return; most monopoly profit is eliminated. Price still exceeds MC (₹8 > ₹3), so it is not perfectly efficient — but it is the practical "fair-return" compromise regulators usually choose.

**B8. Third-degree price discrimination.** An airline splits customers into business (elasticity |Ed| = 1.25) and leisure (elasticity |Ed| = 4). Marginal cost per seat is ₹2,000. Using the optimal-markup rule P = MC / (1 − 1/|Ed|), find each group's price.

**Solution.** Business: P = 2,000 / (1 − 1/1.25) = 2,000 / (1 − 0.8) = 2,000 / 0.2 = **₹10,000.** Leisure: P = 2,000 / (1 − 1/4) = 2,000 / 0.75 = **₹2,667** (approx). The inelastic business traveller pays nearly four times the elastic leisure traveller for the same seat — the exact pattern real revenue-management systems produce. The firm charges the higher price to the group less able to walk away.

## Section C — Interview-Style Questions (with model answers)

**C1. "In one minute, explain monopoly and why it matters to an investor."**

Model answer: "A monopoly is a single seller behind entry barriers, so it faces the whole downward-sloping demand curve — which means marginal revenue sits below price. It maximises profit where MR = MC but then prices *up* on the demand curve, so it restricts output and charges above marginal cost. That earns profit that *persists*, because barriers block the entry that would otherwise compete it away. For an investor this is the definition of a moat: market power is a slice of monopoly, and the durability of the barrier is the durability of the excess return. It's the state Buffett actively hunts for — and the state antitrust regulators try to dismantle."

**C2. "How is a moat just applied monopoly theory?"**

Model answer: "Perfect competition grinds economic profit to zero through free entry. The only way a firm sustains returns above its cost of capital is to *block* that entry — which is exactly what a barrier to entry does. So every durable-competitive-advantage thesis — brand, patent, network effect, switching cost, scale — is really an argument that the firm holds some monopoly power via a barrier. And the *type* of barrier tells you how durable the excess return is. A patent has a scheduled cliff; a network effect can be self-reinforcing and much harder to erode. When I model a DCF fade period, I'm implicitly forecasting how fast that market power decays."

**C3. "A pharma company earns 90% gross margins on a drug. Is that a red flag or a green flag?"**

Model answer: "Both, depending on the barrier and its expiry. The 90% margin is a huge markup of price over marginal cost — classic monopoly power from a patent, with no close substitutes for a novel molecule. That's the reward for the risky R&D that produced it — Schumpeter's bargain. The critical question is *durability*: when does the patent cliff hit? On expiry, generics flood in, the barrier vanishes, the market lurches toward perfect competition, and prices can fall 80–90%. So I'd model the specific patent-expiry date as a scheduled moat-demolition and check the pipeline for the next blockbuster. The margin is a green flag on *current* earnings and a red flag on *terminal-value durability*."

**C4. "Why is valuing a regulated power utility different from valuing a normal company?"**

Model answer: "A power grid is a textbook natural monopoly — colossal fixed cost to lay the wires, tiny marginal cost per kWh, and a second parallel grid would be pure waste. So the state doesn't let it price freely; a regulator sets the tariff, usually to allow a fixed permitted return on the regulated asset base. That completely reshapes the analysis. I'm not forecasting pricing power or market share — I'm modelling the *regulatory contract*: the allowed rate of return, the size and growth of the asset base, the regulatory period, and reset risk. The cash flows are bond-like and stable, which is why pension funds and infrastructure investors prize regulated utilities as low-beta income assets. The monopoly is real but tamed — the deadweight loss is regulated away in exchange for a capped, guaranteed return."

**C5. "Explain deadweight loss to a non-economist, and why regulators care."**

Model answer: "Imagine some customers value a product at more than it costs to make, but the monopolist won't sell to them because cutting the price to reach them would mean cutting it for everyone else too. Those are mutually beneficial trades that simply never happen — value that could have existed but doesn't. That vanished value is deadweight loss. It's crucial that it isn't the monopolist's profit; the profit is a *transfer* from buyers to the seller, which is a fairness issue but not a pure waste. The deadweight loss is different — surplus that benefits *nobody*. That's the efficiency case for antitrust: the point isn't that the firm makes money, it's that society produces less than it should."

**C6. "When is being a monopolist *not* enough to make money?"**

Model answer: "Whenever price at the profit-maximising output can't clear average total cost. Being the only seller doesn't create demand — it just lets you pick the best point on whatever demand curve exists. A monopoly serving a tiny or shrinking market, or one saddled with high costs, can earn only normal profit or lose money: think the last cinema in a dying town, or a patented treatment for an ultra-rare disease. Monopoly guarantees you the *best available* outcome and it blocks entry, but if the best available outcome is a loss, you still lose. So I never treat 'they're a monopoly' as shorthand for 'they're profitable' — I check price against average cost."

**C7. "Is price discrimination good or bad?"**

Model answer: "It's neither inherently — it depends on the type and the effect on competition. Most everyday forms are legal and routine: student and senior discounts, bulk pricing, airline fare buckets, tiered SaaS plans. Economically, price discrimination lets the firm serve customers a single price would have shut out, and *perfect* discrimination is actually allocatively efficient — zero deadweight loss — though it transfers all the surplus to the seller, so it's efficient but maximally unequal. What antitrust polices isn't price differences as such; it's discrimination *used to harm competition* — predatory or exclusionary pricing. For a business analyst, price discrimination is mostly a profit lever: revenue management, versioning, and dynamic pricing are all ways to capture more of the surplus, and they tell you a lot about a company's real margin potential."

## Section D — Multiple-Choice Questions (with reasoning)

**D1.** For a single-price monopolist, the relationship between marginal revenue and price is:
A. MR = P  B. MR > P  C. MR < P  D. MR = 0 always

**Answer: C.** Selling one more unit requires cutting price on *all* units, so the revenue lost on previous units drives MR below price on a downward-sloping demand curve. MR = P (A) holds only for price-takers; MR = 0 (D) is true only at the revenue-maximising output, not always.

**D2.** A profit-maximising monopolist will never produce in the region where demand is:
A. Elastic  B. Unit-elastic  C. Inelastic  D. Perfectly elastic

**Answer: C.** In the inelastic region MR is negative, so raising price and selling less would increase revenue *and* cut costs — an unambiguous improvement. The optimum therefore always lies in the elastic region where MR is positive.

**D3.** A monopolist finds the profit-maximising quantity where MR = MC. To find the price it should:
A. Read MC at that quantity  B. Read MR at that quantity  C. Read up to the demand curve at that quantity  D. Set price equal to MR

**Answer: C.** MR = MC gives the *quantity* only. The price is read *up* to the demand curve above that quantity, and since MR < P it is higher than MR or MC. Confusing quantity-setting with price-setting is the classic exam error.

**D4.** The deadweight loss of monopoly represents:
A. The profit transferred from consumers to the monopolist  B. Surplus destroyed because welfare-improving trades go unproduced  C. The monopolist's total profit  D. The firm's fixed costs

**Answer: B.** DWL is the surplus that vanishes because units worth more than they cost are not produced. The transfer to the monopolist (A) is a redistribution, not a loss to society, and is a separate rectangle from the DWL triangle.

**D5.** Which barrier to entry is generally the *least* durable?
A. Network effects  B. A patent  C. Vast economies of scale (natural monopoly)  D. Control of an essential resource

**Answer: B.** A patent has a fixed statutory life and expires on a known date — a scheduled moat-demolition (the "patent cliff"). Network effects and scale-based natural monopolies tend to be self-reinforcing and far more durable.

**D6.** The Lerner Index equals:
A. (P − MC)/P  B. (P − ATC)/P  C. MC/P  D. Sum of squared market shares

**Answer: A.** L = (P − MC)/P measures the price-cost markup, running from 0 (P = MC, competition) toward 1 (pure monopoly), and equals 1/|Ed| at the optimum. Sum of squared shares (D) is the HHI, a different tool.

**D7.** A natural monopoly cannot be forced to price at marginal cost because:
A. Marginal cost is above average cost  B. Average cost is still falling, so MC lies below AC and P = MC means a loss  C. Marginal cost is zero  D. Regulators are not allowed to set prices

**Answer: B.** With economies of scale so large that AC keeps falling, MC sits below AC; pricing at MC therefore puts price below average cost and the firm loses money on every unit. Hence regulators use average-cost or price-cap rules instead.

**D8.** Perfect (first-degree) price discrimination results in:
A. Maximum deadweight loss  B. Zero deadweight loss but all surplus to the seller  C. Zero profit for the firm  D. A price below marginal cost

**Answer: B.** Charging each buyer their exact willingness to pay makes it profitable to sell up to the efficient P = MC quantity, eliminating deadweight loss — but the producer captures all surplus, leaving consumers none. Efficient yet maximally unequal.

**D9.** Which tool do antitrust regulators use to screen the concentration effect of a merger?
A. The Lerner Index  B. The Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI)  C. Average variable cost  D. The shutdown rule

**Answer: B.** The HHI — the sum of squared market shares of all firms — is the standard concentration gauge used by the CCI, US DOJ/FTC, and EU. A merger that sharply raises HHI in an already-concentrated market draws scrutiny.

**D10.** "A monopolist can charge any price it likes and sell any quantity it likes." This statement is:
A. True — that is what monopoly means  B. False — it faces the whole demand curve and can pick price *or* quantity, not both  C. True only for natural monopolies  D. False — monopolists are price-takers

**Answer: B.** The demand curve is still a binding constraint: set a high price and you sell little; push volume and price collapses. Monopoly power is the freedom to choose the *profit-maximising point* on the demand curve — not to escape the curve. Monopolists are price-makers, not price-takers (ruling out D).
