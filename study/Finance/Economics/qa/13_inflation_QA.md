# Q&A — Inflation

Practice bank for the chapter "Inflation." Sections: **A** concept-check, **B** applied/numerical, **C** interview-style, **D** MCQs with reasoning.

---

## Section A — Concept Check

**A1. Define inflation precisely. Which two words in the definition do most of the work, and why?**

Inflation is a *sustained*, *general* increase in the price level of goods and services over time — equivalently, a sustained decline in the purchasing power of money. "Sustained" rules out a one-off price jump (e.g., a single GST hike that then stays flat); inflation is an ongoing rate of change, period after period. "General" rules out a relative-price change (onions tripling on a bad monsoon while everything else is stable); inflation is a broad-based rise across the whole basket. Getting these two words right dissolves most popular confusion about inflation.

**A2. Distinguish demand-pull from cost-push inflation on the dimension that matters most for policymakers.**

Demand-pull arises when aggregate demand outruns productive capacity — the economy overheats, so it comes *with* rising output, falling unemployment and strong profits. Cost-push arises when production costs rise (oil shocks, wage-push, rupee depreciation), so it comes *with stagnant or falling output*. The decisive difference is the direction of output: demand-pull is the "good problem" attached to a boom and is cured by tightening money; cost-push produces **stagflation** (high inflation + high unemployment), presenting the central bank a cruel dilemma — hike and deepen recession, or cut and inflame inflation. There is no clean fix for cost-push.

**A3. Why does the RBI watch core inflation when its legal target is headline CPI?**

Headline CPI includes food and fuel, which gyrate on weather and geopolitics and add noise that obscures the underlying trend. Core inflation (headline minus food and fuel) strips out that noise to reveal the *persistent, demand-driven* signal that monetary policy can actually influence. If headline is high but core is contained, the spike is likely a transient food/fuel event the RBI can "look through"; if core itself rises, price pressure is becoming broad-based and entrenched, which demands a policy response.

**A4. Explain the difference between deflation, disinflation, and falling inflation.**

Deflation is a *sustained fall* in the price level — negative inflation, prices actually declining. Disinflation is a *slowing* of the inflation rate (say 6% to 3%) — prices are still rising, just more slowly. "Falling inflation" is the same as disinflation, not deflation: when inflation drops from 7% to 3%, prices keep going up. Prices only decline under outright deflation. This is the single most common lay confusion.

**A5. Why do central banks target a positive inflation rate (~2–4%) rather than zero?**

Two reasons. First, mild inflation lets **real wages fall without nominal wage cuts**, which workers resist fiercely — because nominal wages are "sticky downward," a little inflation is the lubricant that lets labour markets clear. Second, a positive target keeps a safety buffer against ever slipping into **deflation**, whose self-reinforcing debt-and-demand spiral and liquidity trap are far harder to escape than moderate inflation. Zero or negative inflation is the real danger zone.

**A6. State Friedman's dictum and explain the nuance it hides.**

"Inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon." The point: *sustained*, general price rises ultimately require the money supply to grow faster than output. The nuance is the word "sustained" — it does not mean every short-run price wiggle is monetary. Supply shocks, demand surges and expectations drive inflation over months and quarters. But *persistent* inflation cannot happen without accommodative money growth. Velocity and the output gap matter too: printing money in a liquidity trap (post-2008 QE) need not spark inflation if the money isn't spent.

## Section B — Applied / Numerical

**B1. A fixed deposit pays a nominal 7% per year. Expected inflation is 6%. (a) What is the approximate real return? (b) If inflation instead runs at 8%, what happens? (c) State the exact Fisher relationship.**

(a) Real ≈ nominal − expected inflation = 7% − 6% = **~1%**. The saver's purchasing power grows only about 1%.

(b) Real ≈ 7% − 8% = **−1%**. The real return is *negative*; despite a positive nominal number the saver is getting poorer in purchasing-power terms. This is the money-illusion trap.

(c) Exact: (1 + nominal) = (1 + real) × (1 + expected inflation). Solving (a) exactly: real = 1.07/1.06 − 1 = 0.943%, close to the 1% approximation.

**B2. Nominal GDP grew 11% over a year while the GDP deflator (inflation) was 6%. Estimate real GDP growth and explain what the deflator is.**

Real GDP growth ≈ nominal growth − inflation = 11% − 6% = **~5%**. Only that 5% is genuinely "more stuff"; the remaining 6% is just higher prices. The GDP deflator is the ratio of nominal GDP to real GDP × 100 — the broadest, economy-wide price measure, covering every domestically produced good and service (consumption, investment, government, net exports) with weights that shift as GDP composition changes, and excluding imports.

**B3. A ten-year Indian G-Sec yields a nominal 7.2%. Expected inflation is 4.5%. (a) What is the real yield? (b) A comparable inflation-linked bond yields 2.5% real. What is the breakeven inflation rate, and what does it tell you?**

(a) Real yield ≈ 7.2% − 4.5% = **~2.7%**.

(b) Breakeven inflation = nominal yield − real (inflation-linked) yield = 7.2% − 2.5% = **4.7%**. The breakeven is the market-implied inflation forecast embedded in the gap between nominal and inflation-linked bonds. Here the market is pricing ~4.7% average inflation over ten years — slightly above the analyst's 4.5% expectation, which (all else equal) would make the inflation-linked bond marginally attractive.

**B4. Inflation surprises to the upside. The RBI hikes the repo rate and the ten-year yield rises from 6.5% to 7.5%. Qualitatively, what happens to a long-duration bond versus a short-duration bond, and why?**

Bond prices move inversely to yields, so both bonds fall in price. But price sensitivity scales with **duration**: the long-duration bond falls *much* more for the same 1-percentage-point yield rise. Mechanically, existing bonds are locked into old, now-inferior coupons, so their price must drop until yield-to-maturity matches the new 7.5% market rate — and the further out the cash flows, the larger the present-value hit. This is exactly why long-dated Treasuries fell 20–30% in 2022 while short bonds fell far less.

**B5. Using M × V = P × Y, if money supply grows 12% a year, velocity is stable, and real output grows 6%, estimate inflation. What assumption is doing the work?**

Rearranging, P = (M × V)/Y, so %ΔP ≈ %ΔM + %ΔV − %ΔY = 12% + 0% − 6% = **~6% inflation**. The load-bearing assumption is that **velocity V is stable** (and output grows at its natural rate). This is the long-run quantity-theory skeleton; in the short run, swings in velocity and the output gap can break the clean relationship.

**B6. Nominal wages rose 8% this year; CPI inflation was 6%. (a) What happened to real wages? (b) A worker feels "richer." What behavioural bias is this?**

(a) Real wage growth ≈ 8% − 6% = **~2%**. Modest real gain.

(b) **Money illusion** — thinking in nominal rather than real terms. People feel richer from a 5% raise during 5% inflation (zero real change) yet feel cheated by a 2% nominal pay cut at zero inflation. The worker here did gain 2% in real terms, but the *feeling* of being richer is driven mostly by the larger nominal number, not the smaller real gain.

## Section C — Interview-Style

**C1. "Walk me through how a US inflation surprise transmits all the way to Indian equity valuations."**

Model answer: A higher-than-expected inflation print raises expectations of tighter policy. Central banks lift the policy rate (repo/fed funds) to cool demand, and free-market lenders demand a bigger inflation premium, so both the short and long end of the yield curve rise — including the risk-free rate. Bond prices fall inversely to those yields. For equities, three channels bite: (1) the *discount-rate channel* — a higher risk-free rate raises the discount rate, cutting the present value of distant cash flows, which punishes long-duration growth/tech stocks hardest; (2) the *earnings channel* — firms with pricing power pass costs on and protect margins, while weak-pricing-power firms get squeezed; (3) the *multiple channel* — P/E ratios compress as uncertainty raises the equity risk premium. Cross-border, US tightening pulls capital toward the dollar, pressuring the rupee and importing inflation into India, which forces the RBI to follow. Net: valuations compress, growth stocks fall most, value/energy/commodity names hold up better. That was the 2022 great repricing in a sentence.

**C2. "Why is cost-push inflation harder for a central bank than demand-pull?"**

Model answer: Because of what it does to output. Demand-pull comes with a boom — cooling demand by raising rates addresses the inflation *and* the overheating simultaneously; the tools and the goal align. Cost-push comes from a supply shock (oil, war, supply chains), so it pairs high inflation with *falling* output — stagflation. Now the central bank's single instrument faces two opposite problems: hiking rates to fight inflation deepens the recession, while cutting rates to support growth inflames inflation. There is no clean monetary fix; the real cure is supply-side (more production, cheaper inputs) which the central bank does not control. That is why the 1970s oil shocks were so punishing and why 2022's supply-side component made policy so fraught.

**C3. "Is deflation really worse than moderate inflation? Convince me."**

Model answer: Often yes. Deflation triggers two vicious mechanisms. First, *deferred consumption*: if prices will be lower next month, rational buyers wait, collapsing current demand, cutting output, jobs and incomes, which lowers spending further. Second, and worse, *debt deflation* (Irving Fisher): debts are fixed in nominal terms, so as prices and incomes fall, the *real* burden of debt *rises*, forcing defaults, fire-sales and bank failures that shrink credit. On top of this, monetary policy loses traction — nominal rates can't fall much below zero (the zero lower bound / liquidity trap), so real rates stay punishingly high exactly when stimulus is needed. Japan's Lost Decades and the Great Depression are the canonical cases. Moderate inflation, by contrast, is manageable and even useful. That asymmetry is precisely why targets are a positive 2%, not zero.

**C4. "Explain India's inflation-targeting framework and the crisis that produced it."**

Model answer: The 2013 "taper tantrum" was the trigger. In 2012–13 Indian CPI ran in double digits on high food prices, fiscal deficits and a wide current-account deficit. When the Fed hinted at tapering bond purchases, capital fled emerging markets and the rupee collapsed from ~55 to ~68 per dollar, spiking imported inflation via costlier oil and gold — a vicious cost-push loop. This discredited the loose framework and led to the **Urjit Patel Committee (2014)**, which recommended formal inflation targeting. In 2016 the RBI Act was amended to give the RBI a legal mandate: **4% CPI inflation with a ±2% tolerance band (2–6%)**, decided by a six-member **Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)**. It is the most consequential institutional change in modern Indian macro — every bond trader now watches the monthly CPI print and MPC minutes to anticipate repo moves.

**C5. "Are equities a good inflation hedge?"**

Model answer: It depends on the horizon and the type of firm. Over the *long run*, equities are an imperfect but reasonable hedge — real assets and nominal earnings eventually reprice upward with the price level. In the *short run*, when inflation spikes and rates rise sharply, equities are a *poor* hedge: the rising discount rate compresses multiples and hammers long-duration growth stocks. Within equities, inflation reshuffles winners and losers — firms with *pricing power* and *real assets* (consumer staples, energy, commodities) protect or grow earnings, while weak-pricing-power firms see margins crushed. Historically equities do best in a "Goldilocks" zone of low, stable inflation (2–3%) and worst under both high inflation and deflation. So the honest answer is "yes over decades, no over the quarter when inflation surprises."

**C6. "Higher inflation should weaken a currency. Why did the US dollar surge in 2022?"**

Model answer: Two forces pull on a currency. Long-run, higher inflation erodes purchasing power and weakens the currency (relative purchasing power parity). Short-run, if the central bank hikes rates aggressively, higher yields *attract* capital (interest-rate parity / carry) and can *strengthen* the currency. In 2022, US inflation was high, yet the Fed hiked faster and further than other major central banks, so the rate-differential effect dominated the PPP effect and the dollar soared to two-decade highs. The lesson: the currency effect of inflation hinges on the *policy response relative to peers*, not inflation alone.

## Section D — MCQs with Reasoning

**D1. Onion prices triple after a failed monsoon while all other prices are stable. This is best described as:**
A) Demand-pull inflation  B) Cost-push inflation  C) A relative-price change  D) Hyperinflation

**Answer: C.** Inflation is a *general*, broad-based rise. A single item spiking while everything else is flat is a change in *relative prices*, not inflation, which requires the overall basket to rise.

**D2. Inflation falls from 7% to 3%. This means:**
A) Prices are falling  B) Prices are rising more slowly  C) The economy is in deflation  D) Purchasing power is rising

**Answer: B.** This is disinflation — the inflation *rate* slows but prices are still rising, just less rapidly. Prices only fall under deflation (negative inflation). Purchasing power is still eroding, only more gently.

**D3. Which index would an analyst watch as a *leading* indicator of pipeline cost pressure that later feeds retail prices?**
A) CPI  B) Core CPI  C) WPI  D) GDP deflator

**Answer: C.** WPI measures wholesale/producer goods prices, is dominated by commodities and excludes services, so it is volatile and moves ahead of retail CPI. A wide WPI-over-CPI gap warns that firms' margins are squeezed and consumer prices may catch up.

**D4. A borrower with a fixed-rate home loan benefits from unexpected inflation because:**
A) Interest rates fall  B) They repay in cheaper rupees while nominal wages and asset values rise  C) The bank forgives part of the loan  D) Their real debt burden rises

**Answer: B.** Unexpected inflation transfers wealth from creditors to debtors. The debt is fixed in nominal terms, so its real value shrinks; meanwhile the borrower's nominal wage and the home's nominal value climb. The bank (creditor) loses. (D describes *deflation*, the opposite.)

**D5. In M × V = P × Y, sustained inflation over the long run is most fundamentally driven by:**
A) Rising V with fixed M  B) Money supply M growing faster than real output Y  C) Falling Y with fixed M and V  D) A one-time rise in P

**Answer: B.** The quantity theory's long-run skeleton: with stable velocity and output at its natural rate, the price level is driven by money supply. Sustained inflation requires money growth exceeding output growth — Friedman's monetary phenomenon.

**D6. Hyperinflation is conventionally defined (Cagan) as inflation exceeding:**
A) 10% per year  B) 50% per year  C) 50% per month  D) 100% per month

**Answer: C.** Cagan's threshold is 50% *per month*, which compounds to over 12,000% per year. It is almost always caused by governments printing money to finance deficits — a fiscal collapse dressed as a monetary event, worsened by exploding velocity.

**D7. When inflation rises sharply, long-duration growth stocks typically fall most because:**
A) Their earnings disappear  B) The rising discount rate cuts the present value of their far-off cash flows  C) They pay high dividends  D) They are real assets

**Answer: B.** Growth stocks derive value from cash flows far in the future (high-duration equities). A higher inflation-driven discount rate slashes the present value of distant cash flows disproportionately, which is why richly-valued tech cratered in 2022.

**D8. A ₹1,00,000 cash pile held from 2015 buys only ~₹65,000 worth of goods by 2025. This illustrates:**
A) Nominal value rising  B) Deflation  C) The erosion of purchasing power  D) A relative-price change

**Answer: C.** The note's nominal value is unchanged, but inflation eroded its real purchasing power by about a third — the "silent robbery" that is the flip side of rising prices.

**D9. Which statement about CPI and WPI is correct?**
A) They must move together  B) WPI includes services; CPI excludes them  C) CPI is commodity-heavy; WPI is food-heavy  D) They can diverge, even with opposite signs, because they cover different baskets

**Answer: D.** WPI covers wholesale *goods only* (no services, heavy commodities); CPI covers a household basket heavy in food and services. In 2020 WPI briefly went negative while CPI stayed near 6–7%. They routinely diverge.

**D10. Printing money does not always cause immediate inflation because:**
A) Money supply never affects prices  B) In a liquidity trap or with collapsing velocity, the money may not be spent  C) CPI ignores money  D) The GDP deflator offsets it

**Answer: B.** If velocity collapses or the economy is in a liquidity trap (post-2008 QE, early COVID), created money isn't spent and need not spark inflation. The 2021–22 inflation came when money *met* reopening demand and supply constraints — velocity and the output gap matter, not just M.

**D11. The main argument for a positive rather than zero inflation target is:**
A) Higher inflation always boosts growth  B) It lets real wages adjust down despite sticky nominal wages, and buffers against deflation  C) It weakens the currency for exporters  D) It raises tax revenue

**Answer: B.** Because nominal wages are sticky downward, mild inflation lets real wages fall without nominal cuts, helping labour markets clear; it also keeps a safety margin above the far more dangerous deflationary trap.

**D12. Stagflation is most associated with:**
A) Demand-pull inflation in a boom  B) A cost-push supply shock  C) Deflation  D) Disinflation

**Answer: B.** Stagflation — high inflation plus stagnant growth/high unemployment — arises from a cost-push supply shock (e.g., the 1970s oil shocks), which raises prices while shrinking output, trapping the central bank in a no-win trade-off.
