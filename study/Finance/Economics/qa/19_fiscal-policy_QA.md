# Q&A — Fiscal Policy

A practice bank for the Economics for Finance chapter "Fiscal Policy." Work each question before reading the answer. Sections: A (concept-check), B (applied/numerical), C (interview-style), D (MCQs with reasoning).

## Section A — Concept Check

**A1. In one sentence, what problem does fiscal policy exist to solve?**

A market economy left to itself lurches between booms and slumps and can get stuck in a bad equilibrium — as in the 1930s Depression, when private spending collapsed and did not self-correct for a decade — so fiscal policy exists to let the government spend and tax deliberately to stabilise demand over the cycle, and to finance and shape the state's permanent role in the economy.

**A2. Define fiscal policy and name its two levers.**

Fiscal policy is the use of **government spending (G)** and **taxation (T)** to influence aggregate demand, output, employment, and prices. The two levers are spending and taxation. **Expansionary** policy raises G, cuts T, or both (widening the deficit to fight unemployment); **contractionary** policy cuts G, raises T, or both (narrowing the deficit to cool inflation).

**A3. How do the two levers enter aggregate demand differently?**

Through the identity AD = C + I + G + (X − M). Government spending **G sits directly inside AD** — build a highway and demand is created immediately. Taxation works **one step removed**: it changes households' disposable income and firms' after-tax profits, which then changes **C and I**. That is why the tax multiplier is weaker than the spending multiplier.

**A4. What is the fiscal deficit, and why do bond traders watch it?**

The fiscal deficit is total expenditure minus total revenue *excluding borrowing* — i.e. how much the government must borrow in a year, expressed as a percentage of GDP. Traders watch it because the deficit is financed by selling bonds: **a bigger deficit means a bigger G-sec supply**, which, other things equal, pushes bond prices down and yields up. It is the direct pipe from the budget to the yield curve.

**A5. Distinguish revenue expenditure from capital expenditure.**

**Revenue expenditure** is spending that creates no durable asset — salaries, pensions, interest payments, subsidies. **Capital expenditure** builds lasting assets — roads, railways, ports. The distinction matters because capex tends to have a much higher multiplier and raises long-run productive potential, which is why recent Indian budgets have shifted toward it.

**A6. What are automatic stabilisers?**

Features of the tax-and-transfer system that dampen the cycle without any new legislation. In a boom, progressive taxes rise faster than income, pulling spending power out automatically; in a recession, tax take falls and welfare/unemployment spending rises automatically. The deficit therefore **widens in downturns and shrinks in booms** on its own — a built-in counter-cyclical force. India's are weaker than advanced economies' because the tax base is narrow and the safety net thin (MGNREGA is a partial one).

**A7. Automatic versus discretionary policy — the core contrast?**

Automatic stabilisers are built into the rules, act **immediately with no lag**, and carry low political risk. Discretionary policy (a stimulus package, a tax cut, a capex push) is a **deliberate decision** — powerful but slow, hampered by recognition, decision, and implementation lags, and exposed to political incentives (easy to start, hard to stop).

**A8. What is the structural (cyclically-adjusted) balance and why do analysts use it?**

It is the deficit the government would run **if the economy were at potential output** — the deficit with the cycle-driven part stripped out. Analysts use it to see the true *discretionary stance*: a deficit that widens purely because of recession is stabilisation working as designed, but a structural deficit widening in good times is a warning about the underlying fiscal position.

**A9. State the simple spending multiplier and the intuition behind it.**

Multiplier = 1 / (1 − MPC), where MPC is the marginal propensity to consume. The intuition: the government pays a worker ₹100, who spends ₹80, whose recipient spends ₹64, and so on — each round smaller because people save (and import) part of every rupee. The infinite series sums to the multiplier, so one rupee of spending lifts demand by more than one rupee.

**A10. What is crowding out, and when is it strong versus weak?**

Crowding out is the fall in private investment caused when heavy government borrowing competes for a limited pool of savings, pushing interest rates up and discouraging interest-sensitive spending. It is **strong near full employment** (savings fully employed, so public borrowing displaces private borrowing) and **weak in a slump** (idle savings, spare capacity, rates pinned low) — where public investment can even **crowd in** private investment by reviving demand.

**A11. What determines whether the debt-to-GDP ratio rises or falls?**

The race between the interest rate on debt (r) and the growth rate of the economy (g). If **g > r**, the economy outgrows the compounding of interest and the ratio tends to fall; if **r > g**, interest compounds faster and the ratio rises unless the government runs a **primary surplus** to offset it. India has historically enjoyed g > r.

**A12. Why is the raw debt-to-GDP ratio a poor guide to danger?**

Because sustainability depends on more than the number: the **currency** of the debt (own-currency debt can, in extremis, be serviced by the central bank; foreign-currency debt cannot be printed), **who holds it** (domestic savers are stickier than fickle foreign investors), the **maturity structure** (long-dated debt avoids rollover risk), and **market confidence** (partly self-fulfilling). Japan is stable at ~250 percent; Greece was not at ~130 percent.

## Section B — Applied / Numerical (Full Solutions)

**B1. The MPC is 0.75. (a) Find the simple spending multiplier. (b) A ₹200 crore increase in government spending raises output by how much in the textbook case?**

*Solution.*
- (a) Multiplier = 1 / (1 − MPC) = 1 / (1 − 0.75) = 1 / 0.25 = **4**.
- (b) Change in output = multiplier × injection = 4 × ₹200 crore = **₹800 crore**.
This is the frictionless textbook figure; real multipliers are smaller because taxes, saving, and imports leak money out of the domestic spending chain.

**B2. With MPC = 0.75, compare the effect of a ₹100 spending increase with a ₹100 tax cut.**

*Solution.* The spending multiplier is 1 / (1 − 0.75) = 4, so ₹100 of spending raises output by **₹400**. The tax multiplier is −MPC / (1 − MPC) = −0.75 / 0.25 = **−3**, so a ₹100 tax *cut* raises output by 3 × ₹100 = **₹300**. The tax cut does less because the first round is only MPC × ₹100 = ₹75 (households save ₹25 immediately), whereas the full ₹100 of spending hits demand directly. Lesson: spending is a stronger lever than an equal-sized tax cut.

**B3. Government debt is 80% of GDP. Nominal GDP grows at 10% and the average interest rate on debt is 7%. Ignoring new primary deficits, does the debt ratio rise or fall, and roughly to what?**

*Solution.* Here g = 10% > r = 7%, so the economy outgrows interest and the ratio **falls**. Approximate new ratio ≈ old ratio × (1 + r) / (1 + g) = 0.80 × 1.07 / 1.10 = 0.80 × 0.9727 ≈ **0.778, i.e. about 77.8% of GDP**. The favourable r-minus-g gap shrinks the ratio automatically even with a balanced primary budget — the mechanism behind India's ability to carry sizeable deficits.

**B4. Now suppose r = 11% and g = 6%, starting again at 80% of GDP with a zero primary balance. What happens, and what primary balance would stabilise the ratio?**

*Solution.* Now r = 11% > g = 6%, so the ratio **rises**: 0.80 × 1.11 / 1.06 = 0.80 × 1.0472 ≈ **0.838, about 83.8%**. To *stabilise* the ratio the government must run a primary surplus roughly equal to (r − g) × debt ratio = (0.11 − 0.06) × 0.80 = 0.05 × 0.80 = **0.04, i.e. a primary surplus of about 4% of GDP**. When r exceeds g, discipline (a primary surplus) is required just to stand still.

**B5. A budget announces a borrowing programme 15% larger than the market expected. State the likely direction of effect on (a) G-sec prices, (b) 10-year yields, (c) the shape of the yield curve.**

*Solution.* A bigger-than-expected borrowing calendar means more bond supply than priced in.
- (a) G-sec prices **fall** (excess supply).
- (b) 10-year yields **rise** (price and yield move inversely).
- (c) The curve tends to **steepen** — persistent large deficits raise the term premium investors demand to hold long-dated bonds, so long yields rise more than short yields.

**B6. India's fiscal deficit blew out to about 9% of GDP during COVID from a pre-pandemic path near 3.5%. (a) Classify the stance. (b) Why was crowding out limited at that time? (c) What framework was relaxed to allow it?**

*Solution.*
- (a) Sharply **expansionary** — a much wider deficit to support demand through the shock.
- (b) The economy had deep slack and the RBI held rates low and bought bonds, so idle savings were mobilised, interest rates barely rose, and crowding out was **minimal**.
- (c) The **FRBM Act** (Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management, 2003) deficit ceilings were relaxed — as they have been repeatedly, most dramatically in the pandemic.

**B7. Explain, with the r-versus-g logic, how a confidence collapse turned Greek debt from awkward into unsustainable in 2010-12.**

*Solution.* Greece owed debt in **euros — a currency it could not print** — held heavily by foreign banks. As doubts grew, investors demanded far higher yields; Greek 10-year yields spiked above 30%. That surge **pushed r far above g**, so interest compounded faster than the economy could grow, and the debt arithmetic turned explosive. Higher yields worsened sustainability, which justified still-higher yields — a self-fulfilling **doom loop**, broken only when the ECB pledged to do "whatever it takes." The number (~130%) was less decisive than currency, holders, and confidence.

**B8. A capex-heavy budget assigns a multiplier of 2.5 to ₹1,000 crore of infrastructure spending and a multiplier of 0.9 to ₹1,000 crore of subsidy. Compute the output impact of each and state the policy implication.**

*Solution.*
- Capex: 2.5 × ₹1,000 crore = **₹2,500 crore** of output.
- Subsidy: 0.9 × ₹1,000 crore = **₹900 crore** of output.
The same rupee outlay produces nearly three times the demand impact when spent on productive assets rather than subsidies (part of which is saved and which builds no capacity). This is precisely why recent Indian budgets shifted the *composition* of spending toward capital expenditure even while consolidating the headline deficit.

## Section C — Interview-Style (Model Answers)

**C1. "Walk me through exactly how a budget announcement moves the government bond market."**

*Model answer.* Start with the deficit, because a deficit must be financed by issuing bonds. The **borrowing calendar** — the schedule of G-sec auctions — is the key release. If the announced borrowing is larger than the market expected, supply of bonds rises relative to demand, so prices fall and yields rise, most visibly at the long end where the **term premium** widens, steepening the curve. Two more channels reinforce this: if the stimulus looks likely to stoke inflation, markets price a higher expected **central-bank rate path**, lifting yields; and if the deficit path looks unsustainable, investors add a **sovereign risk premium**, which feeds rating-agency reviews. Conversely, a credible consolidation that shrinks borrowing rallies bonds and can flatten the curve. Because the government yield curve is the risk-free benchmark, these moves ripple into every corporate bond, loan, and equity valuation in the economy.

**C2. "Is the fiscal multiplier a fixed number? Defend your answer."**

*Model answer.* No — treating it as a constant is a classic analyst error. Its size depends on four things. First, **slack**: in a deep recession with idle factories and unemployed workers, extra demand raises real output and the multiplier is large; near full capacity it just raises prices and the real multiplier collapses. Second, the **MPC of recipients**: money given to poorer households, who spend nearly all of it, multiplies more than money to the rich, who save. Third, the **type of spending**: capital expenditure that builds productive assets multiplies more than a general tax cut, part of which is saved — Indian estimates put capex multipliers well above 2 and subsidy multipliers near or below 1. Fourth, **monetary offset**: if a fiscal expansion prompts the central bank to hike rates, that dampens private demand and shrinks the effective multiplier. So the honest answer is a range that is state-dependent, not a single figure.

**C3. "When does government borrowing crowd out private investment, and when does it crowd it in?"**

*Model answer.* It comes down to the state of the economy. The **classical worry** is that near full employment, savings are fully employed, so when the government borrows heavily it competes for a fixed pool of funds, pushes interest rates up, and displaces private investment roughly one-for-one — crowding out. But in a **slump**, savings sit idle, there is spare capacity, and rates are pinned low, so government borrowing mobilises money that would otherwise have done nothing; rates barely move and crowding out is minimal. Better still, by reviving demand, public investment can **crowd in** private investment — firms invest once they see customers returning. There is also an open-economy version: fiscal expansion that lifts domestic rates can attract capital, strengthen the currency, and crowd out net exports — the Mundell-Fleming channel. So the correct stance is "it depends on slack," not a blanket rule.

**C4. "The UK mini-budget of September 2022 — what does it teach a finance professional?"**

*Model answer.* The Chancellor announced large **unfunded** tax cuts with no credible plan to finance the resulting borrowing. The bond market's reaction was violent and near-instant: gilt yields spiked, the pound fell, and leveraged pension funds using liability-driven strategies faced collapse, forcing an emergency Bank of England intervention. Within weeks the policy was reversed and the government fell. The lesson is that **fiscal credibility is priced by the bond market in real time** — markets can discipline a government faster than any election. It is the cleanest modern demonstration that *how* a deficit is financed and communicated matters as much as its size, and that unfunded giveaways with no consolidation path get punished immediately.

**C5. "Why has India been able to run large deficits that would destabilise other economies?"**

*Model answer.* Several reasons converge. First and most important, India has historically enjoyed **g > r** — nominal GDP growth (real growth plus inflation) has often exceeded the average interest rate on government debt, so the debt ratio tends to fall or stay stable even with sizeable deficits. Second, the debt is **overwhelmingly rupee-denominated and domestically held**, so there is no foreign-currency mismatch and no fickle-foreign-holder flight risk — the trigger for most emerging-market crises. Third, India runs a **rules framework (the FRBM Act)** and has signalled a credible consolidation path from the 9% COVID peak back toward 4.5%. Fourth, structural demand for G-secs — including 2024's **JPMorgan bond-index inclusion**, which channels tens of billions of dollars of foreign buying — supports prices. None of this makes deficits costless, but it explains why the same ratio is safer in India than in a slow-growing, foreign-currency borrower.

**C6. "Give me the three fiscal case studies every analyst should have ready, and the one lesson from each."**

*Model answer.* First, the **US CARES Act, 2020** — roughly $2.2 trillion of stimulus into an economy with deep slack and the Fed at zero, so crowding out was minimal and demand recovered fast; but the 2021-22 inflation surge showed how stimulus can **overshoot** when the economy rebounds faster than expected. Lesson: the multiplier and the inflation risk both depend on how much slack remains. Second, the **euro-area crisis, 2010-12** — Greece and others proved that debt sustainability is about **currency and confidence**, not just the ratio: euro-denominated debt they could not print, held by foreigners, spiralled into a doom loop. Third, the **UK mini-budget, 2022** — unfunded tax cuts met instant bond-market discipline, showing **credibility is priced in real time**. Together they cover the demand side, the sustainability side, and the credibility side of fiscal policy.

## Section D — MCQs (With Reasoning)

**D1. The fiscal deficit is best defined as:**
A) Total revenue minus total expenditure
B) The amount the government must borrow in a year (expenditure minus revenue excluding borrowing)
C) The budget balance excluding interest payments
D) The shortfall on the day-to-day revenue account

**Answer: B.** The fiscal deficit is precisely the year's borrowing requirement. C describes the *primary* deficit; D describes the *revenue* deficit.

**D2. In the identity AD = C + I + G + (X − M), taxation affects demand by:**
A) Entering AD directly as a component
B) Changing disposable income and hence C and I
C) Reducing government spending G
D) Altering net exports only

**Answer: B.** Taxes work one step removed — through disposable income and after-tax profits, which change consumption and investment. Only G enters AD directly.

**D3. With MPC = 0.8, the simple spending multiplier is:**
A) 1.25
B) 2
C) 5
D) 8

**Answer: C.** Multiplier = 1 / (1 − 0.8) = 1 / 0.2 = 5. (A is 1/MPC, a distractor.)

**D4. The tax multiplier is smaller in magnitude than the spending multiplier because:**
A) Taxes are collected with a lag
B) Part of a tax cut is saved, so the first round is only MPC times the cut
C) Governments never cut taxes fully
D) The tax multiplier is positive

**Answer: B.** A ₹100 tax cut injects only MPC × ₹100 in the first round (the rest is saved), whereas ₹100 of spending injects the full amount. The tax multiplier is −MPC/(1 − MPC), negative and smaller in magnitude.

**D5. Automatic stabilisers are best described as:**
A) Deliberate stimulus packages passed each year
B) Tax-and-transfer features that dampen the cycle without new legislation
C) Central bank interest-rate changes
D) One-off capital expenditure programmes

**Answer: B.** Progressive taxes and welfare spending move counter-cyclically on their own, with no decision and no lag. A, C, and D all require deliberate action.

**D6. Crowding out is likely to be strongest when:**
A) The economy is in a deep recession with idle savings
B) Interest rates are pinned near zero
C) The economy is near full employment
D) The central bank is buying bonds

**Answer: C.** Near full employment, savings are fully used, so government borrowing competes directly for funds, raising rates and displacing private investment. In a slump (A, B, D) crowding out is weak.

**D7. If the interest rate on debt (r) exceeds the growth rate (g), the debt-to-GDP ratio:**
A) Always falls automatically
B) Stays fixed regardless of the budget
C) Tends to rise unless the government runs a primary surplus
D) Becomes irrelevant

**Answer: C.** When r > g, interest compounds faster than the economy grows, so the ratio rises unless offset by a primary surplus. Only when g > r does the ratio tend to fall on its own.

**D8. The structural (cyclically-adjusted) balance is used to:**
A) Measure this year's total borrowing
B) Isolate the discretionary fiscal stance by removing cycle-driven effects
C) Calculate interest payments on debt
D) Track the current account deficit

**Answer: B.** It shows the deficit that would exist at potential output, revealing whether the government is actively loosening or tightening beneath the automatic, cycle-driven movements.

**D9. Which spending type generally carries the highest multiplier in India?**
A) Interest payments
B) General subsidies
C) Capital expenditure on infrastructure
D) A broad income-tax cut

**Answer: C.** Capex builds productive assets and injects the full outlay into demand; Indian estimates put its multiplier well above 2, versus near or below 1 for subsidies and tax cuts (part of which is saved).

**D10. Japan sustains a debt ratio above 250% of GDP while Greece could not sustain ~130% chiefly because:**
A) Japan has a smaller economy
B) Japan borrows in its own currency from domestic savers at near-zero rates
C) Greece never had a deficit
D) Debt ratios are meaningless

**Answer: B.** Own-currency, domestically-held, near-zero-rate debt is far safer than foreign-held euro debt a country cannot print. Sustainability turns on currency, holders, and r-versus-g — not the raw ratio.

**D11. The revenue deficit specifically signals that the government is:**
A) Running a primary surplus
B) Borrowing to fund day-to-day (revenue) spending like salaries and interest
C) Building durable capital assets
D) Reducing its total debt

**Answer: B.** A revenue deficit means borrowing to cover current, non-asset-creating spending — a red flag, because the borrowing builds nothing that raises future capacity to repay.

**D12. A larger-than-expected government borrowing calendar typically causes G-sec yields to:**
A) Fall, because demand rises
B) Rise, because bond supply increases relative to demand
C) Stay unchanged
D) Fall, because ratings improve

**Answer: B.** More bonds than the market priced in means excess supply: prices fall and yields rise, often steepening the curve via a wider term premium. This is the direct fiscal-to-yield-curve link.
