1. Forwards & Futures

1.1 What a derivative is

A derivative is a contract whose value is derived from an underlying asset, rate or index — a stock, an index like Nifty 50, a commodity, a currency pair, or an interest rate. Derivatives let you take a view, hedge an exposure, or arbitrage a price discrepancy without necessarily owning the underlying. The four building blocks you will be asked about are forwards, futures, options and swaps; this guide focuses on the first three, which dominate exchange-traded desks and the Indian F&O market.

1.2 Forwards vs futures

A forward contract is a private (over-the-counter, OTC) agreement to buy or sell an asset at a fixed price on a future date. A futures contract is the exchange-traded, standardised cousin of the forward: same economic idea, but standardised in size, expiry and quality, guaranteed by a clearing house, and settled daily. On Indian markets, index futures (Nifty, Bank Nifty) and single-stock futures trade on the NSE with a central counterparty guaranteeing performance.

1.3 Payoff at expiry

Both forwards and futures are linear (also called delta-one) instruments — payoff moves one-for-one with the underlying. If you are long a forward/future struck at price K and the underlying settles at ST, your payoff is ST − K. If you are short, it is K − ST. There is no optionality: losses are symmetric and unbounded on both sides.

Long forward/future payoff
Payoff = ST − K
where: ST = underlying price at expiry; K = agreed delivery / futures price. Short payoff = K − ST.

1.4 Pricing — cost of carry

The fair forward/futures price is set by no-arbitrage: if you could buy the asset today with borrowed money, hold it to the delivery date, and lock in a sale via the forward, the forward price must exactly cover your financing cost minus any income the asset throws off (dividends, coupons) plus any storage cost. That relationship is the cost-of-carry model.

Cost-of-carry (continuous compounding)
F0 = S0 · e(r − q + u) · T
where: F0 = forward/futures price today; S0 = spot price; r = risk-free rate; q = dividend/income yield; u = storage cost yield; T = time to maturity in years. For a non-dividend equity index, u = 0.
Discrete version (equity index)
F0 = (S0 − I) · (1 + r·T) or F0 = S0 · (1 + (r − q)·T)
where: I = present value of dividends paid before expiry. The higher the interest rate, the higher the futures price above spot; the higher the dividend yield, the lower it is.
Worked Example 1 — Fair value of a Nifty future

Nifty spot S0 = 22,000. Risk-free rate r = 7% p.a. Dividend yield q = 1.5% p.a. Time to expiry = 1 month = 1/12 year (≈0.0833).

Using the simple discrete form: F0 = 22,000 × (1 + (0.07 − 0.015) × 0.0833)

= 22,000 × (1 + 0.055 × 0.0833) = 22,000 × (1 + 0.004583) = 22,000 × 1.004583 ≈ 22,101.

So the one-month future should trade about 101 points above spot. If it trades at 22,150 it is "rich" (expensive) and a cash-and-carry arbitrage — buy the basket, sell the future — becomes attractive, ignoring transaction costs.

1.5 Basis, contango and backwardation

The basis is the difference between spot and futures. Definitions vary by desk, so state yours: here we use Basis = Futures − Spot. As expiry approaches, the carry shrinks and the basis converges to zero — futures and spot must meet at settlement (convergence).

Basis
Basis = F − S
where: F = futures price, S = spot. Some commodity desks define Basis = Spot − Futures — always confirm the convention.
  • Contango: futures > spot (positive carry markets — equities usually, when r > q). The forward curve slopes upward.
  • Backwardation: futures < spot. Common when the asset yields a high income or "convenience yield" (e.g. a supply-squeezed commodity, or an index with a very high dividend yield, or when borrowing the stock is expensive/hard).

1.6 Margining & mark-to-market

Because a clearing house guarantees futures, it collects initial margin (a good-faith deposit sized to a worst-plausible daily move, in India via the SPAN + exposure margin system) and then re-settles positions every evening. Daily gains are credited and losses debited from your margin account — this is mark-to-market (MTM). If your balance falls below the maintenance level you get a margin call to top up. MTM is what keeps counterparty risk tiny: no one is allowed to run up a large unpaid loss.

Worked Example 2 — Daily MTM on a long stock future

You go long 1 lot (assume 500 shares, conceptual) of a stock future at ₹1,000. Contract value = ₹5,00,000.

  • Day 1 close ₹1,020: gain (1,020 − 1,000) × 500 = +₹10,000 credited.
  • Day 2 close ₹990: loss (990 − 1,020) × 500 = −₹15,000 debited (may trigger a margin call).
  • Day 3 you close at ₹1,005: gain (1,005 − 990) × 500 = +₹7,500.

Net over three days = 10,000 − 15,000 + 7,500 = +₹2,500, which equals (1,005 − 1,000) × 500 — the same as a single settlement, but realised in daily slices.

Common trap: "Futures and forwards are priced identically." Only approximately. With deterministic interest rates they have the same value; with stochastic rates, daily MTM creates a small correlation effect between the futures gain and the reinvestment rate, so futures and forward prices can differ slightly. For interviews, know the intuition, not the convexity maths.

1.7 Indian F&O context

On the NSE, the flagship equity derivatives are index futures and options on Nifty 50 and Bank Nifty, plus single-stock futures and options on a defined set of large caps. A few practical points a desk expects you to know conceptually (avoid quoting exact numbers, as lot sizes and margins are revised periodically by SEBI/exchanges):

  • Lot size: derivatives trade in fixed lots (a bundle of underlying units), not single shares, so notional per contract is sizeable. The exchange revises lot sizes to keep contract value within a target band.
  • Expiry cycle: index options have weekly and monthly expiries; the huge weekly-options turnover in Nifty/Bank Nifty is a defining feature of the Indian market. Monthly contracts expire on a scheduled day; the near-month is the most liquid.
  • Settlement: index F&O is cash-settled (no delivery); stock derivatives are physically settled on expiry if held to expiry.
  • Style: index options are European; stock options are American-style.
  • Margins: collected via the SPAN + exposure framework, with additional margins for short options; the clearing corporation (NSE Clearing) is the central counterparty.
  • India VIX: the exchange's volatility index, derived from Nifty option prices — effectively the market's near-term implied volatility and a barometer of fear.

Your day-to-day workflow — tracking implied vol, open interest and the Greeks across the Nifty/Bank Nifty option chain — is exactly the practical application of Sections 5 and 6. Open interest (OI), the number of outstanding contracts, is a positioning signal: rising price with rising OI suggests fresh longs; rising price with falling OI suggests short covering. Combine OI shifts with IV to read whether a move is being driven by new option buying (IV up) or writing (IV down).

1.8 Hedging with futures — hedge ratio, minimum-variance & basis risk

Futures are the workhorse of hedging because they are cheap, liquid and delta-one. To hedge a cash position you take an offsetting futures position sized by a hedge ratio. If you can hedge with a future on the same asset the natural ratio is 1 (one future per unit of exposure). When you must hedge with a different but related future (a cross-hedge — e.g. hedging a mid-cap basket with Nifty futures), the right size is the minimum-variance hedge ratio.

Minimum-variance hedge ratio & number of contracts
h* = ρ · (σS / σF) = Cov(ΔS, ΔF) / Var(ΔF)
N* = h* · (VA / VF) (rounded to whole contracts)
where: h* = optimal hedge ratio; ρ = correlation of spot and futures price changes; σS, σF = std dev of spot & futures changes; N* = number of futures contracts; VA = value of the position being hedged; VF = value of one futures contract. h* is exactly the slope (beta) of a regression of ΔS on ΔF.

For an equity portfolio the same logic uses beta: to hedge (or shift beta) with index futures, N* = (βtarget − βportfolio) · Vportfolio / Vfuture. To fully hedge, βtarget = 0, so you short β · V/Vfuture index futures.

Worked Example 2b — Beta hedge with Nifty futures

You hold a ₹5 crore equity portfolio with β = 1.2 vs Nifty. One Nifty future = 22,000 points × ₹50 lot multiplier (conceptual) = ₹11,00,000 notional. To fully hedge: N* = 1.2 × 5,00,00,000 / 11,00,000 ≈ 1.2 × 45.5 ≈ 55 contracts short. Now a −1% Nifty move (portfolio loses ≈1.2% = ₹6 lakh) is offset by a gain on 55 short futures. Residual is basis risk, not directional risk.

Basis risk is the risk that spot and futures do not move perfectly together, so the hedge is imperfect — the P&L of a hedged position is driven by how the basis (F − S) changes over the hedge's life. It arises from cross-hedging (different underlying), a mismatch between the hedge horizon and futures expiry (requiring a rollover), or supply/demand distortions in the future. A perfect same-asset, same-date hedge has zero basis risk; every other hedge trades directional risk for the smaller basis risk.

1.9 Index arbitrage & cash-and-carry

Index arbitrage enforces the cost-of-carry relation between the index future and the basket of underlying stocks. If the future is rich (above fair value), arbitrageurs do a cash-and-carry: buy the stock basket, short the future, and unwind at convergence, locking in the mispricing minus costs. If the future is cheap, they do a reverse cash-and-carry: short the basket, buy the future. In practice frictions (transaction costs, the stock-borrow cost, dividends uncertainty, and the difficulty of trading the whole basket) create a no-arbitrage band around fair value inside which the future can wander.

Key takeaways: Forwards = OTC & custom; futures = exchange, margined, MTM daily. Both are linear/delta-one. Fair price = spot grown at cost of carry (r − q). Basis converges to zero at expiry; contango = futures above spot, backwardation = below. In India: Nifty/Bank Nifty weekly & monthly expiries, index options European & cash-settled, SPAN margins, India VIX = implied vol.

2. Options

2.1 The two basic contracts

An option gives the buyer a right, not an obligation. A call is the right to buy the underlying at the strike K; a put is the right to sell at K. The buyer pays a premium up front. The seller (writer) receives the premium and takes on the obligation to deliver if the buyer exercises. European options exercise only at expiry; American options any time up to expiry. Indian index options (Nifty, Bank Nifty) are European and cash-settled; Indian stock options are American-style (though most are traded, not exercised early).

2.2 Payoffs at expiry

Option payoffs at expiry (before premium)
Long call payoff = max(ST − K, 0)
Long put payoff = max(K − ST, 0)
where: ST = underlying at expiry; K = strike. The writer's payoff is the negative of the buyer's. P&L subtracts (adds) the premium paid (received).

Because payoffs are non-linear (a "kinked" hockey-stick), options are the primary tool for shaping asymmetric risk. The buyer's loss is capped at the premium; the buyer's gain can be large (unlimited for a call). The writer's profit is capped at the premium; the writer's loss can be large — this asymmetry is central to risk on an options desk.

Worked Example 3 — Call and put P&L at expiry

Nifty 22,000 call bought for a premium of ₹150 (per unit; ignore lot multiplier).

  • Nifty expires at 22,000: payoff max(22,000 − 22,000, 0) = 0. P&L = 0 − 150 = −₹150 (lose the premium).
  • Nifty at 22,100: payoff 100. P&L = 100 − 150 = −₹50. (In-the-money but not yet past break-even 22,150.)
  • Nifty at 22,400: payoff 400. P&L = 400 − 150 = +₹250.

Now a Nifty 22,000 put bought for ₹130:

  • Nifty at 21,700: payoff max(22,000 − 21,700, 0) = 300. P&L = 300 − 130 = +₹170.
  • Nifty at 22,000 or above: payoff 0. P&L = −₹130 (full premium lost). Break-even = 22,000 − 130 = 21,870.

2.3 Moneyness

Moneyness describes where the strike sits relative to spot:

TermCallPut
ITM (in-the-money)S > KS < K
ATM (at-the-money)S ≈ KS ≈ K
OTM (out-of-the-money)S < KS > K

2.4 Intrinsic value vs time value

An option premium splits into two parts:

Premium decomposition
Option premium = Intrinsic value + Time value
Intrinsic value (call) = max(S − K, 0); Intrinsic value (put) = max(K − S, 0)
Time value = premium − intrinsic. It reflects the chance the option moves further ITM before expiry, and is largest for ATM options. At expiry, time value = 0 and the option is worth only intrinsic value.

OTM options have zero intrinsic value — their whole premium is time value. Time value decays as expiry nears (this decay is theta, Section 5). ATM options carry the most time value because their outcome is most uncertain.

Common trap: "Intrinsic value can be negative." No. Intrinsic value is floored at zero by the max(·,0). An OTM option has zero intrinsic value, never negative — you would simply not exercise.

2.5 No-arbitrage price bounds

Even without a pricing model, option prices are boxed in by no-arbitrage. These bounds are a favourite interview test of first principles.

Bounds (non-dividend, European)
Lower: c ≥ max(S0 − K·e−rT, 0); p ≥ max(K·e−rT − S0, 0)
Upper: c ≤ S0; p ≤ K·e−rT (European); p ≤ K (American)
A call can never be worth more than the stock (else buy the stock instead). A put can never exceed the PV of the strike. The lower bound says a call is worth at least its "forward intrinsic" S0 − PV(K). Violations create riskless arbitrage.

A key corollary: because the European call lower bound c ≥ S0 − K·e−rT exceeds the intrinsic value S0 − K (since e−rT < 1), a call always carries positive time value — which is why it is never optimal to exercise an American call on a non-dividend stock early (see below).

2.6 American vs European & early exercise

European options exercise only at expiry; American options any time up to expiry, so an American option is worth at least as much as its European twin (more optionality). When is early exercise actually optimal?

  • American call on a non-dividend stock: never optimal to exercise early. You would throw away remaining time value and pay the strike sooner (losing interest). Better to sell the call. So CAmerican = CEuropean here.
  • American call on a dividend-paying stock: can be optimal to exercise just before a large dividend (to capture the dividend that would otherwise drop the stock).
  • American put: can be optimal to exercise early even without dividends — deep in-the-money, you would rather have K in cash now (earning interest) than wait, since the put's upside is capped at K.
Common trap: "Always exercise an in-the-money American option early." Wrong for calls on non-dividend stocks — you sacrifice time value and interest on the strike. Early exercise is mainly a put phenomenon (and a call phenomenon only around dividends).
Key takeaways: Call = right to buy, put = right to sell. Buyer risk = premium (capped); writer risk = large. Payoffs are non-linear. Premium = intrinsic + time value; time value peaks ATM and decays to zero at expiry.

3. Put–Call Parity

3.1 The relationship

Put–call parity is a no-arbitrage identity linking the prices of a European call and put with the same strike and expiry on the same underlying. It says a long call plus cash equal to the present value of the strike replicates a long put plus the stock. If it does not hold, a riskless arbitrage exists.

Put–call parity (no dividends)
C + K·e−rT = P + S0
where: C = European call price; P = European put price (same K, T); K = strike; S0 = spot; r = risk-free rate; T = time to expiry. K·e−rT = present value of the strike. With dividends of PV I: C + K·e−rT + I = P + S0.

The intuition: a "fiduciary call" (long call + cash PV(K) invested to grow to K) and a "protective put" (long stock + long put) both give you exactly max(ST, K) at expiry, so today they must cost the same.

3.2 Rearrangements you should be able to quote

  • Price a put from a call: P = C − S0 + K·e−rT
  • Synthetic long stock: S0 = C − P + K·e−rT (long call + short put + cash)
  • Synthetic forward: C − P = S0 − K·e−rT (long call + short put = long forward)
Worked Example 4 — Put–call parity check & arbitrage

S0 = 100, K = 100, r = 6% p.a., T = 0.5 yr. A call trades at ₹7. What should the put be?

PV(K) = 100 × e−0.06×0.5 = 100 × e−0.03 = 100 × 0.97045 = 97.045.

Fair put P = C − S0 + PV(K) = 7 − 100 + 97.045 = ₹4.045.

Suppose the put actually trades at ₹5 (too expensive). Parity is violated: P + S = 105 vs C + PV(K) = 104.045. Arbitrage: sell the put, sell the stock, buy the call, invest PV(K). You collect 105, spend 104.045, pocket ≈₹0.955 risk-free per unit; the position self-liquidates to zero payoff at expiry regardless of ST.

Common trap: Put–call parity in its clean form holds for European options only. For American options (which can be exercised early), it becomes a pair of inequalities, not an equality. Also remember to add the present value of dividends on the correct side.
Key takeaways: C + PV(K) = P + S. It is a no-arbitrage identity, model-free (no Black–Scholes needed). Use it to derive synthetics, to price a put from a call, and to spot mispricings.

4. Option Pricing — Black–Scholes–Merton & Binomial

4.1 Black–Scholes–Merton intuition

The Black–Scholes–Merton (BSM) model gives a closed-form fair value for a European option. Its key insight is risk-neutral replication: you can continuously trade the stock and cash to build a portfolio that exactly mimics the option's payoff. Because that replication is riskless, the option must earn the risk-free rate — so the expected return of the stock drops out of the pricing, and only volatility, rates, and time matter. This is why the formula does not contain the stock's expected return.

Black–Scholes (European call, no dividends)
C = S0·N(d1) − K·e−rT·N(d2)
P = K·e−rT·N(−d2) − S0·N(−d1)
d1 = [ln(S0/K) + (r + σ²/2)·T] / (σ√T), d2 = d1 − σ√T
where: N(·) = cumulative standard normal distribution; S0 = spot; K = strike; r = risk-free rate; σ = volatility; T = time to expiry. N(d2) = risk-neutral probability the call finishes ITM; N(d1) = the call's delta.

BSM assumptions (and why they matter)

  • Log-returns are normally distributed; price is lognormal — real markets have fat tails.
  • Constant, known volatility — real vol changes and is itself volatile (hence the smile).
  • Continuous trading, no transaction costs, no jumps — real hedging is discrete and costly.
  • Constant risk-free rate; European exercise.

4.2 The binomial model

The binomial (Cox–Ross–Rubinstein) model prices options by chopping time into small steps. In each step the stock can move up by factor u or down by factor d. You compute the option payoff at the final nodes, then work backwards, at each node discounting the risk-neutral expected value. As the number of steps → ∞, the binomial price converges to Black–Scholes. Its great virtue is flexibility: it naturally handles American early exercise (at each node, take max of "hold" vs "exercise now").

Risk-neutral probability (one step)
p = (erΔt − d) / (u − d), option value = e−rΔt·[p·Vup + (1−p)·Vdown]
where: u, d = up/down move factors (often u = eσ√Δt, d = 1/u); Δt = step length; p = risk-neutral (not real-world) up probability; Vup, Vdown = option values at the next nodes.
Worked Example 5 — One-step binomial call

S0 = 100, one step of 1 year. Up: S = 120 (u = 1.2). Down: S = 90 (d = 0.9). r = 5%. Call strike K = 105.

Payoffs: Vup = max(120 − 105, 0) = 15; Vdown = max(90 − 105, 0) = 0.

p = (e0.05 − 0.9)/(1.2 − 0.9) = (1.05127 − 0.9)/0.3 = 0.15127/0.3 = 0.5042.

Call = e−0.05 × [0.5042 × 15 + 0.4958 × 0] = 0.95123 × 7.563 = ₹7.19.

Note we never used the real probability of an up move — risk-neutral valuation only needs p.

Common trap: The p in the binomial model is the risk-neutral probability, an artificial pricing device, not the market's true expectation of an up move. Candidates often call it "the probability the stock rises" — flag that it is not.

4.3 Two-step binomial & backward induction

Extend the tree to two steps and you value by backward induction: price the option at the final (terminal) nodes, discount back one layer at a time using the same risk-neutral p at every node. For an American option, at each intermediate node you also compare the discounted "hold" value with the immediate "exercise" value and keep the larger.

Worked Example 5b — Two-step binomial call

S0 = 100, two steps of 6 months each (T = 1 yr total). u = 1.2, d = 0.9 per step, r = 5% p.a., K = 105. Per-step discount factor e−0.05×0.5 = e−0.025 = 0.97531; risk-neutral p = (e0.05×0.5 − 0.9)/(1.2 − 0.9) = (1.02532 − 0.9)/0.3 = 0.4177.

Terminal stock values: Suu = 144, Sud = 108, Sdd = 81. Payoffs: Vuu = 39, Vud = 3, Vdd = 0.

Node up: Vu = 0.97531 × [0.4177×39 + 0.5823×3] = 0.97531 × [16.29 + 1.747] = 0.97531 × 18.037 = 17.59.

Node down: Vd = 0.97531 × [0.4177×3 + 0.5823×0] = 0.97531 × 1.253 = 1.222.

Today: C = 0.97531 × [0.4177×17.59 + 0.5823×1.222] = 0.97531 × [7.347 + 0.712] = 0.97531 × 8.059 = ≈ ₹7.86. More steps → the price converges to Black–Scholes.

Key takeaways: BSM prices European options via risk-neutral replication; the stock's expected return drops out; volatility is the only unobservable input. Binomial = discrete, converges to BSM, and handles American exercise. Both rest on no-arbitrage.

5. The Greeks

The Greeks measure the sensitivity of an option's value to each input. They are the working language of an options desk: they tell you what you are exposed to and how to hedge it. Learn the signs cold — they are the most commonly tested facts.

5.1 Delta (Δ)

Delta
Δ = ∂V/∂S
Rate of change of option value per ₹1 change in the underlying. Long call Δ ∈ (0, +1); long put Δ ∈ (−1, 0). ATM call ≈ +0.5, ATM put ≈ −0.5. Also the hedge ratio and ≈ risk-neutral probability of finishing ITM (for a call, N(d1)).

Delta is the first-order price sensitivity and the number one Greek for hedging. A delta of +0.5 means the option gains ₹0.50 for a ₹1 rise in the underlying. As a call goes deep ITM, delta → +1 (behaves like the stock); deep OTM, delta → 0.

5.2 Gamma (Γ)

Gamma
Γ = ∂²V/∂S² = ∂Δ/∂S
Rate of change of delta per ₹1 move in the underlying — the "acceleration". Always positive for long options (calls and puts), negative for short options. Largest for ATM options near expiry.

Gamma tells you how fast your delta hedge goes stale. Long gamma is a comfortable place: as the market rises your delta rises (you get longer into a rally) and as it falls your delta falls — you automatically "buy low, sell high" when re-hedging. Short gamma is the opposite and dangerous: you are forced to buy high and sell low to stay hedged, bleeding money in choppy markets. Option sellers are short gamma.

5.3 Theta (Θ)

Theta
Θ = ∂V/∂t (change in value per unit of calendar time passing)
Time decay. Negative for long options (value erodes as expiry approaches — you are "paying rent"), positive for short options. Most negative for ATM options close to expiry.

Gamma and theta trade off: being long gamma (owning options) means paying theta; being short gamma (selling options) means collecting theta but carrying tail risk. "You get paid theta for being short gamma" is the core risk-reward of an options book.

5.4 Vega (ν)

Vega
ν = ∂V/∂σ
Change in option value per 1 percentage-point change in volatility. Positive for long options (both calls and puts) — more vol = more valuable optionality. Largest for ATM options with long time to expiry.
Common trap: "Vega is the Greek letter for volatility sensitivity." Vega is not a letter in the Greek alphabet (there is no "vega"). It is desk jargon, often written with the symbol ν (nu). Interviewers love this. Also: vega is positive for both long calls and long puts — buying either gets you long volatility.

5.5 Rho (ρ)

Rho
ρ = ∂V/∂r
Change in option value per 1 percentage-point change in the risk-free rate. Positive for calls, negative for puts. Usually the least important Greek for short-dated equity options; matters more for long-dated options and rates products.

5.7 Delta hedging

Delta hedging neutralises directional risk. If you are long a call with delta +0.5 on 1,000 units, you are long 500 "delta-equivalent" shares; you sell 500 shares (or short futures) to make the book delta-neutral. Now small moves in the underlying do not change your P&L — to first order. But delta changes as the underlying moves (that is gamma), so you must re-hedge dynamically. A delta-neutral, long-gamma book profits from big moves in either direction but bleeds theta if the market sits still; that is the essence of volatility trading.

Worked Example 6 — Delta of a hedge

You are long 200 Nifty ATM calls (delta +0.52 each, contract multiplier ignored). Portfolio delta = 200 × 0.52 = +104 units long.

To be delta-neutral you short 104 units of Nifty futures (delta of a future ≈ +1). Now net delta ≈ 0.

Nifty rallies; call delta rises to 0.60 (gamma effect). New option delta = 200 × 0.60 = +120; you are short only 104 futures, so net delta = +16 — you are now net long into the rally (long gamma working for you). You sell 16 more futures to re-hedge, locking in the gain from the move. Had you been short the calls, the same move would have left you net short into a rising market — the pain of short gamma.

Key takeaways: Delta = direction/hedge ratio; gamma = convexity/how fast delta moves; theta = time decay; vega = vol sensitivity; rho = rate sensitivity. Long options: +gamma, +vega, −theta. Delta-hedge to kill direction, then manage the residual gamma/vega/theta.

6. Volatility

6.1 Historical vs implied volatility

Historical (realised) volatility is the standard deviation of past returns — a backward-looking, statistical measurement of how much the asset actually moved. Implied volatility (IV) is the volatility figure that, plugged into Black–Scholes, reproduces the option's current market price. It is forward-looking — the market's expectation of future volatility, backed out from prices. You cannot observe it directly; you invert the pricing formula to find it.

Annualising historical volatility
σannual = σdaily × √252
252 = trading days per year. σdaily = standard deviation of daily log-returns. Volatility scales with the square root of time — the same √t rule used in VaR scaling.

Common trap: Confusing the two. Historical vol is measured from returns; implied vol is extracted from prices and is the market's view of the future. When IV > realised, options look "expensive" (sellers favoured); when IV < realised, "cheap" (buyers favoured). On your F&O desk, the India VIX is essentially the market's implied vol on Nifty options.

6.2 The volatility smile / skew

Black–Scholes assumes a single constant volatility, so every strike should imply the same IV. In reality, if you plot IV against strike you get a smile (IV higher for deep OTM and ITM strikes than ATM) or, in equity indices, a skew / smirk (OTM puts carry higher IV than OTM calls). Why? Markets crash down faster than they rally up, so downside protection (puts) is bid up; and real return distributions have fat tails that BSM's normal assumption ignores. The skew is the market's correction to the model.

6.3 Vega exposure & the term structure

A book's vega exposure is its P&L sensitivity to a shift in implied volatility. Long options = long vega (profits if IV rises); short options = short vega. Vol also has a term structure (IV varies by expiry) and you can trade the smile itself (skew trades, calendar spreads). As a vol trader you separate three things: realised vol (what happens), implied vol (what you pay/receive), and the P&L of the gamma/theta grind between them.

6.4 Realised vs implied on the desk — the vol trader's edge

The central bet in volatility trading is realised versus implied. If you buy an ATM option and delta-hedge it continuously, your P&L (before costs) is driven by the gap between how much the underlying actually moves (realised vol) and the implied vol you paid. Buy options when you think realised will exceed implied; sell when you think implied is too rich versus what will realise. On Indian weekly expiries, option writers are effectively short realised vol and collect theta; buyers pay theta hoping for a large realised move (an event, a gap). The vol smile then tells you the price of that bet is not uniform across strikes — downside puts cost more in vol terms because of skew.

Key takeaways: Historical vol = measured from the past; implied vol = the market's forward-looking vol embedded in option prices (India VIX for Nifty). The smile/skew shows IV varies by strike — a sign that real distributions have fat tails and downside is feared. Long options = long vega. The trader's edge is realised-vs-implied: delta-hedge and profit if realised vol beats the implied you paid.

7. Option Strategies

Strategies combine options (and sometimes the underlying) to express a specific view on direction and/or volatility. Below, "max profit/loss" are at expiry and ignore commissions; net premium is debit (you pay) or credit (you receive).

7.1 Covered call

Long stock + short one OTM call. You collect premium and cap your upside at the strike; you keep downside exposure (cushioned only by the premium). Used when mildly bullish/neutral and happy to sell at the strike — an income strategy.

  • Max profit: (K − purchase price) + premium. Max loss: stock can fall to zero, less the premium cushion. View: neutral-to-mildly-bullish, low vol.

7.2 Protective put

Long stock + long put. The put is insurance — it caps your downside at K while leaving upside open. Cost = the premium. Used to hedge a long position through an uncertain event (results, budget, election).

  • Max loss: (purchase price − K) + premium. Max profit: unlimited (stock upside) − premium. View: bullish but wants a floor.

7.3 Straddle

Long a call + long a put at the same strike (usually ATM) and expiry. A pure volatility bet: you profit from a big move in either direction; you lose if the underlying sits still. Used ahead of high-uncertainty events (RBI policy, budget, earnings). A short straddle is the reverse — sell both — betting on calm; big theta income, but unlimited risk.

  • Cost: call premium + put premium (a debit). Break-evens: K ± total premium. Max loss (long): total premium if S = K at expiry. Max profit: large (unlimited on the upside).
Worked Example 7 — Long straddle P&L

Bank Nifty at 48,000. Buy the 48,000 call for ₹400 and the 48,000 put for ₹380. Total debit = ₹780.

Break-evens: 48,000 + 780 = 48,780 (upside) and 48,000 − 780 = 47,220 (downside).

  • Expiry 48,000: both expire near worthless. P&L = 0 − 780 = −₹780 (max loss).
  • Expiry 49,000: call worth 1,000, put 0. P&L = 1,000 − 780 = +₹220.
  • Expiry 46,500: put worth 1,500, call 0. P&L = 1,500 − 780 = +₹720.

You need a move greater than 780 points (≈1.6%) either way to profit. If IV was high going in, the premium is expensive and the move must be even larger — this is the "buy the rumour" trap where IV collapses after the event (vol crush) and a correct directional call still loses.

7.4 Strangle

Long an OTM call + long an OTM put (different strikes, call strike > put strike). Cheaper than a straddle (both legs OTM) but needs a bigger move to pay off. Same volatility view; wider break-evens.

  • Cost: lower than a straddle. Break-evens: upper strike + premium, lower strike − premium. Max loss: total premium. View: big move expected, cheaper entry.

7.5 Vertical spreads (bull / bear)

Buy one option and sell another of the same type and expiry but different strike. Caps both profit and loss — a defined-risk directional bet, cheaper than a naked option.

  • Bull call spread: buy lower-strike call, sell higher-strike call (net debit). Bullish, capped. Max profit = strike difference − net debit; max loss = net debit.
  • Bear put spread: buy higher-strike put, sell lower-strike put (net debit). Bearish, capped. Max profit = strike difference − net debit; max loss = net debit.
  • (Credit versions: bull put spread and bear call spread collect premium up front.)
Worked Example 8 — Bull call spread

Nifty at 22,000. Buy 22,000 call for ₹200, sell 22,300 call for ₹80. Net debit = 200 − 80 = ₹120.

Strike difference = 300. Max profit = 300 − 120 = ₹180 (if Nifty ≥ 22,300 at expiry). Max loss = ₹120 (if Nifty ≤ 22,000). Break-even = 22,000 + 120 = 22,120.

Versus buying the naked 22,000 call (risking ₹200 for unlimited upside), the spread halves the cost and defines the risk — you sacrifice the upside beyond 22,300.

7.6 Iron condor

Sell an OTM put spread and an OTM call spread simultaneously (four legs, all same expiry): short OTM put, long further-OTM put, short OTM call, long further-OTM call. A net credit, range-bound strategy — you profit if the underlying stays between the two short strikes; both spreads cap the risk. Popular on Bank Nifty/Nifty weekly expiries to harvest theta with defined risk.

  • Max profit: net credit received (underlying finishes between the short strikes). Max loss: (width of one spread) − net credit. View: low volatility, range-bound, short vega, positive theta.

7.7 Collar

Long stock + long OTM put (a floor) + short OTM call (finances the put). A protective put made cheaper — often zero-cost if the call premium exactly funds the put. You cap both downside (at the put strike) and upside (at the call strike). Widely used to lock in gains on a holding at little or no cash outlay.

  • Max profit: (call strike − stock cost) ± net premium. Max loss: (stock cost − put strike) ± net premium. View: protect a gain, willing to cap upside.

7.8 Butterfly spread

A defined-risk, low-volatility bet that the underlying pins a central strike. A long call butterfly = buy 1 low-strike call, sell 2 middle-strike calls, buy 1 high-strike call (strikes equally spaced, net debit). Maximum profit if the underlying finishes exactly at the middle strike; small, capped loss otherwise. It is like a cheaper, tighter iron condor with a single sweet spot.

  • Max profit: (strike gap) − net debit, at the middle strike. Max loss: net debit. View: underlying stays near a specific level; short vega.

7.9 Calendar (horizontal) spread

Sell a near-dated option and buy a longer-dated option at the same strike. It profits from time decay (the near leg decays faster) and from a rise in implied vol (the long-dated leg has more vega). A net-debit, positive-theta, long-vega structure — the classic way to be long vol term-structure while collecting front-month theta. Best when the underlying sits near the strike and IV is expected to rise.

  • Max profit: around the strike at the near expiry (not a fixed formula — depends on IV). Max loss: net debit. View: range-bound short-term, long vega.

7.10 Ratio spread & backspread

A ratio spread buys and sells unequal quantities — e.g. buy 1 ATM call, sell 2 further-OTM calls (a call ratio spread). It is cheap or a small credit and profits from a modest move, but the extra short options leave unlimited risk if the underlying runs far. The backspread reverses it (sell 1, buy 2) for defined limited risk and unlimited upside on a big move — a long-volatility, convex structure.

  • Ratio (short-heavy): limited profit, unlimited risk beyond the far strike, short net vega. Backspread (long-heavy): limited loss, large profit on a big move, long vega. View: a directional-plus-volatility bet.

8. Market Risk & Value-at-Risk

8.1 What VaR is

Value-at-Risk (VaR) answers: "Over a given horizon, at a given confidence level, what loss will not be exceeded?" A 1-day 99% VaR of ₹10 lakh means: on 99% of days the loss should be no worse than ₹10 lakh; on the remaining 1% of days (roughly 2–3 days a year) it may be worse. VaR is a single-number summary of downside market risk, used for limits, capital and reporting.

Common trap (the big one): "VaR is the maximum loss." No. VaR is a threshold, not a worst case. It says nothing about how bad losses get beyond the cut-off — the tail. A 99% VaR of ₹10 lakh is silent about whether the 1% tail loss is ₹12 lakh or ₹2 crore. That is precisely what Expected Shortfall fixes.

8.2 Parametric (variance–covariance) VaR

Assume returns are normally distributed. Then VaR is just a multiple of the portfolio's standard deviation.

Parametric VaR (1 day)
VaR = zα · σ · V
where: zα = normal quantile for confidence α (1.645 for 95%, 2.326 for 99%); σ = 1-day return standard deviation (volatility); V = portfolio market value. (If mean return μ is included: VaR = (zα·σ − μ)·V; for 1-day horizons μ is usually set to 0.)
Worked Example 9 — Parametric 1-day VaR

Portfolio value V = ₹10,00,00,000 (₹10 crore). Daily volatility σ = 1.2%. Confidence 99% (z = 2.326).

1-day 99% VaR = 2.326 × 0.012 × 10,00,00,000 = 2.326 × 0.012 = 0.027912 → × 10 crore = ₹27,91,200 ≈ ₹27.9 lakh.

At 95% (z = 1.645): VaR = 1.645 × 0.012 × 10 crore = ₹19,74,000 ≈ ₹19.7 lakh. Higher confidence → larger VaR, as expected.

8.3 Scaling VaR across horizons

Square-root-of-time scaling
VaRT-day = VaR1-day × √T
Valid only if returns are i.i.d. (independent, identically distributed) with zero autocorrelation and constant vol. T = number of days.
Worked Example 10 — Scaling VaR by √t

From Example 9, the 1-day 99% VaR is ₹27.9 lakh. The 10-day 99% VaR (a common regulatory horizon) is:

VaR10 = 27.9 × √10 = 27.9 × 3.162 = ₹88.2 lakh. Note it grows with √10, not ×10 — diversification of time, provided returns don't trend.

Common trap: √t scaling assumes returns are independent with constant volatility. In real markets with volatility clustering and autocorrelation (trends, momentum), √t scaling understates risk during stress. It is a convenient approximation, not a law.

8.4 Historical simulation VaR

No distribution assumed. Take the last N days of actual returns, apply each to today's portfolio, sort the simulated P&Ls, and read off the loss at the chosen percentile. For 99% VaR with 500 days, it is roughly the 5th-worst loss. Pros: captures fat tails and real correlations, no normality assumption. Cons: assumes the past window represents the future; slow to react to new regimes; a quiet look-back understates risk.

8.5 Monte Carlo VaR

Assume a stochastic model for risk-factor returns (e.g. multivariate normal, or with fat tails / jumps), simulate thousands of scenarios, revalue the portfolio in each, sort the P&Ls, and read the percentile. Pros: flexible, handles non-linear portfolios (options) and any distribution. Cons: computationally heavy; results are only as good as the assumed model (model risk).

8.7 Expected Shortfall (CVaR)

Expected Shortfall (ES), also called Conditional VaR (CVaR) or Expected Tail Loss, is the average loss given that you are in the tail beyond VaR. Where VaR says "the loss you won't exceed 99% of the time", ES says "and when you do exceed it, this is how bad it is on average". ES is always ≥ VaR. Basel's Fundamental Review of the Trading Book (FRTB) replaced VaR with 97.5% ES precisely because ES captures tail severity and is a coherent risk measure (VaR is not — it can violate sub-additivity, i.e. it can say a diversified portfolio is riskier than the sum of its parts).

Expected Shortfall
ESα = E[ Loss | Loss > VaRα ]
The expected (average) loss conditional on the loss exceeding the VaR threshold at confidence α. Also = probability-weighted average of all tail losses beyond VaR.

8.8 Backtesting

Backtesting validates a VaR model by comparing predicted VaR against actual daily P&L. A day where the loss exceeds VaR is an exception (breach). For 99% 1-day VaR you expect about 1% of days to breach — roughly 2–3 in a year of 250 days. Regulators use the Basel traffic-light approach: green (0–4 breaches/year, model fine), amber (5–9, add a capital multiplier), red (10+, model rejected). Too many breaches = model understates risk; too few can mean it is overly conservative (wasting capital).

8.9 Marginal, incremental & component VaR

Total VaR does not tell you where the risk comes from. Three decompositions answer that — essential for setting limits and allocating risk capital across a desk.

  • Marginal VaR: the change in portfolio VaR for a small (one-unit) increase in a position — the sensitivity ∂VaR/∂wi. It tells you which position is adding or diversifying risk at the margin.
  • Incremental VaR: the change in total VaR from adding or removing an entire position or trade. Used to decide "does this new trade increase our risk, and by how much?"
  • Component VaR: the part of total VaR attributable to each position, constructed so the components sum to total VaR (Component VaRi = Marginal VaRi × positioni). It is the standard way to report "who owns the risk". A negative component means the position is a net hedge/diversifier.

8.10 Stressed VaR & coherent risk measures

Stressed VaR (sVaR) re-runs the VaR model using data from a historical stress period (e.g. the 2008 crisis) instead of the recent calm window. Basel II.5 added it precisely because a VaR calibrated to a quiet period collapses just when markets blow up; total capital became VaR + sVaR.

A risk measure is coherent if it satisfies four axioms (Artzner et al.): monotonicity (more loss in every state ⇒ more risk), translation invariance (adding cash reduces risk one-for-one), positive homogeneity (scaling the book scales the risk), and sub-additivity (a merged portfolio is no riskier than the sum of its parts — diversification never increases risk). VaR fails sub-additivity (it can say a diversified book is riskier than its pieces), so it is not coherent; Expected Shortfall satisfies all four and is coherent — a core reason FRTB moved to ES.

Common trap: "VaR is a coherent risk measure." No — VaR generally violates sub-additivity, so it can penalise diversification. Expected Shortfall is the coherent alternative.
Key takeaways: VaR = loss threshold at a confidence level over a horizon — NOT the worst case. Three methods: parametric (normal, simple, thin-tailed), historical (real data, window-bound), Monte Carlo (flexible, model-dependent). ES/CVaR averages the tail beyond VaR and is coherent. Backtest with breach counts; scale by √t only under i.i.d.

9. Volatility Modelling — EWMA & GARCH

VaR needs a volatility estimate, and volatility is not constant — it clusters (calm follows calm, storms follow storms). Two models capture this.

9.0 ARCH — the starting point

ARCH (Autoregressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity, Engle 1982) was the first model to make variance time-varying: today's variance is a function of recent squared returns, σ²t = ω + Σαi·r²t−i. "Conditional heteroskedasticity" just means the variance changes over time conditional on the past. Its weakness is that capturing persistent vol needs many lags (many α terms). GARCH fixes this elegantly by adding lagged variance — one β term replaces a long string of α's — which is why GARCH(1,1) is the workhorse.

9.1 EWMA (Exponentially Weighted Moving Average)

A simple moving average weights every past day equally and forgets old data abruptly. EWMA instead weights recent returns more heavily, with weights decaying geometrically by a factor λ (RiskMetrics famously used λ = 0.94 for daily data). It reacts faster to new shocks — a big move today immediately lifts tomorrow's vol estimate.

EWMA variance update
σ²t = λ·σ²t−1 + (1 − λ)·r²t−1
where: σ²t = today's variance estimate; λ = decay factor (0 < λ < 1, typically 0.94); rt−1 = yesterday's return. Lower λ = more responsive, noisier; higher λ = smoother, slower.

9.2 GARCH(1,1)

GARCH (Generalised Autoregressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity) adds a crucial feature EWMA lacks: mean reversion to a long-run average variance. Tomorrow's variance is a weighted mix of a long-run level, yesterday's squared shock, and yesterday's variance. This matches how vol spikes after a shock and then decays back to normal.

GARCH(1,1) variance
σ²t = ω + α·r²t−1 + β·σ²t−1
where: ω = constant (sets the long-run variance = ω/(1−α−β)); α = weight on the latest squared return (reaction to shocks); β = weight on previous variance (persistence). Requires α + β < 1 for stationarity. EWMA is the special case ω = 0, α = 1−λ, β = λ.

Intuition to state in interview: α controls how sharply vol reacts to a new shock; β controls how long the elevated vol persists; α + β close to 1 means vol shocks are very persistent (typical of equities). GARCH's mean reversion is why long-horizon vol forecasts pull toward the long-run average — something EWMA cannot do.

Key takeaways: Volatility clusters, so use adaptive estimators. EWMA weights recent data more (fast, no mean reversion). GARCH(1,1) adds a long-run variance and mean reversion; EWMA is a special case of it. Both feed better VaR and option-vol estimates.

10. Risk-Adjusted Performance

Return alone is meaningless without the risk taken to earn it. These ratios standardise "return per unit of risk" so strategies can be compared fairly.

10.1 Sharpe ratio

Sharpe ratio
Sharpe = (Rp − Rf) / σp
where: Rp = portfolio return; Rf = risk-free rate; σp = standard deviation of portfolio returns (total volatility). Excess return per unit of total risk. Higher is better; typically annualised.

10.2 Sortino ratio

The Sharpe ratio penalises all volatility, including upside. The Sortino ratio divides excess return only by downside deviation — the volatility of returns below a target (often 0 or Rf). It rewards strategies whose volatility is mostly to the upside.

Sortino ratio
Sortino = (Rp − Rf) / σdown
where: σdown = downside deviation = standard deviation of only the negative (below-target) returns. Always ≥ Sharpe's denominator behaviour: for a given series σdown ≤ σp, so Sortino ≥ Sharpe.

10.3 Treynor ratio

Treynor ratio
Treynor = (Rp − Rf) / βp
where: βp = portfolio beta (systematic/market risk). Excess return per unit of market risk. Use when the portfolio is well-diversified so only systematic risk matters.

10.4 Information ratio

Information ratio
IR = (Rp − Rb) / TE
where: Rb = benchmark return; TE = tracking error = standard deviation of (Rp − Rb). Measures active return per unit of active risk — the consistency of a manager's outperformance versus a benchmark.

10.5 Beta

Beta
β = Cov(Rp, Rm) / Var(Rm) = ρp,m · (σp / σm)
where: Rm = market return; ρp,m = correlation of portfolio and market. β = 1 moves with the market; β > 1 amplifies it; β < 1 dampens; β < 0 moves opposite. Beta is the slope of a regression of portfolio returns on market returns.

10.6 Maximum drawdown

Maximum drawdown (MDD) is the largest peak-to-trough decline in portfolio value over a period, expressed as a percentage. It captures the worst pain an investor would have felt — the depth of the deepest hole — and, unlike volatility, focuses purely on downside path risk. The Calmar ratio (annual return ÷ MDD) rewards return per unit of drawdown.

Maximum drawdown
MDD = (Trough value − Peak value) / Peak value
The most negative peak-to-trough return before a new peak is made. A −40% MDD means the portfolio fell 40% from its high before recovering.
Worked Example 11 — Sharpe & Sortino computation

A strategy returns (annualised) Rp = 18%, with total volatility σp = 20%. Risk-free Rf = 6%. Suppose downside deviation σdown = 12%.

Sharpe = (18 − 6) / 20 = 12 / 20 = 0.60.

Sortino = (18 − 6) / 12 = 12 / 12 = 1.00.

The Sortino is higher because it ignores upside volatility — this strategy's swings are disproportionately on the upside, which Sharpe unfairly punished. A Sharpe near or above 1.0 is generally considered good; above 2.0 excellent (though beware overfitting on backtests).

Worked Example 12 — Beta from covariance

Cov(portfolio, market) = 0.018; Var(market) = 0.015. β = 0.018 / 0.015 = 1.20.

Interpretation: the portfolio is 20% more volatile than the market in the systematic sense — for a +1% market move, expect roughly +1.2%. With Rf = 6% and the same 18% return, Treynor = (18 − 6)/1.20 = 10.0 (i.e. 10% excess return per unit of beta).

10.7 Jensen's alpha

Jensen's alpha is the return a portfolio earned above what CAPM says it should have earned for its beta — the intercept of the CAPM regression, and the cleanest single measure of manager skill. Positive alpha = genuine outperformance after adjusting for market risk; zero alpha = the manager only delivered beta (which you could buy cheaply via an index fund).

Jensen's alpha
αJ = Rp − [ Rf + βp·(Rm − Rf) ]
where: the bracketed term is the CAPM-required return for the portfolio's beta. αJ > 0 means the manager beat the risk-adjusted benchmark. It is the intercept when excess portfolio returns are regressed on excess market returns.
Worked Example 12b — Jensen's alpha

Rp = 18%, Rf = 6%, β = 1.2, market Rm = 14%. CAPM-required = 6 + 1.2×(14 − 6) = 6 + 9.6 = 15.6%. Alpha = 18 − 15.6 = +2.4% — the manager added 2.4% beyond what the beta exposure alone justified.

Key takeaways: Sharpe = excess return per total risk; Sortino swaps in downside deviation; Treynor uses beta (systematic risk); Information ratio measures active skill vs benchmark; beta = covariance/variance = regression slope; MDD = worst peak-to-trough fall.

11. Other Risks, Stress Testing & Diversification

11.1 The main risk types

  • Market risk: loss from moves in prices, rates, FX, vol (covered by VaR/ES).
  • Credit risk: loss if a counterparty or borrower defaults or is downgraded. Key measures: Probability of Default (PD), Loss Given Default (LGD), Exposure at Default (EAD); Expected Loss = PD × LGD × EAD. Counterparty credit risk on derivatives is captured via CVA (credit valuation adjustment).
  • Liquidity risk: two flavours — market liquidity (can't exit a position without moving the price) and funding liquidity (can't raise cash / meet margin calls). Widened bid-ask spreads and margin spirals are the classic symptoms.
  • Operational risk: loss from failed processes, people, systems or external events — fat-finger trades, settlement fails, fraud, cyber, model errors. Managed with controls, reconciliation and audit, not with market hedges.

A word on product control, since it is one of your target roles: the product control function owns the independent daily P&L and its explanation. Each day's trading P&L is decomposed into a P&L attribution (or "P&L explain") — how much came from delta (spot moves), gamma (convexity), vega (vol moves), theta (time decay), and carry/rates — reconciled against the front-office number. A large unexplained residual is a red flag for a booking error, a stale mark, or a model issue. Product control also validates independent price verification (IPV), checking traders' marks against independent sources, and reports fair-value adjustments. In short: the Greeks you track on the desk are the same sensitivities product control uses to explain and validate P&L.

11.2 Stress testing & scenario analysis

VaR describes normal days; stress testing asks "what if the abnormal happens?". You revalue the book under severe but plausible scenarios — historical (2008 GFC, March 2020 COVID crash, the 2013 taper tantrum) or hypothetical (rates +200bp, equity −30%, INR devaluation, a vol spike to 40). Reverse stress testing flips the question: what scenario would break us? Stress tests deliberately capture the tail that VaR's confidence cut-off ignores, and are a regulatory requirement.

11.3 Correlation & diversification

Diversification works because combining assets that are not perfectly correlated lowers portfolio variance without necessarily lowering expected return — "the only free lunch in finance". The maths:

Two-asset portfolio variance
σ²p = w1²σ1² + w2²σ2² + 2·w1w2·ρ12·σ1σ2
where: w = weights; σ = volatilities; ρ12 = correlation. The lower ρ, the greater the variance reduction. At ρ = −1 risk can be driven to zero; at ρ = +1 there is no diversification benefit.
Common trap: "Diversification protects you in a crash." Correlations tend to spike toward +1 in a crisis ("all correlations go to one"), so diversification benefits shrink exactly when you need them most. This is why stress testing assumes elevated correlations.

11.4 Counterparty credit risk — CVA & DVA

On OTC derivatives (swaps, forwards) each side faces counterparty credit risk: the risk the other party defaults while the trade is in-the-money to you. CVA (Credit Valuation Adjustment) is the market price of that risk — the amount by which you mark down a derivative's value to reflect the counterparty's chance of default. DVA (Debit Valuation Adjustment) is the mirror image: an adjustment for your own default risk, which (perversely) becomes a gain to you as your own credit worsens. Together with funding (FVA) and capital (KVA) these are the "xVA" adjustments a modern derivatives desk manages.

CVA (intuition)
CVA ≈ (1 − Recovery) · Σ PD(t) · EE(t) · DF(t)
where: PD(t) = counterparty default probability in each future period; EE(t) = expected positive exposure (how in-the-money the trade is expected to be); DF(t) = discount factor; (1 − Recovery) = loss given default. Central clearing and collateral (margin) reduce CVA by cutting exposure.

11.5 Model risk

Model risk is the risk of loss from using a flawed or misapplied model — wrong assumptions, calibration errors, bugs, or a model used outside the regime it was built for (e.g. Gaussian VaR in a fat-tailed crash, or a copula that assumed low correlation). It is managed through independent model validation, back-testing, reserves/adjustments for model uncertainty, and clear documentation of limitations. LTCM (1998) and the 2008 mispricing of structured credit are the textbook cautionary tales: the map was mistaken for the territory.

Key takeaways: Beyond market risk, know credit (EL = PD×LGD×EAD), liquidity (market vs funding) and operational risk. Stress/scenario testing covers the tail VaR misses. Diversification lowers variance via low correlation — but correlations rise in crises.

12. Quant & Statistics Foundations

12.1 Distributions in finance

  • Normal (Gaussian): symmetric bell curve, defined by mean and variance. Used for returns in many models. Tractable but thin-tailed.
  • Lognormal: a variable whose logarithm is normal. Used for prices (they can't go negative and returns compound). Black–Scholes assumes lognormal prices / normal log-returns.
  • Fat tails (leptokurtosis): real return distributions have more mass in the extremes than the normal predicts — big moves happen more often than a bell curve says. This is why VaR under normality understates tail risk and why the vol smile exists.
  • Student-t: a bell-shaped but fat-tailed distribution controlled by a "degrees of freedom" parameter — low df = fat tails, high df → normal. It is the standard fix for modelling returns/VaR when the normal understates extremes, and also the distribution of the t-statistic in regression and hypothesis testing.

12.2 The four moments

Variance, covariance & correlation
Var(X) = E[(X − μ)²]; Cov(X,Y) = E[(X − μX)(Y − μY)]; ρX,Y = Cov(X,Y) / (σXσY)
Correlation ρ ∈ [−1, +1] is the standardised, unit-free version of covariance. ρ = 0 means linearly uncorrelated (not necessarily independent).
Common trap: "Zero correlation means independent." Correlation only measures linear association. Two variables can be strongly related non-linearly (e.g. Y = X²) yet have zero correlation. Independence implies zero correlation, not the reverse.

12.3 Hypothesis testing, p-values & confidence intervals

A hypothesis test weighs a null hypothesis H0 (e.g. "this strategy's true alpha is zero") against an alternative. The p-value is the probability of observing data at least as extreme as yours if H0 were true. Small p (< 0.05 typically) → reject H0: the result is "statistically significant". A confidence interval gives a range that, over repeated sampling, would contain the true parameter a stated proportion of the time (e.g. a 95% CI).

Common trap: "The p-value is the probability the null is true" or "there's a 95% chance the true value is in this CI." Both wrong. The p-value is P(data | H0), not P(H0 | data). And a 95% CI is a statement about the procedure's long-run coverage, not a probability about one fixed interval.

Type I and Type II errors are the two ways a test can be wrong:

 H0 is trueH0 is false
Reject H0Type I error (false positive), prob = αCorrect (power = 1 − β)
Fail to reject H0CorrectType II error (false negative), prob = β

Type I = concluding an effect exists when it does not (its probability is the significance level α, e.g. 5%). Type II = missing a real effect (probability β; power = 1 − β). Lowering α (being stricter) raises β for a fixed sample — the classic trade-off; more data lowers both. In trading terms: a Type I error is trusting a strategy that is really just noise (overfitting); a Type II error is discarding one that genuinely works.

12.4 Linear regression (beta & R²)

Ordinary least squares fits a line minimising squared residuals. In finance the canonical use is the market model: regress a stock's excess returns on the market's excess returns; the slope is beta (systematic risk) and the intercept is alpha (skill/mispricing).

Simple linear regression
Y = α + β·X + ε, β = Cov(X,Y)/Var(X), R² = explained variance / total variance
where: β = slope (sensitivity of Y to X); α = intercept (alpha); ε = residual; R² ∈ [0,1] = fraction of Y's variance explained by X. In the market model R² ≈ how much of a stock's movement is market-driven vs idiosyncratic.

A high R² in a CAPM regression means the stock is mostly driven by the market (systematic); a low R² means idiosyncratic risk dominates (diversifiable). R² is also the square of the correlation between X and Y in a simple regression.

t-statistic & significance of coefficients. Each estimated coefficient has a t-stat = estimate / standard error; if |t| > ~2 the coefficient is significant at 5% (its confidence interval excludes zero). For a beta estimate, a high t-stat says the market exposure is reliably different from zero. Adjusted R² penalises R² for the number of regressors so that adding useless variables no longer inflates the fit — always compare models on adjusted R², not raw R², because raw R² can only ever rise as you add variables.

OLS assumptions & their violations

OLS is "best" (BLUE — best linear unbiased estimator) only if its assumptions hold. The three most-tested violations:

  • Heteroskedasticity: the residual variance is not constant (common in finance — vol clusters). Coefficients stay unbiased but standard errors are wrong, so t-stats mislead. Fix: robust (White/Newey-West) standard errors. Test: Breusch–Pagan, White.
  • Autocorrelation (serial correlation): residuals are correlated across time (common in time-series regressions). Again biases the standard errors, not the coefficients. Detect with the Durbin–Watson statistic (≈2 = no autocorrelation). Fix: Newey–West errors.
  • Multicollinearity: regressors are highly correlated with each other, so the model cannot separate their effects — coefficients become unstable with huge standard errors (though the overall fit/prediction can still be fine). Detect with the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). Fix: drop/combine variables or use PCA.
Common trap (R² pitfalls): A high R² does not mean the model is "good" or causal — you can get high R² from a spurious regression of two unrelated trending (non-stationary) series. And raw R² always rises when you add regressors, so it cannot be used to choose model complexity — use adjusted R² and out-of-sample tests. High R² ≠ good forecasts.

12.5 Monte Carlo simulation

Monte Carlo uses repeated random sampling to estimate quantities that are hard to compute analytically. Simulate thousands of price paths from an assumed process (e.g. geometric Brownian motion, dS = μS dt + σS dW), compute the payoff/P&L in each, and average or take percentiles. It powers exotic option pricing, VaR (Section 8.5), and any problem where the distribution of an outcome is needed but a closed form is unavailable. Accuracy improves with the square root of the number of paths (so 4× the paths ≈ 2× the precision).

12.6 Time series basics

  • Stationarity: a series is stationary if its statistical properties (mean, variance, autocovariance) don't change over time. Prices are typically non-stationary (they trend/random-walk); returns are usually treated as (roughly) stationary. Most time-series models require stationarity — hence we model returns, not prices.
  • Autocorrelation: correlation of a series with its own lagged values. Returns show little linear autocorrelation (weak-form efficiency), but squared or absolute returns are strongly autocorrelated — that is volatility clustering, the very thing GARCH models.
  • Random walk / unit root: a price random walk means tomorrow's best forecast is today's price; tested with the augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) test for a unit root.
  • ARIMA: a general model for a single stationary series combining AR (autoregression on past values), I (differencing d times to induce stationarity — prices need d=1) and MA (moving average of past shocks). ARIMA(p,d,q) forecasts the level; add a GARCH error to model the changing variance. Used for mean/return forecasting.

12.7 Cointegration & pairs trading

Two price series can each be non-stationary (random walks) yet a linear combination of them is stationary — then they are cointegrated, tied together by a long-run equilibrium they keep reverting to. This is the statistical engine of pairs trading / statistical arbitrage: find two related instruments (e.g. two banks, or a stock and its futures), estimate the hedge ratio, and trade the spread — short it when it is stretched wide, long it when compressed, betting it reverts to the mean. Test with Engle–Granger (ADF on the residual) or Johansen.

Common trap: "Correlation is enough for a pairs trade." No — high correlation of returns does not guarantee the spread is mean-reverting; two series can be correlated day-to-day yet drift apart forever. Cointegration (a stationary spread), not correlation, is what makes a pair tradeable. And beware: cointegration can break (a structural regime change), so pairs trades carry the risk the relationship simply stops holding.

12.8 Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

PCA is a dimension-reduction technique: it re-expresses many correlated variables as a few uncorrelated principal components ordered by how much variance they explain. Classic finance use — the yield curve: three components explain almost all its movement, interpreted as level (parallel shift, ~PC1), slope (steepening/flattening, PC2) and curvature (PC3). PCA also compresses large correlation matrices for risk models and factor construction, and cures multicollinearity by replacing correlated regressors with orthogonal components.

Key takeaways: Prices lognormal, log-returns roughly normal but fat-tailed and negatively skewed. Know the four moments. Correlation is linear only. p-value = P(data|H0), not P(H0). Regression slope = beta, R² = variance explained. Monte Carlo = random sampling for intractable problems. Model stationary returns, not trending prices; volatility clusters (autocorrelated squared returns).

13. Swaps, FRAs & Interest-Rate Options

13.1 Interest-rate swaps (IRS)

An interest-rate swap is an OTC agreement to exchange interest cash flows on a notional principal (the notional is never exchanged). The plain-vanilla IRS swaps a fixed rate for a floating rate (e.g. an overnight index like MIBOR/SOFR). A firm with a floating-rate loan that fears rising rates can "pay fixed / receive floating" to lock its cost — converting floating exposure into fixed. Swaps are the largest derivatives market in the world.

Swap valuation (concept)
Vswap = PV(floating leg) − PV(fixed leg)
A swap is a portfolio of forward rate agreements, or equivalently a long/short pair of bonds (one fixed-coupon, one floating). At inception the fixed rate (the "swap rate") is set so V = 0. As rates move the swap gains value to one side. The floating leg resets to par at each reset date, which simplifies its valuation.

A swap's rate sensitivity is measured by DV01 / PV01 — the P&L for a 1-basis-point parallel move in rates — the rates-desk analogue of delta.

13.2 Currency swaps

A currency swap exchanges principal and interest in two different currencies (e.g. pay INR fixed, receive USD fixed). Unlike an IRS, the notional principals are exchanged at the start and re-exchanged at maturity (at a pre-agreed FX rate). Used to fund cheaply in one currency while needing another, and to hedge long-dated FX exposure.

13.3 Forward rate agreements (FRAs)

An FRA locks in an interest rate for a single future period on a notional — the interest-rate equivalent of a forward. A "3×6 FRA" fixes the rate for a 3-month period starting in 3 months. The buyer (who locks a borrowing rate) profits if rates rise above the agreed rate; settlement is a single cash payment at the start of the period (discounted). A swap is essentially a strip of FRAs.

13.4 Caps, floors & collars (rate options)

These are options on rates, giving asymmetric protection:

  • Cap: a series of call options on a floating rate (each one a "caplet"). It pays out whenever the rate exceeds the cap strike — insurance for a floating-rate borrower against rising rates, while keeping the benefit if rates fall.
  • Floor: a series of put options on the rate ("floorlets") — protects a floating-rate lender/investor against falling rates.
  • Interest-rate collar: buy a cap and sell a floor (or vice versa) to cheapen the protection — same idea as an equity collar.

13.5 Exotic options (concept)

"Exotics" are options with non-standard payoffs beyond vanilla calls/puts, usually OTC and priced by Monte Carlo or trees. Know the names and one line each:

  • Barrier options: knock-in / knock-out only if the underlying touches a barrier (cheaper than vanillas).
  • Asian options: payoff on the average price over the life — less sensitive to expiry-day manipulation, lower vol.
  • Digital / binary: pay a fixed amount if in-the-money, nothing otherwise.
  • Lookback: payoff uses the best/worst price over the life.
  • Bermudan: exercisable on a set of specified dates (between European and American).
Key takeaways: IRS swaps fixed for floating on a notional (never exchanged); value = PV(floating) − PV(fixed), zero at inception, sensitivity = DV01. Currency swaps exchange principal in two currencies. FRA = forward on a single rate; a swap = a strip of FRAs. Caps/floors = options on rates (borrower/lender insurance). Exotics (barrier, Asian, digital, lookback, Bermudan) = non-standard payoffs, priced by simulation/trees.

14. Portfolio Theory & CAPM

14.1 Systematic vs unsystematic risk

Total risk splits into two parts. Systematic (market) risk is undiversifiable — it hits the whole market (rates, growth, geopolitics) and is measured by beta. Unsystematic (idiosyncratic/specific) risk is firm-specific (a fraud, a product recall) and is diversifiable — it washes out as you add uncorrelated names. Because only systematic risk cannot be diversified away, the market only pays you (a risk premium) for bearing that — the central idea behind CAPM.

14.2 Markowitz mean–variance & the efficient frontier

Markowitz mean–variance optimisation chooses portfolio weights to get the highest expected return for a given variance (or lowest variance for a given return). Because assets are imperfectly correlated, combining them curves the risk–return set into a bullet shape. The efficient frontier is its upper edge — the set of portfolios that are not dominated (no other portfolio offers more return for the same risk). Rational investors hold only frontier portfolios.

14.3 Capital Market Line (CML) & the tangency portfolio

Introduce a risk-free asset and you can combine it with any risky portfolio along a straight line. The best such line — tangent to the efficient frontier — is the Capital Market Line; its tangency point is the market portfolio. Every investor holds a mix of the risk-free asset and the same market portfolio, differing only in the proportion (the two-fund separation theorem).

Capital Market Line
E(Rp) = Rf + [ (E(Rm) − Rf) / σm ] · σp
The CML prices efficient portfolios by total risk σp. Its slope, (E(Rm) − Rf)/σm, is the market Sharpe ratio — the "price of risk".

14.4 CAPM & the Security Market Line (SML)

The Capital Asset Pricing Model prices any asset (not just efficient portfolios) by its beta — its systematic risk only. The relationship, plotted as expected return against beta, is the Security Market Line.

CAPM / Security Market Line
E(Ri) = Rf + βi · (E(Rm) − Rf)
where: (E(Rm) − Rf) = market risk premium; βi = asset's systematic risk. Assets on the SML are fairly priced; above the SML = underpriced (positive alpha, buy); below = overpriced. Alpha is the vertical distance from the SML.
Worked Example 13 — CAPM required return

Rf = 6%, market premium (Rm − Rf) = 8%, stock β = 1.3. Required return = 6 + 1.3×8 = 6 + 10.4 = 16.4%. If you actually expect the stock to return 19%, it plots above the SML — a positive alpha of 2.6%, i.e. a buy.

Common trap: Confusing the CML and the SML. The CML uses total risk (σ) and only holds for efficient portfolios; the SML uses beta and holds for every asset. A single inefficient stock lies below the CML but on the SML (if fairly priced). Also: CAPM assumes one factor (the market); reality shows multiple factors (size, value, momentum — the Fama–French extensions).
Key takeaways: Total risk = systematic (beta, undiversifiable, paid for) + unsystematic (diversifiable, not paid for). Markowitz → efficient frontier of best risk/return portfolios. Add a risk-free asset → CML through the market portfolio (prices efficient portfolios by total risk). CAPM/SML prices any asset by beta: E(R)=Rf+β(Rm−Rf); alpha = distance above the SML.

15. Rapid-Fire Interview Q&A

Q1: What is the difference between a future and a forward?
A: Both lock in a price for future delivery. A forward is OTC, customised, settled once at maturity, and carries counterparty risk. A future is exchange-traded, standardised, guaranteed by a clearing house, and marked-to-market daily with margin. Futures are far more liquid and have negligible default risk.
Q2: How is a futures price determined?
A: By no-arbitrage cost-of-carry: F = S·e^((r−q)T). It's the spot grown at the financing cost, net of any income (dividends) the asset pays. If the market future deviates, cash-and-carry (or reverse) arbitrage forces it back.
Q3: What is basis, and what are contango and backwardation?
A: Basis = futures − spot; it converges to zero at expiry. Contango is futures above spot (normal for equities when r > q); backwardation is futures below spot (high income/convenience yield or costly borrow).
Q4: What is mark-to-market and why does it matter?
A: Daily settlement of gains and losses on a futures position against the closing price, debited/credited to the margin account. It prevents large unpaid losses from accumulating, which is how the clearing house keeps counterparty risk minimal.
Q5: Explain a call and a put option.
A: A call is the right (not obligation) to buy the underlying at the strike; a put is the right to sell at the strike. The buyer pays a premium; max loss is that premium. The writer receives it and takes on the obligation, with much larger potential loss.
Q6: What is moneyness?
A: Where the strike sits vs spot. A call is ITM if S > K, ATM if S ≈ K, OTM if S < K (puts reversed). ITM options have intrinsic value; OTM options are pure time value.
Q7: What is intrinsic value versus time value?
A: Intrinsic = max(S−K,0) for a call — the immediate exercise value, floored at zero. Time value = premium − intrinsic, reflecting the chance of finishing further ITM. Time value is largest ATM and decays to zero by expiry.
Q8: Explain put–call parity and why it holds.
A: C + PV(K) = P + S for European options on the same strike/expiry. A fiduciary call (call + cash) and a protective put (stock + put) both deliver max(S_T, K) at expiry, so by no-arbitrage they must cost the same today. If violated, you can lock in a riskless profit.
Q9: What does Black–Scholes assume, and what are its weaknesses?
A: It assumes lognormal prices (normal log-returns), constant known volatility, continuous frictionless trading, no jumps, constant rates, European exercise. Weaknesses: real returns have fat tails and skew, volatility is stochastic (hence the smile), hedging is discrete and costly, and markets jump.
Q10: Why doesn't the stock's expected return appear in Black–Scholes?
A: Because of risk-neutral replication — you can hedge the option with the stock and cash, making the position riskless, so it must earn the risk-free rate. The real-world drift cancels out; only volatility, rate and time remain.
Q11: What is the binomial model and when is it preferred?
A: It models the stock as moving up or down each step, prices by backward induction using risk-neutral probabilities, and converges to Black–Scholes as steps increase. It's preferred for American options because at each node you can compare holding vs early exercise.
Q12: What is delta?
A: The rate of change of option value per ₹1 move in the underlying — the first-order price sensitivity and the hedge ratio. Long call delta 0 to +1, long put −1 to 0, ATM ≈ ±0.5. It's also approximately the risk-neutral probability of finishing ITM.
Q13: What does gamma tell you?
A: Gamma is the rate of change of delta per unit move — the convexity or "acceleration" of the option. It tells you how fast your delta hedge decays. Long options are long gamma (comfortable: re-hedging buys low/sells high); short options are short gamma (dangerous in choppy markets). Gamma peaks ATM near expiry.
Q14: What is theta and what sign is it for a long option?
A: Theta is time decay — the value lost per day as expiry nears. It is negative for long options (you pay for the passage of time) and positive for short options. It is most negative for ATM options close to expiry.
Q15: What is vega? Is "vega" a Greek letter?
A: Vega is sensitivity of option value to a 1-point change in implied volatility, positive for both long calls and long puts. "Vega" is NOT actually a Greek letter — it's desk jargon, often written ν (nu). Vega peaks for ATM, long-dated options.
Q16: How do the Greeks behave with moneyness and time?
A: Delta → ±1 deep ITM, → 0 deep OTM, ±0.5 ATM. Gamma and theta both peak ATM and grow sharply as expiry approaches. Vega peaks ATM but grows with time to expiry (longer-dated = more vega). Rho matters most for long-dated options.
Q17: Explain delta hedging.
A: Offset an option's directional exposure by taking an opposite position in the underlying equal to the option's delta, making the book delta-neutral. Because delta changes with the underlying (gamma), you must re-hedge dynamically. It isolates volatility/gamma/theta from pure direction.
Q18: What is the gamma–theta trade-off?
A: Owning options gives positive gamma but costs theta (you pay time decay hoping for movement). Selling options collects theta but is short gamma (you profit if the market is calm but suffer in big moves). "You get paid theta to be short gamma."
Q19: What is implied volatility?
A: The volatility that, put into Black–Scholes, reproduces the option's current market price. It's forward-looking — the market's expectation of future volatility — and the only BSM input you can't directly observe, which is why traders quote options in vol terms. India VIX is implied vol on Nifty options.
Q20: Historical vs implied volatility — what's the difference?
A: Historical (realised) vol is the standard deviation of past returns — a backward-looking measurement. Implied vol is extracted from current option prices — forward-looking, the market's expectation. When implied > realised, options are richly priced; when implied < realised, cheap.
Q21: What is the volatility smile/skew and why does it exist?
A: Plotting implied vol against strike gives a smile (or, for equity indices, a downward skew where OTM puts have higher IV). It exists because real return distributions have fat tails and downside crashes are feared, so puts are bid up. It's the market correcting BSM's flawed constant-normal-vol assumption.
Q22: What is a straddle and when do you use it?
A: Buy a call and a put at the same strike and expiry (usually ATM). It's a volatility bet — you profit from a large move in either direction and lose the premium if the underlying stays put. Use it before high-uncertainty events (RBI policy, budget, earnings) when you expect a big move but aren't sure of direction.
Q23: Straddle vs strangle?
A: A straddle uses the same strike (usually ATM) for both legs; a strangle uses OTM strikes (call strike above, put strike below). The strangle is cheaper but needs a bigger move to profit — wider break-evens.
Q24: A trader buys a straddle before earnings, the stock moves a lot, but he loses money. How?
A: Vol crush. Implied vol was elevated before the event, making the straddle expensive; after the announcement uncertainty resolves and IV collapses, so both options lose vega value. If the realised move is smaller than the priced-in move, the straddle loses despite a real move.
Q25: What is a covered call?
A: Long the stock plus a short OTM call. You earn the premium as income and cap upside at the strike; downside is the stock's (cushioned by the premium). It's a neutral-to-mildly-bullish, low-vol income strategy.
Q26: What is a protective put?
A: Long stock plus a long put — insurance. The put floors your loss at the strike while leaving upside open; cost is the premium. Used to hedge a holding through an uncertain period.
Q27: Explain a bull call spread and its P/L.
A: Buy a lower-strike call, sell a higher-strike call, same expiry (net debit). Moderately bullish with defined risk. Max profit = strike difference − net debit; max loss = net debit; break-even = lower strike + net debit. Cheaper than a naked call, but upside is capped.
Q28: What is an iron condor?
A: Sell an OTM put spread and an OTM call spread (four legs, net credit). You profit if the underlying stays between the short strikes; both spreads cap the risk. It's a range-bound, short-vega, positive-theta strategy — popular for harvesting premium on weekly Nifty/Bank Nifty expiries.
Q29: What is Value-at-Risk?
A: The loss over a given horizon at a given confidence level that won't be exceeded. E.g. a 1-day 99% VaR of ₹10 lakh means losses stay within ₹10 lakh on 99% of days. It's a standard single-number market-risk summary for limits and capital.
Q30: Is VaR the maximum possible loss?
A: No — that's the classic trap. VaR is a threshold/quantile, not a worst case. It says nothing about the size of losses beyond the confidence level. On the ~1% of breach days, the loss can be far larger. Expected Shortfall addresses this.
Q31: How would you compute a parametric 1-day VaR?
A: Assume normal returns: VaR = z × σ × V, where z is the confidence quantile (1.645 for 95%, 2.326 for 99%), σ is 1-day volatility and V is portfolio value. E.g. ₹10 crore, σ=1.2%, 99%: 2.326 × 0.012 × 10cr ≈ ₹27.9 lakh.
Q32: Compare the three VaR methods.
A: Parametric (variance–covariance) assumes normality — simple and fast but thin-tailed and poor for options. Historical simulation replays actual past returns — captures fat tails and real correlations but is window-dependent and backward-looking. Monte Carlo simulates from a chosen model — flexible for non-linear books but carries model risk and compute cost.
Q33: What are the limitations of VaR?
A: It ignores tail severity beyond the cut-off; it's not sub-additive (not coherent — can penalise diversification); it's backward-looking / assumption-dependent; it can be gamed; and √t scaling and normality break down in stressed, fat-tailed, correlated markets. Complement it with ES and stress testing.
Q34: What is Expected Shortfall / CVaR and why is it better?
A: ES is the average loss given that the loss exceeds VaR — it measures tail severity, not just the threshold. It's always ≥ VaR, is coherent (sub-additive, rewards diversification), and is what Basel FRTB adopted (97.5% ES) in place of VaR.
Q35: How do you scale VaR from 1 day to 10 days?
A: Multiply by √10 (square-root-of-time rule), valid only if returns are i.i.d. with constant vol. It understates risk when there's autocorrelation or volatility clustering, so it's an approximation, not exact.
Q36: What is backtesting a VaR model?
A: Comparing predicted VaR to realised daily P&L and counting breaches (days the loss exceeds VaR). For 99% VaR you expect ~1% breaches (~2–3/year). Basel's traffic-light system flags too many breaches (model understates risk) via green/amber/red zones and capital multipliers.
Q37: Explain EWMA and how it differs from a simple moving average.
A: EWMA weights recent returns more heavily with geometric decay (σ²_t = λσ²_{t−1} + (1−λ)r²_{t−1}, λ≈0.94), so it reacts faster to shocks. A simple moving average weights all window days equally and drops old data abruptly, reacting slower and showing "ghosting".
Q38: What does GARCH add over EWMA?
A: Mean reversion to a long-run variance. GARCH(1,1): σ²_t = ω + α·r²_{t−1} + β·σ²_{t−1}. α controls reaction to shocks, β controls persistence, and ω anchors the long-run level. EWMA is the special case ω=0, α=1−λ, β=λ (no mean reversion).
Q39: What is the Sharpe ratio?
A: Excess return per unit of total risk: (R_p − R_f)/σ_p. It standardises reward-for-risk so strategies are comparable. Higher is better; ~1 is good, >2 excellent — but beware backtest overfitting and non-normal returns.
Q40: Sharpe vs Sortino — when prefer Sortino?
A: Sharpe penalises all volatility including upside; Sortino divides only by downside deviation. Prefer Sortino for strategies with asymmetric/skewed returns (e.g. option-selling or trend strategies) where upside volatility shouldn't be punished. Sortino ≥ Sharpe for the same series.
Q41: What is beta and how is it computed?
A: Beta measures systematic (market) risk: β = Cov(R_p, R_m)/Var(R_m), the slope of a regression of portfolio returns on market returns. β=1 moves with the market, >1 amplifies, <1 dampens, <0 inverse.
Q42: What is the Treynor ratio and how does it differ from Sharpe?
A: Treynor = (R_p − R_f)/β — excess return per unit of systematic risk. Sharpe uses total risk (σ). Treynor is appropriate for well-diversified portfolios where idiosyncratic risk is already diversified away, so only beta matters.
Q43: What is the information ratio?
A: Active return over active risk: (R_p − R_b)/tracking error. It measures the consistency of a manager's outperformance vs a benchmark — a key metric for active managers.
Q44: What is maximum drawdown?
A: The largest peak-to-trough percentage decline in portfolio value over a period. It captures worst-case path pain and downside path-dependence, which volatility misses. The Calmar ratio uses it as the risk denominator.
Q45: Name the main risk types besides market risk.
A: Credit risk (default/downgrade; Expected Loss = PD×LGD×EAD), liquidity risk (market — can't exit; and funding — can't raise cash/meet margin), and operational risk (failed processes, people, systems, external events — fat-fingers, fraud, cyber, model error).
Q46: Why do we stress test if we already have VaR?
A: VaR describes normal days up to a confidence cut-off; it ignores the tail. Stress testing revalues the book under severe but plausible scenarios (2008, COVID, rate shocks) — including elevated correlations — to size losses in the regime VaR excludes. It's also regulatorily required.
Q47: What happens to correlations in a crisis?
A: They spike toward +1 — "all correlations go to one" — so diversification benefits collapse precisely when you need them. That's why stress scenarios assume higher correlations than the calm-period estimates in a VaR model.
Q48: Why do we model prices as lognormal and returns as normal?
A: Prices can't go negative and compound multiplicatively, which the lognormal captures; log-returns are then additive and roughly normal, making the maths tractable. Reality departs via fat tails and negative skew, which is why models are adjusted (smile, jumps).
Q49: Explain skewness and kurtosis in a return distribution.
A: Skewness is asymmetry — equities are typically negatively skewed (long left tail, crash-prone). Kurtosis measures tail heaviness; excess kurtosis > 0 means fat tails (extreme moves more frequent than normal predicts). Both mean the normal distribution understates real risk.
Q50: What's the difference between correlation and covariance? Does zero correlation mean independence?
A: Covariance measures co-movement in raw units; correlation is the standardised, unit-free version in [−1,+1]. Zero correlation means no linear relationship, not independence — a non-linear relationship (e.g. Y=X²) can have zero correlation. Independence implies zero correlation, but not vice versa.
Q51: What does a p-value actually mean?
A: The probability of observing data at least as extreme as yours if the null hypothesis were true — P(data | H₀). It is NOT the probability the null is true. A small p-value (<0.05) leads us to reject H₀ as statistically significant.
Q52: In a market-model regression, what do beta, alpha and R² tell you?
A: Beta (slope) is sensitivity to the market — systematic risk. Alpha (intercept) is return unexplained by the market — skill or mispricing. R² is the fraction of the stock's variance explained by the market; high R² = market-driven, low R² = idiosyncratic.
Q53: What is Monte Carlo simulation used for in finance?
A: Estimating outcomes with no closed form — pricing exotic/path-dependent options, computing VaR for non-linear portfolios, projecting P&L distributions. Simulate many random paths, evaluate the payoff/loss in each, and average or take percentiles. Precision grows with √(number of paths).
Q54: What is stationarity and why model returns instead of prices?
A: A stationary series has stable mean, variance and autocovariance over time. Prices trend/random-walk (non-stationary); returns are roughly stationary. Most time-series models (and statistical inference) require stationarity, so we model returns.
Q55: Returns show little autocorrelation, yet volatility clusters — how?
A: Raw returns have near-zero linear autocorrelation (weak-form efficiency), but squared or absolute returns are strongly autocorrelated: big moves follow big moves. That is volatility clustering — the phenomenon EWMA and GARCH are built to capture.
Q56: A book is delta-neutral but long gamma and short theta. What is the trader betting on?
A: On realised volatility exceeding what he's paying in time decay. Long gamma means big moves (either direction) let him re-hedge profitably (buy low/sell high); short theta means he bleeds if the market sits still. He wins if realised vol > the implied vol embedded in the options' cost.
Q57: How do you hedge a ₹5 crore equity portfolio with beta 1.2 using Nifty futures?
A: Short N = β × portfolio value / futures notional index contracts. If a Nifty future is worth ₹11 lakh notional, N ≈ 1.2 × 5,00,00,000 / 11,00,000 ≈ 55 contracts short. This neutralises market (systematic) risk; what remains is basis risk and the portfolio's idiosyncratic (alpha) risk.
Q58: What is the minimum-variance hedge ratio, and what is basis risk?
A: h* = ρ·(σ_S/σ_F) = Cov(ΔS,ΔF)/Var(ΔF) — the regression slope of spot changes on futures changes; it minimises the variance of the hedged position. Basis risk is the residual risk that spot and futures don't move perfectly together (cross-hedge, expiry mismatch/rollover), so the hedge is imperfect — you've swapped larger directional risk for smaller basis risk.
Q59: When is it optimal to exercise an American option early?
A: Never for a call on a non-dividend stock (you'd forfeit time value and interest on the strike — better to sell it). It can be optimal for a call just before a large dividend, and for a deep-ITM put even without dividends (you'd rather hold K in cash earning interest, since the put's upside is capped). So early exercise is mainly a put phenomenon.
Q60: State the no-arbitrage lower bound for a European call and the intuition.
A: c ≥ max(S₀ − K·e^(−rT), 0). If the call traded below S₀ − PV(K), you'd buy the call, short the stock, invest PV(K), and lock a riskless profit. Because this bound exceeds intrinsic value S₀ − K, a European call always has positive time value — the reason not to exercise a non-dividend call early.
Q61: Price a two-step binomial call quickly — what's the method?
A: Backward induction. Compute payoffs at the three terminal nodes, discount back one layer using the risk-neutral p = (e^(rΔt) − d)/(u − d) at every node, then discount that to today. For an American option, at each node take max(hold value, immediate exercise). More steps converge to Black–Scholes.
Q62: What is a collar and why use it?
A: Long stock + long OTM put + short OTM call. The short call finances the protective put — often making it zero-cost — in exchange for capping upside at the call strike. Used to lock in a gain cheaply while staying invested.
Q63: Butterfly vs iron condor — what's the difference?
A: Both are defined-risk, short-vol, range strategies. A butterfly (+1/−2/+1) peaks at a single central strike — narrow sweet spot, cheap. An iron condor has a flat profit zone between two short strikes — a wider profit range but usually lower max profit. Butterfly bets on a pin; condor bets on a range.
Q64: What does a calendar spread bet on?
A: Sell near-dated, buy longer-dated at the same strike. It's long theta (front leg decays faster) and long vega (back leg has more vol sensitivity) — a bet the underlying stays near the strike short-term while implied vol rises or the term structure steepens. Max loss = net debit.
Q65: Explain marginal, incremental and component VaR.
A: Marginal VaR = the sensitivity of total VaR to a small increase in a position (∂VaR/∂w). Incremental VaR = the change in total VaR from adding/removing an entire trade — used for pre-trade checks. Component VaR = each position's share of total VaR, built to sum to total VaR — the standard risk-attribution report; a negative component means the position is a diversifier.
Q66: Is VaR a coherent risk measure? What are the four axioms?
A: No — VaR generally violates sub-additivity, so it can penalise diversification. Coherence requires monotonicity, translation invariance, positive homogeneity and sub-additivity. Expected Shortfall satisfies all four and is coherent — a key reason Basel FRTB replaced 99% VaR with 97.5% ES.
Q67: What is stressed VaR?
A: VaR recomputed using data from a severe historical stress window (e.g. 2008) rather than the recent calm period. Basel II.5 requires capital = VaR + stressed VaR, because a VaR calibrated to quiet markets understates risk exactly when a crisis hits.
Q68: What are CVA and DVA?
A: CVA (Credit Valuation Adjustment) is the price of a counterparty's default risk on an OTC derivative — you mark the trade down by the expected loss from their default. DVA is the mirror for your own default risk (a "gain" as your credit worsens). With FVA/KVA they form the xVA suite. Collateral and central clearing reduce CVA by cutting exposure.
Q69: Distinguish systematic from unsystematic risk.
A: Systematic (market) risk hits everything and can't be diversified away — measured by beta and compensated with a risk premium. Unsystematic (firm-specific) risk is diversifiable and, per CAPM, earns no premium because a diversified investor can eliminate it for free. Only beta is priced.
Q70: State CAPM and compute a required return.
A: E(R) = R_f + β(R_m − R_f). With R_f = 6%, market premium 8%, β = 1.3: required = 6 + 1.3×8 = 16.4%. If you expect more than that, the stock has positive alpha (plots above the SML) — a buy.
Q71: What's the difference between the CML and the SML?
A: The CML plots expected return against total risk (σ) and only prices efficient portfolios (combinations of the risk-free asset and the market portfolio). The SML plots expected return against beta and prices every asset by its systematic risk. An inefficient single stock sits below the CML but on the SML if fairly priced.
Q72: Compute the variance of a two-asset portfolio.
A: σ²_p = w₁²σ₁² + w₂²σ₂² + 2w₁w₂ρσ₁σ₂. Example: 50/50 weights, σ₁=20%, σ₂=30%, ρ=0.2: σ²_p = 0.25×0.04 + 0.25×0.09 + 2×0.25×0.2×0.2×0.3 = 0.01 + 0.0225 + 0.006 = 0.0385, so σ_p = √0.0385 ≈ 19.6% — below the 25% weighted-average vol, the diversification benefit from ρ < 1.
Q73: What is Jensen's alpha?
A: The return above the CAPM-required return for the portfolio's beta: α = R_p − [R_f + β(R_m − R_f)]. It's the CAPM regression intercept — the cleanest measure of skill after adjusting for market exposure. Positive alpha = genuine outperformance, not just leverage on beta.
Q74: Explain Type I and Type II errors in the context of testing a trading strategy.
A: Type I (false positive, prob α) = concluding a strategy has edge when it's really noise — the overfitting trap. Type II (false negative, prob β) = discarding a strategy that genuinely works. Lowering α raises β for fixed data; more data reduces both. Power = 1 − β.
Q75: What is cointegration and how does it differ from correlation for a pairs trade?
A: Two non-stationary price series are cointegrated if a linear combination (the spread) is stationary — mean-reverting to a long-run equilibrium. Pairs trading needs a stationary spread, not just correlated returns: two series can be highly correlated yet drift apart forever. Cointegration, tested via Engle–Granger/Johansen, is what makes the spread tradeable — but it can break in a regime shift.
Q76: Your CAPM regression has R² = 0.9. Is that good? Any pitfalls?
A: It means 90% of the stock's variance is market-driven (high systematic exposure) — fine as a description, but R² alone doesn't validate a model. Raw R² only rises as you add regressors (use adjusted R²), and a high R² can be spurious if you regress non-stationary trending series. High R² ≠ good forecasts or causation.
Q77: Name the three OLS-violation problems and their effect.
A: Heteroskedasticity (non-constant residual variance) and autocorrelation (correlated residuals) both leave coefficients unbiased but make standard errors — and thus t-stats — wrong; fix with robust/Newey–West errors. Multicollinearity (correlated regressors) inflates coefficient standard errors and destabilises estimates; detect with VIF, fix by dropping variables or using PCA.
Q78: What does PCA reveal about the yield curve?
A: PCA reduces the correlated set of maturity rates to a few orthogonal components. For the yield curve, three explain almost all variation: level (parallel shift), slope (steepening/flattening) and curvature. It's used for dimension reduction in risk models, factor building, and to cure multicollinearity.
Q79: What is an interest-rate swap and how is it valued?
A: An OTC exchange of fixed-for-floating interest on a notional (notional never exchanged). Value = PV(floating leg) − PV(fixed leg); it's zero at inception (the swap rate is set to make it so) and drifts as rates move. Equivalently a strip of FRAs, or a long/short pair of bonds. Rate sensitivity is the DV01.
Q80: Cap vs floor — who buys which?
A: A cap is a strip of call options on a floating rate — a floating-rate borrower buys it to cap rising funding costs while keeping the benefit if rates fall. A floor is a strip of puts — a floating-rate lender/investor buys it to protect against falling rates. Buy a cap and sell a floor to get a cheaper collar.

14.3 Traps checklist (say these before the interviewer asks)

  • VaR is a threshold, not the maximum loss.
  • "Vega" is not a Greek letter; it's positive for long calls and long puts.
  • Theta is negative for long options (you pay time decay).
  • Historical vol is measured from the past; implied vol is the market's forward-looking view.
  • A long straddle is a volatility bet, not a directional one; watch out for vol crush.
  • Put–call parity equality holds for European options only.
  • √t VaR scaling assumes i.i.d. returns — it understates risk in trending/clustered markets.
  • Zero correlation ≠ independence; p-value ≠ probability the null is true.
  • Diversification benefits shrink in a crisis as correlations rise toward +1.
  • The binomial "p" is a risk-neutral probability, not the real chance of an up move.
  • VaR is not coherent (fails sub-additivity); Expected Shortfall is.
  • Never early-exercise an American call on a non-dividend stock; early exercise is mainly a put phenomenon.
  • CML uses total risk σ (efficient portfolios only); SML uses beta (every asset) — don't mix them up.
  • Correlation ≠ cointegration: a pairs trade needs a stationary (mean-reverting) spread, not just correlated returns.
  • High R² ≠ good/causal model; raw R² always rises with more regressors — use adjusted R² and beware spurious regressions.
Final word: On a derivatives/risk/product-control desk the recurring theme is separating what you're exposed to from what you're paid for: direction (delta) vs convexity (gamma) vs volatility (vega) vs time (theta); and expected losses (VaR) vs tail losses (ES/stress). Master the signs, the parity relation, the cost-of-carry, and the VaR/ES distinction, and you can reason through almost any question they throw at you.
