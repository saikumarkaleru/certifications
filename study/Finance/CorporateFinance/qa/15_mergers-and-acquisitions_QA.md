# Q&A — Mergers & Acquisitions

A mixed bank of theory and numerical questions. Theory answers include a model answer plus a crisp "how to say it in an interview" line. Numericals are fully solved with self-verified arithmetic.

---

## Theory

### Q1. What are the three main types of M&A by economic relationship, and what synergy does each target?

**Model answer.**
- **Horizontal** — acquirer and target are competitors in the same industry at the same stage of the value chain. Rationale: market share, scale economies, eliminating a competitor. Dominant synergy: **cost** (overlapping functions to cut).
- **Vertical** — the two firms are at different stages of one supply chain. *Backward integration* buys upstream (toward suppliers); *forward integration* buys downstream (toward customers). Rationale: secure supply, capture margin, control distribution. Synergy: **margin capture and supply security**.
- **Conglomerate** — unrelated businesses. Rationale: diversification and cash deployment. Synergy: mostly **financial/diversification**, which the market discounts.

**Interview line:** "Horizontal is same-industry-same-stage and it's about cost synergies; vertical is up or down your own supply chain for margin and supply security; conglomerate is unrelated diversification, which the market usually discounts because shareholders can diversify themselves cheaply."

---

### Q2. Explain the difference between cost synergies and revenue synergies, and which you should trust.

**Model answer.** Cost synergies reduce the combined cost base — headcount and overhead elimination, facility consolidation, purchasing scale, systems consolidation. They are largely within management's control, so they are **credible and quantifiable**. Revenue synergies increase combined revenue — cross-selling, new geographies, bundling — but they depend on *customers'* behaviour, which management can't control, so they are **unreliable**. Trust cost synergies; discount revenue synergies heavily or value them at zero, and justify the premium on cost synergies alone.

**Interview line:** "Cost synergies are your own costs — you control them, so they're credible. Revenue synergies depend on customers cross-buying — heavily discount them."

---

### Q3. Why is a control premium necessary, and what determines how high it can rationally go?

**Model answer.** A target's market price reflects the company as currently run; a shareholder can already sell at that price. To induce shareholders to give up the *whole company and control*, the acquirer must offer more — the premium. It's economically justified only because control lets the acquirer capture synergies (cut costs, change strategy, redeploy assets) that a passive shareholder can't. The **maximum rational premium equals the present value of net after-tax synergies** — pay more and the acquirer destroys value.

**Interview line:** "The premium is the price of control; it's only worth paying up to the PV of the synergies control unlocks."

---

### Q4. "A deal is 8% EPS-accretive." Is that the same as saying it creates value? Explain.

**Model answer.** No. Accretion means pro-forma EPS rose versus standalone EPS — an *earnings* screen. Value creation means the PV of after-tax synergies exceeds the premium paid. The two diverge routinely: a cheap all-debt deal can boost EPS while loading on leverage and destroying value; a stock-funded growth deal can dilute EPS yet create value. Always cross-check accretion against synergies-versus-premium in present value.

**Interview line:** "Accretion is an EPS screen, not a verdict — I'd still check whether the PV of synergies beats the premium before calling it a good deal."

---

### Q5. Cash vs stock financing — compare the economics and the signal each sends.

**Model answer.**
- **Cash (usually debt-funded):** no dilution; existing shareholders keep 100% of synergy upside and bear 100% of the risk; adds interest expense and a tax shield; raises leverage. Signals **confidence** — the acquirer isn't willing to dilute or share the upside, implying its shares aren't overvalued.
- **Stock:** dilutes existing holders; risk and reward are **shared** with the target's shareholders; no cash out, preserves debt capacity. Can signal the acquirer thinks its shares are **overvalued** (paying with expensive currency), which is why acquirer stocks often dip on stock-deal announcements.

**Interview line:** "Cash keeps the upside and signals confidence; stock shares the risk but can signal the acquirer's stock is pricey. Cheap debt favours cash; a rich P/E favours stock."

---

### Q6. Why do the majority of large acquisitions fail to create value for the acquirer?

**Model answer.** Four structural forces: (1) **the winner's curse** — the most optimistic bidder wins the auction and tends to overpay; (2) **asymmetry** — the premium is certain and paid up front while synergies are uncertain and phased over years; (3) **integration risk** — culture clash, talent flight, systems that won't merge, customer churn; (4) **agency/hubris** — managers are rewarded for size and overconfidence drives overpayment. The net effect is that value **transfers** from acquirer shareholders to target shareholders — which is why target stocks pop and acquirer stocks often fall on announcement.

**Interview line:** "Premium is certain, synergy is a risky promise — add the winner's curse and integration risk and value just transfers from the buyer's shareholders to the seller's."

---

### Q7. State the two accretion/dilution rules of thumb and the intuition behind each.

**Model answer.**
- **All-stock, no synergies:** accretive if the acquirer's P/E is **higher** than the P/E paid for the target. Intuition: you issue your own shares as currency; if your stock is richly valued relative to the earnings you buy, each new share buys more earnings than it dilutes.
- **Cash/debt-funded:** accretive if the target's **after-tax earnings yield** on the price paid exceeds the **after-tax cost of funding** (after-tax interest rate, or forgone yield on cash). Intuition: you're arbitraging the spread between the yield you buy and the rate you pay.

**Interview line:** "Stock: high P/E buying low P/E is accretive. Cash: buy a yield higher than your after-tax cost of debt and it's accretive."

---

### Q8. What is the winner's curse and how does it apply to M&A auctions?

**Model answer.** In a competitive auction with uncertain value, the winning bidder is the one who estimated value most optimistically. If estimates are unbiased on average, the *highest* estimate is systematically too high — so winning is itself evidence you overpaid. In M&A, the acquirer who wins a contested target tends to be the one who was most bullish on synergies, and therefore most likely to have paid more than the synergies are worth.

**Interview line:** "The winner of an auction is the most optimistic bidder, so winning is evidence you overpaid — that's the winner's curse."

---

### Q9. Walk through the skeleton of a basic merger model.

**Model answer.** Seven steps: (1) **Purchase price** = offer per share × target diluted shares (apply premium to the unaffected price first). (2) **Sources & uses** — fund the price with debt, cash, and/or stock; sources must equal uses. (3) **New shares** = stock consideration ÷ acquirer share price. (4) **Combine** the two income statements. (5) **Adjust**: + after-tax synergies, − after-tax new interest, − forgone interest on cash, − incremental D&A from write-ups. (6) **Pro-forma EPS** = combined adjusted net income ÷ (acquirer shares + new shares). (7) **Accretion/dilution** = compare to standalone EPS.

**Interview line:** "Price, sources and uses, new shares, combine income statements, adjust for synergies and interest, divide by the new share count, compare to standalone EPS."

---

### Q10. Why does finance treat conglomerate diversification skeptically?

**Model answer.** Because shareholders can diversify themselves far more cheaply than a company can — by simply buying both stocks for the cost of a commission. When a company diversifies on their behalf by acquiring an unrelated business, it adds no value the investor couldn't create alone, and it often adds bureaucracy and capital-misallocation risk. The market's verdict is the **conglomerate discount**: diversified groups frequently trade below the sum of their standalone parts.

**Interview line:** "Investors diversify for a brokerage commission; a conglomerate can't beat that, so the market applies a diversification discount, not a premium."

---

### Q11. What is the difference between a stock purchase and an asset purchase?

**Model answer.** In a **stock purchase**, the acquirer buys the target's shares and inherits *everything* — all assets and all liabilities, including hidden or contingent ones. It's simpler legally but riskier. In an **asset purchase**, the acquirer buys specified assets and assumes only specified liabilities — it can cherry-pick and leave bad liabilities behind, and may get a stepped-up tax basis for depreciation, but it's more cumbersome (retitle every asset) and can trigger taxes. Sellers usually prefer stock sales; buyers often prefer asset deals for the protection and tax step-up.

**Interview line:** "Stock purchase = you inherit all assets and all liabilities; asset purchase = you cherry-pick assets and liabilities, cleaner but more work and often a tax step-up."

## Numerical problems

### Q12. All-stock deal, no synergies — accretive or dilutive?

**Given.** Acquirer: NI ₹800 cr, 400 cr shares, price ₹80 (EPS ₹2.00, P/E 40×). Target: NI ₹150 cr, price ₹30, 100 cr shares (EPS ₹1.50, P/E 20×). All-stock deal at a **20% premium**, no synergies.

**Solution.**
1. Offer price = 30 × 1.20 = **₹36/share.**
2. Purchase price = 36 × 100 = **₹3,600 cr.**
3. New shares = 3,600 ÷ 80 = **45 cr.**
4. Combined NI = 800 + 150 = **₹950 cr.**
5. Pro-forma shares = 400 + 45 = **445 cr.**
6. Pro-forma EPS = 950 ÷ 445 = **₹2.1348.**
7. Accretion = (2.1348 − 2.00) ÷ 2.00 = **+6.7% → accretive.**

**Self-check (P/E rule).** P/E paid = 3,600 ÷ 150 = **24×**; acquirer P/E 40× > 24× → accretive. Confirmed.

### Q13. All-cash, debt-funded deal.

**Given.** Same acquirer and target as Q12 (acquirer NI ₹800 cr, 400 cr shares, EPS ₹2.00). Buy target for the same **₹3,600 cr**, funded 100% by debt at **7%**, tax **25%**, no synergies.

**Solution.**
1. Interest = 3,600 × 7% = ₹252 cr pre-tax.
2. After-tax interest = 252 × 0.75 = **₹189 cr.**
3. Combined NI = 800 + 150 − 189 = **₹761 cr.**
4. Shares unchanged = **400 cr.**
5. Pro-forma EPS = 761 ÷ 400 = **₹1.9025.**
6. Accretion = (1.9025 − 2.00) ÷ 2.00 = **−4.9% → dilutive.**

**Self-check (yield rule).** Target after-tax yield on price = 150 ÷ 3,600 = **4.17%**; after-tax cost of debt = 7% × 0.75 = **5.25%.** Buying a 4.17% yield with 5.25% money loses (5.25% − 4.17%) × 3,600 = ₹39 cr ≈ 800+150 standalone-combined 950 − 761 pro-forma... check: unlevered combined NI = 950; drag = 189 interest; but target contributes 150, so vs standalone acquirer of 800, the deal adds 150 − 189 = **−39 cr.** 761 vs 800 = −39. Confirmed.

### Q14. Break-even premium on an all-stock deal.

**Given.** Acquirer P/E 30×, target NI ₹100 cr, target unaffected price ₹25, 100 cr target shares. All-stock, no synergies. **What premium makes the deal exactly break even (neither accretive nor dilutive)?**

**Solution.** Break-even for a no-synergy stock deal is when P/E paid = acquirer P/E.
- Required purchase price = acquirer P/E × target NI = 30 × 100 = **₹3,000 cr.**
- Break-even offer price = 3,000 ÷ 100 = **₹30/share.**
- Break-even premium = (30 − 25) ÷ 25 = **+20%.**

**Interpretation.** Below a 20% premium the deal is accretive; above 20% it turns dilutive. The high 30× currency lets the acquirer pay up to a 20% premium before EPS dilution kicks in.

### Q15. Full model — mixed financing with synergies.

**Given.** Acquirer: NI ₹1,200 cr, 600 cr shares, price ₹120 (EPS ₹2.00). Target: 200 cr shares, unaffected price ₹45, NI ₹300 cr. Terms: **25% premium**, financed **60% stock / 40% debt**, debt **8%**, tax **25%**, pre-tax cost synergies **₹200 cr** (fully phased).

**Solution.**
1. Offer price = 45 × 1.25 = **₹56.25/share.**
2. Purchase price = 56.25 × 200 = **₹11,250 cr.**
3. Stock portion = 60% × 11,250 = ₹6,750 cr; debt portion = 40% × 11,250 = **₹4,500 cr.**
4. New shares = 6,750 ÷ 120 = **56.25 cr.** Pro-forma shares = 600 + 56.25 = **656.25 cr.**
5. After-tax interest = 4,500 × 8% × 0.75 = 360 × 0.75 = **₹270 cr.**
6. After-tax synergies = 200 × 0.75 = **₹150 cr.**
7. Pro-forma NI = 1,200 + 300 + 150 − 270 = **₹1,380 cr.**
8. Pro-forma EPS = 1,380 ÷ 656.25 = **₹2.1029.**
9. Accretion = (2.1029 − 2.00) ÷ 2.00 = **+5.1% → accretive.**

**Did it create value?**
- Premium paid = (56.25 − 45) × 200 = 11.25 × 200 = **₹2,250 cr.**
- PV of synergies at a 10% discount, perpetuity = 150 ÷ 0.10 = **₹1,500 cr.**
- Value created = 1,500 − 2,250 = **−₹750 cr.**

**Punchline.** The deal is **+5.1% accretive yet destroys ₹750 cr of value** — the 25% premium (₹2,250 cr) far exceeds the ₹1,500 cr of synergy value. Break-even required after-tax synergy = premium × discount = 2,250 × 10% = ₹225 cr after tax = ₹300 cr pre-tax, versus the ₹200 cr assumed. Classic "accretion masks overpayment."

### Q16. Contribution analysis — who should own how much of the combined company?

**Given.** In an all-stock merger, Acquirer contributes NI ₹900 cr and Target contributes NI ₹300 cr. Ignoring synergies, what ownership split does an EPS-neutral (contribution-based) deal imply?

**Solution.** Contribution analysis splits ownership by each side's contribution to combined earnings.
- Combined NI = 900 + 300 = ₹1,200 cr.
- Acquirer share = 900 ÷ 1,200 = **75%.**
- Target share = 300 ÷ 1,200 = **25%.**

**Interpretation.** On an earnings-contribution basis, target holders should end up owning ~25% of the combined company. If the target negotiates a premium, its ownership rises above 25%, and the excess above the earnings-contribution split is effectively the premium being paid in stock. Contribution analysis is a sanity check on whether the exchange ratio is fair.

### Q17. Exchange ratio and resulting ownership.

**Given.** Acquirer price ₹100, target offered ₹50/share in an all-stock deal. Acquirer has 500 cr shares; target has 100 cr shares. Find the **exchange ratio** and target holders' resulting **ownership %**.

**Solution.**
1. Exchange ratio = offer price ÷ acquirer price = 50 ÷ 100 = **0.5 acquirer shares per target share.**
2. New shares issued = 0.5 × 100 = **50 cr.**
3. Pro-forma shares = 500 + 50 = **550 cr.**
4. Target holders' ownership = 50 ÷ 550 = **9.09%.**

**Self-check.** Value handed to target = 50 cr new shares × ₹100 = ₹5,000 cr = 50 × 100 offer. Consistent.

### Q18. Cash vs stock — same deal, opposite outcome.

**Given.** Acquirer: NI ₹1,000 cr, 500 cr shares, price ₹100 (EPS ₹2.00, P/E 50×, earnings yield 2%). Target: NI ₹200 cr, purchase price ₹5,000 cr. Tax 25%. Compare **(a) all-stock** vs **(b) all-debt at 8%**, no synergies.

**Solution.**
**(a) All-stock.** New shares = 5,000 ÷ 100 = 50 cr. Pro-forma shares = 550 cr. Combined NI = 1,200 cr. EPS = 1,200 ÷ 550 = **₹2.1818 → +9.1% accretive.**

**(b) All-debt.** After-tax interest = 5,000 × 8% × 0.75 = **₹300 cr.** Combined NI = 1,000 + 200 − 300 = ₹900 cr. Shares 500 cr. EPS = 900 ÷ 500 = **₹1.80 → −10% dilutive.**

**Punchline.** Identical target, identical price — **accretive on stock, dilutive on debt.** The acquirer's stock is a 2% earnings-yield (50× P/E) currency, far cheaper than 8% (6% after-tax) debt relative to the target's 4% yield (200 ÷ 5,000). **Financing choice alone flips the deal.** This is the single most important A/D lesson.

### Q19. Maximum premium justified by synergies.

**Given.** Target unaffected equity value ₹4,000 cr. Expected annual **after-tax** synergies ₹300 cr in perpetuity; discount rate 12%; one-time integration cost ₹500 cr (paid now). What is the maximum premium (₹ and %) the acquirer can pay without destroying value?

**Solution.**
1. PV of synergies = 300 ÷ 0.12 = **₹2,500 cr.**
2. Net of integration cost = 2,500 − 500 = **₹2,000 cr.**
3. Max premium = PV of net synergies = **₹2,000 cr.**
4. Max premium % = 2,000 ÷ 4,000 = **+50%.**

**Interpretation.** The acquirer can rationally pay up to a 50% premium (₹2,000 cr) — above that, it pays away more than the synergies are worth. Any premium *below* 50% leaves value for the acquirer's shareholders.

### Q20. Effect of purchase-accounting amortisation on accretion.

**Given.** A deal is otherwise **+₹80 cr** accretive to combined net income (after synergies and interest). Purchase accounting creates **₹160 cr** of newly amortisable intangibles per year; tax 25%. Does the deal stay accretive to net income after intangible amortisation?

**Solution.**
1. Pre-tax amortisation drag = ₹160 cr.
2. After-tax drag = 160 × 0.75 = **₹120 cr.**
3. Net effect on combined NI = +80 − 120 = **−₹40 cr → dilutive.**

**Interpretation.** The "rule of thumb" that ignored purchase-accounting amortisation would have called this accretive; the extra ₹120 cr after-tax amortisation drag flips it to a ₹40 cr dilution. Always ask whether the model includes new intangible amortisation and asset write-up D&A — they are real EPS drags (though non-cash, so cash EPS may still look better).

### Q21. Premium computed off the correct (unaffected) price.

**Given.** A target traded at ₹40 thirty days before any news. A leak pushed it to ₹46 the day before the announcement. The offer is ₹52/share. What premium should be reported, and why does the base price matter?

**Solution.**
- Premium off the **unaffected** price (₹40): (52 − 40) ÷ 40 = **+30%.** ✔ correct measure.
- Premium off the **affected** price (₹46): (52 − 46) ÷ 46 = **+13%** — understates the true premium because ₹6 of run-up already reflected leaked deal information.

**Interpretation.** Always measure the premium against the unaffected price (a pre-leak spot or a 20–30-day VWAP) so the run-up from leaks isn't hidden. Reporting 13% here would badly understate what shareholders are actually being paid.

### Q22. Combined P/E after the deal — does the market re-rate?

**Given.** Acquirer market cap ₹50,000 cr at 25× P/E (NI ₹2,000 cr). It acquires a target with NI ₹500 cr for ₹7,500 cr (15× P/E paid) in an all-stock deal, no synergies. If the market values the *combined* earnings at the acquirer's 25× P/E, what value is created for the acquirer's original shareholders?

**Solution.**
1. Combined NI = 2,000 + 500 = ₹2,500 cr.
2. Combined value at 25× = 2,500 × 25 = **₹62,500 cr.**
3. New shares issued = 7,500 ÷ (share price). Value paid to target = ₹7,500 cr, so target holders own 7,500 ÷ 62,500 = **12%** of the combined ₹62,500 cr = ₹7,500 cr (consistent).
4. Acquirer's original holders now own 88% of ₹62,500 cr = **₹55,000 cr**, versus ₹50,000 cr standalone.
5. Value created for original holders = 55,000 − 50,000 = **+₹5,000 cr.**

**Where did it come from?** The market re-rated the target's ₹500 cr of earnings from the 15× the acquirer paid up to the acquirer's 25× — a **multiple arbitrage** of (25 − 15) × 500 = **₹5,000 cr.** This is the real mechanism behind high-P/E acquirers buying low-P/E targets: *if* the market grants the combined entity the higher multiple, value is created even with zero operating synergies. The risk: if the market instead applies a *blended* multiple, this paper gain shrinks or vanishes — which is why serial acquirers relying on multiple arbitrage are fragile.
