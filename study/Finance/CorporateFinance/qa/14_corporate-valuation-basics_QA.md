# Q&A — Corporate Valuation Basics

A mix of theory (with interview-ready phrasing) and fully-solved numericals. Numbers are self-verified and internally consistent.

---

## Theory

### Q1. What is the difference between enterprise value and equity value, and why does it matter?

**Answer.** Enterprise value (EV) is the value of the entire operating business — the capital claimed by *all* investors, debt and equity together. Equity value is only the shareholders' portion, left over after debt (and preferred and minority interests) are satisfied. They are linked by the bridge:

```
EV = Equity Value + Debt + Preferred + Minority Interest − Cash
```

It matters because EV is *capital-structure-neutral*: two companies with the same operations but different debt loads have (roughly) the same EV but very different equity values. So EV lets you compare businesses on an apples-to-apples basis and pairs with pre-interest metrics (EBITDA, EBIT, revenue). Equity value pairs with post-interest metrics (net income, EPS).

**How to say it:** *"EV is the price of the whole engine; equity value is the owners' slice after the lenders are paid. Bridge between them with net debt. Use EV to compare across capital structures, equity value for what shareholders actually own."*

---

### Q2. Why is cash subtracted when moving from equity value to enterprise value?

**Answer.** Cash is a non-operating asset that isn't needed to run the business. When you acquire a company, you effectively "get its cash back" — you could use it to repay part of the purchase price the day you close. So the true economic cost of owning the *operations* is the equity value plus debt *minus* the cash sitting on the balance sheet. EV is meant to isolate the operating business, so cash is stripped out.

**How to say it:** *"You buy the operations, not the bank account — the cash reduces your net cost, so EV subtracts it."*

---

### Q3. Explain FCFF and FCFE, and which discount rate goes with each.

**Answer.** FCFF (free cash flow to the firm) is unlevered cash flow — generated by operations *before* any financing, available to both lenders and shareholders. FCFE (free cash flow to equity) is levered — what remains for shareholders *after* servicing debt.

```
FCFF = EBIT×(1−t) + D&A − Capex − ΔNWC
FCFE = Net Income + D&A − Capex − ΔNWC + Net Borrowing
```

The consistency rule:

| Cash flow | Discount rate | Gives you |
|---|---|---|
| FCFF | WACC | Enterprise value |
| FCFE | Cost of equity (Kₑ) | Equity value |

**Why:** FCFF is paid to everyone, so discount at everyone's blended cost (WACC). FCFE is paid to equity only, so discount at equity's required return. Discounting FCFF at Kₑ or FCFE at WACC is a classic disqualifying error.

### Q4. Walk me through a DCF from start to per-share value.

**Answer (in order):**
1. Project FCFF for 5–10 years: EBIT → tax to NOPAT → add D&A → subtract capex and ΔNWC.
2. Discount each year's FCFF at WACC.
3. Estimate terminal value at the end of the forecast — Gordon growth `FCFFₙ×(1+g)/(WACC−g)` or an exit EBITDA multiple — and discount it back.
4. Sum the PVs → enterprise value.
5. Subtract net debt (and preferred, minority) → equity value.
6. Divide by fully diluted shares → per-share value.
7. Sanity-check TV as a % of EV, verify g < WACC and g ≤ GDP, and run WACC/g sensitivities.

**Key line:** *"A DCF output is a range, not a point — the sensitivity table is part of the answer."*

---

### Q5. What are the two ways to compute terminal value, and how do you sanity-check them?

**Answer.** (1) **Gordon growth:** `TV = FCFFₙ×(1+g)/(WACC−g)`, assuming steady perpetual growth. (2) **Exit multiple:** `TV = EBITDAₙ × chosen EV/EBITDA`, as if you sold at year n.

Sanity checks: g must be below long-run nominal GDP (~2–4%) — otherwise the firm outgrows the economy; g must be below WACC — otherwise the formula is nonsensical; and the two methods should *agree* — back out the implied g from your exit multiple and the implied multiple from your g, and make sure neither is absurd. Also check the implied perpetuity ROIC is realistic.

---

### Q6. Why is EV/EBITDA usually preferred to P/E?

**Answer.** EV/EBITDA is (a) capital-structure-neutral — EBITDA is before interest, so leverage doesn't distort it; (b) tax-neutral — before taxes, so different regimes are comparable; (c) depreciation-policy-neutral — before D&A, so asset age and accounting choices don't skew it. Both numerator and denominator are enterprise-level, so they match. P/E sits below the interest line, so it's distorted by financing (buybacks and cheap debt flatter EPS), is meaningless for loss-makers, and is sensitive to one-off items and depreciation.

**Caveat to mention:** EV/EBITDA ignores capex and working-capital intensity, so for capital-heavy businesses EV/EBIT or EV/FCFF can be better.

### Q7. Does growth always create value? Explain.

**Answer.** No. Growth creates value *only when ROIC > WACC*. Growth is a multiplier applied to the spread between the return on invested capital and its cost:

- ROIC > WACC → growth creates value.
- ROIC = WACC → growth is value-neutral.
- ROIC < WACC → growth destroys value (you tie up more capital at a negative spread).

A low-growth, high-ROIC business can be worth far more than a high-growth, low-ROIC one. This is the single most important idea in valuation and the one most candidates miss.

**How to say it:** *"Growth is a lever on the ROIC-minus-WACC spread. Positive spread, growth is gold; negative spread, growth is a wealth destroyer."*

---

### Q8. What is economic profit (EVA) and how does it relate to enterprise value?

**Answer.** Economic profit is profit *after* charging for all capital used, not just debt:

```
Economic Profit = Invested Capital × (ROIC − WACC) = NOPAT − (IC × WACC)
```

Accounting profit charges for debt (interest) but lets equity ride free; economic profit charges for both. Its link to valuation:

```
Enterprise Value = Invested Capital + PV of all future economic profits
```

A firm is worth more than its invested capital only if it earns above WACC. This reframes management's job: not to grow, but to grow the *spread × capital*.

---

### Q9. What are the core value drivers, and how do they connect?

**Answer.** Growth (g), operating margins, ROIC (capital efficiency), and reinvestment/WACC. They funnel into the value-driver formula:

```
Value = NOPAT × (1 − g/ROIC) / (WACC − g)
```

Margins lift NOPAT and, holding capital fixed, lift ROIC. ROIC is the spread itself and also determines how much you must reinvest to grow (reinvestment rate = g/ROIC). Growth multiplies the spread. All roads lead to the **ROIC–WACC spread** as the master variable. Lower WACC and lower reinvestment need (high ROIC) both raise value.

---

### Q10. What is value-based management and how does it change decisions?

**Answer.** VBM runs a company so that every major decision is judged by value creation — returns above the cost of capital — rather than accounting earnings or revenue. In practice: fund only positive-NPV / positive-economic-profit projects; measure and reward economic profit or ROIC–WACC spread rather than EPS (which leverage and buybacks can game); exit or fix businesses earning below WACC even if profitable on paper; and tie incentives to a sustained spread so managers don't chase empire-building growth.

---

### Q11. If a company issues debt and uses it to buy back stock, what happens to EV and equity value?

**Answer.** Enterprise value is roughly unchanged — operations are untouched. Net debt rises by the amount borrowed, and equity value falls by the amount of stock repurchased, so the bridge still balances (EV = equity + net debt stays flat). Over time WACC and the value of the interest tax shield change, which can nudge EV, but at the moment of the transaction EV is approximately constant while the *split* between debt and equity shifts toward debt.

## Numerical problems

### Q12. EV/EBITDA and P/E from a balance sheet.

**Given:** 80m shares at $25; total debt $600m; cash $150m; preferred $50m; EBITDA $220m; net income $96m.

**Solution.**
```
Equity value = 80 × 25 = $2,000m
Net debt     = 600 − 150 = $450m
EV = Equity + Debt + Preferred − Cash = 2,000 + 600 + 50 − 150 = $2,500m
EV/EBITDA = 2,500 / 220 = 11.4×
P/E       = 2,000 / 96 = 20.8×
```

Note EV/EBITDA pairs enterprise with enterprise; P/E pairs equity with equity. ✓

---

### Q13. Reverse bridge — from EV to per-share value.

**Given:** A DCF yields enterprise value of $3,400m. The firm has total debt $900m, cash $200m, minority interest $100m, and 120m shares.

**Solution.**
```
Equity value = EV − Debt − Minority + Cash
             = 3,400 − 900 − 100 + 200 = $2,600m
Value/share  = 2,600 / 120 = $21.67
```

### Q14. Terminal value both ways, and the cross-check.

**Given:** Year-5 FCFF = $500m; year-5 EBITDA = $900m; WACC = 9%; assumed perpetual g = 3%.

**Gordon growth:**
```
TV = 500 × (1.03) / (0.09 − 0.03) = 515 / 0.06 = $8,583.3m
```

**Implied exit EV/EBITDA:**
```
Implied multiple = TV / EBITDA₅ = 8,583.3 / 900 = 9.5×
```

**Cross-check the other direction:** if instead comps suggested an exit multiple of 8.0×, then TV = 900 × 8.0 = $7,200m, which implies:
```
7,200 = 515 / (0.09 − g)  →  0.09 − g = 515/7,200 = 0.0715  →  g = 1.85%
```

So an 8.0× exit implies ~1.9% perpetual growth — internally consistent and reasonable. The two methods triangulate.

### Q15. Full two-stage FCFF DCF to per share.

**Given:** FCFF Year 1 = $300m, growing 6%/yr through Year 5. WACC = 10%. Terminal g = 3%. Net debt = $700m. Shares = 150m.

**Step 1 — FCFF and PV (discount at 10%).**

| Year | FCFF | 1/1.1ᵗ | PV |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 300.0 | 0.9091 | 272.7 |
| 2 | 318.0 | 0.8264 | 262.8 |
| 3 | 337.1 | 0.7513 | 253.3 |
| 4 | 357.3 | 0.6830 | 244.1 |
| 5 | 378.8 | 0.6209 | 235.2 |

(FCFF grows 6%: 300 → 318 → 337.1 → 357.3 → 378.8.)
Sum of PV = 272.7 + 262.8 + 253.3 + 244.1 + 235.2 = **$1,268.1m**.

**Step 2 — terminal value at Year 5.**
```
FCFF₆ = 378.8 × 1.03 = 390.2
TV₅   = 390.2 / (0.10 − 0.03) = 390.2 / 0.07 = $5,574.3m
PV of TV = 5,574.3 × 0.6209 = $3,461.1m
```

**Step 3 — EV, equity, per share.**
```
EV           = 1,268.1 + 3,461.1 = $4,729.2m
Equity value = 4,729.2 − 700 = $4,029.2m
Value/share  = 4,029.2 / 150 = $26.86
```

**Step 4 — TV as % of EV** = 3,461.1 / 4,729.2 = **73.2%** — normal, and flags terminal-assumption sensitivity.

### Q16. Does this project create value? (ROIC vs WACC)

**Given:** A project needs $500m of invested capital and generates NOPAT of $45m/year in perpetuity (no growth). WACC = 10%.

**Solution.**
```
ROIC = NOPAT / IC = 45 / 500 = 9.0%
Since ROIC (9%) < WACC (10%), the project DESTROYS value.

Economic profit = IC × (ROIC − WACC) = 500 × (0.09 − 0.10) = −$5m/year
Value = NOPAT / WACC − IC = 45/0.10 − 500 = 450 − 500 = −$50m  (negative NPV)
```

The project earns 9% on capital that costs 10% — a value leak of $5m/year, or $50m in present-value terms. Reject it.

### Q17. Same NOPAT, opposite outcomes — the value of growth.

**Given:** Two firms, each NOPAT = $80m next year, WACC = 9%, growth g = 4%. Firm X: ROIC = 18%. Firm Y: ROIC = 7%. Use `Value = NOPAT×(1 − g/ROIC)/(WACC − g)`.

**Firm X:**
```
Reinvestment = 4%/18% = 0.2222 ; conversion = 0.7778
Value = 80 × 0.7778 / (0.09 − 0.04) = 62.22 / 0.05 = $1,244.4m
```
No-growth value = 80/0.09 = $888.9m → **growth added $355.5m**.

**Firm Y:**
```
Reinvestment = 4%/7% = 0.5714 ; conversion = 0.4286
Value = 80 × 0.4286 / 0.05 = 34.29 / 0.05 = $685.7m
```
No-growth value = 80/0.09 = $888.9m → **growth destroyed $203.2m**.

**Takeaway:** identical NOPAT, WACC, and growth — but X's growth is worth +$355m and Y's is worth −$203m. The only difference is ROIC vs WACC.

### Q18. Multiples valuation with a comp set.

**Given:** Target EBITDA = $180m; net debt = $250m; 40m shares. Comps trade at EV/EBITDA of 6.0×, 8.0×, 8.0×, 11.0×.

**Solution.**
```
Median multiple = (8.0 + 8.0)/2 = 8.0×
EV = 180 × 8.0 = $1,440m
Equity value = 1,440 − 250 = $1,190m
Value/share  = 1,190 / 40 = $29.75

Range using 6.0× and 11.0×:
Low:  EV 1,080 → equity 830  → $20.75/share
High: EV 1,980 → equity 1,730 → $43.25/share
```

**Answer:** ~$29.75/share central case, range ~$21–$43. Justify positioning within the range by comparing the target's margins/growth to the comps.

### Q19. FCFF from net income (reconciliation).

**Given:** Net income = $150m; interest expense = $40m; tax rate = 25%; D&A = $90m; capex = $110m; ΔNWC = +$30m.

**Solution.**
```
FCFF = Net Income + Interest×(1−t) + D&A − Capex − ΔNWC
     = 150 + 40×0.75 + 90 − 110 − 30
     = 150 + 30 + 90 − 110 − 30 = $130m
```

The after-tax interest is added back because FCFF is *pre-financing* — it belongs to lenders too, so we undo the interest deduction (net of its tax shield).

---

### Q20. FCFE from FCFF.

**Given (from Q19):** FCFF = $130m; interest = $40m; tax = 25%; net borrowing (new debt − repayments) = +$20m.

**Solution.**
```
FCFE = FCFF − Interest×(1−t) + Net Borrowing
     = 130 − 40×0.75 + 20
     = 130 − 30 + 20 = $120m
```

Check against the direct formula: FCFE = NI + D&A − Capex − ΔNWC + Net Borrowing = 150 + 90 − 110 − 30 + 20 = **$120m**. ✓ Both agree.

### Q21. WACC and its effect on a valuation.

**Given:** Equity value $3,000m (Kₑ = 12%); debt $1,000m (pre-tax Kd = 6%); tax 25%.

**Solution.**
```
V = 3,000 + 1,000 = $4,000m
E/V = 0.75 ; D/V = 0.25
WACC = 0.75×12% + 0.25×6%×(1−0.25)
     = 9.0% + 0.25×4.5%
     = 9.0% + 1.125% = 10.125%
```

If a no-growth firm generates FCFF of $400m/year, EV = 400 / 0.10125 = **$3,951m**. Had WACC been 11%, EV = 400/0.11 = $3,636m — an ~8% swing from ~0.9 points of WACC. Small discount-rate changes move value a lot; this is why WACC is worth getting right.

### Q22. Economic profit and market value added.

**Given:** Invested capital = $2,000m; NOPAT = $260m; WACC = 10%.

**Solution.**
```
ROIC = 260 / 2,000 = 13.0%
Economic profit = IC × (ROIC − WACC) = 2,000 × (0.13 − 0.10) = $60m/year

If this economic profit is a level perpetuity:
PV of future economic profit = 60 / 0.10 = $600m  (market value added)
Enterprise value = IC + MVA = 2,000 + 600 = $2,600m
```

Cross-check via the value formula (no growth): EV = NOPAT/WACC = 260/0.10 = $2,600m. ✓ The company is worth $600m *more* than the capital invested in it — purely because it earns a 3% spread over WACC.
