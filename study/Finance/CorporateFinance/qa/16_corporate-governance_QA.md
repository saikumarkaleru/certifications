# Q&A — Corporate Governance

A mixed bank of theory (with model answers and interview phrasing) and fully solved numerical problems. Numbers are self-verified for internal consistency.

---

## Theory

### Q1. What is the agency problem, and why is it the root of corporate governance?

**Model answer.** A company is a legal fiction; every action is taken by a person spending money that isn't theirs. Whenever **control rights** (who decides) are separated from **cash-flow rights** (who owns the residual), the decision-maker's incentives diverge from the owner's, and — because owners can't observe everything (information asymmetry) — the controller can divert value. That divergence is the agency problem. Governance is the system of monitors, incentives, and legal rights that shrinks it.

**How to say it:** *"Separation of ownership and control is the root; every governance tool is a patch on the resulting incentive gap."*

---

### Q2. Distinguish Type I and Type II agency conflicts. Why does it matter for how you analyze an Indian vs. a US firm?

**Model answer.** **Type I** is manager vs. dispersed owners — the US/UK case: no owner is large enough to monitor, so managers gain de facto control and can shirk, empire-build, or over-consume perks. **Type II** is controlling shareholder vs. minority — the emerging-market/European case: a promoter or family holds a controlling block, so the danger isn't a lazy manager but an insider **tunneling** value away from minorities via related-party transactions.

It matters because the *tools* differ. Type I is fixed with pay alignment, board independence, and the takeover market. Type II is fixed with related-party controls, majority-of-the-minority votes, disclosure, and legal minority rights — aligning the controller's pay is pointless because they already own huge equity.

**How to say it:** *"Diagnose the conflict first. In India the promoter is the CEO — the risk is expropriation of minorities, not manager shirking."*

---

### Q3. Walk me through the main functions and fiduciary duties of a board.

**Model answer.** Functions: (1) hire, evaluate, pay, and fire the CEO — the most important; (2) approve strategy and major capital allocation; (3) oversee risk, controls, and integrity of financial reporting; (4) ensure compliance, ethics, and succession. Fiduciary duties: **duty of care** (act with diligence — protected by the business judgment rule, which shields informed, good-faith, disinterested decisions); **duty of loyalty** (company's interest over personal — this is what related-party self-dealing and insider trading violate); and **duty of good faith / candor**.

**How to say it:** *"A board's one irreplaceable job is hiring and firing the CEO; loyalty is the duty that RPTs breach."*

---

### Q4. Are independent directors truly independent? Why do they so often fail?

**Model answer.** On paper yes; in practice often not. Failure modes: **selection capture** (the CEO/promoter still shapes nominations), **information asymmetry** (directors see only what management shows them and meet a few days a year), **reputation and fee incentives** (they want to keep the seat), **diffusion of responsibility**, and an **expertise gap** (a retired diplomat can't challenge a derivatives book). Satyam had a Harvard professor on its board and still missed a fabricated $1bn+ cash balance.

**How to say it:** *"Independence is necessary but not sufficient — you also need real information access, committee power, and skin in the game."*

---

### Q5. Name the key board committees and why each is independent.

**Model answer.** **Audit** — oversees financial reporting, internal controls, and the internal/external auditors; must be independent and financially literate because management prepares the numbers and the committee must be able to challenge the CFO (SOX requires full independence + a financial expert + power to hire/fire the auditor). **Nomination/Governance** — board composition and succession; independence breaks the CEO's grip on who joins the board. **Compensation/Remuneration** — designs and approves executive pay; independence stops the CEO setting his own pay. **Risk** — sets risk appetite separate from revenue-driven management (critical for banks).

**How to say it:** *"The audit committee is the one that must be able to say no to the CFO — that's why it's fully independent."*

---

### Q6. Why do stock options misalign incentives even though they're meant to align them?

**Model answer.** An option is a **call** — a convex payoff. The executive captures all the upside but the downside is capped at zero; he doesn't fund losses. So options reward **volatility**, not just value (high vega): he'll take a risky bet that could double the stock even if it's value-neutral or negative for diversified shareholders. They also invited backdating and repricing. Fixes: blend in **restricted stock** (symmetric downside), add **clawbacks and deferral**, use **relative performance** vesting, and impose **share-ownership requirements**.

**How to say it:** *"Delta aligns direction; vega distorts risk. Give them real stock, not just convex claims."*

---

### Q7. Explain tunneling and why pyramids make it structurally tempting.

**Model answer.** Tunneling is the transfer of resources out of a firm to benefit the controlling shareholder — via related-party transactions at off-market prices (cash-flow tunneling), asset transfers (asset tunneling), or dilutive insider allotments and unfair merger ratios (equity tunneling). Pyramids (A owns B owns C) let a promoter **control** C with a small **economic** stake, because control is the minimum majority link while ownership multiplies down the chain. The promoter keeps 100% of what he diverts to a wholly-owned entity but bears only his small cash-flow share of the firm's loss — so stealing pays far more than the same rupee of honest profit.

**How to say it:** *"It's a wedge story: control 100%, own 13% — diversion pays about 6–7× more than creation."*

---

### Q8. What concrete mechanisms protect minority shareholders?

**Model answer.** **Structural** — independent directors, independent audit/RPT oversight, split Chair/CEO, sometimes cumulative voting to elect a minority director. **Transactional** — RPT approval by **majority of the minority** (interested controller barred from voting), mandatory open offers/tag-along on control change, appraisal rights in squeeze-outs, preemptive rights against dilution. **Legal** — derivative suits, class actions, oppression-and-mismanagement remedies (NCLT §241–242 in India). **Market** — proxy advisors, activists, and cross-listing on stricter exchanges (bonding). LLSV showed stronger protection → higher multiples and deeper markets.

**How to say it:** *"Structural, transactional, legal, market — and the crown jewel is the majority-of-the-minority vote on RPTs."*

---

### Q9. Financial vs. double materiality in ESG — and how should a finance analyst treat ESG?

**Model answer.** **Financial (single) materiality** — the ISSB/IFRS S1-S2 lens — is about sustainability issues that affect **enterprise value** (investor view). **Double materiality** — the EU CSRD lens — additionally covers issues where the **company affects society/environment** even without a clear financial hit. A finance analyst should frame ESG — especially the "G" — as **material risk and cash-flow protection**, not virtue: weak governance is a tail-risk multiplier that belongs in the discount rate and the downside scenario. Be aware of greenwashing and the low correlation across ESG raters.

**How to say it:** *"ISSB = enterprise value; CSRD = impact on the world. I price the G as risk, not ethics."*

---

### Q10. Is a poison pill good or bad for shareholders?

**Model answer.** Both. A poison pill lets existing holders buy cheap shares if a raider crosses a threshold, massively diluting the raider; it deters coercive lowball bids and gives the board **bargaining power** to extract a higher premium — good if the board is a faithful agent. But it also **entrenches** weak management against value-improving takeovers — bad if the board is captured. The tell: is it a short-term negotiating tool subject to a shareholder vote, or a permanent shield paired with a staggered board?

**How to say it:** *"It cuts both ways — depends entirely on whether the board is a faithful agent."*

---

### Q11. What is shareholder activism, and how does a proxy fight work?

**Model answer.** Activism is the market's answer to the collective-action problem: a large investor internalizes enough of the monitoring gains to make engagement worthwhile. A hedge-fund activist (Elliott, Icahn, ValueAct) builds a stake, engages privately, then goes public with letters and demands — buybacks, spin-offs, cost cuts, a sale, or a CEO change. If the board resists, it escalates to a **proxy fight**: soliciting other shareholders' proxies to vote its director slate or resolution against the board's. Winning means persuading the big index funds — which is why **proxy advisors** (ISS, Glass Lewis) hold outsized power.

**How to say it:** *"Activists monetize governance improvement; the battleground is the institutional vote, refereed by ISS and Glass Lewis."*

---

### Q12. What is the "market for corporate control" and why does it matter?

**Model answer.** It's the ultimate external discipline: if managers underperform, the share price falls, an acquirer buys control and replaces them. The mere *threat* keeps managers honest. Managers defend with poison pills, staggered boards, golden parachutes, supermajority provisions, and dual-class shares — defenses that can protect long-term value from lowball raids **or** entrench weak managers, depending on the board's faithfulness.

**How to say it:** *"The takeover threat is the discipline of last resort; anti-takeover defenses either strengthen bargaining or entrench — know which."*

---

### Q13. Say-on-pay, clawbacks, and relative performance evaluation — what problem does each solve?

**Model answer.** **Say-on-pay** gives shareholders a vote on the comp package — advisory in the US (reputational force), binding on policy in the UK — checking captured comp committees. **Clawbacks/malus** recover paid or forfeit unvested pay on restatement or misconduct (SOX §304, Dodd-Frank), so managers can't keep bonuses earned on numbers later reversed. **Relative performance evaluation** pays on performance *relative to peers*, filtering out luck so a rising market doesn't enrich a mediocre CEO. Together they attack the "managerial power" side of pay.

**How to say it:** *"Say-on-pay checks the comp committee; clawbacks strip ill-gotten gains; RPE removes luck."*

---

### Q14. Give a governance-failure case and its lesson.

**Model answer (Satyam).** Founder-chairman Ramalinga Raju fabricated over ₹7,000 crore of cash and margins for years, and tried to bury the hole by having Satyam acquire promoter-owned Maytas firms — a related-party deal the market rejected, which unravelled the fraud. The star independent board and auditors (PwC) missed it. Root causes: promoter dominance, weak audit challenge, toothless independent directors. It drove India's Clause 49 and Companies Act 2013 reforms — audit-committee independence, mandatory auditor rotation, and RPT controls.

**How to say it:** *"Promoter dominance plus a captured board and auditor — the reform response was RPT controls and auditor rotation."*

## Numerical problems

### Q15. Pyramid control and the tunneling incentive

**Problem.** A promoter owns 60% of A; A owns 55% of B; B owns 52% of C. All firms are one-share-one-vote. (a) Does the promoter control C? (b) What is the promoter's economic (cash-flow) stake in C? (c) If C is made to overpay ₹200 to a 100%-promoter-owned private firm, what is the promoter's net gain? (d) Compare with earning ₹200 honestly.

**Solution.**
(a) Each link is a majority (60%, 55%, 52%), so the promoter controls each firm and therefore **controls C** decisively.
(b) Cash-flow stake = 0.60 × 0.55 × 0.52 = **0.1716 = 17.16%.**
(c) Tunneling ₹200: promoter receives the full ₹200 via the private firm, but bears his 17.16% share of C's ₹200 loss = 0.1716 × 200 = ₹34.32.
Net gain = 200 − 34.32 = **+₹165.68.**
(d) Honest ₹200 profit paid up: promoter receives only his economic share = 0.1716 × 200 = **₹34.32.**
Ratio: 165.68 / 34.32 = **4.83× more from stealing than creating.**

**Self-check.** 0.60 × 0.55 = 0.33; × 0.52 = 0.1716 ✓. 200 − 34.32 = 165.68 ✓. 165.68/34.32 = 4.83 ✓.
**Takeaway.** The wedge (control 100% vs. own 17%) makes diversion ~4.8× more rewarding than honest profit — the structural driver of tunneling.

### Q16. Governance discount in valuation

**Problem.** Firm G and Firm B are identical: next-year FCFE = ₹80 crore, g = 4% perpetual. Firm G's ke = 11%. Firm B has poor governance: investors demand a 4% governance premium (ke = 15%) and expect 12% of cash flow to be tunneled before minorities receive it. (a) Value G. (b) Value B on discount-rate effect only. (c) Value B to minorities including leakage. (d) Total governance discount.

**Solution.**
(a) G = 80 / (0.11 − 0.04) = 80 / 0.07 = **₹1,142.86 crore.**
(b) B (rate only) = 80 / (0.15 − 0.04) = 80 / 0.11 = **₹727.27 crore.**
(c) Minorities receive 88% of cash flow → FCFE₁ = 0.88 × 80 = 70.4. Value = 70.4 / 0.11 = **₹640.00 crore.**
(d) Total discount = (1142.86 − 640.00) / 1142.86 = 502.86 / 1142.86 = **44.0%.**
Split: discount-rate channel = (1142.86 − 727.27)/1142.86 = **36.4%**; cash-flow channel adds ~7.6 points.

**Self-check.** 80/0.07 = 1142.86 ✓. 80/0.11 = 727.27 ✓. 70.4/0.11 = 640.00 ✓. 502.86/1142.86 = 0.440 ✓.
**Takeaway.** Poor governance destroys ~44% of value from the minority's seat — split between a higher discount rate and cash flow that never arrives. Governance is priced.

### Q17. Executive stock options — cost, dilution, alignment, risk

**Problem.** A CEO gets 500,000 at-the-money options, strike ₹400, on a firm with 50m shares. Black–Scholes value = ₹120/option; delta ≈ 0.55. (a) Grant-date expense (and per-year over 3-year vesting). (b) CEO gross gain if the stock reaches ₹550 at vesting. (c) % dilution to old holders (net-share basis). (d) Alignment: CEO wealth change per ₹1 stock move. (e) The risk distortion in one line.

**Solution.**
(a) Expense = 500,000 × ₹120 = **₹6,00,00,000 (₹6 crore)**, amortized ≈ **₹2 crore/year** over 3 years.
(b) Intrinsic = 550 − 400 = ₹150. Gross gain = 500,000 × 150 = **₹7,50,00,000 (₹7.5 crore).**
(c) New shares = 500,000 on 50,000,000 → 500,000 / (50,000,000 + 500,000) = 500,000 / 50,500,000 = **0.990%** of enlarged count.
(d) Δ wealth per ₹1 = 500,000 × 0.55 = **₹2,75,000 per ₹1** rise.
(e) The option caps his downside at zero but not his upside, so he prefers volatility even when it's value-neutral for diversified shareholders.

**Self-check.** 500k × 120 = ₹6 cr ✓; /3 = ₹2 cr ✓. 150 × 500k = ₹7.5 cr ✓. 0.5/50.5 = 0.990% ✓. 500k × 0.55 = ₹2.75 lakh ✓.
**Takeaway.** Delta gives real directional alignment (₹2.75 lakh per ₹1) but the convex payoff distorts risk — pair options with restricted stock and clawbacks.

### Q18. Majority-of-the-minority defeats a self-dealing RPT

**Problem.** Listco has 200m shares; promoter owns 120m (60%), public 80m. The promoter proposes a material RPT (₹40 crore/yr royalty to a promoter firm). Under MoM rules the promoter cannot vote and approval needs a majority of votes cast by the rest. Of the 80m public shares, 50m vote: 18m for, 32m against. (a) Does it pass under MoM? (b) Under the ordinary rule (promoter votes for)? (c) Approximate value protected at a 10% discount rate.

**Solution.**
(a) Only 80m non-promoter shares eligible; 50m voted. Threshold = >50% of votes cast = >25m. For = 18m < 25m → **FAILS.**
(b) Ordinary: promoter's 120m + 18m = 138m for; 32m against; total cast = 170m; majority = >85m. 138m > 85m → **PASSES.**
(c) Value of avoided leakage ≈ ₹40 crore / 0.10 = **₹400 crore** of enterprise value defended.

**Self-check.** MoM threshold >25m; 18 < 25 → fail ✓. Ordinary: 138 > 85 → pass ✓. 40/0.10 = 400 ✓.
**Takeaway.** The identical transaction fails or passes purely on **who may vote** — disenfranchising the conflicted controller converts a rubber stamp into a real minority veto.

### Q19. Dual-class shares — the control/ownership wedge

**Problem.** A founder holds 10m Class B shares (10 votes each); the public holds 90m Class A shares (1 vote each). (a) Founder's economic (cash-flow) stake. (b) Founder's voting control. (c) The wedge, and one governance risk it creates.

**Solution.**
(a) Total shares = 10m + 90m = 100m. Economic stake = 10m / 100m = **10%.**
(b) Votes: founder = 10m × 10 = 100m; public = 90m × 1 = 90m; total = 190m. Voting control = 100m / 190m = **52.6%.**
(c) Wedge = 52.6% control vs. 10% ownership = **~42.6 points.** Risk: the founder is immune from removal and takeover discipline while bearing only 10% of the cost of value-destroying decisions — entrenchment and reduced accountability. (Some indices exclude or down-weight dual-class firms for this reason.)

**Self-check.** Founder votes 100m of 190m = 52.63% ✓. Economic 10/100 = 10% ✓.
**Takeaway.** Dual-class delivers majority control on a minority economic stake — great for founder vision, dangerous for entrenchment; the wedge is the thing to flag.

### Q20. Pay-for-performance sensitivity and relative performance

**Problem.** CEO holds 2m shares. (a) If the stock rises ₹15, what is her wealth change — her pay-performance sensitivity? (b) The sector index rose 20% and the stock rose from ₹200 to ₹215 (7.5%). Under **relative performance evaluation**, did she outperform, and should the market-driven gain be rewarded? (c) One-line design implication.

**Solution.**
(a) Δ wealth = 2,000,000 × ₹15 = **₹3,00,00,000 (₹3 crore)** — her wealth moves ₹3 crore per ₹15, i.e., ₹20 lakh per ₹1.
(b) Stock return = 15/200 = **7.5%**; sector = 20%. She **underperformed** the sector by 12.5 points. Under RPE, the ₹15 gain is largely a **market/luck** effect, not skill — most of it should *not* trigger incentive pay; a peer-relative plan would pay little or nothing.
(c) Implication: use **relative TSR** vesting so beta-driven gains in a rising market don't enrich underperformance.

**Self-check.** 2m × 15 = ₹3 cr ✓. 15/200 = 7.5% < 20% → underperform ✓.
**Takeaway.** Absolute pay-for-performance rewards luck; RPE strips out the market to pay for genuine outperformance.

### Q21. Agency cost trade-off

**Problem.** A firm can add monitoring (independent directors, auditors, proxy oversight) at annual cost. Current residual loss (value lost to agency slack) = ₹120 crore. Option A: spend ₹10 crore monitoring, cutting residual loss to ₹80 crore. Option B: spend ₹40 crore, cutting residual loss to ₹55 crore. Ignore bonding. (a) Total agency cost now and under each. (b) Which is optimal? (c) Principle.

**Solution.**
Total agency cost = Monitoring + Residual loss.
- Now: 0 + 120 = **₹120 crore.**
- Option A: 10 + 80 = **₹90 crore.**
- Option B: 40 + 55 = **₹95 crore.**
(b) **Option A** minimizes total agency cost (₹90 cr < ₹95 cr < ₹120 cr). Option B cuts residual loss more but the extra ₹30 crore of monitoring only buys ₹25 crore of reduced slack — negative marginal return.
(c) Governance minimizes the **sum** of monitoring + bonding + residual loss, not residual loss alone. Beyond a point, more monitoring destroys value.

**Self-check.** A: 10+80=90 ✓. B: 40+55=95 ✓. Marginal B vs A: spend +30 to save +25 → worse ✓.
**Takeaway.** There is an *optimal*, not maximal, level of governance spending — the goal is the lowest total agency cost.

### Q22. Cumulative voting — can a minority elect a director?

**Problem.** A company elects 5 directors. There are 10,000,000 shares. Under **cumulative voting**, each share carries votes equal to the number of seats, and a shareholder may pile all votes on one candidate. (a) How many shares must a minority bloc control to guarantee electing **one** director? (b) If the minority holds 1.5m shares, can they guarantee a seat? (c) Contrast with straight voting.

**Solution.**
(a) The formula for shares needed to elect *n* of *D* directors with *S* shares outstanding:
Shares = [ n × S / (D + 1) ] + 1 = [1 × 10,000,000 / (5 + 1)] + 1 = 1,666,667 + 1 = **1,666,668 shares (≈16.67%).**
(b) 1.5m < 1,666,668 → **No**, 1.5m cannot *guarantee* a seat (they'd need ~16.67%).
(c) Under **straight voting**, each seat is a separate majority contest, so a 60% controller wins **every** seat and a minority can elect **none**. Cumulative voting lets a ~16.7% bloc guarantee one of five — a structural minority protection.

**Self-check.** 10,000,000/6 = 1,666,666.7; +1 → 1,666,668 ✓. 1.5m < that → cannot guarantee ✓.
**Takeaway.** Cumulative voting converts board elections from winner-take-all into proportional-ish, letting a sizable minority secure representation — a genuine structural protection.
