# Cup & Handle

## What it is & why it works

The cup and handle is one of the most reliable **continuation** patterns in technical analysis — a bullish base that forms after a prior advance and signals the trend is preparing to resume. It was popularised by William O'Neil in *How to Make Money in Stocks* as a cornerstone of his CAN SLIM growth-stock methodology, and it remains a workhorse for swing and positional traders, including on Indian equities and indices.

The pattern looks exactly like its name: a **rounded, U-shaped "cup"** followed by a smaller, downward-drifting **"handle"** on the right side, with a breakout above the cup's rim completing the setup. Picture a teacup viewed from the side.

The reason it works is a clean story about **supply absorption and the resetting of sentiment**:

1. A stock has run up, then pulls back. If the pullback is a gentle, *rounded* decline rather than a sharp V, it means selling pressure is *fading gradually* — holders are giving up slowly, not dumping in panic. The rounded bottom is the market working off excess supply and rebuilding a base of committed holders.
2. As price rounds back up toward the old high (the cup's left rim), it meets the traders who bought at the prior peak and are now "breakeven sellers" — people who bought the top, sat through the drawdown, and just want their money back. This overhead supply causes the **handle**: a small, controlled pullback or sideways drift near the highs as that last tranche of weak-handed supply is shaken out.
3. Once the handle has flushed those sellers, there is little resistance left overhead. A breakout above the rim, on rising volume, sees price accelerate into "blue sky" — the trend resumes.

Crucially, the cup and handle is a **continuation pattern**: it works best when it forms *after* a meaningful prior uptrend, as a pause-and-reload, not as a bottom after a long downtrend. The prior advance is the fuel; the cup is the digestion; the handle is the final shakeout; the breakout is the resumption. The **handle is the key tell** — it separates a genuine, tradeable cup and handle from a mere rounded bottom, because it proves the overhead supply has been tested and absorbed right at the point of maximum resistance.

There is also an inverted (bearish) version — an "inverted cup and handle" or "cup and handle top" — a rounded top followed by a small upward handle that breaks *down*, used to trade the resumption of a *downtrend*. The logic is the mirror image.

## The mechanics — construction, rules and settings

O'Neil laid down fairly specific proportions; a serious trader treats them as guidelines, not laws, but they matter.

| Element | Guideline | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Prior uptrend | 30%+ advance before the cup | Confirms it's a *continuation*, gives fuel |
| Cup depth | 12–33% retrace of the prior advance (up to ~50% in wild markets) | Too shallow = no real base; too deep = damage done |
| Cup shape | Rounded "U", not a sharp "V" | Gentle rounding = gradual supply absorption |
| Cup duration | 7 weeks to 65 weeks typically (weeks-to-months) | A real base takes time |
| Handle location | In the upper third of the cup, near the rim | Handle should form on strength, not deep |
| Handle depth | Shallow — usually 10–15% pullback (up to ~1/3 of cup depth) | A deep handle signals real weakness |
| Handle drift | Downward-sloping or sideways | Shakes out weak longs |
| Handle duration | 1–4 weeks (shorter than the cup) | A quick final flush |
| Breakout | Close above the rim / handle high on volume ≥ ~1.4× average | Confirms demand overwhelmed supply |

**Volume signature** — this is central to the pattern's reliability:

- Volume should **decline into the base** (the left side of the cup and the bottom) as selling dries up.
- Volume often **picks up on the right side** of the cup as demand returns.
- Volume should **dry up further within the handle** — the shakeout should be *quiet*, indicating few sellers left.
- The **breakout must come on a volume surge** — ideally 40%+ above the average (in O'Neil's work, often much more). A breakout on weak volume is the number-one reason cups fail.

**The measured move / target.** The standard projection is the **depth of the cup added to the breakout point**. If the rim is at ₹1,000 and the cup bottom was ₹850, the cup depth is ₹150, so the target is ₹1,000 + ₹150 = ₹1,150. Some traders use a more conservative "handle-height" projection or scale out in stages. The stop typically sits **below the handle low**.

**Timeframe.** O'Neil's original work was on *daily/weekly* charts for growth stocks, and that's where the pattern is most robust (bases of weeks to months). But the fractal pattern also appears intraday — a cup and handle on a 15-minute Bank Nifty chart works on the same logic over hours. The larger and longer the base, the more significant the eventual move.

## Reading it — a worked India example, phase by phase

Let's build a **cup and handle on a quality Indian largecap**, say a stock trading around **₹1,000** after a strong run, on the daily chart.

**Phase 1 — the prior advance (the fuel).** The stock has rallied from ₹720 to ₹1,000 over three months on strong earnings and institutional buying — a ~39% advance. This qualifies as a proper prior uptrend, so any base that forms is a *continuation* candidate.

**Phase 2 — the left side of the cup.** The stock tops at ₹1,000 (the **left rim**) and begins a *gentle, rounded* decline — not a crash. Over five weeks it drifts to a low of **₹850** (a ~15% retrace of the prior advance — a healthy, moderate cup depth). Volume tapers as it falls: sellers are exhausting, not panicking. The daily chart shows a smooth U, not a spike-down V.

**Phase 3 — the right side of the cup.** Demand returns. Over the next five weeks the stock rounds back up from ₹850 toward the ₹1,000 rim, with volume picking up on up-days. It reaches ₹995 — right at the old high — where it stalls. This is the overhead supply: breakeven sellers from the ₹1,000 top are offloading.

**Phase 4 — the handle (the shakeout).** Instead of breaking straight through, the stock forms a small, downward-drifting handle: it pulls back from ₹995 to **₹945** over eight sessions — a shallow ~5% dip, in the upper third of the cup, on *declining* volume. This quiet drift shakes out the impatient longs and the last of the breakeven sellers. The handle low, ₹945, becomes the reference for the stop.

**Phase 5 — the option-chain and volume tell.** Because this is an F&O stock, the option chain is informative. As the handle forms, **call OI at the ₹1,000 strike starts to unwind** (writers who sold the 1,000 calls are covering, sensing the resistance won't hold) and **put writing builds at ₹950** (participants confident the handle low holds). This positioning shift foreshadows the breakout.

**Phase 6 — the breakout.** The stock closes at **₹1,020**, decisively above the ₹1,000 rim and the handle high, on volume ~1.7× its 50-day average. The base is complete; the pattern triggers.

**Phase 7 — the measured move.** Cup depth = ₹1,000 − ₹850 = **₹150**. Target = ₹1,000 (rim) + ₹150 = **₹1,150**. Over the next three weeks the stock advances to ₹1,155, pausing once to *retest* the ₹1,000 rim (now support) which holds cleanly — the textbook breakout-retest — before completing the move. A trader who bought the breakout at ₹1,020 with a stop at ₹940 risked ₹80 to make ₹130+, roughly 1.6R, with a runner beyond the target if momentum persisted.

## Trading it — entries, stops, targets, management

**Primary entry — the breakout:**
- **Trigger:** a daily *close* above the cup rim / handle high on volume ≥ ~1.4× average. In the example, buy at ₹1,020 on the confirmed breakout.
- **Stop:** below the handle low (₹940–945). This is the pattern's natural invalidation — if price falls back below the handle, the shakeout failed and demand wasn't real.
- **Target:** cup depth added to the breakout point → ₹1,150. Book partial there; trail the rest under rising swing lows if the trend extends into blue sky.

**Aggressive entry — inside the handle:**
- Buy as the handle finds support and turns up (near ₹945–950) *before* the breakout, anticipating it. This gets a lower entry and better R:R but risks the breakout failing to confirm. Use a tighter stop just below the handle low and *reduce size* to reflect the lower certainty. Only for experienced traders comfortable with a lower hit-rate.

**Conservative entry — the retest:**
- Let the breakout happen, then wait for the pullback that *retests* the broken rim (₹1,000) and holds. Enter on the bounce with a stop below the retest low. Fewer trades, but the highest reliability — you've seen the rim flip from resistance to support.

**Multiple scenarios and management:**

1. **Clean breakout, no look-back:** price runs from the breakout. Trail stops under swing lows; take the measured-move target as a partial and let a runner ride the resumed trend.
2. **Breakout-and-retest (ideal):** as above — the retest confirms and offers a second, tighter entry.
3. **Failed breakout / undercut:** price closes back below the rim and into the handle within a couple of sessions. Honour the stop. A failed cup often means the overhead supply was heavier than the handle suggested; don't argue with it.
4. **Handle too deep:** if what you thought was a handle deepens past a third of the cup (here, below ~₹900), the "handle" is really a new down-leg — the base needs more time. Stand aside; the pattern isn't ready.
5. **Low-volume breakout:** if price nudges above the rim but volume is *flat*, treat it as suspect. Many such breakouts fade back. Wait for a volume-backed close or the retest.

**Position sizing** off the stop distance and fixed-fractional risk (say 1%). Because the base took weeks to build, the resulting move can be substantial and worth holding — this is a pattern that rewards patience on both the *wait* and the *hold*.

## Confluence — stacking the odds

- **Volume** is the pattern's built-in confirmer and the first thing to check: taper into the base, dry-up in the handle, surge on the breakout. A cup and handle without a volume surge on the break is a coin-flip, not a setup.
- **Prior trend & relative strength:** the pattern is strongest on stocks that are *leaders* — outperforming the Nifty, near 52-week highs, with strong earnings (O'Neil's CAN SLIM emphasises this). A cup and handle in a market/sector uptrend, on a stock with rising relative strength, is far higher-probability than the same shape on a laggard.
- **Moving averages:** a cup whose bottom finds support on the 50- or 200-DMA, and a breakout that occurs with price above a rising 50-DMA, adds structural support. The handle ideally holds above a key MA.
- **Option chain / OI (for F&O stocks and indices):** call-writing *unwinding* at the rim strike and put-*writing* building at the handle low — as in the example — is a strong positioning confirmer that the breakout will hold. A breakout that traps short call-writers accelerates fastest.
- **Market context:** the pattern works best when the broader Nifty is trending up or at least constructive. Breakouts into a weak or falling market fail more often — check the index before trusting the stock.
- **Candlestick trigger:** a bullish engulfing or a strong wide-range up-close on the breakout day gives a precise, higher-conviction entry candle.

The layered read: the cup tells you a base has formed, the handle tells you overhead supply has been tested, volume tells you demand overwhelmed it on the break, relative strength tells you it's a leader worth owning, and OI tells you positioning agrees. When these stack, it's a high-conviction continuation trade.

## Pitfalls & false signals

- **No handle = no trade (or a weaker one).** A rounded bottom *without* a handle hasn't tested the overhead supply at the rim, so breakouts are more likely to stall into the old-high sellers. The handle is what makes the pattern reliable — don't skip it.
- **V-shaped "cup."** A sharp V recovery means the decline was panic and the rebound was a reflex, not a patient absorption of supply. Genuine cups are *rounded*; V-bottoms that "break out" fail more often because no real base was built.
- **Handle too deep or too low in the cup.** A handle that retraces more than about a third of the cup, or forms in the *lower* half rather than the upper third, signals real weakness — it's a new down-leg masquerading as a handle. Wait; the base isn't done.
- **Low-volume breakout.** The most common failure. Without a volume surge, the breakout lacks the demand needed to overwhelm overhead supply and typically fades back into the base. Demand the volume.
- **Cup too deep.** A cup that retraces well over half the prior advance means serious damage was done to the trend; the "continuation" thesis weakens. Deep cups can still work but are lower-probability and behave more like bottoms than continuations.
- **Trading it as a bottom after a downtrend.** The classic misuse: labelling any rounded low a "cup and handle." Without a *prior uptrend* to continue, you've lost the pattern's core logic. It's a continuation setup first.
- **Impatience.** Bases take time. Buying inside a still-forming cup, or before the handle completes, front-runs the pattern and racks up whipsaws. Wait for the structure and the volume-backed break.
- **Broad-market blindness.** A perfect cup on a great stock still fails if the Nifty is rolling over. Pros filter every breakout through market context and only press size when the index is supportive.

Professionals treat the cup and handle as a *checklist* pattern: prior uptrend, proper cup depth and rounding, a shallow high-forming handle on quiet volume, and a volume-backed breakout — plus leadership/relative strength and a constructive market. Missing pieces downgrade it from a trade to a watch.
