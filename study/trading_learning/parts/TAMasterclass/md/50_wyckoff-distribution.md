# Wyckoff: Distribution & the Three Laws

## What it is & why it works

Distribution is the mirror image of accumulation: the process by which large operators — institutions, HNIs, DIIs, FIIs, prop desks — quietly transfer the stock or index they accumulated cheaply into the hands of an excited, late-arriving public near the top of a move. Richard D. Wyckoff, watching the tape of the early 20th-century US markets, reverse-engineered how the "Composite Operator" (his fictional single smart-money entity) works, and codified it into a repeatable schematic and three governing laws. A century later the behaviour is unchanged because the driver is unchanged: markets are auctions, and a big seller cannot dump size at a single price without collapsing the bid. So distribution *must* look like strength — a broad, choppy, sideways ceiling that keeps the public bullish while supply is fed out into every rally.

Why does it work as an analytical framework? Because it forces you to read **cause and effect through volume**, not price alone. A retail chartist sees Nifty stuck in a 1,200-point range near an all-time high and calls it "consolidation before the next leg up." A Wyckoffian asks a different question: *is this range absorbing supply (re-accumulation) or absorbing demand (distribution)?* The answer is written in the relationship between the size of the up-bars and down-bars, the volume behind each, and how price behaves at the edges of the range. Distribution is where the market's character silently flips from "buy the dip" to "sell the rip" — often weeks before the trend actually breaks. Recognising it early is the difference between exiting your longs near ₹1,000 and watching them get marked down to ₹720.

Distribution matters especially in Indian markets because our indices are top-heavy and expiry-driven. Bank Nifty, with its handful of heavyweight constituents (HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank, SBI, Axis, Kotak), can be distributed through the option chain and the cash tape simultaneously — you see the price capped while call writers pile in at a strike and the index makes a series of lower-volume highs. The schematic and the option-chain OI tell the same story from two angles.

## The mechanics

Wyckoff rests on **Three Laws** and a **schematic of labelled events/phases**. Master both.

**Law 1 — Supply and Demand.** Price rises when demand exceeds supply and falls when supply exceeds demand. Trivial as a sentence, powerful as a lens: every bar is evidence about which side is winning. Wide-spread up-bars on rising volume = demand in control; wide-spread down-bars on rising volume = supply in control; narrow bars = balance or lack of interest.

**Law 2 — Cause and Effect.** A cause must be built before an effect appears. The sideways range *is* the cause; the subsequent trend *is* the effect. Wyckoff measured cause with the **Point-and-Figure horizontal count**: the wider the distribution range (more P&F columns), the bigger the eventual markdown. A three-week ceiling produces a shallow fall; a three-month ceiling produces a bear leg.

**Law 3 — Effort vs Result.** Volume is effort; the price change is result. When effort and result diverge, the trend is failing. Huge volume (effort) on a rally that closes with a tiny gain (result) means supply is meeting every buyer — a classic distribution tell. This law is the single most useful diagnostic on the tape.

**The distribution schematic — events, in order:**

| Event | Full name | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| PSY | Preliminary Supply | First heavy selling into the uptrend; big volume, wide spread, first warning |
| BC | Buying Climax | Blow-off top; euphoric volume, widest spread, news-driven; smart money sells into it |
| AR | Automatic Reaction | Sharp fall as buying dries up; defines the **lower** boundary of the range |
| ST | Secondary Test | Rally back toward BC on *lower* volume and narrower spread — supply confirmed |
| SOW | Sign of Weakness | Down-move that breaks below the range on rising spread/volume |
| UT | Upthrust | Push above resistance that fails and closes back inside — a bull trap |
| UTAD | Upthrust After Distribution | The decisive terminal upthrust; final liquidity grab above the range before markdown |
| LPSY | Last Point of Supply | Weak, low-volume rally after a SOW — the last good short entry |

**Phases A–E** organise these:
- **Phase A** — stopping the prior uptrend (PSY, BC, AR, ST).
- **Phase B** — building the cause; the range oscillates, institutions distribute; often the longest phase.
- **Phase C** — the test: the UTAD (or a final ST) that traps late buyers.
- **Phase D** — supply dominates; lower highs inside the range, then SOW breaks; rallies to LPSY get sold.
- **Phase E** — markdown; the trend down runs, punctuated by weak reactions back to the underside of the range.

**Distribution vs re-accumulation — the discriminators:** volume on the UTAD (climactic supply = distribution), behaviour on the test (a range that *cannot* rally back on volume is distributing), and the character of the break (SOW on expanding volume with poor rallies confirms the bear case). If tests come back *up* on strong volume and shakeouts get bought, it was re-accumulation instead — flip the schematic.

## Reading it — a worked Nifty example

Let me walk a stylised but realistic Nifty distribution across roughly ten weeks, with levels in index points (and think of an equivalent ₹-per-lot P&L for a 50-lot position where useful).

**Phase A.** Nifty has trended from 21,800 to 24,900. On a Tuesday it prints a **Buying Climax (BC)** at 24,980 — the widest daily range in months, ~380 points high-to-low, on the heaviest cash volume of the quarter, propelled by a rate-cut headline. Everyone on TV is bullish. Over the next four sessions Nifty falls to 24,180 — the **Automatic Reaction (AR)**, ~800 points, sharp and impulsive. This pair (BC + AR) sets the box: resistance ~24,980, support ~24,180. A **Secondary Test (ST)** rallies price back to 24,820 but on visibly *lower* volume and a narrower daily spread than the BC. Effort-vs-result screams: buyers pushing, price barely reaching the old high. Warning logged.

**Phase B.** For five weeks Nifty chops between roughly 24,200 and 24,950. To the crowd this is "healthy consolidation near highs." Under the hood: up-days show shrinking volume, down-days show swelling volume. Every rally to 24,900 stalls; Bank Nifty (the leader) starts making *lower* highs while Nifty holds — a leadership divergence. Supply is being fed out.

**Phase C — the trap.** On expiry week Nifty spikes to **25,060**, a new marginal high above the range — the **UTAD**. It looks like a breakout; breakout-buyers and short-stops get triggered. But volume on the push is climactic and price closes the day back at 24,760, *inside* the range. That failed breakout is the smart-money's final liquidity harvest. This is your highest-conviction signal that the top is in.

**Phase D.** Nifty rolls over. It slices the range support at 24,180 on a wide-spread, high-volume down-day — the **Sign of Weakness (SOW)** — reaching 23,900. A weak, low-volume bounce carries it back to 24,150 (the underside of the old range) — the **Last Point of Supply (LPSY)**. Narrow spread, no conviction. This retest of broken support from below is textbook.

**Phase E — markdown.** From the LPSY, Nifty trends down: 24,150 → 23,400 → 22,600, with each bounce weaker than the last. The **cause** built in the five-week Phase B (a wide P&F count) delivers its **effect** — roughly a 2,300-point markdown, in line with a horizontal count taken across the range.

## Trading it

You get several distinct, riskable entries. Never short the BC blindly — it can extend. Wait for structure.

**Entry 1 — the UTAD failure (aggressive).** Short when price pushes above the range high (25,060) and *closes back inside* (below 24,980). Stop just above the UTAD high — say 25,120 (a ~60-point risk from a 25,060 fill, or place it beyond the intraday spike). First target the range low, 24,180 (~880 points), second target the measured move. This is the best reward-to-risk entry because your stop is defined by the exact high the operator created.

**Entry 2 — the SOW break (confirmation).** Short the close below range support (24,180) on expanding volume, or short the *retest* of that broken level (the LPSY near 24,150). Stop above the LPSY swing, ~24,320. This entry sacrifices some price but adds confirmation — supply has now visibly overwhelmed demand.

**Entry 3 — LPSY rally-sell (safest).** Sell the weak bounce into the underside of the range. Tight stop above the range midpoint; you're now trading *with* a confirmed down-trend.

**Measured move / target.** Use the P&F horizontal count across the widest part of the Phase B congestion, or a simpler heuristic: project the AR depth (BC 24,980 − AR 24,180 = 800) down from the breakdown (24,180 − 800 = 23,380) as a first objective, then the full count for the extended target (~22,600).

**Management.** Trail the stop above each successive lower high in Phase E. Scale out: book a third at the range low, a third at the first measured objective, let the last third run under a trailed stop. If instead of breaking down price re-enters the range and closes back above the midpoint on strong volume, the distribution has *failed* (it was re-accumulation) — exit shorts immediately. Distribution that doesn't distribute is a warning to flip.

**Scenario B — failed distribution.** Say the SOW break at 24,180 gets bought aggressively and price snaps back above 24,600 on huge volume within two sessions. That's a spring-like reversal; the range was re-accumulation. Your Entry-2 short stops out for a small loss (the discipline of the stop above LPSY saves you). Respect it — do not average a losing short into strength.

## Confluence

Distribution reads far better with corroboration.

**Volume / VSA.** The whole schematic *is* volume analysis, but overlay Volume Spread Analysis explicitly: no-demand up-bars (narrow spread, low volume, up close) throughout Phase B, and a climactic up-bar on the UTAD, are the confirming fingerprints.

**Option chain & OI (India-critical).** During genuine index distribution you typically see aggressive **call writing** stacking at and just above the range ceiling — say heavy Call OI build at the 25,000 strike capping every rally — while Put OI at the floor gets *unwound* as the support weakens. A rising Put-Call Ratio that then rolls over, combined with call writers defending 25,000 through the UTAD, is powerful confluence: the derivatives crowd is short the top just as the schematic says supply dominates. Watch for the shift where **puts start getting written lower** in Phase D — that's the options market pricing the markdown.

**Relative strength / breadth.** Leadership divergence (Bank Nifty topping while Nifty holds), a falling advance-decline line, and fewer stocks above their 50-DMA all corroborate a topping index even as the headline price holds.

**Moving averages & structure.** A break of the 50-DMA on the SOW, then rejection at a falling 20-DMA on the LPSY bounce, aligns MA structure with the Wyckoff labels.

**Higher-timeframe context.** Distribution near a weekly all-time high or a long-term trendline/round number (Nifty 25,000) carries far more weight than a range in the middle of a trend.

## Pitfalls & false signals

**Every range is not distribution.** The single biggest error is labelling a re-accumulation range as distribution and shorting into a continuation. The discriminator is behaviour on the test and the break: distribution *fails to rally on volume* and *breaks down easily*; re-accumulation *shakes out and gets bought*. If you can't tell, wait for the SOW/spring to resolve.

**UTAD vs genuine breakout.** Not every push above the range fails. A real breakout closes *and holds* above resistance on strong, sustained volume with follow-through. Only call it a UTAD once price closes back *inside*. Shorting a breakout before it fails is how traders get run over near highs.

**Hindsight labelling.** Wyckoff schematics are seductive in hindsight — you can always find a BC, AR, ST after the fact. The honest test is whether you'd have labelled it *in real time* with a defined stop. If a label only appears once you know the outcome, it's curve-fitting, not analysis.

**News noise in India.** Budget day, RBI policy, expiry, and global gaps can produce climactic bars that mimic BCs but are event artefacts. Weight the schematic, not any single bar. A BC that isn't followed by an AR and an ST on lower volume is just a volatile day.

**Effort-vs-result whipsaws.** High volume with small result can also mean a genuine battle that demand *wins*. Confirm with the next few bars — one divergent bar is a flag, not a verdict.

**How pros filter.** They demand at least two of {UTAD failure, effort-vs-result divergence, option-chain call-writing confirmation, breadth/leadership divergence} before pressing shorts, and they let the market *prove* supply with a SOW before sizing up. They also respect that distribution can morph into re-accumulation — the stop above the range is non-negotiable.

## Interview-ready summary

"Distribution is Wyckoff's model for how smart money sells its position near a top without crashing the price — so it looks like a broad, choppy ceiling that keeps the public bullish while supply is fed into every rally. It's governed by three laws: supply-and-demand (who's winning each bar), cause-and-effect (the width of the range sets the size of the fall), and effort-versus-result (climactic volume with no price progress = supply absorbing demand). The schematic runs Buying Climax → Automatic Reaction → Secondary Test in Phase A, a distributing range in Phase B, a terminal UTAD bull-trap in Phase C, a Sign of Weakness break and weak LPSY rally in Phase D, then markdown in Phase E. On Nifty near 25,000 I'd short the UTAD failure with a stop above the spike high, add on the SOW break of range support, and target a measured move from the P&F count — and I'd confirm the read with aggressive call-writing at the ceiling in the option chain and a breadth divergence. The discipline is admitting when it's actually re-accumulation: if the range gets bought back on volume, I'm out. TA gives me a high-probability map of a top, not a certainty — the stop above the range is what makes it tradeable."
