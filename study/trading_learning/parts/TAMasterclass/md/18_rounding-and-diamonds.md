# Rounding Tops/Bottoms & Diamond Patterns

## What it is & why it works

This chapter covers two of the more subtle, less-hyped patterns in technical analysis — the **rounding** formation (also called a saucer or, at bottoms, a "rounding bottom" or "cup") and the **diamond**. Both are patterns of *transition*, and both reward the patient trader who can read structure rather than chase every squiggle.

A **rounding bottom** is a slow, curved, U-shaped base that marks the gradual transition from a downtrend, through a period of apathetic accumulation, to a new uptrend. There is no sharp V; instead price drifts down, flattens, and drifts back up in a smooth arc. A **rounding top** is the inverse — a dome-shaped (∩) distribution that marks the gradual roll from an uptrend into a downtrend. The defining feature is **gradualism**: the trend doesn't snap, it *bends*. Volume typically traces its own bowl — high at the edges, drying up in the middle, and expanding again as the new trend asserts itself.

Why does it work? Because a rounding bottom is a picture of **quiet accumulation with no urgency**. After a decline, selling pressure slowly exhausts. There's no panic capitulation and no aggressive buying — just a gradual shift in the supply/demand balance as long-term buyers absorb stock at a pace that doesn't move price much. The volume bowl is the tell: participation dies as the market loses interest (the boring middle), then revives as the new trend attracts attention. By the time the arc turns up, sellers are gone and the path of least resistance is up. Rounding tops mirror this — enthusiasm fades gradually, distribution occurs quietly, and the dome rolls over. These patterns work precisely *because* they lack drama; they represent a genuine, unhurried change in ownership.

A **diamond** is a rarer and more complex pattern, most often a **reversal** (a diamond top after an uptrend is the classic). Visually it looks like a Head & Shoulders whose neckline has been bent into a rhombus. It forms in two halves: first a **broadening** phase (price swings get *wider* — higher highs and lower lows, reflecting rising volatility and indecision/emotional extremes), followed by a **contracting** phase (a symmetrical triangle where swings *narrow* as the market coils). Connect the highs and lows and you get a four-sided diamond (◇). 

Why does a diamond work? The broadening half is a market losing its composure — volatility expanding, participants whipsawed, a distribution climax of emotion at a top. The contracting half is that emotion resolving into a decision. A break out of the lower edge of the contracting phase signals that the decision is *down*, and because the pattern brewed so much volatility, the ensuing move is often sharp. Diamonds are, in essence, a two-act play: chaos, then resolution.

Honesty check: both patterns are **interpretive**. Rounding formations have fuzzy boundaries — reasonable analysts will draw the arc differently, and there is no single crisp trigger line until a breakout confirms. Diamonds are genuinely rare and frequently mis-labelled (many "diamonds" are really just messy ranges). Treat both as *context* patterns whose signal only becomes actionable on a confirmed breakout with volume. They are lower-frequency, and you should never force the label onto ambiguous price action.

## The mechanics

**Rounding bottom — construction and rules:**

| Feature | Specification |
|---|---|
| Prior trend | Down (or a long base after a decline) |
| Shape | Smooth U / saucer; no sharp V |
| Duration | Weeks to many months; the longer, the more significant |
| Volume | Bowl-shaped: high→low (middle)→high; dries up at the base |
| Confirmation | Breakout above the pattern's left-side rim / resistance, on rising volume |
| Handle (optional) | A small pullback after the rim break — the "cup and handle" variant |
| Target | Depth of the bowl projected up from the breakout, or a measured move equal to the pattern's height |

The **rim** (or lip) is the horizontal resistance at the height where the pattern began on the left — the confirmation level. The **cup-and-handle** is a rounding bottom followed by a small, downward-drifting consolidation (the handle) near the rim; the buy trigger is the break above the handle's high. Target for a cup and handle = depth of the cup added to the breakout point.

**Rounding top** mirrors all of this: dome shape (∩), volume that is heavy early then thins, confirmation on a **break below** the pattern's rim/support, target = dome height projected down.

**Diamond — construction and rules:**

| Phase | What happens | Lines to draw |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Broadening | Swings widen: higher highs, lower lows | Up-sloping resistance off the highs? No — diverging trendlines: rising highs, falling lows |
| 2. Contracting | Swings narrow: lower highs, higher lows | Converging trendlines (a symmetrical triangle) |
| Result | Four trendlines form a rhombus | ◇ |
| Confirmation | Close beyond the contracting phase's boundary (usually the lower line for a top) | Break of the second-half triangle |
| Volume | Often elevated in broadening phase, contracts, then surges on breakout | — |

**Diamond measured move.** Measure the **maximum height** of the diamond (the vertical distance from its highest high to its lowest low at the widest point) and project it from the breakout point in the breakout direction.

- Diamond-top target = Breakout price − Diamond height

**Confirmation filters (both patterns):**

1. **Breakout close beyond the confirmation line** — daily close standard; a 1–3% or ~0.5×ATR filter to reduce whipsaws.
2. **Volume expansion** on the breakout is important — rounding patterns especially depend on the volume bowl reviving; a breakout on dead volume is suspect.
3. **The retest** — price often returns to the rim (rounding) or the broken diamond edge before continuing; a held retest is the cleaner entry.

## Reading it — a worked India example

**Rounding bottom on Tata Motors (weekly/daily), realistic levels.** After a long decline and a period of drift, Tata Motors carves a saucer. Price bottoms out around **₹580** over several months, having declined from a left-side rim near **₹720**. Through the base the volume is anaemic — the "boring middle" where nobody cares. As the arc curls up, weekly volume begins to expand. Price grinds back toward the **₹720** rim.

**Phase 1 — left rim & decline.** Price rolls over from ₹720, declining in a smooth curve (not a crash) toward ₹580 over ~3 months, volume tapering the whole way.

**Phase 2 — the base.** For several weeks price oscillates quietly between ₹580 and ₹610. Volume is at its lowest — classic apathy. RSI hovers near 45–50, no divergence needed; the *shape* is the signal.

**Phase 3 — the right side & rim test.** Buyers gradually take control; price arcs up from ₹610 to ₹700, then ₹715, on steadily rising volume, approaching the ₹720 rim.

**Phase 4 — breakout.** Tata Motors **closes at ₹735**, above the ₹720 rim, on the heaviest volume in months. The rounding bottom is confirmed. Depth of the bowl = 720 − 580 = **₹140**; target = 720 + 140 = **₹860**.

**Phase 5 — the handle (optional).** Instead of running immediately, price drifts back to **₹705** in a tight, low-volume handle for two weeks, then breaks the handle high at ₹725. This cup-and-handle completion is the textbook low-risk entry; over the following months the stock works toward ₹855.

**Diamond top on Bank Nifty (daily), realistic levels.** After a strong rally to around **52,500**, Bank Nifty enters a volatile, choppy phase. First the **broadening** half: swings widen — a high at 52,800, then a lower low at 51,200, then a higher high at 53,100, then a lower low at 50,900 — volatility expanding, participants whipsawed. Then the **contracting** half: 52,600 high, 51,400 low, 52,200 high, 51,700 low — swings narrowing into a coil. Connecting these gives a diamond with its widest point spanning roughly 53,100 to 50,900 — a **height of ~2,200 points**.

Bank Nifty then **closes at 51,300**, below the lower boundary of the contracting triangle, on heavy volume. Diamond-top confirmed. Target = 51,300 − 2,200 = **~49,100**. A brief retest to 51,600 fails, and the index sells off toward 49,000 over the next weeks.

## Trading it

**Rounding bottom — entry.** Three options: (1) **anticipatory** small buys within the base as the arc turns up (₹610–650 zone) — early, cheapest, but unconfirmed; (2) **rim breakout** on the confirming close above ₹720 (buy ₹735); (3) **handle/retest entry (preferred)** — buy the break of the handle high (₹725) or the retest of the rim, giving a tight stop.

- **Stop:** below the base low (₹575) for a conservative position, or below the handle low (₹700) for a tight one. On a handle entry at ₹725 with a stop at ₹700 (₹25 risk) and target ₹860 (₹135), that's **~5:1**.
- **Target:** bowl depth projected (₹860). Scale out at intermediate resistance (₹800) and trail the rest.

**Diamond top — entry.** Enter on the confirming close below the lower contracting boundary (short Bank Nifty at 51,300), or preferably on the **retest** of the broken edge (short ~51,600 on the failed bounce).

- **Stop:** above the last lower-high inside the contracting phase, or above the diamond's midline (e.g., 52,300). On a retest entry at 51,600 with a stop at 52,300 (700 points) and target 49,100 (2,500 points) = **~3.5:1**.
- **Target:** diamond height projected (49,100). Book partial at a structural level (50,000, a round number and prior support) and trail.

**Scenario A — clean success.** Tata Motors handle entry ₹725, stop ₹700, runs to ₹860. Textbook.

**Scenario B — false breakout / failed retest.** Bank Nifty breaks 51,300, throws back, and spikes to 51,900 before failing. A stop at exactly 51,400 gets hit; a stop beyond structure (52,300) survives and the target is still met. Place stops beyond the pattern's noise.

**Scenario C — outright failure.** Tata Motors breaks ₹720, then reverses and closes back below ₹580 (the base low) — the rounding bottom has failed, likely on bad news; exit immediately. For the diamond, a close back above the upper contracting boundary voids the top; diamonds that fail can resolve as continuations, so respect the invalidation.

**Management.** Rounding patterns move *slowly* — be patient; the target may take weeks to months. Diamonds move *fast* after the break — take partials quickly and trail aggressively, since the volatility that built the pattern can reverse just as violently.

## Confluence

- **Volume is central**, especially for rounding patterns: the bowl-shaped volume profile (drying up in the middle, expanding on breakout) *is* the confirmation. A rounding bottom that breaks its rim on weak volume is not trustworthy. In Indian stocks, rising **delivery percentage** through the right side of the saucer signals genuine accumulation by longer-term holders.
- **Moving averages:** a rounding bottom is powerfully confirmed when the 50-DMA crosses above the 200-DMA (golden cross) as the rim breaks; a rounding/diamond top aligns with a death cross.
- **Support/resistance & round numbers:** a rim at a prior support shelf or a psychological round number (₹720, 52,000) strengthens the level.
- **Momentum:** for a diamond top, bearish RSI/MACD divergence during the broadening phase adds conviction that the emotional highs lack real strength.
- **Option-chain / OI (India edge):** on the Bank Nifty diamond top, check where the OI walls sit. If the broadening-phase highs are being capped by **heavy Call OI**, and the breakdown level coincides with **Put OI building below** while **Call writers add overhead**, the distribution read is confirmed. Rising India VIX during the broadening phase fits the "volatility climax" story; a VIX that then eases as the triangle contracts and spikes again on the break is classic. For a rounding bottom, watch for **Put writing building at the base** (writers confident the low holds) and **Call unwinding** as price breaks the rim — signs option desks expect higher prices.
- **Sector/breadth:** a rounding bottom in an index or heavyweight is more reliable when breadth is quietly improving beneath the surface (more stocks above their 50-DMA even as the index looks dull).

The premium rounding-bottom trade: a saucer with a **textbook volume bowl**, breaking its rim **on a golden cross** at a **round-number support-turned-support**, with **Put writers defending the base**. The premium diamond-top trade: a clean broadening-then-contracting structure with **bearish divergence**, **rising VIX**, breaking down on **volume** into a **Put-OI build**.

## Pitfalls & false signals

1. **Over-drawing the arc.** Rounding patterns are the easiest to imagine where none exists. Any messy sideways drift can be "rounded" with a curved line. Demand the full picture: a genuine prior trend, a smooth (not jagged) arc, *and* the volume bowl. Without the volume signature, be skeptical.

2. **No confirmation trigger until the breakout.** Rounding patterns lack a crisp neckline; the *only* real trigger is the rim/handle breakout on volume. Don't commit a full position on the shape alone — it can drift sideways for months longer or roll back over.

3. **Diamonds are rare and over-diagnosed.** Most "diamonds" are just noisy ranges or poorly-drawn Head & Shoulders. Require a genuine broadening phase followed by a genuine contracting phase, with four definable trendlines. If you have to squint, it isn't a diamond.

4. **Broadening-phase whipsaw.** Trading *inside* a diamond's broadening half is a meat-grinder — that's the point of the pattern (maximum volatility). Wait for the contraction and the breakout; don't try to catch the wild swings.

5. **Weak-volume breakouts.** Both patterns depend on volume expansion at the break. A rim break or diamond break on dead volume is prone to failure and retest-through. Insist on participation.

6. **Impatience (rounding) vs. over-holding (diamond).** Rounding moves are slow — traders bail too early out of boredom. Diamond moves are fast — traders over-hold and give back gains when the sharp move mean-reverts. Match your management style to the pattern's tempo.

7. **News override.** As with all patterns, a macro shock (RBI, Budget, global risk event) can gap through the structure. These interpretive patterns are especially vulnerable to being invalidated by a single fundamental catalyst. Size conservatively and respect stops.

Pros treat rounding and diamond patterns as **lower-frequency, context-rich** setups: they wait for the volume-confirmed breakout, prefer the retest/handle entry, size so a failed interpretive pattern is survivable, and never force the label onto ambiguous price action — if it isn't obvious, it isn't there.

## Interview-ready summary

"Rounding tops and bottoms are gradual, curved reversals — a U-shaped saucer base (rounding bottom) or a dome (rounding top) — where the trend bends rather than snaps. They work because they picture quiet, unhurried accumulation or distribution, and the tell is a bowl-shaped volume profile: participation dies in the boring middle and revives on the breakout. There's no crisp trigger until price breaks the rim on rising volume; the cup-and-handle is the version with a small consolidation before the run, and my target is the bowl depth projected from the breakout. Diamonds are rarer reversal patterns — a Head-and-Shoulders whose neckline is bent into a rhombus: first a broadening phase of expanding volatility, then a contracting triangle, resolving on a break of the lower edge, with the target being the diamond's height projected down. I confirm both with a volume-backed close beyond the trigger and prefer the retest or handle entry for a tight stop. In India I cross-check the option chain — Put writers defending a rounding-bottom base, or Call-OI capping a diamond's highs with rising VIX. And I stay honest: both are interpretive and easy to over-draw, so if the shape and the volume don't both agree, I don't trade it — and I never force the pattern onto messy price action."
