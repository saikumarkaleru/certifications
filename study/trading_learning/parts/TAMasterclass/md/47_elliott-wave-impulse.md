# Elliott Wave I: Impulse Waves

## What it is & why it works

Elliott Wave Theory, developed by Ralph Nelson Elliott in the 1930s, says that markets do not move randomly but in repetitive, self-similar patterns that reflect the swings of crowd psychology between optimism and pessimism. The core claim is simple but powerful: a trending market unfolds in a five-wave sequence in the direction of the larger trend, followed by a three-wave correction against it. This 5-3 structure is the fundamental "beat" of the market, and it repeats across every degree of time — from a one-minute Bank Nifty chart to a multi-decade Nifty supercycle.

The **impulse wave** is the five-wave motive structure that drives price in the direction of the trend. Labelled 1-2-3-4-5, it is the engine of every bull leg (and, inverted, every bear leg). Waves 1, 3 and 5 are the *action* waves that push in the trend direction; waves 2 and 4 are the *reaction* waves that pull back. The reason it "works" is not magic — it is a formalisation of how participation builds in a trend. Wave 1 is the early money nibbling after a bottom, disbelieved by the crowd. Wave 2 is the sceptics selling the bounce, testing whether the low holds. Wave 3 is the recognition phase — news turns positive, breakouts trigger, institutions pile in, and price travels the furthest and fastest. Wave 4 is profit-taking and consolidation as early buyers book gains. Wave 5 is the final push driven by the last, most euphoric latecomers, often on narrowing breadth and bearish divergence.

For an Indian trader, Elliott Wave is best treated as a **probabilistic map of structure**, not a crystal ball. Its real value is threefold. First, it tells you *where you are* in a move — a wave 3 is a very different trade from a wave 5. Second, it imposes discipline through hard rules that, if broken, immediately invalidate your count and force you out. Third, it dovetails beautifully with Fibonacci, giving measurable targets and stops. It is not certainty; it is a framework for organising probability, and alternate counts always exist. Honest wave analysts hold two counts and let price choose.

## The mechanics

An impulse wave must satisfy **three inviolable rules**. Break any one and it is not a valid impulse — you must relabel.

| Rule | Statement | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Rule 1 | Wave 2 never retraces more than 100% of wave 1 | If price undercuts the start of wave 1, the low was not the origin of an impulse |
| Rule 2 | Wave 3 is never the shortest of waves 1, 3 and 5 | Wave 3 is usually the longest and most powerful; it may not be the shortest |
| Rule 3 | Wave 4 never overlaps the price territory of wave 1 | In a standard impulse, wave 4's low must stay above wave 1's high (in an uptrend) |

Beyond the rules are **guidelines** — strong tendencies that hold most of the time and help you pick the most probable count:

- **Alternation:** if wave 2 is a sharp, deep correction, wave 4 tends to be a shallow, sideways one — and vice versa. The market rarely corrects the same way twice in a row.
- **Extension:** one of the three action waves usually extends (becomes disproportionately long and subdivides clearly). In equity indices, wave 3 is the most common extension.
- **Fibonacci proportion:** wave 2 commonly retraces 50-61.8% of wave 1; wave 4 commonly retraces 38.2% of wave 3. Wave 3 frequently measures 1.618x wave 1. Wave 5 often equals wave 1, or measures 0.618x the net travel of waves 1 through 3.
- **Wave 4 channel:** drawing a trendline across the ends of waves 2 and 4, with a parallel off wave 3, often contains the whole impulse and projects where wave 5 ends.

Each wave belongs to a **degree**. Elliott's nested labelling runs (largest to smallest): Grand Supercycle, Supercycle, Cycle, Primary ((1)(2)...), Intermediate ((1) in circles), Minor (1 2 3), Minute (i ii iii), Minuette, Subminuette. The key discipline is that a wave 3 of one degree is itself a five-wave impulse of the next degree down. This **fractal self-similarity** is the theory's backbone — and its main difficulty in practice.

Two special impulse types exist. A **diagonal** is a wedge-shaped motive wave where wave 4 *does* overlap wave 1 (an exception to Rule 3 that applies only to diagonals). A **leading diagonal** appears in wave 1 or wave A; an **ending diagonal** appears in wave 5 or wave C and warns of exhaustion and a sharp reversal.

## Reading it — a worked India example

Consider a stylised but realistic Nifty 50 advance of the kind seen after a major correction low. Suppose Nifty bottoms at **21,150** after a grinding decline. What follows is an Intermediate-degree impulse.

**Wave 1 (21,150 -> 22,000):** Price lifts 850 points over a couple of weeks. Volume is unconvincing, the media is still bearish ("dead-cat bounce"), and most traders are looking to short. This disbelief is the signature of wave 1. The advance is choppy and overlapping in its internals — typical early-trend behaviour.

**Wave 2 (22,000 -> 21,470):** A sharp pullback retraces about **62%** of wave 1 (850 x 0.618 = 525 points; 22,000 - 525 = 21,475). It is fast and scary, convincing the crowd the bounce is over. Crucially, it holds above 21,150, satisfying Rule 1. The deep, sharp character of this wave 2 tells us by alternation to expect a *shallow, sideways* wave 4 later.

**Wave 3 (21,470 -> 23,850):** This is the recognition wave. RBI turns dovish, FII flows return, quarterly earnings beat, and price accelerates. Wave 3 travels **2,380 points** — very close to 1.618x wave 1 (850 x 1.618 = 1,375... note in strong markets wave 3 often runs 2.618x; here 850 x 2.618 = 2,225, near the move). Gaps appear, breadth is broad, and momentum (RSI) prints its highest reading of the entire sequence. Wave 3 is clearly the longest and is not the shortest — Rule 2 is safe. Inside wave 3 you can count a clean five-wave subdivision of Minor degree.

**Wave 4 (23,850 -> 23,400):** Profit-taking sets in. Because wave 2 was sharp, wave 4 alternates into a shallow, sideways triangle/flat retracing about **38.2%** of wave 3's net advance from wave 2's low... in practice around 450 points. It stays comfortably above wave 1's high of 22,000, so Rule 3 (no overlap) holds. This grind frustrates both bulls and bears.

**Wave 5 (23,400 -> 24,250):** The final push. It travels roughly **850 points — equal to wave 1**, a very common wave-5 relationship. But breadth narrows: fewer stocks make new highs, and the daily RSI prints a *lower high* than wave 3 despite price making a higher high — a classic **momentum divergence** flagging exhaustion. The euphoria peaks here; retail participation and options call-buying are heaviest.

At **24,250**, the impulse is complete. What follows next is a three-wave A-B-C correction of the entire five-wave advance (covered in the corrections chapter), typically retracing to the wave 4 zone near 23,400, or a 38.2% retracement of the whole impulse.

## Trading it

The single most important practical takeaway: **the highest-probability, lowest-risk trade in Elliott Wave is entering wave 3.** You do this by identifying a completed wave 1 and buying the wave 2 pullback.

**The wave-3 entry (primary trade):**
- **Setup:** wave 1 up is complete; price is correcting in wave 2 toward the 50-61.8% retracement of wave 1.
- **Entry trigger:** buy as price holds the 61.8% zone and turns up — ideally confirmed by a bullish reversal candle, a break of the wave-2 down trendline, or a small five-wave move up on the lower timeframe signalling wave 3 has begun. In our example, buy near **21,470-21,500**.
- **Stop:** just below the start of wave 1 (below **21,150**). The logic is airtight: if wave 2 exceeds 100% of wave 1, Rule 1 is broken, the count is dead, and you must be out. Risk = ~350 points.
- **Target:** wave 3 minimum projection of 1.618x wave 1 gives 21,470 + (850 x 1.618) = **~22,850**; an extended wave 3 at 2.618x targets **~23,700**. Reward of 1,380-2,230 against 350 risk is a 4:1 to 6:1 setup — the reason wave 3 is the trade professionals wait for.
- **Management:** trail the stop up under each Minor-degree wave 2 as wave 3 subdivides. Book partial profits into the 1.618 extension.

**The wave-5 trade (lower conviction):** After wave 4 completes near 23,400, a long targeting wave-1-equals-wave-5 (~24,250) is valid, but treat it as a scalp. Tighten stops below the wave-4 low, and *watch for divergence* — wave 5 often fails or truncates. This is not where to be a hero; it is where to prepare to reverse.

**The post-5 short (contrarian trade):** When wave 5 completes with divergence and an ending-diagonal or exhaustion candle at 24,250, aggressive traders short for the A-B-C correction, targeting the wave-4 area at 23,400, with a stop above the wave-5 high. A truncated wave 5 (failing to exceed the wave-3 high) is an especially bearish tell.

**Scenario planning** is mandatory. Always hold an alternate count. If your "wave 2" pullback is unusually deep and slow, the market may actually be forming a larger correction, not building toward wave 3. Predefine: "If price breaks 21,150, I am wrong — the primary count is invalid and I flip to the alternate." Elliott Wave without an invalidation level is astrology.

## Confluence

Elliott Wave gains enormous power when fused with other tools — it should rarely be traded naked.

**Fibonacci** is the natural partner. Wave 2 and wave 4 retracements and wave 3/5 extensions are all Fibonacci-derived, so a wave-3 entry that lines up with a 61.8% retracement *and* a prior support shelf *and* the 200-day moving average is a triple confluence far stronger than any single signal.

**Momentum oscillators** validate the count. Wave 3 should carry the strongest RSI/MACD reading; wave 5 should show *negative divergence*. If your supposed "wave 3" shows weaker momentum than "wave 1", your count is probably wrong — a superb reality check.

**Volume** confirms: expanding on wave 3, contracting on wave 4, and often lighter-than-wave-3 on wave 5 despite higher price.

**Option-chain and OI** add a derivatives lens especially useful in Nifty and Bank Nifty. During a powerful wave 3, you typically see aggressive call writing get repeatedly overrun and rolled higher, PCR rising, and IV firm — confirmation of genuine institutional trend. As wave 5 tops, watch for **heavy call OI building at a strike that price fails to breach**, forming a call wall, alongside falling PCR and complacent (low) IV — a derivatives fingerprint of exhaustion that dovetails with the momentum divergence at the wave-5 top. Combining the ending-diagonal wave-5 structure with a hard call-OI ceiling gives a high-conviction reversal zone for a bear put spread.

**Market structure / Dow Theory** should agree: an impulse is just a formal description of a series of higher highs and higher lows, so a clean impulse and a healthy Dow uptrend are the same phenomenon seen through two lenses.

## Pitfalls & false signals

Elliott Wave is notorious for **subjectivity** — two skilled analysts can produce different counts on the same chart. Guard against it:

- **Overcounting in choppy markets.** Impulses need a trend. In a rangebound, whipsawing tape (much of Nifty's sideways phases), forcing a five-wave count produces garbage. If waves overlap constantly, it is corrective, not impulsive — step aside.
- **Ignoring the rules to save a pet count.** The three rules are non-negotiable. If wave 4 overlaps wave 1, it is *not* a standard impulse — you either have a diagonal or a wrong count. Traders who "bend" the rules blow up. Let the rules invalidate you fast and cheaply.
- **Hindsight bias.** Counts look obvious *after* the fact. In real time the right edge of the chart is ambiguous. This is why you always carry an alternate count and trade only the highest-probability, rule-protected setups (the wave-3 entry).
- **Truncated and extended fifths.** Wave 5 can fail to make a new high (truncation) or extend dramatically. Do not assume wave 5 will behave; use divergence and tight stops.
- **Wrong degree.** Mislabelling degree is the commonest error — treating a small Minute pullback as a large Intermediate wave 2, or vice versa. Anchor your degree to the timeframe you actually trade and the size of the swings.

Professionals filter Elliott Wave by demanding **confluence and a hard invalidation level**, by preferring the cleanest counts over clever ones, and by only sizing up on the textbook wave-3 setup. Elliott is a map; price is the territory. When the map and territory disagree, follow the territory and redraw the map.

## Interview-ready summary

"Elliott Wave says trends unfold in a five-wave impulse — 1-2-3-4-5 — in the direction of the larger trend, followed by a three-wave A-B-C correction, and this pattern is fractal across all timeframes. An impulse has three unbreakable rules: wave 2 can't retrace more than 100% of wave 1, wave 3 can't be the shortest of 1-3-5, and wave 4 can't overlap wave 1. Wave 3 is usually the longest, strongest wave, so the best trade is buying the wave-2 pullback — typically the 50-61.8% Fibonacci retracement — to ride wave 3, with the stop just below the wave-1 origin because a break there invalidates the count. Wave 5 is the euphoric final push and usually shows momentum divergence, warning of the coming correction. I always combine it with Fibonacci, momentum and, on Nifty, option-chain OI to confirm the count, and I always hold an alternate count with a defined invalidation level — because Elliott Wave is a probability framework, not a prediction."
