# Double & Triple Tops and Bottoms

## What it is & why it works

Double and triple tops and bottoms are the market's most intuitive reversal patterns because they encode a simple, human story: price tests a level, fails, tests it again, fails again — and the failure to break through tells you the trend behind it has run out of fuel. They are patterns of **rejection at a level**.

A **double top** is two peaks at roughly the same price with a trough (the "valley") between them, resembling the letter **M**. It forms at the end of an uptrend. The first peak is a normal high; price pulls back; buyers push it up a second time but **cannot exceed the first peak**. That second failure at the same resistance is evidence that supply now overwhelms demand at that price. The pattern completes — and the reversal is confirmed — only when price closes **below the intervening trough** (the "neckline" or "confirmation line").

A **double bottom** is the mirror image — two troughs at a similar low with a peak between, resembling the letter **W** — forming at the end of a downtrend. It completes on a close **above the intervening peak**.

**Triple tops and bottoms** extend the idea to three tests of the same level. A triple top is three peaks at similar highs; a triple bottom is three troughs at a similar low. They are rarer, take longer to build, and — because the level has been defended or attacked three times — are often considered more significant when they finally break, though the extra test also gives more opportunity for the pattern to fail or morph.

**Why they work.** These patterns are a visible auction record. At a double top, the first peak establishes a price where sellers appeared. When buyers return and price revisits that exact level, everyone who sold near the first peak and regretted it, plus new sellers, plus profit-takers, all cluster their supply there. If that supply is heavier than the incoming demand, price is rejected a second time — and now a *second* cohort of buyers is trapped long near the high. When price finally breaks the middle trough, those trapped longs bail out, accelerating the decline. The pattern works because it maps real inventory: **overhead supply that hasn't been absorbed.**

The equivalent for a double bottom: the low marks a price where buyers (often value-based or short-covering) appeared. A revisit that holds shows that demand is still there and sellers are exhausted; the break above the middle peak traps the shorts, who must cover.

As always, honesty matters. A **double top is not confirmed until the neckline breaks** — until then, two peaks at a similar level are just as likely to be a base for a *continuation* higher. The single most common error is shorting the second peak on the *assumption* the pattern will complete. The edge is not in predicting the completion but in trading it once confirmed, with a defined stop and a measured target.

## The mechanics

**Anatomy:**

| Element | Double top | Double bottom |
|---|---|---|
| Shape | M | W |
| Prior trend | Up | Down |
| Two extremes | Peaks at similar highs | Troughs at similar lows |
| Middle point | Reaction trough (valley) | Reaction peak |
| Confirmation | Close below the trough | Close above the peak |
| Volume tell | Lower volume on 2nd peak; surge on break | Lower volume on 2nd trough; surge on break |

**How similar must the two peaks be?** Not identical. A tolerance of roughly ±1–3% between the two extremes is acceptable. A slightly **lower** second peak (double top) is actually *more* bearish — it shows buyers couldn't even reach the first high. A slightly **higher** second peak that then reverses is still valid if it fails to hold. What matters is the *rejection*, not pixel-perfect equality.

**Spacing.** The two peaks should be separated by a meaningful trough — a good rule is that the valley between them should be a notable pullback (often 10–20% of the move, or a clear multi-day/multi-week correction). Two peaks a day apart with a shallow dip is noise; two peaks weeks apart with a real correction between is a genuine pattern.

**The measured move (target).** Measure the height of the pattern — from the peaks down to the middle trough (for a double top) — and project that distance **down from the neckline break point**.

- Double top target = Trough price − (Peak price − Trough price)
- Double bottom target = Peak price + (Peak price − Trough price)

**Triple top/bottom mechanics.** Three peaks at a similar level, two intervening troughs. The neckline is drawn along the **lower of the two troughs** (for a top) — price must close below that to confirm. Volume ideally diminishes with each successive peak. The measured move projects the pattern's height from the neckline. Triple bottoms mirror this using the higher of the two intervening peaks.

**Confirmation filters:**

1. **Close beyond the neckline**, not an intrabar spike — daily close standard; weekly for major positions.
2. **Penetration filter** — 1–3% for indices, or ~0.5×ATR, to avoid whipsaws.
3. **Volume** — ideally lighter on the second (or third) test and heavier on the break; for double *bottoms*, the breakout above the neckline should show clear volume expansion to be trusted.
4. **The retest** — price often returns to the broken neckline (now support-turned-resistance for a top). A failed retest is the cleanest entry.

## Reading it — a worked India example

Consider a **double top on Reliance Industries** on the daily chart with realistic levels.

**Phase 1 — first peak.** Reliance has trended up strongly and prints a high of **₹1,540** on heavy volume, then pulls back over two weeks to **₹1,430** as the rally cools. RSI at the first peak was ~70.

**Phase 2 — second peak.** Buyers return and drive the stock back up to **₹1,535** — within 0.3% of the prior high — but it stalls and reverses. Critically, the second push comes on **lighter volume**, and RSI reaches only **~62**, a bearish divergence. The two peaks at ₹1,540 / ₹1,535 with the ₹1,430 trough form a clean **M**. The neckline is at **₹1,430**.

**Phase 3 — the neckline break.** Reliance rolls over and, eight sessions later, **closes at ₹1,415**, about 1% below the ₹1,430 neckline, on the heaviest down-volume in a month. The double top is confirmed.

**The measured move.** Peak ≈ ₹1,540; trough ₹1,430; height = **₹110**. Break point ₹1,430. Target = 1,430 − 110 = **₹1,320**.

**Phase 4 — the retest.** Four sessions later the stock bounces to **₹1,428**, kissing the underside of the ₹1,430 neckline, and fails on a bearish engulfing candle. This is the ideal short entry. Over the next three weeks Reliance declines to **₹1,325**, essentially fulfilling the target, before stabilising.

Now a **double bottom on Nifty 50**, daily. Nifty falls to **23,400** (first trough), bounces to **24,000** (middle peak), then sells off again to **23,450** — holding near the prior low on *lighter* volume and with a bullish RSI divergence (RSI 30 → 38). The **W** is set, neckline at **24,000**. Nifty then rallies and **closes at 24,120**, above the neckline, on strong volume — confirmation. Height = 24,000 − 23,400 = **600**; target = 24,000 + 600 = **24,600**. A brief throwback to 23,980 holds, and Nifty grinds up to 24,650 over the following weeks.

## Trading it

**Entry (three styles):**

1. **Break entry (aggressive):** enter on the confirming daily close beyond the neckline (short Reliance at ₹1,415; long Nifty at 24,120). Best participation, most whipsaw risk.
2. **Retest entry (preferred):** wait for the throwback to the neckline and enter on the reversal candle (short Reliance at ₹1,428; long Nifty at 23,980). Tighter stop, better reward-to-risk.
3. **Second-peak/trough entry (anticipatory, small size):** for experienced traders showing a clear rejection candle at the second test *before* confirmation — smallest position because the pattern isn't yet complete.

**Stop-loss.** For a double top, the logical invalidation is a close **above the higher of the two peaks** (₹1,545 for Reliance). On a retest entry at ₹1,428, a stop at ₹1,455 (above the retest swing) risks ~₹27 for a ~₹108 reward to ₹1,320 = **4:1**. On a break entry at ₹1,415 with a stop at ₹1,545, you risk ₹130 for ₹95 — poor — which is exactly why the retest entry is preferred.

**Target.** Primary = measured move (₹1,320 / 24,600). Scale out: book half at an intermediate structural level, trail the remainder. For the Nifty double bottom, book part at 24,400 (prior resistance) and let the rest run to 24,600.

**Scenario A — clean success.** Nifty double bottom: long at 23,980 on the retest, stop at 23,850 (below the retest low, 130 points), target 24,600 (620 points) = ~5:1.

**Scenario B — failed retest overshoot.** Reliance breaks ₹1,430, throws back, and spikes to ₹1,448 intraday before failing. A stop placed exactly at the neckline (₹1,432) gets hit; a stop beyond the noise (₹1,455) survives and the target is still reached. Place stops beyond swing structure, not on the line.

**Scenario C — outright failure.** Reliance breaks ₹1,415, then reverses and closes above ₹1,545, taking out both peaks. The pattern has failed; trapped shorts must cover, often producing a sharp squeeze. Exit on the close above the peaks. A failed double top frequently becomes a bullish breakout (the "double top" was really a base).

**Management.** Move to breakeven once price travels halfway to target; then trail below each lower high (top) or above each higher low (bottom).

## Confluence

- **Volume:** lighter volume on the second/third test and a surge on the neckline break is the classic confirmation. For Indian stocks, rising delivery percentage on down days at a double top confirms genuine distribution.
- **Momentum divergence:** bearish RSI/MACD divergence between the two peaks (Reliance: 70 → 62) or bullish divergence between two bottoms (Nifty: 30 → 38) is strong corroboration that the second test lacks conviction.
- **Support/resistance & Fibonacci:** a double bottom neckline that sits on a prior support shelf or a 61.8% retracement, or a double top at a well-defined resistance/round number, strengthens the read.
- **Moving averages:** a double top break that also loses the 50-DMA, or a double bottom break that reclaims it, adds weight.
- **Option-chain / OI (India edge):** at a double top on Nifty, if the peak level coincides with a strike carrying the **highest Call OI** (a strong resistance wall being defended by writers), the two failed tests make sense — writers keep capping it. A neckline break with **Put OI building at lower strikes** and **Call OI rising overhead** confirms the down-move. For a double bottom, watch for **heavy Put writing at the trough level** (writers defending support) and **Put unwinding / Call writing reduction** as price breaks the neckline up. A falling PCR that reverses upward at a double bottom, or max-pain drifting toward the pattern target, are useful cross-checks.
- **Breadth:** a Nifty double top is more reliable when advance-decline and Bank Nifty are simultaneously rolling over.

The premium setup: a double top **at a heavy Call-OI resistance**, with **bearish RSI divergence**, breaking the neckline **on rising volume** at a prior support shelf. Four independent tools, one direction.

## Pitfalls & false signals

1. **Shorting the second peak prematurely.** The cardinal sin. Two peaks are not a double top until the neckline breaks — many "double tops" simply base and break higher. Wait for confirmation or trade only tiny anticipatory size.

2. **Unconfirmed patterns.** A picture-perfect M that never closes below its trough is not a trade. Continuation is a live possibility until the neckline gives way.

3. **Whipsaw breaks.** Intrabar pokes below the neckline that reverse. Require a daily close, use a 1–3%/ATR filter, and favour the retest entry.

4. **Peaks too close together.** Two highs a day or two apart with a shallow dip are noise, not a pattern. Insist on a meaningful trough between the tests. Bigger, wider patterns are more reliable.

5. **The "adam & eve" and shape nuances.** Not every double bottom is symmetrical — one sharp V trough and one rounded trough is common and valid, but sloppy, uneven patterns reduce reliability. Demand a genuine test of the same zone.

6. **Triple-top morphing.** A triple top can decay into a rectangle/range or morph into a Head & Shoulders. Don't force the label; trade the confirmed break of the range, whatever you call it.

7. **Second trough undercut (bottoms).** In a double bottom, the second trough sometimes dips slightly *below* the first (a stop-run) before reversing. This is common and often bullish — but only if it reclaims quickly. A sustained close below the first low invalidates the bottom.

8. **News override.** A Budget, RBI decision, or global shock can gap through any pattern. TA structures risk; it doesn't remove it. Respect stops.

Pros filter by waiting for the confirmed close, trading the retest for reward-to-risk, sizing so one failed pattern is survivable, and treating a failed double top/bottom (a close back beyond both extremes) as a reversal signal to trade the other way.

## Interview-ready summary

"Double and triple tops and bottoms are reversal patterns of rejection at a level. A double top is two peaks at a similar high (an M) after an uptrend; it works because the second failure to break shows supply beats demand and traps a second cohort of buyers. It's only confirmed on a close below the middle trough — the neckline — never at the second peak itself. My target is the pattern height projected from the neckline break. I confirm with a daily close plus a filter, look for lighter volume and bearish RSI divergence on the second test and a volume surge on the break, and I prefer entering on the throwback retest of the neckline for a tight stop and better reward-to-risk, with the stop beyond both peaks. Double bottoms are the W mirror at a low. Triple versions test the level three times and are rarer but weightier. In India I cross-check the option chain — a double top at a heavy Call-OI resistance with Put building below is high-conviction. And I stay honest: two peaks aren't a pattern until the neckline breaks, and a close back above both peaks means it failed and I exit or flip."
