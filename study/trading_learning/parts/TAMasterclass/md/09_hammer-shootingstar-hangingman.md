# Hammer, Shooting Star, Hanging Man & Inverted Hammer

## What it is & why it works

These four are the **single-candle reversal signals** built on one common shape: a small real body with one long wick and little-to-no wick on the other side. What distinguishes them is not the shape but the **location** — where in the trend they appear — and the *direction* of the long wick. The same silhouette that is bullish at the bottom of a downtrend is bearish at the top of an uptrend. That is the single most important idea in this chapter: **context, not shape, names the candle.**

The four:
- **Hammer** — long *lower* wick, small body near the top, at the **bottom of a downtrend** → bullish reversal.
- **Hanging Man** — the *identical shape* as the hammer, but at the **top of an uptrend** → bearish reversal.
- **Shooting Star** — long *upper* wick, small body near the bottom, at the **top of an uptrend** → bearish reversal.
- **Inverted Hammer** — the *identical shape* as the shooting star, but at the **bottom of a downtrend** → bullish reversal.

Why do they work? Each is a **rejection footprint** — the market probed hard in one direction and was decisively pushed back before the close. A hammer forms when, after a decline, price sells off sharply during the period (the long lower wick), but buyers step in with size and drive it all the way back up to close near the open. That long lower shadow is *visual proof that demand appeared at lower prices*. In a downtrend, that is the first sign the selling pressure is being absorbed — sellers pushed, buyers said "no," and won the period back. The behavioural essence: **the side that was winning the trend tried to extend it, failed, and surrendered the ground within a single session.** That failure of continuation is what precedes reversals.

The shooting star is the mirror: after a rally, price spikes up (long upper wick) but sellers overwhelm the move and drive it back down to close near the open, proving supply appeared at higher prices. The hanging man and inverted hammer are the "wrong-side" versions — the same rejection shape but appearing against the immediately preceding trend, which is why they demand extra confirmation.

These candles are the workhorses of intraday and swing trading on the NSE because they mark *specific price levels* — the wick tip is an exact rejection point — giving unusually tight, well-defined stops. When a hammer prints on Reliance at a demand zone with a 200-point Nifty backdrop of support, a trader has a precise, low-risk, high-reward entry. That combination of clear signal and clear risk is why these four candles appear in nearly every practical trading playbook.

## The mechanics

**The common template.** All four are single candles with:
- A **small real body** (roughly the top or bottom third of the range).
- **One long wick** — at least ~2× the body length, often 2–3×.
- **A very short or absent wick on the opposite side.**

Body colour is secondary but matters at the margin (see below). It is the *shape + location* that defines the signal.

**Construction and rules table**

| Candle | Long wick | Body position | Appears after | Signal | Ideal wick:body |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hammer | Lower | Top third | Downtrend | Bullish reversal | ≥ 2:1 |
| Hanging Man | Lower | Top third | Uptrend | Bearish reversal | ≥ 2:1 |
| Shooting Star | Upper | Bottom third | Uptrend | Bearish reversal | ≥ 2:1 |
| Inverted Hammer | Upper | Bottom third | Downtrend | Bullish reversal | ≥ 2:1 |

Notice the pairs share a shape:
- **Hammer = Hanging Man** in shape (long lower wick). Bottom-of-downtrend → bullish hammer; top-of-uptrend → bearish hanging man.
- **Shooting Star = Inverted Hammer** in shape (long upper wick). Top-of-uptrend → bearish shooting star; bottom-of-downtrend → bullish inverted hammer.

**Body-colour nuance (strengthens or weakens the signal):**
- A **hammer with a green body** (close > open) is slightly stronger — buyers not only reclaimed the drop but closed above the open.
- A **hanging man with a red body** is slightly stronger bearish.
- A **shooting star with a red body** is slightly stronger bearish.
- An **inverted hammer with a green body** is slightly stronger bullish.
These are refinements; location dominates.

**Why hanging man and inverted hammer need MORE confirmation.** Both are "counter-intuitive" candles. The hanging man has a *bullish-looking* long lower wick (buyers defended) yet is a *bearish* top signal — the bearish read only makes sense because the long lower wick reveals that sellers were, for the first time in the uptrend, able to force a sharp intraday sell-off at all. It signals fragility, but the buyers *did* win the session back, so you **must** wait for a red confirmation candle. Likewise the inverted hammer has a *bearish-looking* long upper wick (sellers rejected the rally) yet is a *bullish* bottom signal — it works because buyers were finally able to mount a real rally at all, but since sellers pushed it back you need a green confirmation candle. Rule of thumb: **hammer and shooting star are strong enough to trade on a modest confirmation; hanging man and inverted hammer demand explicit follow-through.**

**Confirmation trigger.** For bullish candles (hammer, inverted hammer): a break above the candle's high, ideally a next-candle green close. For bearish candles (shooting star, hanging man): a break below the candle's low, ideally a next-candle red close.

## Reading it — a worked India example

**A hammer on Reliance daily at a demand zone.**

**Phase 1 — the decline.** Reliance has fallen from ₹1,485 to ₹1,415 over six sessions on broad-market weakness, a clean downtrend of lower lows, mostly red candles. Price is now approaching a well-tested demand zone at ₹1,405–1,412, an area that held twice before and roughly coincides with the rising 200-day EMA — a confluence of support.

**Phase 2 — the hammer.** The session opens at ₹1,414, sells off hard in the morning to a low of ₹1,398 (piercing into the demand zone), then buyers step in aggressively and drive price back up to close at ₹1,416. The candle:
- Open ₹1,414, Close ₹1,416 → small green body of ₹2, in the top of the range
- Low ₹1,398 → lower wick ≈ ₹18 (long)
- High ₹1,418 → upper wick ≈ ₹2 (tiny)
- Lower-wick:body ≈ 9:1 → a textbook **hammer**, and green-bodied (slightly stronger)

The story: sellers made a new low into the demand zone and were *rejected* — buyers reclaimed the entire ₹18 drop and closed green. After a six-day decline, at tested support, on the 200-EMA, this is a high-quality bullish reversal signal. The evening research note reads: *"Reliance hammered off the ₹1,398 demand zone / 200-EMA today — sellers rejected. Long above ₹1,418 with stop under ₹1,395."*

**Phase 3 — confirmation.** The next session opens at ₹1,420 and closes at ₹1,438 on a strong green body, breaking above the hammer's ₹1,418 high — confirmation delivered. Buyers have follow-through; the reversal is engaged.

**Phase 4 — the move.** Over the next seven sessions Reliance rallies to ₹1,472, reclaiming most of the prior decline. A swing trader who entered on the break of ₹1,418 with a stop under ₹1,395 risked ₹23 to make roughly ₹54 to the ₹1,472 area — about a **2.3R** trade. On a position of, say, 500 shares that is ₹54 × 500 = **₹27,000 gross** against ₹11,500 risk. The hammer marked the exact turn a day before the trend visibly changed.

**A shooting-star counter-example (Nifty).** Nifty rallies from 25,000 to 25,480 into a supply zone at 25,500. One session it opens 25,455, spikes to 25,540 (new high, into supply), then sellers drive it back to close 25,445 — small red body at the bottom of the range, long upper wick of ~85 points, tiny lower wick. That is a **shooting star** at resistance: buyers made a new high and were rejected, supply reclaimed the entire spike. Confirmation comes with a red close below the star's ~25,430 low next day, and Nifty rolls over toward 25,150. Mirror logic: rejection of *higher* prices at *resistance* after an *uptrend*.

## Trading it

**Entry trigger.**
- **Hammer / Inverted Hammer (bullish):** break **above the candle's high**. Reliance hammer → long above ₹1,418.
- **Shooting Star / Hanging Man (bearish):** break **below the candle's low**. Nifty shooting star → short below ~25,430.
For hanging man and inverted hammer specifically, wait for the *confirmation candle to close* in your direction before triggering — do not anticipate.

**Stop-loss.** Beyond the long wick plus a buffer — this is the great advantage of these candles, because the wick tip is a precise, market-defined invalidation. 
- Reliance hammer: stop under the ₹1,398 low, say ₹1,393. Risk = 1418 − 1393 = ₹25. 
- Nifty shooting star: stop above the 25,540 high, say 25,560. Risk = 25,560 − 25,430 = 130 points.

**Target / measured move.** Structure first (next opposing zone), then a risk multiple. Reliance: nearest resistance ₹1,470–1,485; a 2R target off ₹25 risk is 1418 + 50 = ₹1,468 — reached. Nifty short: support at 25,150; 2R off 130 points = 25,430 − 260 = 25,170 — reached.

**Scenario A — clean reversal (hammer).** Long triggers ₹1,420. Price runs to ₹1,472; exit near resistance for ~₹52 ≈ 2.3R. On 500 shares: **₹26,000 gross** vs ~₹13,500 risk. The hammer-at-support ideal.

**Scenario B — failed hammer / stop-out.** Long triggers ₹1,420, stalls at ₹1,428, then broad-market selling drags Reliance back through ₹1,398 to stop at ₹1,393. Loss ≈ ₹27 × 500 = **₹13,500**. Hammers fail when the *broader trend* (Nifty, the sector) overwhelms the single-stock signal — which is why you check market context before trusting any single-stock hammer.

**Scenario C — hanging man needing confirmation.** Bank Nifty prints a hanging man at 52,500 after a rally. You do *not* short immediately — the shape's long lower wick shows buyers still defended. You wait. Next candle closes green and price pushes to 52,650 → the hanging man failed, no trade, no loss. Had the next candle closed red below the hanging man's low, you would short with confirmation. This discipline is exactly why the "wrong-side" candles demand patience.

**Management.** After entry, the reversal should keep working. For the hammer long, if price closes back *below* the hammer's low, the demand failed — exit early rather than wait for the hard stop. Trail behind each new higher swing low (longs) or lower swing high (shorts) as the new trend develops.

## Confluence

Single-candle reversals are strong signals but *far* stronger with confluence. Stack:

- **Location — the non-negotiable.** The candle must appear at a level: support/demand for hammers and inverted hammers, resistance/supply for shooting stars and hanging men. The Reliance hammer earned its weight because it printed *at a tested demand zone on the 200-EMA*. A hammer in no-man's-land is a weak signal.
- **Trend context.** These are *reversal* candles — they need a clear preceding trend to reverse. A hammer requires a downtrend into it; a shooting star requires an uptrend. No trend, no reversal.
- **Volume.** A hammer or shooting star on a **volume spike** confirms genuine institutional absorption/distribution — real hands stepped in at the wick. Low volume weakens the signal. In stocks, a hammer on 2–3× average volume at support is a premium long.
- **Moving averages / Fibonacci.** A hammer landing on the 50 or 200-EMA, or at the 61.8% Fibonacci retracement of the prior up-move, adds a mechanical support confluence — as in the Reliance example.
- **Option-chain / OI confluence (F&O).** Decisive for index traders. A **hammer or inverted hammer at the max-put-OI strike** (the put wall / structural support) is a high-probability long — put writers defend that floor, and the candle shows buyers doing exactly that. A **shooting star or hanging man at the max-call-OI strike** (call wall / ceiling) is a high-probability short. Watch **intraday OI change**: fresh *put writing* building as your hammer forms confirms buyers are committing; fresh *call writing* as your shooting star forms confirms sellers are committing. When the candle's rejection and the option writers' positioning agree, size up.
- **Multi-timeframe.** A 15-minute hammer that also sits on the daily demand zone is a two-timeframe agreement — trade it with conviction. A lower-timeframe hammer against the higher-timeframe trend is a scalp at most.

The model: the candle is the *trigger*, but location + trend + volume + OI is what turns a coin-flip into a real edge.

## Pitfalls & false signals

- **Naming by shape, not location.** The cardinal error. Traders see a long-lower-wick candle in an *uptrend* and call it a "bullish hammer" — but at the top it is a *bearish hanging man*. Always ask *where in the trend am I?* before naming the candle.
- **Trading them in a vacuum.** A hammer floating mid-range with no support beneath and no downtrend before it is noise. No level + no trend = no trade.
- **Skipping confirmation on the weak members.** Hanging man and inverted hammer fail often when traded on the raw shape because the session's *action* contradicts the signal's *message*. Always demand the confirmation candle for these two; hammer and shooting star tolerate lighter confirmation but still benefit from it.
- **Ignoring the wick:body ratio.** A "hammer" with a lower wick barely longer than its body is not a hammer — it is an ordinary candle. Hold the ≥2:1 discipline; the longer and cleaner the wick relative to the body, the more decisive the rejection.
- **Single-stock hammer against a falling market.** A beautiful hammer on an individual F&O stock will often fail if Nifty and the sector are cratering — the systematic tide overwhelms the single-name signal. Always check the index/sector context before trusting a stock-level reversal.
- **Gaps and expiry distortion (India-specific).** Overnight gaps (GIFT Nifty, US markets) can create wick shapes that reflect the gap-fill rather than genuine intraday rejection. And on volatile expiry days, wicks get exaggerated by option-hedging flows; read reversal candles on such days with extra caution.
- **Over-trading the shape.** Pros take *few* of these, at *good levels*, with *confirmation and confluence*, and accept that even the best hammer at the best support fails a meaningful fraction of the time. The way professionals filter false signals is structural: require location, require trend, require volume or OI agreement, keep the stop tight behind the wick, and let the reward-to-risk carry the edge across many trades rather than betting on any one.
