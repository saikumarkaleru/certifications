# Elliott Wave II: Corrective Patterns

## What it is & why it works

If impulse waves are the market taking two steps forward, **corrective waves are the one step back**. Every five-wave impulse is followed by a correction that moves against the larger trend, and mastering these corrective structures is what separates a competent Elliott analyst from a beginner. Beginners obsess over the exciting five-wave rallies; professionals know that markets spend the *majority* of their time correcting — sideways, choppy, frustrating — and that most trading losses come from misreading corrections as reversals or reversals as corrections.

Corrective waves are labelled with letters — **A-B-C** for simple corrections — to distinguish them from the numbered impulses. The reason they exist is psychological equilibrium-seeking. After a strong impulse, the trend is temporarily "overbought" or "oversold" in sentiment terms; the crowd that missed the move wants a second chance, holders want to lock gains, and the market must digest the advance before it can continue. But because a correction moves *against* the prevailing trend — which is still fundamentally in force — it is inherently more overlapping, complex and irregular than an impulse. This is Elliott's key insight: **corrections are harder to read precisely because they are fights against the dominant trend**, and the market resolves that tension in several distinct, catalogued shapes.

For an Indian trader, understanding corrections answers the two questions that matter most in a trend: "Is this pullback just a pause I should buy, or the start of a real top?" and "How deep and how long is this likely to go before the trend resumes?" Get corrections right and you buy Nifty's wave-4 dips with confidence; get them wrong and you either sell the bottom of a healthy pullback or hold through a genuine trend change. Corrections are also where **alternation** lives — knowing that a sharp wave 2 implies a sideways wave 4 lets you anticipate structure before it forms.

## The mechanics

Corrective patterns fall into a handful of catalogued families. The four you must know cold are **zigzags, flats, triangles and combinations**.

**1. Zigzag (5-3-5):** The sharpest, deepest simple correction. Structure: wave A is a five-wave move, wave B is a three-wave partial retrace, wave C is a five-wave move that typically travels beyond the end of A. It looks like a clean, steep A-B-C staircase. Key relationships: B usually retraces 38.2-61.8% of A; **C often equals A** or measures 1.618x A. Zigzags most commonly appear as wave 2, or as wave A/X in larger structures. A "double zigzag" strings two zigzags together with an X wave for deeper corrections.

**2. Flat (3-3-5):** A sideways, shallow correction. Structure: wave A is a three-wave move, wave B is a three-wave move that retraces most of A, wave C is a five-wave move. Flats come in three flavours defined by where B and C end:

| Flat type | Wave B | Wave C | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| Regular flat | ~100% of A | Ends slightly beyond A | Balanced sideways |
| Expanded flat | >100% of A (new extreme) | Ends well beyond A | Most common; "irregular" |
| Running flat | >100% of A | Fails to reach end of A | Very strong underlying trend |

The **expanded flat** is the single most common corrective pattern in real markets and the most treacherous — wave B makes a *new high* (in an uptrend correction) that traps breakout buyers before wave C collapses. Flats typically appear as wave 4, or wave B.

**3. Triangle (3-3-3-3-3):** A five-legged sideways contraction labelled A-B-C-D-E, each leg a three-wave move, price coiling between converging trendlines. Triangles signal *the trend is resting before one final thrust*. Critically, a triangle appears **only in positions that precede the final move** — as wave 4 (before wave 5), as wave B (before wave C), or as wave X. It never appears as wave 2. After the triangle completes at E, price "thrusts" out of the triangle, and the thrust often approximates the widest part of the triangle. Types: contracting (symmetrical, ascending, descending) and the rarer expanding triangle.

**4. Combinations (WXY / WXYZ):** When the market wants to consume time sideways, it strings simple corrections together, joined by connecting **X waves**: a double-three (W-X-Y) or triple-three (W-X-Y-Z). These are the messy, drawn-out consolidations that frustrate everyone.

Two **guidelines** govern which pattern appears. **Alternation**: if wave 2 was a sharp zigzag, wave 4 is likely a sideways flat or triangle. **Depth**: wave 2 tends to be deep (50-78.6%), wave 4 shallow (23.6-38.2%). And the golden rule of counting: **corrections subdivide into threes, impulses into fives** — if you can only count three overlapping waves, you are in a correction, and the trend is not over.

## Reading it — a worked India example

Continue the Nifty advance from the impulse chapter, which topped at **24,250** after a complete five-wave rally from 21,150. Now the market must correct that entire advance. Suppose the larger trend is still up, so this is an expected corrective pullback, not a bear market.

**Wave A (24,250 -> 23,300):** Price drops 950 points in a visible **five-wave** structure. That five-wave A is the first clue this correction will be a **zigzag** (sharp), not a flat — a five-wave A only fits zigzags. Momentum rolls over, and the fall is decisive, breaking the wave-5 rising trendline.

**Wave B (23,300 -> 23,850):** A three-wave bounce retraces about **58%** of wave A (950 x 0.58 = 550; 23,300 + 550 = 23,850). This is the bull trap — the recovery feels like the uptrend resuming, and retail buys the bounce and writes puts. But it is corrective (three waves, overlapping), it fails below the 24,250 high, and volume is lighter than wave A's decline. A disciplined analyst labels it wave B and prepares for wave C down.

**Wave C (23,850 -> 22,900):** The five-wave decline that catches everyone. Using **C = A**, project 23,850 - 950 = **22,900**. Price accelerates lower, breaks the wave-A low at 23,300 (confirming the zigzag), and washes out into 22,900 — close to the **38.2% retracement of the entire 21,150-24,250 impulse** (3,100 x 0.382 = 1,184; 24,250 - 1,184 = 23,066) and near the prior wave-4 support zone at 23,400. Panic peaks; puts are expensive; the crowd is bearish exactly at the low.

**The completed A-B-C** at 22,900 marks the end of the correction. Because the primary trend is still up, the next impulse begins here, targeting new highs above 24,250. Notice how the correction did its job: it shook out weak longs, reset momentum, and offered a superb re-entry — *if* you correctly read it as a corrective zigzag rather than the start of a bear market.

Now contrast an **expanded flat** as an alternate: had wave A been only three waves down to 23,500, wave B could have pushed to a *new high* at 24,400 (trapping breakout buyers above 24,250), before wave C collapsed in five waves to ~23,000. The new-high B wave is the expanded flat's calling card — and the reason a marginal new high after a strong run is not automatically bullish.

## Trading it

Corrections offer two distinct trade families: **fading the correction's completion to rejoin the trend** (the high-value trade) and **trading the correction's internal legs** (for the nimble).

**Rejoining the trend — buy the completed correction:**
- **Setup:** a clear three-wave A-B-C (or triangle) is completing at a Fibonacci confluence, against an intact larger uptrend.
- **Entry trigger:** in the zigzag example, wait for wave C to reach the C = A projection at **22,900** *and* stall at the 38.2% retracement / prior support, then buy on a bullish reversal candle or a five-wave move up on the lower timeframe confirming the new impulse. Entry ~22,950.
- **Stop:** below the correction's likely invalidation. For a wave-2/wave-4 correction, the ultimate stop is where the correction would retrace 100% of the prior impulse (a break below 21,150 in a wave-2 case). For a tactical entry, place it just below the wave-C low with buffer (~22,800). Risk ~150 points.
- **Target:** the next impulse minimum — a retest of 24,250 and then measured extensions above it. Reward of 1,300+ against ~150 risk is why patient trend-followers wait for corrections to complete rather than chasing.

**Trading the C-wave down (aggressive):** Once wave B completes below the prior high with three overlapping waves and lighter volume, short for wave C targeting C = A. In the example: short near **23,850**, stop above the 24,250 high (or above B with buffer), target 22,900. On Nifty this is often expressed as a **bear put spread** or buying puts, sized for the measured move.

**Triangle thrust trade:** When a wave-4 triangle is identifiable (five contracting legs A-B-C-D-E), trade the breakout thrust in the direction of the trend as wave 5 launches. Enter on the break of the triangle's upper boundary after E completes; target the thrust ≈ the triangle's widest width added to the breakout point; stop back inside the triangle.

**Scenario discipline:** the make-or-break skill is distinguishing a *correction* from a *reversal*. Rule of thumb: **three waves = correction (trend continues); five waves against the trend = the trend may have changed.** If your "wave B bounce" unfolds in a clean impulsive five waves to new highs, your bearish count is wrong — the correction ended earlier and the uptrend resumed. Predefine this: "If price prints five waves up and exceeds 24,250, I abandon the correction count."

## Confluence

Corrections are where confluence earns its keep, because the patterns are ambiguous in real time.

**Fibonacci** is essential: wave-2 corrections cluster at 50-61.8%, wave-4 at 23.6-38.2%, and C-wave targets come from C = A (1.0x) or 1.618x A. A wave-C low that coincides with a Fibonacci retracement of the larger impulse *and* a horizontal support shelf is a high-odds bounce zone.

**Momentum divergence** flags the end of C waves: as wave C makes a lower low, RSI/MACD often makes a higher low — bullish divergence signalling the correction is exhausting. This is one of the most reliable "correction is ending" tells.

**Volume and breadth**: corrective declines typically show diminishing volume into the low; a genuine reversal (new bear trend) shows expanding down-volume and deteriorating breadth. This helps you decide correction vs reversal.

**Option-chain and OI** are especially clarifying in Nifty/Bank Nifty corrections. At a genuine wave-C bottom you often see **peak put OI and a spiked PCR** (max fear), rich put IV, and put writers absent — classic capitulation that marks the low. In an **expanded flat's wave-B new high**, watch for the rally to occur on *falling* PCR with aggressive call writing that quickly caps price — a derivatives signature that the "breakout" is a trap, not a trend. Combining the expanded-flat structure with a fading call wall gives a high-conviction short into wave C.

**Structure/support-resistance**: corrections very often terminate at the prior wave-4 low (Elliott's own guideline that wave 4 of the prior impulse is a common target for a later correction), or at a well-tested support. Aligning the pattern target with real S/R levels sharpens the entry.

## Pitfalls & false signals

Corrections are the graveyard of over-eager Elliott analysts. The main traps:

- **Calling a reversal too early.** A sharp three-wave drop *feels* like a top but is often just wave A of a correction or a wave 2/4 within an ongoing trend. Demand five impulsive waves against the trend before declaring a reversal.
- **The expanded-flat B-wave trap.** A new marginal high after a strong run is not automatically bullish — it is frequently wave B of an expanded flat, designed to trap breakout buyers before wave C collapses. Treat suspicious new highs on weak internals with caution.
- **Overcomplicating with combinations.** When a correction drags on, it is tempting to invent elaborate WXYZ counts. Often the honest answer is "this is a messy sideways combination; stand aside until it resolves." Complexity is a signal to reduce size, not to trade more.
- **Forcing zigzag vs flat.** Miscounting wave A (five waves = zigzag, three waves = flat) throws off the entire projection. Count wave A's subdivision carefully; it tells you which pattern — and which C target — to expect.
- **Ignoring alternation.** Expecting wave 4 to look like wave 2 leads to bad stops. If wave 2 was a sharp zigzag, plan for a shallow, time-consuming, sideways wave 4 (flat or triangle), and be patient.
- **No invalidation level.** As always, a correction count without a price level that proves it wrong is not a trade plan. Define where five-waves-against-you or a key retracement breach kills the count.

Professionals filter these by counting subdivisions honestly (threes vs fives), demanding Fibonacci and momentum confluence at turning points, reading the derivatives tape for capitulation vs traps, and — above all — by respecting that corrections are meant to be confusing. When a correction is genuinely unclear, the professional move is to reduce size and wait for it to resolve into a countable structure.

## Interview-ready summary

"After every five-wave impulse comes a three-wave A-B-C correction against the trend, and corrections are trickier than impulses because they fight the dominant trend. The four families are: zigzag (5-3-5, sharp and deep, where C often equals A), flat (3-3-5, sideways — the expanded flat is the most common and traps buyers with a new-high B wave), triangle (3-3-3-3-3, a five-legged contraction that only appears before the final move, as wave 4 or B), and combinations (WXYZ) for drawn-out consolidations. The golden rule is corrections subdivide into threes and impulses into fives — so a three-wave pullback means the trend continues, while five clean waves against the trend warn of a real reversal. Alternation tells me a sharp wave 2 implies a sideways wave 4. I trade corrections mainly by buying their completion at Fibonacci confluence to rejoin the trend — targeting the C = A projection with a stop below the wave-C low — and I confirm with momentum divergence and, on Nifty, option-chain OI showing put capitulation at C-wave lows. Every count carries a hard invalidation level, because reading corrections is about probability, not certainty."
