# Flags & Pennants

## What it is & why it works

Flags and pennants are the **continuation patterns of momentum** — short, sharp pauses that appear *after* a violent directional move and, more reliably than almost any other classical pattern, resolve in the same direction. Where triangles form over weeks as a slow war of absorption, flags and pennants form over a *handful of sessions* (or bars, intraday) as a fast, orderly breather in a stampeding market. For a momentum trader on Nifty, Bank Nifty or a breakout stock, they are among the highest-probability, best-reward-to-risk setups available.

The anatomy is always three parts:
1. **The flagpole (the impulse):** a steep, near-vertical move on heavy volume — the breakout thrust. This is the *trend leg* whose energy the pattern will continue.
2. **The consolidation (the flag or pennant):** a brief, low-volume pause.
3. **The continuation (the resolution):** a breakout that extends the original impulse, ideally by roughly the length of the flagpole.

The difference between a flag and a pennant is only the *shape* of the pause:
- A **flag** is a small rectangular channel that slopes *gently against* the trend. In an up-move the flag drifts slightly *down* (a bull flag drifting down-right); in a down-move it drifts slightly up (a bear flag drifting up-right). It looks like a little flag flying on the pole.
- A **pennant** is a small *symmetrical-triangle-like* coil — converging lower highs and higher lows — that forms right after the pole. It looks like a tiny pennant on the mast.

They function identically; the distinction is cosmetic. Both are traded the same way.

Why do they work? A flagpole represents a sudden, decisive shift in the supply-demand balance — a wave of aggressive buyers (or sellers) overwhelming the book. After such a thrust, some early participants take quick profits and the move pauses. But crucially, **the counter-pressure is weak**: the pullback is shallow, orderly, and on *fading volume*, because there is no real opposing conviction — just profit-taking and a pause for breath. The strong hands who drove the pole are not selling; they are letting weak holders shake out. When that profit-taking is absorbed, the *same* dominant force reasserts and price launches again. The low-volume, shallow, brief nature of the pause is the tell that the trend is merely resting, not reversing. The market behaviour encoded is: **strong momentum + orderly, low-conviction pullback = high probability of continuation.**

Honesty: the reliability hinges on the *quality of the flagpole* and the *shallowness of the pause*. A deep, high-volume, choppy "flag" that retraces most of the pole is not a flag — it is a reversal warming up. And in a news-driven Indian market, a gap can turn any flag into a stop-out. These are probabilities, traded with tight, defined risk — which is precisely why their R:R is so attractive.

## The mechanics

**Construction requirements — the checklist that separates a real flag from noise:**

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| Flagpole | A sharp, steep, one-directional move — often a breakout or gap — on *above-average, expanding* volume. Steeper and cleaner = better. |
| Pause duration | Short: typically **1–3 weeks** on the daily chart (5–15 bars); intraday flags form over minutes-to-hours. A "flag" lasting many weeks is really a different, weaker pattern. |
| Retracement | Shallow — ideally the flag retraces no more than ~**one-third to one-half** of the flagpole. A retrace beyond ~50–61.8% invalidates the momentum thesis. |
| Slope | A flag slopes *against* the trend (or sideways); a pennant converges. A flag that slopes *with* the trend (a bull flag pointing up) is weaker/less reliable. |
| Volume | **Contracts** sharply through the flag/pennant — the defining tell — then **expands** on the breakout. |
| Breakout | A close beyond the flag's boundary in the trend direction, on renewed volume. |

**The measured move — the flagpole projection.** This is the signature target and it is simple and powerful: **the pattern typically continues by the length of the flagpole**, measured from the breakout point. Flags are said to "fly at half-mast" — the pause often forms around the midpoint of the total move.

> Bull-flag target = Breakout point + (Top of flagpole − Base of flagpole)
> Bear-flag target = Breakdown point − (Top of flagpole − Base of flagpole)

Example: a stock rockets from ₹800 (base of pole) to ₹900 (top of pole) — a ₹100 pole — then flags down to ₹870 and breaks out at ₹902. Target ≈ 902 + 100 = **₹1,002**.

**Volume is the master signal.** The ideal footprint: heavy volume on the pole → volume *dries up* through the flag (the single most important confirmation that this is a pause, not a top) → volume *surges* again on the breakout. A "flag" that consolidates on *rising* volume is suspect — that is distribution/accumulation by the other side, and the pattern is likely to fail.

**Tools & settings.** On TradingView, draw the flagpole with a trendline/ruler (the measure tool gives you the pole length directly for the target), bound the flag with two parallel trendlines (or converging lines for a pennant), and keep the volume pane visible with a 20-period average line. Flags are ubiquitous on intraday charts (5-min, 15-min) for Bank Nifty and Nifty scalps, and on daily charts for swing trades on breakout stocks. On Chartink, screen for stocks up sharply over 1–3 days now pausing on shrinking volume near the highs.

## Reading it — a worked India example

**Bull flag on Bank Nifty (intraday, 15-minute chart) around an event.** This is a bread-and-butter momentum setup for an index trader.

- **The flagpole:** On a strong global open plus PSU-bank buying, Bank Nifty gaps up and drives from **50,400 to 51,000** in the first 75 minutes — a **600-point** near-vertical thrust on heavy futures volume. That is a textbook flagpole.
- **The flag forms:** Instead of reversing, the index drifts *sideways-to-slightly-down* between **50,880 and 50,980** for the next hour and a half, making tiny lower highs and holding a rising floor — an orderly, shallow pause (it retraces barely 100–120 of the 600-point pole, ~20%). Critically, **15-minute volume shrinks** through this drift — buyers are resting, not fleeing. On the option chain, the 51,000 call OI stops building; 50,800 puts see fresh writing (buyers defending the floor).
- **The pre-break tell:** Bank Nifty coils tighter into the ₹50,950 area, volume at its lowest of the session. India VIX ticks up slightly. The spring is loaded and the pole's direction is up.
- **The breakout:** A 15-minute candle closes **51,080**, clearing the flag's upper boundary on a *volume surge* (well above the session's recent bars) with 51,000 call OI unwinding — writers covering. Momentum resumes. Flagpole projection: 600 points from the ~51,000 breakout → **target ≈ 51,600**.
- **Follow-through:** Bank Nifty runs to 51,550 into the afternoon, tags near the measured move, and a shallow pullback holds the old 51,000 flag-top as support (polarity flip) — a second entry for latecomers.

**Bull flag on an NSE stock (daily, swing).** A mid-cap breaks out of a base from ₹450 to ₹540 in five sessions on huge volume (an earnings surprise) — a **₹90** flagpole. Over the next seven sessions it drifts down gently to ₹510 on steadily *declining* volume — a clean bull flag retracing ~33% of the pole. It then closes **₹545** on a volume expansion, clearing the flag. Target ≈ 545 + 90 = **₹635**. A swing trader risks to just below the flag low (₹508), giving a tight stop against a ₹90 objective — roughly a 3:1 reward-to-risk.

## Trading it

**Entry triggers (two main styles):**
1. **Breakout entry (standard):** enter on the *close* beyond the flag/pennant boundary in the trend direction, on renewed volume. For a bull flag, buy the close above the upper channel line; for a bear flag, sell the close below the lower line.
2. **Anticipation entry (aggressive):** enter *inside* the flag on a bounce off its lower boundary (bull flag) near support, anticipating the resolution. Tighter stop and better R:R, lower hit-rate — size smaller.

**Stop placement:**
- Bull flag: below the flag's low (or below the last minor higher low inside the flag). In the stock example, below ₹508.
- Bear flag: above the flag's high.
- The stop is naturally tight because the flag is small — this is the source of the pattern's excellent R:R.

**Targets & management:**
- Primary: the **flagpole measured move** projected from the breakout.
- Scale: book part at the measured target; trail the remainder with a short EMA (e.g., 9- or 20-EMA) or under successive higher lows to capture momentum extensions, which flags often deliver.
- Because flags ride momentum, letting a runner go beyond the measured move with a trailing stop frequently pays.

**Scenarios:**
- *A — clean breakout to target:* textbook; scale and trail.
- *B — breakout then retest of the flag boundary as support:* add on the retest, move initial stop to break-even.
- *C — false breakout:* price pokes out on weak volume and falls back into the flag — the volume filter usually saves you; if entered, exit on the close back inside.
- *D — deep flag / failure:* if the "flag" retraces beyond ~50–61.8% of the pole or the pause runs long on rising volume, the momentum thesis is broken — stand aside or exit; a failed bull flag can reverse sharply.

**Position sizing:** fixed fractional risk (e.g., 1%). Size = risk ÷ (entry − stop). The tight flag stop lets you take a meaningful position for a small rupee risk while targeting the full pole — which is why disciplined momentum traders lean heavily on flags.

**Intraday note (indices):** on Bank Nifty/Nifty, flags on the 5-/15-minute chart around the open or a news catalyst are prime. Combine with the option chain — a bull-flag breakout with call-OI unwinding gives conviction; be wary near expiry when pinning distorts intraday flags.

## Confluence

**Trend & the pole itself.** The flag *is* a with-trend pattern — its reliability rises when it aligns with the higher-timeframe trend (a daily bull flag inside a weekly uptrend, above a rising 20/50-EMA). The **quality of the flagpole** is itself the primary confluence: a steep, high-volume, gap-driven pole is a far better flag than a limp one.

**Volume & volatility.** Insist on the contraction-then-expansion signature. For indices, an India VIX uptick on the breakout confirms the volatility expansion; Bollinger Band width pinching through the flag then expanding on the break is the same story in different clothing.

**Option-chain / OI (Indian edge):**
- *Bull flag:* the flag often forms just under a heavy **call strike**; if that call OI *unwinds* as price breaks out (writers covering), the breakout is validated. Fresh **put writing** at the flag's floor confirms buyers defending the pause.
- *Bear flag:* the flag forms just above a heavy **put strike**; put OI collapsing on the breakdown validates it, while call writers piling in above confirm the cap.
- A PCR turning in the break direction and max-pain shifting the same way add confidence. Around expiry, discount intraday flags distorted by pinning.

**Breakout stocks & bases.** Flags that form *right after a base breakout* (a stock clearing a long consolidation, then flagging) are premium — the pole is the breakout thrust and the flag is the first shallow pullback, offering a lower-risk entry into a fresh trend. Pair with strong **relative strength** vs the sector/index.

**Momentum oscillators.** RSI staying elevated (but not extreme) and MACD holding its bullish posture through the flag support the continuation; a *bearish RSI divergence* into the pole warns the pole may be exhausting and the flag may fail.

**Multiple-timeframe alignment.** An intraday bull flag in the direction of the daily trend is far stronger than one fighting it. Stack the timeframes.

The premium setup: strong trend + steep high-volume pole (ideally a base breakout) + shallow, brief, low-volume flag + breakout on volume/VIX expansion + call OI unwinding + strong relative strength. That is where momentum traders press size.

## Pitfalls & false signals

**The deep pullback masquerading as a flag.** The most common error: labelling a deep, choppy retracement a "flag." If the pause retraces more than ~50–61.8% of the pole, or lasts many weeks, the momentum is gone and it is likely a reversal. **Filter:** demand a *shallow* (≤~50%), *brief* pause on *declining* volume.

**Rising volume in the flag.** A pause on *increasing* volume is not a rest — it is the other side accumulating/distributing. **Filter:** volume must contract through the flag; expanding volume during the pause is a red flag for failure.

**No real flagpole.** Without a genuine steep, high-volume impulse, there is no stored energy to continue — a "flag" off a weak, drifting move projects nothing. **Filter:** insist on a clean, forceful pole.

**Fakeout breaks.** Price pokes out of the flag on thin volume and snaps back. **Filter:** require a closing break with volume expansion; consider the retest entry for confirmation.

**Gap risk (very real in India).** Overnight news, results, or global moves can gap price straight through your tight flag stop. **Management:** size for gap risk on event-sensitive names; avoid holding daily flags through earnings unless that is the explicit thesis.

**Expiry-day distortion (indices).** On Nifty/Bank Nifty expiry, option pinning and max-pain gravity can create and destroy intraday flags artificially. **Filter:** discount intraday flags near expiry; lean on OI shifts rather than the pattern alone.

**Over-extension.** A flag forming after price is already very extended from a long trend (the third or fourth flag up in a mature run) is lower probability — the pole's energy is waning. **Filter:** the first one or two flags after a fresh breakout are the best; treat late-stage flags with caution and tighter targets.

Pros treat the flag as a momentum-continuation *hypothesis* with a razor-tight invalidation: a close back inside the flag, or a pause that turns deep and heavy, kills the trade. The edge is taking many high-quality, tight-stop flags in strong trends, projecting the pole, and trailing the runners.

## Interview-ready summary

"Flags and pennants are short, sharp continuation patterns that form after a steep, high-volume impulse move — the flagpole. A flag is a small channel that drifts gently against the trend; a pennant is a tiny converging coil. Both mean the same thing: the trend is resting, not reversing. The tell is that the pause is *shallow* — under about half the pole — *brief*, a few sessions, and on *contracting* volume, because the pullback is just profit-taking with no real opposing conviction. I enter on a close out of the flag in the trend direction on renewed volume, stop just beyond the flag on the other side — which is naturally tight — and target the *flagpole length projected from the breakout*, since flags tend to fly at half-mast. That tight stop against a full-pole target is why the reward-to-risk is excellent, often 3:1 or better. On Bank Nifty or a breakout stock I add option-chain confluence — a bull-flag breakout with call OI unwinding is high-conviction. The whole thing is a volatility contraction inside strong momentum. It's a probability, not a certainty: if the pause turns deep, long, or heavy-volume, the momentum thesis is broken and I stand aside or cut."
