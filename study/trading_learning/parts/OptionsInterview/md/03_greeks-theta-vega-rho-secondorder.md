# The Greeks II: Theta, Vega, Rho & Second-Order

Theta pays the bills or eats them; vega is where books actually blow up; rho is minor in India but not zero; and the second-order greeks are what separate a trader who manages a book from one who manages a position. This chapter goes deep on all four.

**Q1. Describe the theta decay curve. Why isn't decay linear?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* whether you know decay accelerates and why.
- *Deep answer:* Time value doesn't bleed evenly — for an ATM option it decays roughly with **√T**, so the *rate* of decay accelerates as expiry approaches, going near-vertical in the final days. An ATM option loses a small slice of its time value each day when far-dated, but in the last week the daily theta balloons, and on expiry day the remaining extrinsic can evaporate in hours. Intuition: time value is the price of the remaining uncertainty, and uncertainty shrinks proportional to √(time left), so halving the time doesn't halve the value — it removes only ~30%, meaning the back-loaded chunk falls off fast at the end. The trader's consequence: as a *seller*, the last few days are where I earn the most theta per day — but that's also exactly where gamma is largest, so I'm collecting the fat theta precisely when a move hurts most (the gamma-theta identity). As a *buyer*, I avoid holding near-expiry ATM options for a directional view because theta is savage; I'd rather buy time. In India's weekly cycle this curve resets every week, so the steep back-end of decay is a recurring Wednesday-Thursday phenomenon I structure around.
- *Say it crisply:* ATM theta decays with the square root of time, so it accelerates into expiry — the last days pay sellers the most per day, but that's also peak gamma, so the rich theta and the sharp risk arrive together.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Does OTM decay the same way?" No — deep OTM time value is smaller and can actually decay more linearly or even peak mid-life; the sharp accelerating curve is an ATM phenomenon.

**Q2. Contrast theta decay for ATM versus deep-OTM options.**
- *Interviewer is testing:* nuance that the "options decay fastest at expiry" cliché misses.
- *Deep answer:* The cliché is only true for ATM options. **ATM:** theta is largest in absolute terms and accelerates into expiry (√T curve), because ATM options carry the most extrinsic value to lose and their fate is most uncertain until the very end. **Deep OTM:** the story is different — a far-OTM option has little time value to begin with, and its theta is small and can decay more steadily; some deep-OTM options actually see their theta *peak well before* expiry and then flatten, because once they're clearly going to expire worthless, there's little value left to decay day-over-day. **Deep ITM:** almost all value is intrinsic, so theta is tiny — it behaves like the underlying. Practical use: if I want to *sell* theta efficiently, ATM/near-ATM gives the most decay but the most gamma risk; selling OTM gives less theta but a wider margin for error before the strike is threatened. When I structure a premium-selling book I'm consciously trading theta yield against gamma/tail — ATM strangles harvest more decay but are far more sensitive to a surprise than OTM strangles.
- *Say it crisply:* ATM theta is biggest and accelerates into expiry; deep-OTM theta is small and can peak early and flatten — so "decay is fastest near expiry" is really only an ATM statement.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "So where do you sell for income?" Slightly OTM — enough theta to be worth it, enough distance that normal noise doesn't threaten the strike, and I size the tail.

**Q3. What is weekend theta and how do you handle it?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* practical calendar awareness that only real traders think about.
- *Deep answer:* Options decay on calendar time, but the market is only open on trading days — so the ~3 days of theta over a weekend (Fri close to Mon open) get *priced in* somewhere. Two schools, and the market blends them: pure BSM says Friday should lose three days of time value, but if it did, buyers would dump options Friday and repurchase Monday. In practice market-makers "bleed" the weekend decay gradually across Thursday and Friday, marking implied vol down slightly so the option doesn't gap lower on Monday's open. As a *seller*, I like holding short premium over a weekend/long holiday because I collect multiple days of theta for one day of gamma risk — but I'm aware the market has partly pre-charged that decay, so it's not free, and a weekend can also deliver a gap (global news over the weekend) that my short gamma can't hedge. Around long holidays (Diwali, multiple market holidays), the accumulated theta is larger and I explicitly factor it: sell into the holiday to bank the calendar decay, but reduce size because any gap on reopening is unhedgeable. It's a recurring small edge if managed, a recurring small trap if the weekend brings a shock.
- *Say it crisply:* Weekends carry three days of calendar decay that the market bleeds in across Thursday-Friday — I like being short premium into them to bank theta, but I size down because a weekend gap is unhedgeable.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "How does the market avoid a Monday IV gap?" Dealers quietly mark IV down through Friday so the option's price glides rather than jumps when the clock 'skips' the weekend.

**Q4. How does vega vary across strikes and expiries?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* whether you understand the vega surface, the core of a vol book.
- *Deep answer:* Vega — sensitivity to a 1-point change in IV — is largest for **ATM** options and for **longer-dated** options. Across strikes: ATM has peak vega; it falls off symmetrically into the wings (deep ITM/OTM options barely respond to vol because their fate is nearly decided). Across expiries: vega scales roughly with **√T**, so a 3-month option has far more vega than a weekly — long-dated options are the vol instruments, weeklies are the gamma instruments. This split is fundamental to how I run a book: if I want a *pure vol view* (I think IV is too low ahead of results season), I express it in longer-dated ATM options to load vega with minimal gamma noise; if I want *realized-vol/movement*, I use weeklies for gamma with less vega drag. The surface also isn't flat — skew means OTM puts carry a vol premium, so their *effective* vega exposure to a skew shift matters. A real vol book is managed by **vega buckets** across the term structure: I might be long 1-month vega and short 3-month vega (a term-structure trade) with near-zero net vega but a strong view on the *shape* of the curve. Netting all vega to one number hides that.
- *Say it crisply:* Vega peaks ATM and grows with the square root of time, so long-dated ATM options are my vol instruments and weeklies are my gamma instruments — and I manage vega bucketed across the term structure, not as one number.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Why bucket vega by expiry?" Because front and back vol move differently — a spike is usually front-loaded — so a book flat on total vega can be very exposed to a term-structure twist.

**Q5. How do you think about vega risk in a whole book, not a single option?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* portfolio-level vol risk management.
- *Deep answer:* At book level, net vega tells me my P&L per 1-point move in the vol surface — but the naive net number is dangerous because it assumes a *parallel* shift, which never happens. Vol moves by term structure (front spikes more than back), by skew (puts bid in a selloff), and by underlying (Nifty vs Bank Nifty IV don't move 1:1). So I decompose: **vega by expiry bucket** (weekly/monthly/quarterly), **skew exposure** (am I long or short the wings vs the body), and **cross-underlying vega**. A book can show "net vega ≈ 0" yet be massively short front-month vega and long back-month — a calendar bet that gets crushed if the front spikes on a shock. I also stress vega jointly with spot, because in India a Nifty selloff *raises* IV (negative spot-vol correlation) — so my short-vega and short-delta risks compound in a crash. My risk sheet carries a scenario grid: spot down 3% *and* VIX up 5 points simultaneously, which is the realistic joint shock, not each in isolation. That joint scenario is where books that looked balanced discover they were long correlation.
- *Say it crisply:* I never trust net vega — I bucket it by expiry, split out skew, and stress it jointly with spot, because in a selloff spot drops and vol spikes together and those exposures compound.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "What's the single most dangerous vega position?" Short front-month vega/gamma in size — one gap event spikes front vol and realized vol at once and hits you on both greeks.

**Q6. Vega versus gamma — they both love volatility, so how are they different?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* the classic confusion between realized and implied vol exposure.
- *Deep answer:* This is the distinction that separates real vol traders. **Gamma is exposure to *realized* volatility** — actual movement in the underlying; you monetize it by re-hedging (gamma scalping), and it pays off *as the market moves*, right now, regardless of what IV does. **Vega is exposure to *implied* volatility** — the market's *price* of future movement; it pays off when the option's IV re-rates, whether or not the underlying has moved yet. You can win on one and lose on the other simultaneously: buy a long-dated straddle before an event, IV rises (vega gain) but the underlying sits still (gamma/theta bleed) — net could be positive from vega alone. Or, short-dated: the market moves violently (gamma gain) but IV was already high and crushes post-event (vega loss). Time and strike separate them: **near-expiry ATM = mostly gamma, little vega**; **long-dated ATM = mostly vega, little gamma**. So when I choose an expiry I'm choosing which vol I want exposure to. If my thesis is "the market will *move*," I buy gamma (weeklies). If my thesis is "the *price* of vol is wrong," I trade vega (longer-dated). Confusing the two — buying weeklies to express a "vol is cheap" view — means paying enormous theta for almost no vega and losing even if you're right.
- *Say it crisply:* Gamma is realized-vol exposure you harvest by re-hedging as the market moves; vega is implied-vol exposure that pays when IV re-rates — near-expiry is gamma, long-dated is vega, and you must trade the right one for your thesis.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "I think vol is underpriced but the market's quiet — what do I buy?" Long-dated options for vega — weeklies would just bleed theta while you wait for IV to re-rate.

**Q7. Why is rho minor in India but not zero?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* whether you dismiss rho entirely (wrong) or place it correctly.
- *Deep answer:* Rho — sensitivity to interest rates — is the smallest greek for the products most Indian traders touch, because those are dominated by short-dated index options where the discounting effect over a few days/weeks is tiny. A weekly Nifty option's value barely moves if repo shifts 25 bps, so on a weekly-gamma book rho is noise. But it's *not* zero, and it matters in three places: (1) **long-dated options** — LEAPS-style or multi-month positions have real rho because e^(−rT) discounting bites over a year; (2) **put-call parity and the cost of carry** — the forward embedded in option prices depends on rates, so the *relationship* between calls and puts (and futures basis) is a rate function, which matters for parity/box arbitrage and for pricing synthetics; (3) **the financing of the position itself** — margin funding cost, the borrow on stock for physical-settlement hedges, and the futures basis are all rate-driven, and in a high-rate regime that carry is a real P&L line even if "rho" on the option screen looks small. So I'd tell an interviewer: rho is negligible for my weekly trading greeks, but rates live in my *carry and parity* math, and I'd never say it's zero — that would be sloppy.
- *Say it crisply:* Rho is negligible on short-dated index options that dominate Indian trading, but it's real in long-dated options and it lives inside carry, futures basis, and put-call parity — so minor, not zero.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Where would rho actually bite you?" A long-dated position or a box-spread/parity arb, where the discounting and financing rate directly set fair value.

**Q8. Explain vanna and why it matters around an event.**
- *Interviewer is testing:* second-order greek fluency and real book management.
- *Deep answer:* Vanna is the cross-sensitivity ∂Delta/∂σ (equivalently ∂Vega/∂Spot) — how your delta changes when *implied vol* moves, or how your vega changes when *spot* moves. It matters enormously in India because of the strong **negative spot-vol correlation**: when Nifty falls, IV rises. If I hold OTM puts as a hedge, a selloff *both* moves spot toward them *and* raises their IV, which fattens their delta faster than spot alone would — my hedge grows more potent exactly when I need it (good if long puts, painful if short them). Around events, vanna is what makes a delta-hedge unstable: I can be delta-neutral at current vol, but the moment IV jumps on the announcement, my delta is no longer neutral — the vol move *re-deltas* my book even before spot moves. Desks that only hedge for spot get caught by this. Concretely, if I'm short OTM Nifty puts (short vanna in the wing) and the market gaps down with an IV spike, my delta blows out faster than a pure-gamma calculation predicts. Management: around scheduled events I re-mark deltas at a *stressed* vol, not the current one, and I keep vanna in mind when I hold skew positions — being short the put wing is a short-vanna position that bites hardest in exactly the crash scenario.
- *Say it crisply:* Vanna is how vol changes your delta — and with India's negative spot-vol correlation, a selloff spikes IV and re-deltas your book before spot even settles, so around events I re-hedge at stressed vol, not spot vol.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "So a delta-neutral book isn't neutral in a crash?" Right — vanna means the IV spike alone shifts your delta, so 'neutral at today's vol' can be very long/short once vol moves.

**Q9. What is charm, and when have you had to care about it?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* awareness of delta decay over time — an overnight/weekend management detail.
- *Deep answer:* Charm is ∂Delta/∂Time — the drift of your delta as time passes, holding spot constant. It's why a hedge that's neutral tonight isn't neutral tomorrow morning even if nothing moves. Charm is largest for near-expiry, near-ATM options: as time runs out, an option that's slightly ITM sees its delta creep toward 1 and a slightly-OTM option's delta drifts toward 0 — the distribution collapses onto the strike. Where I care: **holding a delta-hedged book over a weekend or into expiry.** Say I'm delta-flat Friday evening with a slightly-ITM short call; by Monday, purely from time passing, that call's delta has drifted higher and I'm now net short delta without spot having moved — charm silently un-hedged me. On expiry day charm is fierce: the deltas of everything near the strike race to 0 or 1 through the session, so a book that was neutral at open needs continuous re-hedging just to *stay* neutral as the clock runs. Practically I anticipate charm by projecting my morning delta the night before — "if spot opens unchanged, where will my delta be?" — and pre-planning the hedge rather than being surprised at the open. It's a small greek most retail ignores, but it's exactly the kind of overnight drift that turns a "flat" book into a directional one.
- *Say it crisply:* Charm is how your delta drifts just from time passing — near expiry and over weekends a hedged book silently un-hedges itself, so I project tomorrow's delta tonight and pre-plan the re-hedge.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "When is charm worst?" Near-ATM into expiry — deltas race toward 0 or 1 through the day, so staying neutral needs constant re-hedging.

**Q10. Explain vomma/volga and what it means for holding wings.**
- *Interviewer is testing:* convexity-of-vega understanding — advanced vol-book intuition.
- *Deep answer:* Vomma (a.k.a. volga) is ∂Vega/∂σ — the convexity of your position with respect to implied vol, i.e. how your vega *grows* as vol rises. It's the "vol gamma." Vomma is largest for **OTM options (the wings)** and near zero for ATM, where vega is at a peak and locally flat. This is the whole reason wings behave the way they do in a vol spike: an OTM option starts with modest vega, but as IV rockets, its vega *increases* — so it gains value at an accelerating rate. Positive vomma means "long options catch a tailwind in a vol explosion." Practical consequence: if I want to be long a potential vol *blowup* (not just a small vol move), I buy OTM options / the wings, because their positive vomma means my long-vol position gets *longer* vega exactly as vol spikes — convex payoff in a crisis. Conversely, **short OTM options are short vomma** — a market-maker short the put wing finds their short-vega position getting *more* short as IV explodes in a crash, so losses compound non-linearly. That's precisely why selling cheap-looking wings is dangerous: the greek that gets you isn't first-order vega, it's vomma turning a manageable short-vol into a runaway one. Skew and smile *exist* largely because the market prices this vol-of-vol convexity into the wings.
- *Say it crisply:* Vomma is vega's convexity — largest in the wings — so long OTM options get *longer* vega as vol explodes (convex crisis payoff), and selling cheap wings is short vomma, where a vol spike makes your short-vol position run away from you.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "So why does skew exist?" Partly because the wings carry vol-of-vol (vomma) risk and demand for crash protection, so the market prices OTM puts at a higher IV than ATM.

**Q11. If you could only monitor three greeks on a live book, which and why?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* prioritization and real risk-management judgment.
- *Deep answer:* **Delta, gamma, and vega** — in that order of immediacy, though which dominates depends on the book. **Delta** first because it's my raw directional exposure — a gap moves it into P&L instantly, and an unhedged delta is how you lose the most money the fastest; it's the number I must always know in rupees. **Gamma** second because it tells me how *unstable* my delta is — how fast a move turns a flat book directional and how much re-hedging pain (or gain) a big move brings; short gamma is my hidden tail, so I watch it especially into expiry. **Vega** third because it's where a vol book actually blows up — a delta/gamma-hedged position can still be destroyed by a vol re-rate, and in India spot and vol move together so vega compounds my directional risk in a crash. Theta I treat as the *scorecard* — it tells me the daily cost/income but I don't manage *to* it; theta is just the sign of my gamma. I'd tell the interviewer: I set hard limits on rupee delta and net vega, watch gamma as my stability/tail gauge, and stress all three *jointly* (spot down + vol up) rather than one at a time — because the real risk isn't any single greek, it's their correlation in a shock.
- *Say it crisply:* Delta for raw direction, gamma for how fast that direction runs away from me, vega for where a vol book actually dies — theta's just the scorecard, and I stress all three jointly for the crash.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Why not theta in the top three?" Theta is deterministic and just the price of your gamma — it tells you the bill, not the risk; the risk lives in the moves, which are delta, gamma, and vega.
