# Volatility: IV vs HV, Skew, Term Structure & the Surface

Volatility is the product an options trader actually trades. This chapter drills the stuff a desk head probes to see whether you understand vol as a tradeable asset with a risk premium, a shape, and a term structure — not just a Black-Scholes input.

**Q1. What's the difference between implied vol and realized/historical vol, and how do you actually trade the spread between them?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* whether you see IV as a market price and HV as an outcome, and know the trade that connects them.
- *Deep answer:* Historical/realized vol is backward-looking — the annualized standard deviation of actual returns the underlying printed (close-to-close, or Parkinson/Yang-Zhang using highs/lows for efficiency). Implied vol is forward-looking — the vol number the market is charging in the option premium, backed out of the price via the model. The core vol trade is the spread: if I think IV (say Nifty ATM at 14%) overstates what the index will actually realize over the option's life, I sell the straddle and delta-hedge, harvesting the gap; if IV is cheap versus expected realized, I buy and hedge. The P&L of a delta-hedged short option is essentially the difference between the variance I sold (IV²) and the variance that realized (HV²), scaled by gamma/dollar-gamma each day. In practice on Nifty weeklies I'm comparing current IV to trailing 10/20-day realized AND to the realized I expect given the event calendar — a quiet week post-expiry realizes far less than a budget/RBI/Fed week. The discipline is not "IV is high" in the abstract, it's "IV is high relative to what THIS underlying will realize over THIS window."
- *Say it crisply:* HV is what the stock did; IV is what the market's charging for what it might do. I trade the gap — sell and hedge when implied is rich to expected realized, buy and hedge when it's cheap.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "How do you capture a rich IV without a directional bet?" Sell the ATM straddle and delta-hedge daily so P&L is driven by the IV-minus-realized spread, not by direction.

**Q2. Why is implied vol usually higher than subsequent realized vol?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* whether you know the variance risk premium and its economic reason.
- *Deep answer:* Empirically, across most markets and most of the time, IV trades above the vol that subsequently realizes — the variance risk premium (VRP). The reason is structural: option sellers are supplying insurance and demand compensation for tail risk and for the fact that vol spikes exactly when everything else in a portfolio hurts (vol is negatively correlated with the market, so short-vol has bad "beta" in a crash). Buyers — hedgers, funds buying protective puts, retail buying lottery-ticket OTMs — are structurally willing to overpay. So the seller earns a premium on average, the same way an insurer earns above fair actuarial value. That's WHY systematic short-vol/premium-selling works over time — and also why it blows up: you're collecting small premiums for taking a rare, correlated, fat tail (Feb 2018 VIX, March 2020, a gap-down on Bank Nifty). In India the VRP is real on index options but you must respect that a single gap can erase months. My mental model: I'm getting paid the VRP to be short insurance, so I size for the tail, not for the median outcome.
- *Say it crisply:* IV > realized because option sellers are selling insurance against correlated tail risk and demand a premium — the variance risk premium. It's a real edge that pays small and often and occasionally takes a big bite.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "So should you always be short vol?" No — the VRP compresses or inverts before/around known events and in stressed regimes; you want to be short when the premium is fat and defensive when it's thin or inverted.

**Q3. Explain the volatility skew/smile. Why are puts bid?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* whether you understand skew as crash-risk pricing, not a model artifact.
- *Deep answer:* If the market priced every strike at one vol, IV would be flat across strikes. It isn't — for equity indices you get a downward skew: OTM puts trade at materially higher IV than ATM, and OTM calls at lower IV. Two reasons. First, the crash-risk reason: equity indices go down faster than they go up (leverage effect, forced deleveraging, correlation → 1 in a sell-off), so the real return distribution is left-skewed and fat-tailed on the downside — puts must price that. Second, the supply/demand reason: everyone is structurally long equities and buys downside protection, so puts are bid; nobody needs to insure the upside as urgently, so calls are offered. On Nifty/Bank Nifty you'll routinely see a 25-delta put a few vol points over the 25-delta call. For individual stocks you can even see reverse skew around takeover/upside events. The skew matters practically because it prices your spreads: selling a put spread you're selling expensive vol and buying cheaper vol; a risk reversal (sell call, buy put) is a direct bet on skew.
- *Say it crisply:* Indices crash down, not up, and everyone hedges downside — so OTM puts carry a vol premium. That downward skew is the market pricing correlated crash risk.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "What happens to skew in a panic?" It steepens — put IV blows out faster than ATM as demand for protection spikes and dealers get short gamma.

**Q4. What does it mean for skew to "steepen," and how do you trade it?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* second-order thinking on the shape of vol, not just the level.
- *Deep answer:* Skew steepening means the IV difference between downside strikes and ATM widens — the whole put wing lifts relative to the body. It happens in fear: as the index falls, demand for puts surges, dealers who are short those puts get shorter gamma and bid vol, and the left tail gets repriced fatter. You can be right on vol level but wrong on skew. Trades: a put ratio backspread (sell 1 near put, buy 2 further puts) is long skew and long tail — it profits if the crash extends and skew steepens. A risk reversal expresses a directional skew view. If I think skew is too steep (puts overpriced after a scare has passed), I'll sell the put wing relative to ATM — e.g., a put spread — collecting the rich downside vol. The nuance: skew mean-reverts but slowly, and it can stay steep through a whole nervous regime, so I treat skew trades as slower, position-sized bets rather than scalps, and I watch the 25-delta put/call vol spread as my gauge.
- *Say it crisply:* Steepening = the put wing lifting versus ATM as fear bids protection. I trade it with ratio backspreads or risk reversals, and I fade over-steep skew with put spreads once the panic fades.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "What's your quick metric for skew?" The 25-delta risk reversal — put IV minus call IV at 25 delta; watch how it moves versus its own range.

**Q5. Explain the vol term structure — contango and backwardation of volatility.**
- *Interviewer is testing:* whether you think across expiries, not just one series.
- *Deep answer:* Term structure is IV plotted across maturities for a given strike (usually ATM). Normal state is contango/upward-sloping: near-dated vol is low, longer-dated is higher, because the near term looks calm and uncertainty accumulates with time. In stress it inverts to backwardation: front-month/weekly vol spikes ABOVE longer-dated because the market expects the storm now and mean-reversion later. This is the single most useful regime signal. In contango, calendars (sell front, buy back) and being short front-week theta are comfortable. In backwardation the front is screaming — being naked short the front week is where people die, and long-front/short-back or long gamma can pay. On Nifty you'll see the weekly's IV rip above the monthly's into a big event or a sell-off; once it passes, the curve re-steepens into contango. I read the weekly-vs-monthly ATM IV spread every morning as my "what regime am I in" check before I decide to be a net seller or a net buyer of the front.
- *Say it crisply:* Contango = near vol cheaper than far, the calm default; backwardation = front vol above back, the stress signal. The weekly-vs-monthly IV spread tells me whether to sell the front or respect it.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Which structure likes contango?" Calendars and short-front-theta positions; you're selling the cheap-decaying front and owning the richer back.

**Q6. What is the vol surface, and why does a desk care about the whole thing?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* whether you can hold skew AND term structure together as one object.
- *Deep answer:* The surface is IV as a function of both strike (skew/moneyness) and time (term structure) — a 3D sheet. A serious desk doesn't quote one vol; it fits and risk-manages the whole surface because every option you hold sits at a point on it and your Greeks aggregate across it. Why it matters: your book can be flat "vega" in aggregate but be long front-week put-wing vega and short back-month call vega — a surface-shape risk that a single vega number hides. The surface also must be arbitrage-free (no calendar or butterfly arbitrage), so when one point dislocates — a weekly put wing gaps on a scare — a relative-value trader sells the rich point and buys a cheap neighbouring point. As an experienced trader I don't literally recalibrate SABR by hand, but I think in surface terms: "where on the surface am I long/short vega, and what move in the SHAPE (not just level) hurts me?" That's what separates someone who's short vol from someone who knows exactly which corner of the surface will blow up their book.
- *Say it crisply:* The surface is IV across strike and expiry at once. I manage it as one object because my real risk is the shape — which corner I'm long or short — not a single vega number.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Give a surface-shape risk example." Being short front-week ATM straddle but long monthly wings: you're short the point that moves most in a gap, so a big move hurts far more than your net vega implies.

**Q7. IV rank vs IV percentile — what's the difference and which do you use?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* precision on the two most-confused vol-context metrics.
- *Deep answer:* Both contextualize today's IV against its own history (typically 1 year). IV Rank = (current IV − 52-week low) / (52-week high − 52-week low), so it only cares about where you sit in the min-max range. IV Percentile = the % of days over the lookback that IV closed BELOW today's level, so it accounts for the whole distribution of days, not just the extremes. They diverge when one spike distorts the range: a single crash pushing the yearly high way up can leave IV Rank permanently low even when IV has actually spent most days elevated — IV Percentile catches that, IV Rank doesn't. I lean on IV Percentile as the more robust "is vol rich or cheap versus its own norm" gauge, and I use IV Rank as a quick supplement. Crucially, neither is a trade by itself — high IV rank tells me premium is rich to SELL, but I still overlay the event calendar and term structure. On Indian indices I effectively build this off India VIX history for the index and off per-stock IV series for single names.
- *Say it crisply:* IV Rank is where you sit in the year's high-low range; IV Percentile is the fraction of days IV was lower than now. Percentile is more robust because one spike doesn't distort it.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "High IV rank — do you just sell?" No; I check whether it's high because of a pending event (don't sell into it) or ambient fear (sellable), and I check term structure first.

**Q8. What exactly is India VIX and how do you use it as a trader?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* India-specific vol literacy and practical use.
- *Deep answer:* India VIX is NSE's volatility index — the market's expected annualized Nifty volatility over the NEXT 30 calendar days, computed from the order book of near- and next-month Nifty option prices using the variance-swap/CBOE-style methodology (not from any single strike; it aggregates the whole strip). So VIX at ~14 means ~14% annualized expected 30-day move; divide by √365≈19.1 for a rough daily 1-sigma (~0.73%). Uses: (1) regime gauge — sub-12 is complacent, 20+ is nervous, 30+ is genuine stress; (2) contextualizing premium — high VIX means richer index premium to sell but bigger tails; (3) it's mean-reverting and negatively correlated with Nifty, so spikes tend to fade. Caveats a pro respects: VIX is a 30-day construct, but I mostly trade WEEKLY options whose realized/implied can diverge hard from the 30-day number, especially around events; and VIX itself isn't directly tradeable in India the way US VIX futures/options are, so it's a signal, not an instrument. I use it to size and to decide net-long-vs-short-vol posture, never as a standalone entry.
- *Say it crisply:* India VIX is the market's expected 30-day annualized Nifty vol, backed out of the whole option strip. I use it as a regime and sizing gauge — complacent under ~12, stressed over ~20 — not as a tradeable instrument.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "VIX is 15 — what daily move is priced?" Roughly 15/19.1 ≈ 0.78% one-sigma per day on Nifty.

**Q9. Explain vega and IV crush. When does IV crush hurt or help you most?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* whether you manage the vol-level Greek and the classic event trap.
- *Deep answer:* Vega is P&L per 1 vol-point change in IV — longest-dated, ATM options carry the most vega; it decays as expiry nears (a weekly the morning of expiry has almost no vega, all gamma/theta). IV crush is the collapse in IV right after a known event resolves the uncertainty — earnings, budget, RBI/Fed, election result. Before the event IV is bid because the market prices a big move; the moment it's known, that uncertainty evaporates and IV drops sharply, often more than the actual move justifies. The trap: a retail trader buys a straddle before results, the stock moves, but IV crushes so hard the long options LOSE money even though direction was "right." As a seller I harvest this deliberately — sell rich event vol and let the crush pay me — but I size for the gap because the crush protects me only if the move stays inside the premium I collected. On a single stock into earnings I might sell a strangle knowing IV will halve overnight; the danger is the fat move that overwhelms the crush. Long-vega positions want to be OWNED before IV rises and closed before the crush.
- *Say it crisply:* Vega is your P&L per vol point; IV crush is the post-event vol collapse. Sellers harvest it, buyers get trapped when a "right" directional bet still loses to the crush.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "How do you avoid buying into a crush?" Prefer structures that are short or neutral vega into the event, or buy vol well before it's bid, not the day before.

**Q10. Are you a net vol buyer or vol seller, and why?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* self-awareness, regime-dependence, and risk maturity — there's no single right answer, only a defensible framework.
- *Deep answer:* The mature answer is "it depends on the regime, and I know my default bias and its failure mode." Structurally I'm a net vol SELLER because the variance risk premium pays sellers over time — on Indian index weeklies the theta/VRP edge is real. BUT I only lean short when: IV is rich to expected realized (high IV percentile, no big event pending), term structure is in contango, and skew isn't screaming. I flip to buyer or flat when the front is in backwardation, VIX is spiking, or a fat event sits inside the option's life — because that's exactly when short-vol blows up. The key is that a good vol trader is not dogmatically one thing; being permanently short vol is picking up nickels in front of a steamroller, and being permanently long bleeds theta. So: default seller for the VRP, disciplined about WHEN, always sized for the tail, and willing to own gamma/vega when the market's paying me to. What I never do is sell cheap vol or buy expensive vol just to "have a position."
- *Say it crisply:* Default seller — the variance risk premium pays me — but only when IV is rich to realized, the curve's in contango, and no event's pending; I flip to buyer or flat in backwardation and stress, and I always size for the tail.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "What kills a career short-vol trader?" Selling because vol is 'high' without checking realized/events, and sizing for the median instead of the tail — one correlated gap wipes out a year of premium.

**Q11. How do you tell whether vol is genuinely cheap or rich right now?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* a repeatable process, not a gut call.
- *Deep answer:* I triangulate. (1) IV vs expected realized: current ATM IV against trailing 10/20-day realized AND against the realized I expect given the calendar — rich only if IV clearly exceeds what this window should print. (2) IV percentile/rank: where does today's IV sit in its own 1-year distribution. (3) Term structure: contango or backwardation, and how steep — front vol above back is a warning that "cheap-looking" back vol or "rich-looking" front vol may be regime-driven. (4) Skew: is the wing I'm about to trade rich or cheap versus its history (25-delta RR). (5) Event map: what's inside the option's life — budget, RBI, Fed, expiry, stock results. Only when several of these line up do I call it. Example: Nifty weekly ATM IV at 11% with 20-day realized at 9%, IV percentile ~30, curve in contango, no event — that's a modest sell. Same 11% the day before the RBI policy with the weekly IV above the monthly is NOT cheap to sell, it's the market correctly pricing an event.
- *Say it crisply:* I triangulate IV vs expected realized, IV percentile, term structure, skew, and the event calendar — vol's only 'cheap' or 'rich' when several of those agree, not on the headline number.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "One number if you had to pick?" IV versus expected realized over the option's exact life — everything else is context around that spread.

**Q12. Walk me through how a delta-hedged short straddle actually makes or loses money.**
- *Interviewer is testing:* the deepest vol concept — that a hedged option is a bet on realized vs implied, expressed through gamma and theta.
- *Deep answer:* Sell an ATM Nifty straddle and delta-hedge it (trade futures to keep net delta ~0). Now direction is neutralized; the P&L is the fight between theta and gamma. Each day I collect theta (the premium decaying in my favor) but I'm short gamma, so when the index moves I'm forced to hedge at bad prices — buy futures higher, sell lower — bleeding money proportional to the SQUARED move (dollar-gamma). The daily P&L ≈ theta earned minus the cost of hedging the realized move. If realized vol comes in BELOW the IV I sold, theta more than covers the hedging losses and I win — that's collecting the VRP. If the index realizes MORE than the IV I sold, my gamma-hedging losses exceed theta and I lose, even with zero net directional view. So a delta-hedged short option is literally a short position in realized variance struck at the IV I sold: P&L ∝ (IV² − realized²). Worked feel: sell the straddle at 14% IV; if Nifty grinds and realizes 10%, the daily theta swamps my small hedge costs and I keep most of the premium; if a headline hits and it realizes 22%, my re-hedging losses blow past the premium. This is the mechanical heart of why IV-vs-realized IS the trade.
- *Say it crisply:* Delta-hedged, a short straddle is a short realized-variance bet: I earn theta and pay for every move via short gamma, so I win if realized comes in under the IV I sold and lose if it comes in over.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Why hedge daily and not continuously?" Continuous hedging is the textbook ideal; in practice discrete hedging adds path noise, so I hedge on delta/time bands to balance slippage and costs against tracking the theoretical P&L.
