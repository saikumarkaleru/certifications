# Adjustments, Rolling & Managing a Trade Through Pain

This chapter separates traders who have actually managed size through a drawdown from those who only know the payoff diagrams. The interviewer wants to hear a decision framework — adjust vs close vs do nothing — plus the honesty to say when adding to a loser is a trap. Rules and specs date-stamped as of July 2026; verify current NSE/SEBI lot sizes and expiry structure before quoting.

**Q1. When do you adjust a position versus just close it versus do nothing?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* a clean decision hierarchy, not reflexive tinkering.
- *Deep answer:* My default is do nothing — most adjustments are just paying costs to soothe anxiety. I only act when the thesis or the risk profile has actually changed. Close when the reason I put the trade on is gone (thesis invalidated, or I've captured most of the edge). Adjust when the thesis is intact but the risk has grown beyond my bounds — e.g. one side got tested and my delta/gamma is now outside limits, but I still believe in mean-reversion. Do nothing when it's just noise inside my expected range. The discipline: an adjustment must improve my risk-adjusted position on its own merits, i.e. I'd put the adjusted structure on fresh today. If I wouldn't, I'm just averaging into a loser to avoid booking a loss. I also weigh transaction costs and STT — over-adjusting weeklies bleeds you via costs and the ITM-exercise STT trap.
- *Say it crisply:* Do nothing by default; close when the thesis is dead; adjust only when the thesis holds but risk breached my limits — and only if I'd put the adjusted structure on fresh today.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "How do you avoid over-adjusting?" I require each adjustment to stand alone as a trade I'd open today; if not, I close instead.

**Q2. Explain rolling the untested side. Why and when?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* the standard strangle/condor defence and its limits.
- *Deep answer:* On a short strangle, if the market moves toward my short call, the put side (untested) has decayed and is now far OTM collecting almost nothing. Rolling the untested side means buying back that cheap put and selling a new one closer to spot — collecting more credit to offset the loss on the tested call side and re-centering the position. It reduces net delta a touch and improves breakeven on the tested side. The limit, and where amateurs get hurt: rolling the untested side closer increases risk on the side that's currently winning — if the market whips back, you've now created a new tested side. So I only roll the untested side when I still expect range-bound behaviour, I don't roll it so close that I've just built a fresh problem, and I never let the total position grow beyond my original max-loss bounds. It's a credit-and-recenter tool, not a rescue.
- *Say it crisply:* Rolling the untested side buys back the decayed leg and re-sells it closer to collect credit and recenter — good in a range, but don't roll it so tight you manufacture a new tested side on a whipsaw.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "What if both sides get tested after?" Then I've over-rolled — that's why I cap how close I bring the untested leg.

**Q3. Rolling out (in time) and rolling down/up — mechanics and the trap.**
- *Interviewer is testing:* whether you understand rolling can hide losses.
- *Deep answer:* Rolling out means closing the current-expiry short and opening the same or adjusted strike in a later expiry, usually for a net credit because further-dated options carry more premium. Rolling down (puts) or up (calls) moves the strike away from spot to reduce assignment/ITM risk. Combined, "roll out and down" is the classic defence on a tested short put: go to next month, lower strike, collect credit, buy time for mean-reversion. The trap: you can almost always roll for a credit, which makes it feel free — but you're extending duration and often adding size/risk to avoid realising a loss. That's how a small losing trade becomes a huge multi-month position you're trapped in. My rule: I'll roll out/down once, for a genuine credit, if the thesis holds and the new position is within my limits — but I never roll purely to avoid booking a loss, and I never increase size to make the roll "work."
- *Say it crisply:* Roll out/down for a credit to buy time when the thesis holds, but never roll just to dodge a realised loss — that's how a small loser becomes a position you're trapped in.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "How many times will you roll?" Ideally once; a second roll usually means the thesis is wrong and I should close.

**Q4. Converting a losing short into a spread — when and why?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* using defined risk as a defensive tool.
- *Deep answer:* If a naked short (say a short call) is being tested and my tail risk is now uncomfortable, I can buy a further-OTM call to convert it into a vertical spread — capping the loss. Yes, I pay premium and give up some potential recovery, but I've turned undefined risk into defined risk exactly when the tail matters most. I do this when IV has expanded (the wing is relatively cheaper to buy in vol terms early) and when my modelled 3-sigma loss on the naked leg has breached my limit. The alternative — converting a short into a spread by rolling the short strike and adding a long — should keep me inside original max-loss. This is often smarter than closing outright because it keeps me in the trade with a known worst case. On single stocks near expiry, converting to defined risk also protects against the physical-settlement/assignment tail.
- *Say it crisply:* When a naked short's tail breaches my limit, I buy a wing to convert it to a defined-risk spread — I pay for the cap, but I've bought certainty on my worst case right when it matters.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Why not just close?" Sometimes I do — but converting keeps me in a thesis I still believe while capping the tail, at lower cost than exiting into a spike.

**Q5. "Adding to defend" — when is it legitimate and when is it a trap?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* the single most dangerous habit in option selling.
- *Deep answer:* Adding to a tested position (selling more premium as it moves against you, or "doubling the strangle") is legitimate ONLY if: the thesis is genuinely intact, the added risk keeps the total position inside your original max-loss/3-sigma bounds, and you'd open that new structure fresh today. It's a trap — and the classic blow-up — when you add purely to lower your average and "get back to breakeven faster," because you're increasing size into a move that's proving you wrong, right as gamma and vega work against you. The margin also expands as you add and as vol rises, so you're leveraging up into stress precisely when a margin call can force you out. My discipline: adding must reduce, not increase, my portfolio risk relative to my limits; if adding pushes my 3-sigma loss past my budget, it's forbidden regardless of how attractive the premium looks. Nine times out of ten the correct "defence" is to reduce, not add.
- *Say it crisply:* Adding to defend is legitimate only if it keeps total risk inside my original bounds and I'd open it fresh today — otherwise it's just leveraging into a move that's proving me wrong, and the answer is to reduce.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "So martingale option selling?" Never — increasing size into a losing thesis with expanding margin is exactly how books blow up.

**Q6. How do you manage a tested short strike as it goes near/at-the-money?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* concrete management of the danger zone.
- *Deep answer:* Once my short strike is ATM, gamma is at its worst and delta is ~0.5 and rising — small moves now cost a lot and my delta is unstable. My options: (1) delta-hedge with the underlying/futures to neutralise the runaway delta; (2) roll the strike further out of the money (up/out for a call), accepting a debit or small credit, to step out of the gamma zone; (3) cap it with a wing (buy the further strike) to define the risk; (4) just close and take the loss. Which one depends on time to expiry and my thesis. Near expiry with high gamma, I lean to closing or capping — you cannot safely delta-hedge a huge-gamma expiry-day short because the hedge itself whips. With time left, rolling out/away buys room. The mistake is freezing — an ATM short strike on expiry day is the most dangerous single thing in the book and needs a decision, not hope.
- *Say it crisply:* At an ATM short strike gamma is maximal, so I delta-hedge or roll out with time left, but near expiry I cap or close — you can't safely hedge expiry-day gamma, and freezing is the real mistake.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Delta-hedge or close on expiry day?" Close or cap — expiry-day gamma makes hedging a losing game of chasing your own delta.

**Q7. Do you take profits at 50%? Defend the rule.**
- *Interviewer is testing:* understanding of the risk/reward of holding winners to expiry.
- *Deep answer:* Yes, a common mechanical rule on short premium is to buy back at ~50% of max profit. The logic: the remaining premium is small but the risk (gamma, tail, a reversal) is not — you're risking the rest of the position to earn the last, low-return rupees. Closing at 50% frees margin, removes tail exposure, and lets me redeploy into a fresh, better-priced trade with more time value to decay. Over many trades, taking 50% and re-deploying beats squeezing the last 50% because it improves win consistency and cuts the chance a winner turns into a loser near expiry. I flex the threshold with time and vol — I'll take profits faster if IV has collapsed (little left to gain) or if we're near expiry (gamma risk rising). It's not dogma; it's a favourable risk-adjusted exit.
- *Say it crisply:* Yes — at 50% of max profit the remaining premium is small but the tail and gamma risk aren't, so I book it, free the margin, and redeploy into fresh time value.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Why not hold to expiry for full premium?" Because the last 50% earns little while carrying most of the reversal and gamma risk — poor risk-adjusted return.

**Q8. Mechanical vs discretionary management — where do you land?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* self-awareness and process.
- *Deep answer:* I'm mostly mechanical with a discretionary overlay. Mechanical rules — 50% profit-take, 2× credit stop, defined max-loss, "no new short gamma on expiry day" — exist because my judgement is worst under stress, and pre-committed rules stop me negotiating with myself after a loss. But pure mechanics are blind to regime: an earnings event, an RBI policy day, a global risk-off, or a vol spike changes the distribution, and there I overlay discretion — smaller size, wider strikes, or standing aside. The key is that discretion only ever makes me more conservative than the rules, never more aggressive. If I'm tempted to override a stop to stay in, that's exactly when the mechanical rule must win. So: rules set the floor of discipline; discretion only tightens risk, never loosens it.
- *Say it crisply:* Mechanical rules for exits and limits because my judgement is worst under stress; discretion only ever to get more conservative around events — never to loosen a rule.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "When has discretion helped?" Sizing down or standing aside ahead of a policy day or a VIX spike — mechanics don't see the event, I do.

**Q9. Walk me through a real trade that went against you — exactly what you did.**
- *Interviewer is testing:* honesty, process under fire, and a clean postmortem.
- *Deep answer:* Nifty short strangle, weekly, sold 24,000 put / 24,600 call for ~₹140 total, expecting a quiet range. Midweek a global risk-off gapped Nifty down ~1.6% to ~23,650 at open; India VIX jumped ~4 points. My put side went ATM/ITM, delta ran, and both spot and vega hit the short put. First move at the open: I did NOT add — I checked my 3-sigma budget and saw the position was near my daily limit. I bought a 23,400 put to convert the naked put into a spread, capping the tail (paid up in the vol spike, but capping mattered more). I rolled the now-worthless 24,600 call down to 24,100 for a small credit to offset. Then, since the thesis (range) was invalidated, I closed the whole structure into the mid-morning stabilisation rather than hoping for a bounce, taking a loss of ~1.4% of the book — inside my limit. Postmortem: entry was fine, but I'd sized for a normal week and the strangle was a hair too big for an event-risk calendar; I should have been one lot smaller given the setup. Lesson logged: size down when the vol-of-vol is cheap and complacent.
- *Say it crisply:* On a gap-down I capped the tested put into a spread, rolled the untested call down for credit, then closed the invalidated thesis inside my loss limit — and logged that I'd sized a touch big for an event week.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Why cap before closing?" To stop the tail bleeding instantly while I made the calm decision to exit — capping buys you the composure to close well.

**Q10. What's your rule for when to just close and stop managing?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* knowing when management is denial.
- *Deep answer:* I close outright — no more adjusting — when any of these hit: the thesis is invalidated (the reason for the trade is gone), the position has breached my max-loss or daily limit, I've already rolled/adjusted once and it's still going against me, or costs/STT from repeated adjustments are eating the edge. The signal that management has become denial is when each "adjustment" is really just averaging in or extending time to avoid booking a loss — at that point I'm managing my emotions, not the trade. Booking the loss cleanly frees margin and mental capital to put on a fresh, well-priced trade. Experienced traders know the expensive losses are the ones you refuse to close; the cheap ones are taken early and forgotten. So my bias under real stress is: cap the tail instantly, then close, then move on.
- *Say it crisply:* I close and stop managing once the thesis is dead, a limit is breached, or I've already adjusted once and it's still losing — because past that point I'm managing my emotions, not the trade.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "How do you know it's denial?" When the adjustment is really averaging-in or buying time to avoid the loss — that's the tell to close.
