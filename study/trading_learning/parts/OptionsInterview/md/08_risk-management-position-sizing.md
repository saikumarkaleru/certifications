# Risk Management & Position Sizing (Options Book)

This chapter is where a desk head decides whether you can be trusted with capital. They are less interested in whether you know the Greeks and more in whether you think in max-loss, aggregate exposure, and survival. Numbers below are date-stamped where a rule can move; verify current NSE/SEBI/broker specs before you quote them live (as of July 2026).

**Q1. How do you size an options position — walk me through the actual number.**
- *Interviewer is testing:* whether you size from loss, not from "conviction" or premium collected.
- *Deep answer:* I size off max-loss-per-trade as a fixed fraction of book equity — typically 0.5–1% risk per idea, 2% hard ceiling. Say the book is ₹50 lakh and I risk 1% = ₹50,000. If I'm selling a Bank Nifty 1000-wide iron condor with lots of 35 (verify current — BN lot has moved; assume 35 for the example), and my defined max loss on the untested-through scenario is (1000 − credit)×35, I back into how many lots fit under ₹50,000, then haircut for the fact that real exit happens before max loss but gap risk can exceed it. For undefined-risk shorts (naked strangle), I can't use structural max loss, so I size off a modelled 3-sigma move: estimate the loss if the index gaps 3–4% overnight, and make THAT number ≤ my risk budget. The premium I collect never sizes the trade — a ₹120 credit on a strangle that loses ₹9,000/lot in a gap is not a "₹120 trade," it's a ₹9,000 trade. I also cap by margin: even if risk allows more, I won't deploy beyond ~50–60% of margin so a SPAN spike doesn't force liquidation.
- *Say it crisply:* I size from max loss or a modelled 3-sigma loss — 0.5–1% of equity per trade, never from the premium collected, and always leaving margin headroom for a SPAN spike.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Why not size off premium?" Because premium is the reward; risk is the loss distribution, and on short options the loss dwarfs the credit.

**Q2. Defined-risk vs undefined-risk — how does your max-loss thinking differ?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* respect for tail risk on naked shorts.
- *Deep answer:* On defined risk (spreads, condors, flies) the max loss is structural and known at entry: width minus credit, times lot size, times lots. I can size that to the rupee and I know a gap can't hurt me beyond it — margin is also lower (SPAN recognises the long leg). On undefined risk (naked calls/puts/strangles) there is no structural cap; a naked short call is theoretically unlimited loss. So my "max loss" is a scenario number, not a fact — I model a 3–4 sigma gap and treat that as my working max. I demand far more premium per unit of tail for undefined risk, keep the position smaller, and I'm quicker to convert to defined risk (buy a wing) if IV or the underlying starts moving. In India the physical-settlement regime on single stocks makes naked short stock options especially dangerous into expiry, so I strongly prefer defined risk on stocks and reserve naked shorts for index (cash-settled).
- *Say it crisply:* Defined risk has a known structural max I can size exactly; undefined risk only has a modelled 3-sigma max, so I size it smaller, price the tail higher, and cap it with a wing when it moves.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "When do you buy the wing?" When IV expands or the short delta doubles from entry — I'd rather pay for a cap than pray.

**Q3. Talk me through the aggregate Greeks of a book. What do you keep in bounds and why?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* portfolio-level thinking, not per-trade thinking.
- *Deep answer:* I run net delta, gamma, vega, theta at the book level, normalised so they're comparable. Net delta I keep near a target (often near-flat, or a small deliberate directional tilt expressed in index-point terms — e.g. book delta = +₹8,000 per 100 Nifty points). Gamma is the one that bites: near expiry, short gamma means my delta flips fast on a move, so I cap gamma such that a 1% index move doesn't swing my delta beyond what I can hedge in one clip. Vega I bound by "how much does the book lose if India VIX jumps 5 points" — a short-vega book that's fine today can bleed on a vol spike. Theta is my income but it's the mirror of short gamma/vega, so I never chase theta by piling on short gamma into expiry. The discipline: no single Greek should be able to produce a day's loss bigger than my daily loss limit under a plausible move. I re-aggregate intraday because new fills and spot moves change everything.
- *Say it crisply:* I keep net delta near target, cap gamma so my delta can't run away on a 1% move, bound vega by a VIX-shock rupee number, and treat theta as a byproduct — never a reason to over-short gamma.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Which Greek kills books?" Short gamma near expiry — it converts a normal move into a delta explosion faster than you can hedge.

**Q4. What is margin-expansion risk and how does it force your hand?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* understanding that margin is dynamic, not the entry number.
- *Deep answer:* SPAN margin is recomputed on volatility and the exchange can raise the volatility scan range or impose additional/ad-hoc margins when markets get stressed — exactly when your shorts are losing. So the ₹1.2 lakh margin on a strangle at entry can balloon to ₹2 lakh mid-crisis, on the same position, purely from a vol/scan-range hike. If you were near fully deployed, you now face a margin shortfall and the broker can square you off at the worst possible price — a forced liquidation at the bottom. That's why I never run above ~50–60% margin utilisation on short vol: the buffer is specifically for margin expansion, not for new trades. I also watch that peak-margin/intraday-margin reporting (SEBI's four-snapshot regime) means I must hold margin through the day, not just at entry. The lesson experienced traders learn the hard way: you can be right on direction and still get liquidated because margin expanded and you had no cash buffer.
- *Say it crisply:* SPAN margin expands on volatility right when shorts are hurting, so I run at 50–60% utilisation — the buffer is there so a margin hike can't force-liquidate me at the lows.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "What's the first sign?" Rising India VIX and the exchange raising scan-range/ELM — margin creeps up before the big move.

**Q5. How much can this book lose in a 3-sigma overnight gap? Show me you can compute it.**
- *Interviewer is testing:* whether you can actually stress your book, not hand-wave.
- *Deep answer:* I take my net short options and shock spot by 3 sigma. If Nifty is 24,000 and 1-day sigma from IV is ~0.9%, a 3-sigma gap ≈ 2.7% ≈ 650 points. Say I'm short 20 lots of a 24,000 straddle-ish structure with net short gamma; I revalue every leg at 23,350 AND 24,650 (gaps cut both ways), including the IV jump that accompanies a crash (down-gaps come with a vol spike, so I also bump IV +5–8 vols on the downside). The larger of the two revaluations is my 3-sigma loss. If that number exceeds my daily loss limit or my cash buffer, the book is too big — I cut lots or add wings until the shocked loss fits. I do this on the whole book jointly, not trade by trade, because correlated shorts all lose together. The key nuance: on a down-gap, spot AND vega move against a short-put/short-vol book simultaneously — you must shock both, or you'll badly underestimate the loss.
- *Say it crisply:* I revalue every short leg at spot ±3 sigma with a vol bump on the downside, take the worse side, and if that shocked loss beats my loss limit or cash buffer, the book gets cut until it fits.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Why bump vol on the downside only?" Because equity crashes are vol-up events — a symmetric shock understates crash losses.

**Q6. Stop-loss on options — do you stop on premium, on the underlying, or on Greeks?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* stop discipline and its pitfalls on convex instruments.
- *Deep answer:* I mainly stop on the underlying level or a multiple of credit, not on a fixed premium tick, because option premium is noisy and gappy. For a short premium trade I use "stop at 2× the credit received" (lose twice what I collected) as a default mechanical stop, combined with a spot level that invalidates the thesis. The catch: a hard premium stop can be gapped through — near expiry a short option can 10× on a move, so the stop is a decision level, not a guaranteed fill. For that reason I prefer defined-risk structures where the stop is built in (the wing). On longs I stop on premium decay/time as much as level. And I never widen a stop to avoid taking the loss — that's the single most common way traders blow up short-vol books.
- *Say it crisply:* I stop on the spot level that kills the thesis plus a 2× credit rule, but I treat it as a decision point not a guaranteed fill — which is why I lean to defined risk so the stop is structural.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "What if it gaps past your stop?" Then defined risk already capped me; on undefined risk I take the fill immediately and re-hedge, never hope.

**Q7. How do you handle correlation across positions?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* whether you see hidden concentration.
- *Deep answer:* The trap is thinking you're diversified because you have five tickers, when they're all long-beta short-vol expressions that lose together in a crash. I map every position to its real risk factors: index direction, vol level, and sector/single-name idiosyncratics. Short Nifty puts, short Bank Nifty puts, and short Reliance puts are effectively one big short-crash bet — I aggregate their delta and vega as if correlated ~1 in a stress scenario, because in a real gap correlations go to 1. So my portfolio 3-sigma shock (Q5) treats them jointly. I deliberately keep some genuinely offsetting exposure or dry powder, and I size the aggregate short-vol bet, not each ticker. Bank Nifty is more volatile than Nifty, so a "balanced" delta-neutral pair across the two isn't really neutral in a shock — I beta-adjust.
- *Say it crisply:* In a real gap all my long-beta short-vol trades become one trade, so I aggregate their delta and vega as correlated ~1 and size the whole cluster, beta-adjusting Bank Nifty vs Nifty.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "So diversification is fake?" In the tail, yes — correlations converge to 1, so I only trust diversification I've stress-tested jointly.

**Q8. What's your daily and per-trade loss limit, and what happens when you hit it?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* discipline and pre-commitment.
- *Deep answer:* Pre-committed numbers: ~1% of equity per trade, ~3% daily book stop, and a monthly drawdown circuit-breaker (say 6–8%) that cuts my size in half. When the daily limit hits, I flatten to a pre-defined "safe" residual and stop opening risk for the day — no revenge trading, which is where the real damage happens. The point of a hard number is that it removes discretion at exactly the moment my judgement is worst (after a loss). I log it. Hitting the monthly breaker triggers a review, not just smaller size — I want to know if it was variance or a broken edge. Experienced traders treat these limits as inviolable; the ones who blow up are always the ones who "just this once" moved the line.
- *Say it crisply:* 1% per trade, 3% daily stop, a monthly drawdown breaker that halves size — hard numbers set in advance so I can't negotiate with myself after a loss.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Ever overridden it?" No — the entire value of the limit is that it's non-negotiable.

**Q9. How does physical settlement of stock options change your risk sizing?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* India-specific tail awareness.
- *Deep answer:* All single-stock F&O in India is physically settled (since 2019, still current 2026). Any stock option ITM at expiry that you don't square off results in a delivery obligation — you must give/take actual shares, which needs full contract value in cash/stock, not just option margin. So a "small" short stock option can turn into a multi-lakh delivery obligation overnight if it drifts ITM into expiry. That's a step-change in required capital and a huge tail. I therefore size stock-option shorts assuming I might be assigned, keep clear of holding ITM stock options into expiry, and use the do-not-exercise facility / square off ITM positions before close. For index (cash-settled) this doesn't apply. Practically I treat expiry week on stock options as a different risk regime — smaller, defined, and closed out early.
- *Say it crisply:* Stock options are physically settled, so an ITM short at expiry becomes a full-value delivery obligation — I size assuming assignment and square off ITM stock options before expiry rather than hold them.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "And index?" Cash-settled, no delivery — the only expiry tail there is gamma/pin risk, not delivery.

**Q10. Give me a worked position-sizing example on a Bank Nifty short strangle.**
- *Interviewer is testing:* can you actually do the arithmetic under pressure.
- *Deep answer:* Book ₹50 lakh, risk budget 1% = ₹50,000. Bank Nifty at 52,000, weekly expiry, I sell the 51,000 put and 53,000 call, collecting ~₹150 total per lot (₹75 each side), lot size 35 (verify current — SEBI/NSE have revised lot sizes; confirm live). Credit per lot = 150 × 35 = ₹5,250. It's undefined risk, so I size off a 3-sigma gap, not the credit. 1-day sigma ~1.1% ≈ 570 points; 3-sigma ≈ 1,700 points. Shock to 50,300: the 51,000 put goes ~700 points ITM, revalue with a vol bump — call it a ~₹850/lot loss on the put side net of the ₹75 credit and the now-worthless call ≈ ₹27,000/lot loss in that gap (illustrative). To keep the 3-sigma loss ≤ ₹50,000, I can carry ~1–2 lots, not 10. Margin check: ~₹1.3–1.5 lakh/lot SPAN+exposure, so 2 lots ≈ ₹3 lakh, well under 60% of a ₹50L book — margin isn't the binding constraint here, the tail is. So: 1–2 lots, sized by the gap, not the ₹10,500 of premium two lots would collect.
- *Say it crisply:* On ₹50L with 1% risk, a Bank Nifty strangle sizes to 1–2 lots because the 3-sigma gap loss — not the ₹150 credit — is what fills the ₹50,000 budget.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Margin allowed 5 lots — why only 2?" Because margin isn't the risk; the 3-sigma loss is, and that caps me at 2.

**Q11. How do you keep the book's net vega and theta honest as expiry approaches?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* dynamic risk, gamma/theta tradeoff.
- *Deep answer:* Near weekly expiry, theta accelerates but so does gamma — the two are inseparable on short options. My theta looks great on Wednesday afternoon, but the same position that pays ₹X/day of theta will lose multiples of that on a 1% move because gamma is huge. So I cap short-gamma exposure into the last day even though it means giving up theta. On vega: front-week options have low vega but high gamma; further-dated have high vega, low gamma. If I want income without the expiry-day gamma bomb, I sometimes move the short leg out a week (less gamma per rupee of theta) or run a calendar. I re-mark net vega/theta at least at open, midday, and pre-close on expiry day, and I have a rule: on expiry day itself I don't add new short gamma — I only reduce.
- *Say it crisply:* Theta and short gamma are the same coin, so into expiry I cap gamma even at the cost of theta, and on expiry day I only reduce short gamma, never add.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Calendar vs same-week short?" Calendar gives me theta with far less expiry-day gamma — safer income, at the cost of some vega exposure.

**Q12. What single risk failure have you seen blow up an options book, and how do you guard against it?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* scar tissue and a concrete safeguard.
- *Deep answer:* The classic: a short-vol book that's "always" profitable, run at high margin utilisation, meets a gap-plus-vol-spike (think a policy shock or a global risk-off open). Three things hit at once — spot moves against the shorts, IV explodes lifting every short option, and SPAN margin expands forcing liquidation. The trader is right that it'll mean-revert, but gets margin-called out at the worst tick and never sees the recovery. Guard: (1) never above ~55% margin utilisation so expansion can't force me out; (2) portfolio 3-sigma shock with a downside vol bump sized under my loss limit; (3) defined risk / wings so there's a structural floor; (4) a hard daily stop that I take without negotiation. The meta-lesson: you don't survive by being right on direction, you survive by never letting one gap plus a margin spike put you in forced-liquidation territory.
- *Say it crisply:* Short-vol books die when a gap, a vol spike, and a margin expansion hit together and force liquidation before the mean-reversion — I guard with low margin utilisation, wings, a stress-sized book, and a hard daily stop.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "So short vol is un-tradeable?" No — it's fine if it's defined, stress-sized, and run with a cash buffer; it only blows up when it's naked, oversized, and fully margined.
