# The Greeks I: Delta & Gamma (Deep)

Delta and gamma are the first two things a desk head will pressure-test, because they reveal whether you think in *positions* or in *risk*. This chapter drills the mechanics, the trader's feel, and the India-specific management nuance.

**Q1. What is delta, really — give me all three interpretations and when you use each.**
- *Interviewer is testing:* whether you hold delta as one number or as three distinct mental tools.
- *Deep answer:* Delta is three things at once, and a seasoned trader switches between them by context. **One, hedge ratio:** ∂Option/∂Spot — how many futures/shares neutralize the position. A 0.40-delta Nifty call on 75 lot size means 30 "spot units" of exposure; to hedge one lot I short ~0.40 of a futures lot equivalent. **Two, probability-ITM proxy:** under risk-neutral pricing, an option's delta is roughly the probability it expires ITM (call delta ≈ N(d1), and N(d2) is the actual RN probability — close for near-ATM, diverges in the wings). So a 15-delta OTM put is the market saying ~15% chance of finishing there — I use this to sanity-check whether a tail hedge is cheap. **Three, equivalent position:** my *net rupee delta* tells me how the book moves per point of Nifty. When someone asks "how long are you?" I answer in delta, not lots. The subtlety: delta itself moves with spot (that's gamma), decays toward 0 or 1 with time for OTM/ITM, and shifts with vol — higher IV pulls every delta toward 0.5 because it makes ITM/OTM less certain. I never treat delta as static; I treat it as a snapshot of a moving surface.
- *Say it crisply:* Delta is my hedge ratio, my rough probability of finishing ITM, and my rupee exposure per point — same number, three lenses.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Which interpretation breaks down first?" The probability proxy — it's N(d2) not N(d1), and skew makes market-implied and true probabilities diverge in the wings.

**Q2. How does an increase in implied volatility change the delta of an OTM option?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* whether you understand delta as a function of the whole surface, not just spot.
- *Deep answer:* Higher IV pushes deltas *toward 0.5*. An OTM call at, say, 0.20 delta rises toward 0.30–0.35 as IV jumps, because higher vol makes it more plausible the option finishes ITM — the market can no longer be confident it stays out. Symmetrically, an ITM option's delta falls from 0.80 toward 0.65. Mechanically, delta = N(d1), and d1 has σ√T in the denominator of the moneyness term and adds a σ√T/2 drift — raising σ compresses the distribution of d1 toward zero, so N(d1) drifts to 0.5. Practical consequence on a real desk: when India VIX spikes into an event, my OTM wings suddenly carry *more* delta than my hedge assumed — I get "delta bleed" from vega. If I bought cheap 10-delta puts as a crash hedge and vol doubles, those puts are now maybe 18-delta and hedging more than I intended; conversely my short OTM calls have grown teeth. I re-mark deltas at the *current* IV, never at yesterday's, especially around RBI policy or budget day.
- *Say it crisply:* Rising IV drags every delta toward 0.5 — OTM options get more delta, ITM options get less — so my hedge has to be re-struck at live vol.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "So vol changes your delta hedge?" Yes — that cross-effect is vanna, and it's why I re-hedge after big IV moves, not just big spot moves.

**Q3. Walk me through delta-hedging a short option position in practice on Nifty.**
- *Interviewer is testing:* real mechanics, hedge cadence, and cost awareness — not the textbook.
- *Deep answer:* Say I've sold 40 lots of the 24,000 ATM Nifty call at ~0.50 delta. My net delta is short 40 × 75 × 0.50 = short 1,500 deltas. To neutralize I buy Nifty futures worth ~1,500 deltas = 20 lots of futures (20 × 75 = 1,500). Now I'm flat delta but **short gamma** — as Nifty rises, the call's delta grows past 0.50, my short-delta deepens, and I must *buy more* futures at higher prices; as it falls I sell futures at lower prices. That "buy high, sell low" re-hedging is the gamma bill I pay for the theta I collect. The art is *cadence*: hedge too often and transaction costs (STT, brokerage, slippage) eat you; too rarely and a gap leaves you exposed. I typically band-hedge — let delta drift within a tolerance (say ±300–400 deltas) and only re-hedge at the band edge, wider bands when realized vol is low and premium collection is the point. Overnight I tighten because Nifty can gap on SGX Nifty/GIFT Nifty and US cues. Near expiry I hedge more nervously because gamma explodes. Every re-hedge is a real cost in India — futures incur STT and exchange charges — so I budget hedging cost against theta collected before I even put the trade on.
- *Say it crisply:* I flatten delta with futures, then re-hedge in bands — buying rallies and selling dips — and that gamma-scalp cost is exactly what my theta has to beat.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "What sets your band width?" Realized vs implied vol and hedging cost — wide bands when I'm being paid a lot of theta relative to how much the market is actually moving.

**Q4. What is rupee (dollar) delta and why do you manage the book in it?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* whether you aggregate risk across strikes/underlyings correctly.
- *Deep answer:* Rupee delta is delta translated into money per unit move of the underlying — net delta × lot size × point value, summed across every strike. A book with +2,000 Nifty deltas makes/loses ₹2,000 per Nifty point (since Nifty point value = ₹1 per index point per unit, ×75 per lot). Why manage in it: raw option counts are meaningless — 100 lots of 5-delta puts and 10 lots of 50-delta puts carry the *same* directional risk, and only rupee delta reveals that. Across a multi-name book (Nifty, Bank Nifty, Reliance, HDFC Bank options) you *can't* net raw deltas because a Bank Nifty point ≠ a Nifty point; you convert everything to rupees to see true net exposure. On my desk the first line of the risk sheet is net rupee delta by underlying and aggregate — that's the "how long am I in money" number that a 4% overnight gap will multiply. It's also how I set limits: a delta limit of, say, ₹5 lakh per Nifty point means a 200-point gap = ₹1 crore swing, which I size against capital.
- *Say it crisply:* Rupee delta is money-per-point; it's the only way to net risk across strikes and underlyings and the number I actually set limits on.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "How do you net Nifty and Bank Nifty delta?" You don't net them directly — convert each to rupee delta, and even then treat them as correlated-but-separate risks, beta-adjusting only for a rough overlay.

**Q5. What's the delta of a bull call spread, and how does it behave into expiry?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* combining greeks across legs and understanding pin behavior.
- *Deep answer:* A bull call spread (long lower strike K1, short higher K2) has net delta = δ(K1) − δ(K2), always positive but *bounded*. Say Nifty 24,000, long 24,000 call (0.50δ), short 24,300 call (0.35δ) → net 0.15 delta. The beauty and the trap is how it evolves: if Nifty rallies to 24,300, the long is ~0.65 and short ~0.50, net still ~0.15 — the spread's delta stays modest because the short leg fights you. Into expiry the profile sharpens brutally: if both legs are OTM, delta collapses toward 0; if spot sits *between* the strikes near expiry, gamma is violent — a small move swings you between the legs. The classic pain is being short the upper strike and having Nifty pin *right at* K2 on weekly expiry: your short call's delta whipsaws between 0 and 1 in the last hour ("pin risk"), and for stock options that means uncertainty over physical delivery. I manage spreads into expiry by squaring off before the last hour rather than gambling on the pin, especially on single stocks where assignment means shares and STT-on-exercise.
- *Say it crisply:* A bull call spread has small, bounded positive delta because the short leg caps it — but if spot pins the short strike into expiry, gamma and pin risk go wild and I close early.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Why close early on stock spreads specifically?" Physical settlement — an ITM short leg means delivering shares plus STT charged on notional, so I never carry pin risk on stocks to the close.

**Q6. Contrast the *feel* of being long gamma versus short gamma.**
- *Interviewer is testing:* lived experience, not the definition.
- *Deep answer:* Long gamma *feels good on big days and bleeds on quiet ones*. When I'm long gamma (long options, e.g. a long straddle), every big move makes my delta move *in my favor* — as spot rises my delta grows longer, as it falls my delta grows shorter, so I'm always positioned toward the move. I actively harvest this by scalping: buy dips, sell rips, locking in the convexity. The cost is theta — I wake up every morning poorer if nothing moved. Short gamma is the mirror and psychologically far worse: I collect theta daily (feels like easy money — that's the seduction), but every large move hurts *more* the further it goes, because my delta moves *against* me. Short gamma is being **short surprise** — I'm implicitly betting the world stays calm, and I get paid a small amount to take unlimited tail pain. The tell of an experienced trader is that they respect short gamma: I size it so a 3-sigma gap is survivable, I know exactly where my delta blows out, and I never let the daily theta drip lull me. The quiet weeks collecting premium are precisely when the position is building the risk that hurts.
- *Say it crisply:* Long gamma bleeds theta but loves big moves; short gamma drips easy premium but is short surprise — it hurts more the further the market goes.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "So when do you want to be short gamma?" When implied vol is rich versus what I expect realized to be, sized small enough that a fat-tail gap doesn't end me.

**Q7. Explain gamma scalping with a concrete Nifty example.**
- *Interviewer is testing:* whether you can actually run a long-gamma book profitably.
- *Deep answer:* Gamma scalping monetizes realized volatility against the theta you paid. Setup: buy a Nifty ATM straddle at 24,000 — long call + long put, net delta ≈ 0, long gamma, paying theta. As Nifty moves I stay delta-neutral by trading futures *against* the drift: Nifty rises to 24,100, my straddle delta is now +30 (say), so I **sell** futures to flatten — locking a gain. Nifty falls back to 24,000, delta goes to ~−15, so I **buy** futures — buying low after selling high. Each round trip banks a small realized-vol profit. Over the day, if the sum of my scalps exceeds the theta decay, I win. The equation every long-gamma trader lives by: **P&L ≈ ½ × Γ × (ΔS)² − θ×Δt**. I profit when realized variance beats implied variance — i.e. when the market moves *more* than the option price assumed. So gamma scalping is really a bet that realized > implied vol. In India I watch two frictions hard: transaction costs on every futures scalp (STT, slippage in fast markets) and the fact that weekly ATM gamma is enormous but so is theta, so the move has to be sharp to pay. I scalp more aggressively intraday around 9:15–10:30 and the close when Nifty actually moves.
- *Say it crisply:* I stay delta-flat on a long straddle and trade futures against every wiggle — selling rallies, buying dips — and if my scalps beat the theta I paid, realized vol beat implied and I win.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "What kills a gamma scalp?" A quiet, grinding market — theta bleeds daily and there's no realized move to harvest, so implied was too rich for what showed up.

**Q8. Where is gamma largest, and what does that mean for weekly expiry risk in India?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* understanding of the gamma surface and India's weekly-expiry regime.
- *Deep answer:* Gamma peaks **at-the-money** and **explodes as expiry approaches** — for a near-expiry ATM option gamma goes hyperbolic, because delta has to transition from ~0 to ~1 (or 0.5 to either extreme) over a vanishing time window, so tiny spot moves flip the option's character. Away from ATM gamma is small; deep ITM/OTM options are nearly linear. The India angle: with weekly Nifty expiries, there is a fresh burst of monstrous ATM gamma every single week, concentrated on expiry day. On Thursday/expiry afternoon, ATM weekly options have gamma so high that market-makers who are short those options must re-hedge frantically — this is the mechanism behind expiry-day "pinning" (spot gets magnetized to the max-open-interest strike) and violent last-hour whipsaws. If I'm short weekly ATM straddles for the theta, expiry day is where a 150-point Nifty swing in an hour can erase a week's premium. Management: I cut short-gamma weekly positions *before* the final session, or I keep them tiny, or I flip to defined-risk spreads (iron flies/condors) so my gamma is capped. Long-gamma expiry-day scalping, conversely, can be extremely profitable *if* the market actually moves.
- *Say it crisply:* Gamma is biggest ATM and blows up into expiry — so India's weekly ATM options carry violent expiry-day gamma, which drives pinning and forces me to either shrink or define-risk my short-gamma books before the close.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "What's pinning?" Dealers short huge expiry gamma hedge by buying dips and selling rips right at the max-OI strike, which mechanically pins spot there into settlement.

**Q9. Explain the gamma-theta tradeoff. Can you ever have one without the other?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* whether you grasp that they're two sides of one coin.
- *Deep answer:* Gamma and theta are inseparable and *opposite in sign* for a given option — you cannot buy convexity without paying rent. Long options = long gamma *and* short theta (positive gamma, negative theta); short options = short gamma *and* long theta. Mathematically they're linked through the BSM PDE: for a delta-hedged position, theta ≈ −½ σ² S² Γ (the "theta pays for gamma" identity). This says the theta you bleed each day is exactly the fair price of the gamma you hold, *assuming* the market realizes vol equal to implied. So the tradeoff is never free — the real question is always **realized vs implied vol**. If I'm long gamma and the market realizes *more* vol than implied, my gamma scalps out-earn my theta bleed and I win; if it realizes *less*, theta wins and I lose. Short gamma is the reverse bet. You can *tilt* the ratio — a calendar spread is long vega but can be near-flat gamma; buying wings vs body reshapes where the gamma sits — but you can't own positive gamma with positive theta simultaneously on the same option. Any structure claiming "free theta" is just short gamma somewhere, i.e. short a tail you haven't priced.
- *Say it crisply:* Gamma and theta are the same trade with opposite signs — theta is the rent on gamma, and whether the rent is worth it comes down entirely to realized versus implied vol.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "So a positive-theta strategy is always...?" Short gamma somewhere — you're being paid to be short a move, and the only question is whether that move shows up.

**Q10. You're short gamma into an RBI policy announcement. Walk me through your risk.**
- *Interviewer is testing:* event risk management and scar tissue.
- *Deep answer:* Short gamma into a scheduled event is short surprise at the worst possible moment — the event is *designed* to produce a jump, and jumps are exactly what short gamma can't hedge. Concretely, say I'm short Bank Nifty weekly straddles collecting fat premium because IV is elevated pre-policy. Two dangers: (1) the **gap** — RBI surprises on rates/stance, Bank Nifty jumps 800 points on the announcement, and my short gamma means my delta blows out violently against me faster than I can hedge; there's no re-hedging a discontinuous move. (2) The **vol crush** cuts the other way and is my *friend* here — post-announcement IV collapses, helping my short vega. So the trade is really "I'm betting the realized move is smaller than the priced-in move, and I'm getting paid the IV premium for that." Management: I size so the *worst historical policy-day move* (Bank Nifty has done 3–5% intraday on shock policies) is survivable within my loss limit; I often convert to a defined-risk iron fly so my gamma/tail is capped by long wings; I pre-place the hedge or reduce before the print; and I never hold naked short options through an event in size. The premium looks like free money right up until the one meeting it isn't — that's the trade that teaches everyone this lesson once.
- *Say it crisply:* Short gamma into RBI is short the jump the event is built to create — I cap the tail with long wings, size to survive a 4–5% Bank Nifty shock, and take the IV-crush profit if the move underdelivers.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Why not just be long the straddle then?" Because IV is usually pumped pre-event — you often pay so much for the straddle that even a decent move doesn't cover it; the edge is in whether priced-in over/under-states realized.

**Q11. How does gamma interact with a stock option's physical settlement in India?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* India-specific expiry mechanics that catch out theory-only candidates.
- *Deep answer:* This is a pure-India trap that index-only traders miss. Single-stock options in India are **physically settled** — if a stock option is ITM at expiry, it's exercised into actual shares: short a call, you deliver stock; short a put, you take delivery and pay full cash. Now layer gamma: on expiry day an ATM stock option has explosive gamma, and if the stock oscillates around the strike in the final hour, your option flickers between ITM and OTM — meaning you don't *know* until settlement whether you'll be assigned a large physical position. Getting assigned means suddenly needing the full delivery margin / cash for lakhs of rupees of shares overnight, plus **STT charged on the entire notional** of the exercised contract (the ITM-exercise STT trap), which dwarfs the premium. I've seen traders left holding a delivery obligation they never intended because they held a near-ATM short stock option to the close and it pinned ITM. Management is strict: I square off single-stock options *before* the last hour of expiry — never carry ATM/near-ATM stock options into settlement — and if I want expiry exposure I use index options which are cash-settled and carry no delivery or notional-STT risk. The gamma that's just P&L noise on Nifty becomes a physical-delivery and tax event on stocks.
- *Say it crisply:* On expiry day stock-option gamma is a delivery landmine — pin near the strike and you're physically assigned shares plus STT on full notional, so I close single-stock options before the close and keep expiry-day gamma in cash-settled index options.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "What's the STT trap exactly?" STT on *exercised* ITM options is levied on intrinsic/settlement notional, not premium — often many times the premium — so letting a deep-ITM option auto-exercise instead of squaring off is a costly mistake.
