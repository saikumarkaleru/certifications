# Income, Hedging, Synthetics & Relative-Value

This chapter covers the working trades of a real book: harvesting premium for income, hedging a portfolio properly, the synthetic relationships that keep pricing honest, and the relative-value structures (calendars, dispersion) a senior trader reaches for. The theme throughout: WHEN does each actually earn its keep, and where does it bite.

**Q1. Walk me through premium-selling for income — theta harvesting — and its real risk.**
- *Interviewer is testing:* whether you respect that income selling is short a fat tail, not free carry.
- *Deep answer:* Income selling means systematically selling options (strangles, condors, put spreads) to collect theta and the variance risk premium in a range-bound, high-IV regime. The mechanics: I'm short vega and short gamma, long theta — every calm day the premium decays into my pocket. On Nifty/Bank Nifty weeklies the theta is steep in the last few days, so a lot of income traders live in the front week selling ~16-delta strangles or condors. The edge is real (IV > realized on average). The REAL risk is the shape of the P&L: you win small and often, then a gap or vol spike hands you a loss that dwarfs many weeks of collected premium — the classic "picking up nickels in front of a steamroller." Being short gamma means losses ACCELERATE as the market moves against you, exactly when you can least afford it. So mature income selling is all about the tail: defined-risk (condors, not naked strangles), position sizing as a small fraction of capital, hard loss thresholds (cut at 1.5–2× credit), avoiding fat events, and NOT scaling up after a good run (that's how the eventual gap ends careers). The income is the byproduct of disciplined tail management, not the point.
- *Say it crisply:* Theta harvesting sells rich IV in a calm range to collect decay — a real edge, but you're short a fat tail, so it's won or lost on sizing, defined risk, and cutting before a gap, not on the premium collected.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "How do you size income trades?" As a small fraction of capital with defined risk, so the worst realistic gap is a survivable loss — never sized on the median outcome.

**Q2. How do you hedge an equity portfolio with index options — protective puts vs collars?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* practical portfolio hedging and cost-awareness.
- *Deep answer:* Simplest is a protective put: buy OTM Nifty puts against a long portfolio so downside past the strike is capped — clean insurance, but it costs premium that bleeds (theta) and drags returns in an up/flat market, and IV is usually rich so you overpay. To cut the cost I sell an OTM call against it — a COLLAR: the call premium finances the put, so protection becomes cheap or zero-cost, but I cap upside at the call strike. Worked: portfolio ~₹50L tracking Nifty at 24,000; buy the 23,000 put (~5% OTM) for downside, sell the 25,000 call to fund it — I'm protected below 23,000, give up gains above 25,000, near-zero net cost. Sizing the hedge is via BETA and notional: number of lots ≈ (portfolio value × beta) / (Nifty × lot size), so the index puts offset the portfolio's index exposure. When each earns its keep: buy outright puts when I specifically fear a crash and want full upside; use a collar when I want cheap protection and am willing to cap upside (e.g., locking in gains before an event). The discipline is hedging the RIGHT notional (beta-adjusted) and accepting that perfect hedges cost money — a hedge that never costs anything isn't hedging much.
- *Say it crisply:* Protective puts = clean insurance that bleeds premium; collars sell an upside call to fund the put for near-zero cost but cap gains. I size lots by beta-adjusted notional and pick based on whether I'll pay for full upside or cap it to cheapen the hedge.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Zero-cost collar — catch?" You've sold your upside above the call strike; the protection is 'free' only because you financed it by giving away gains.

**Q3. What's a put-spread collar and why use it over a plain collar?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* nuanced, cost-efficient hedging beyond the textbook.
- *Deep answer:* A plain protective put is expensive; a plain collar caps upside hard. A put-spread collar refines both: instead of buying a single put, you buy a put SPREAD (buy the near put, sell a lower put) for cheaper downside protection over a defined band, and finance it by selling an upside call. The logic: full crash protection via a lone deep put is costly and, in a moderate pullback, you often don't need protection all the way to zero — a put spread covering, say, a 5–12% drop is far cheaper, and the premium saved means you sell a FURTHER-OUT call, giving up less upside. Worked: Nifty 24,000 — buy 23,000 put, sell 21,000 put (protection over the 4–12% down band), sell 25,500 call to finance. Cheaper than a full collar and preserves more upside. The tradeoff/catch: below the lower put strike (21,000) you're UNPROTECTED again — a true crash punches through your put spread and you're naked below it. So a put-spread collar is the right tool when I fear a MODERATE correction and want cost-efficiency, but a plain protective put or wider spread is better if I genuinely fear a tail crash. It's the classic cost-vs-coverage refinement a real hedger makes.
- *Say it crisply:* A put-spread collar buys a put spread for cheaper banded downside and funds it with an upside call — better cost and more retained upside than a plain collar, but you're unprotected below the lower put in a true crash.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "So when NOT to use it?" When you fear a genuine tail crash — the lower short put re-exposes you, so a full protective put or a wider spread is safer there.

**Q4. Explain put-call parity and how synthetics fall out of it.**
- *Interviewer is testing:* the arbitrage backbone that makes synthetics and conversions possible.
- *Deep answer:* Put-call parity says: Call − Put = Forward − Strike (present-valued), i.e., a long call plus a short put at the same strike/expiry equals a synthetic long forward/underlying. Rearranging gives every synthetic: synthetic long stock = long call + short put; synthetic long call = long stock + long put; synthetic long put = short stock + long call; and so on. This isn't academic — it's the law that keeps calls, puts, and the underlying/future consistently priced, because any deviation is arbitrageable. Practically, synthetics let me replicate a position when the direct instrument is illiquid, expensive, or margin-inefficient — e.g., create a synthetic long via options if the future is dislocated, or synthetically flip a position without unwinding legs. It's also how I sanity-check pricing: if the call, put and future don't line up per parity (adjusting for dividends/interest/carry), there's either an arb or I'm mis-reading something. On Indian index options parity holds tightly against the future; small deviations reflect carry, and any real gap gets arbed by desks in seconds.
- *Say it crisply:* Put-call parity — long call plus short put equals synthetic long underlying — is the law that keeps calls, puts and the future consistent, and every synthetic and conversion is just a rearrangement of it.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Build a synthetic long put." Short the underlying/future and buy a call at that strike — same payoff as owning the put.

**Q5. What are conversions and reversals, and when do they matter?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* arbitrage literacy and how desks lock risk-free edges.
- *Deep answer:* A conversion locks a risk-free position when options are mispriced RICH: buy the underlying, buy a put, sell a call at the same strike/expiry — the long put + short call is a synthetic SHORT that offsets the long underlying, so you're market-neutral and pocket any pricing discrepancy versus parity. A reversal is the mirror for when options are CHEAP: short the underlying, sell a put, buy a call — synthetic long offsetting the short. Both are pure put-call-parity arbitrages: they extract the gap when Call − Put deviates from Forward − Strike beyond carry/costs. In liquid markets these edges are tiny and fleeting — market-maker/desk territory, gone in seconds — so as a directional trader I rarely put them on for profit. Where they matter to me: (1) they're the conceptual proof that synthetics are equivalent, which lets me leg into or out of positions via whichever instrument is cheapest/most liquid; (2) recognizing a conversion/reversal helps me understand pin risk and early-exercise/settlement quirks at expiry; (3) on Indian markets, dividend/carry and the ITM-exercise-STT trap can create apparent parity gaps that AREN'T free — a naive "arb" can get eaten by STT on exercise, so I check settlement mechanics before assuming an edge is real.
- *Say it crisply:* Conversion (long underlying + long put + short call) and reversal (the mirror) are pure parity arbitrages that lock the gap when options are mispriced — mostly market-maker edges, but knowing them lets me leg positions via the cheapest instrument and spot fake 'arbs' that STT would eat.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Why might a parity 'arb' in India not be free?" STT on ITM exercise and carry/dividend effects can consume the apparent gap — the mispricing must clear real settlement costs to be genuine.

**Q6. How do you beta-hedge a multi-stock book with index options?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* portfolio-level hedging math and its limits.
- *Deep answer:* A portfolio of individual stocks has a net market exposure = Σ(position value × stock beta). To hedge that systematic exposure with index options (or futures), I convert to index notional: hedge notional = portfolio value × portfolio beta, then lots = hedge notional / (index level × lot size). I then buy Nifty puts (or a put spread/collar) sized to that notional so a market-wide drop is offset. Worked: ₹1cr book, portfolio beta 1.2 → effective market exposure ₹1.2cr; at Nifty 24,000 with lot size, that's a specific number of put lots to cover the down move. Why index options and not stock options: cheaper, more liquid, cash-settled (no physical-delivery trap), and I usually want to hedge MARKET risk, not each name. The critical limitation: index hedging only neutralizes SYSTEMATIC (beta) risk — it does nothing for idiosyncratic/single-stock risk (a fraud, a bad result in one name) or for basis risk if my portfolio drifts from the index. Beta is also unstable and rises in a crash (correlations → 1), so the hedge can under- or over-cover exactly when it matters. So I treat the beta hedge as a systematic-risk overlay, re-estimate beta periodically, and keep separate single-name hedges for concentrated positions.
- *Say it crisply:* Size index puts to the portfolio's beta-adjusted notional — value × beta / (index × lot) — to offset market risk; it neutralizes systematic beta only, leaving single-name and basis risk, and beta itself rises in a crash.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "What does it NOT hedge?" Idiosyncratic single-stock risk and basis risk — and beta instability means the coverage drifts, worst just when correlations spike.

**Q7. A calendar spread as a term-structure trade — when does it earn its keep?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* using calendars as a vol/term-structure view, not a generic neutral trade.
- *Deep answer:* A calendar (sell front, buy back, same strike) is really a bet on TWO things: that the underlying stays near the strike (so the front decays faster than the back), and on the vol term structure (you're net long vega, so you want back-month IV to hold or rise relative to front). It earns its keep specifically when: (1) the curve is in CONTANGO — front vol cheap, back richer — so you're selling relatively expensive fast-decaying front theta; (2) the underlying is expected to pin near the strike; and (3) ideally back-month IV is set to firm up. Worked: Nifty 24,000, sell the current-week 24,000 straddle/call, buy the monthly 24,000, net debit — if Nifty hovers, the weekly bleeds to zero and I keep the richer monthly. Where it bites: a big directional move away from the strike (spread value collapses as both legs move together) and, crucially, BACKWARDATION — if front vol is already above back (a stress/event regime), you're selling the cheap leg and the trade is upside-down, plus an event can crush back-month IV. So the calendar is my go-to when I have an explicit "front vol overpriced vs back, market rangebound" view, and something I avoid in backwardation or ahead of an event that lives in the front week.
- *Say it crisply:* A calendar is a long-vega, term-structure trade that wants contango and the underlying pinning the strike — it earns its keep selling rich front vol vs cheaper back, and it inverts on you in backwardation or on a big directional move.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Front week has an event — calendar good or bad?" Bad — event vol inflates the front you're short and can crush the back you're long; calendars want a calm front and contango.

**Q8. Explain dispersion trading at a high level. What's the actual bet?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* whether you understand correlation as a tradeable factor — senior-desk concept.
- *Deep answer:* Dispersion trades the relationship between INDEX vol and the vol of its CONSTITUENTS. Index variance ≈ average single-stock variance scaled by average correlation — so index vol is low relative to the average stock vol when correlations are low, and high when stocks move together. The classic dispersion trade: SELL index vol (short index straddles/variance) and BUY single-stock vol (long straddles on constituents) — you're short correlation. It profits when stocks move a lot INDIVIDUALLY but the index stays calmer than implied (low realized correlation) — the single-name longs pay while the index short decays. The opposite (long index vol, short single-stock vol) is long correlation, a bet that a macro shock drives everything together. The economic driver is the correlation risk premium: index puts are structurally bid (hedgers), so index IV embeds a correlation premium that dispersion harvests. In India this is hard to run cleanly — single-stock option liquidity is thin outside the top names, physical settlement complicates the constituent legs, and margin is heavy — so it's more a large-desk/quant strategy than a retail one, but understanding it shows you grasp that index vol is a correlation bet.
- *Say it crisply:* Dispersion sells index vol and buys constituent vol — a short-correlation bet that pays when stocks move individually but the index stays calm, harvesting the correlation risk premium embedded in bid index puts.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Why is it hard in India?" Thin single-stock option liquidity, physical settlement of stock options, and heavy margin make the constituent legs impractical outside the largest names — it's a big-desk trade here.

**Q9. When does a ratio spread earn its keep in an income/hedging context?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* using ratios purposefully, with tail-awareness, in a real book.
- *Deep answer:* In an income context a call (or put) ratio spread — buy 1, sell 2 further out — lets me lean directional AND collect extra premium: e.g., mildly bullish, buy 1 ATM call, sell 2 OTM calls for a net credit, max profit at the short strike, and if it stays below there I keep the credit. It "earns its keep" when I expect a move TO a level but not through it, and want the short-vol carry. In a HEDGING context, the put ratio backspread (sell 1 near put, buy 2 further puts) is the star — often zero/low cost, it's cheap convex crash protection that explodes if the index gaps down and skew steepens, while bleeding little if nothing happens. So I use backspreads to hedge tail risk cheaply and ratio (short-heavy) spreads to add income to a directional-with-a-ceiling view. The non-negotiable discipline: the short-heavy ratio has a NAKED tail beyond the short strikes — if the move overshoots, losses run, so I size small, define an adjustment/exit if the short strike is tested, and never treat the collected credit as free. The backspread's risk is the opposite — it bleeds the small net debit/carry if the market goes quiet.
- *Say it crisply:* Short-heavy ratio spreads add income to a move-to-a-level view but carry a naked tail past the short strikes; put backspreads are cheap convex crash hedges — I use each for its purpose and size the ratio's tail carefully.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Ratio spread tested — what do you do?" Roll or close before the naked short strike is breached, or buy a wing to cap it; never let the uncovered tail run in size.

**Q10. Bottom line — how do you decide which of these earns its keep in a given month?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* the synthesizing judgment that ties income, hedging and RV to regime.
- *Deep answer:* I map each tool to the regime and the book's needs. Income/premium-selling (condors, put spreads, strangles) earns its keep in a CALM, high-IV-percentile, CONTANGO month with no fat events — that's when the VRP pays and the tail is quiet — sized small and defined-risk. Hedging (protective puts, collars, put-spread collars, beta-hedge) earns its keep when I'm carrying long exposure into uncertainty or elevated crash risk, or after a strong run when I want to lock gains — the cost is justified by the tail I'm insuring. Calendars earn their keep when the term structure specifically favors them (contango, rangebound, back-vol firming). Backspreads/ratios earn their keep as cheap convex tail hedges or move-to-a-level income respectively. Synthetics/conversions matter for execution efficiency and pricing checks more than standalone profit. Dispersion is a large-desk correlation bet, not a monthly staple here. The unifying logic: I read regime first (VIX level, term structure, event calendar, IV percentile), decide whether I'm net short vol (harvest income), net long/convex (hedge, own gamma), or flat, and then reach for the structure that expresses that at the best cost — and if nothing fits, the best trade is no trade.
- *Say it crisply:* Regime decides: calm/contango/high-IV with no events → harvest income defined-risk; elevated crash risk or locking gains → hedge with puts/collars; explicit term-structure view → calendars; cheap tail → backspreads. Read regime first, pick the cheapest structure that expresses it, and pass when nothing fits.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "One signal to set posture?" The term structure plus VIX — contango and low-to-moderate VIX says harvest income; backwardation or a VIX spike says hedge, own convexity, or stand aside.
