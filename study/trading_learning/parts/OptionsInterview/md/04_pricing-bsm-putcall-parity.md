# Pricing: BSM, Binomial & Put-Call Parity

Pricing questions separate people who *use* models from people who *believe* them. A senior interviewer wants to hear that you know where Black-Scholes breaks, why you still use it, and how parity gives you a model-free check on the market. This chapter drills all three.

**Q1. State the assumptions of Black-Scholes and tell me which one breaks first in real markets.**
- *Interviewer is testing:* whether you know the model's foundations *and* its failures.
- *Deep answer:* BSM assumes: (1) the underlying follows **geometric Brownian motion** with **constant volatility**; (2) **no jumps** — prices move continuously; (3) **lognormal** returns; (4) frictionless markets — no transaction costs, continuous costless hedging, unlimited borrowing/lending at a single risk-free rate; (5) constant risk-free rate; (6) no dividends (in the base form); (7) European exercise. The one that breaks *first and hardest* is **constant volatility** — the market's own option prices prove it false, because if vol were constant, every strike would trade at the same IV. Instead we see a **volatility smile/skew**: OTM Nifty puts trade at higher IV than ATM, which is the market pricing in fat left tails and jumps that lognormal-constant-vol can't produce. The **no-jumps** assumption fails right alongside it — gaps on RBI policy, budget, global shocks are jumps, and jumps are un-hedgeable, which is precisely why short-gamma books blow up. **Continuous costless hedging** also fails immediately in India, where every re-hedge pays STT and slippage. So BSM's world is a frictionless, gap-free, single-vol fantasy — the smile is the market's correction sheet pinned onto the model.
- *Say it crisply:* BSM assumes constant vol, no jumps, lognormal returns and costless continuous hedging — and constant vol breaks first, which is exactly why the market shows a volatility skew.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "How does the market patch constant vol?" It doesn't fix the model — it quotes a different IV per strike/expiry (the surface), effectively using BSM as a translation device, not a truth.

**Q2. If BSM is so wrong, why does everyone still use it?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* mature understanding of models as tools, not truth.
- *Deep answer:* Because BSM isn't used as a *truth machine* — it's used as a **common language and a translation device**. Its real value: it's a one-to-one, monotonic map between price and *implied volatility*, so instead of quoting a messy rupee price we quote IV, which is comparable across strikes, expiries and underlyings. That's how a "vol surface" even exists. It also gives us the **greeks** — a consistent, tradeable decomposition of risk (delta, gamma, vega, theta) that lets me hedge and aggregate a book, even if the exact numbers are approximations. And it's *fast and closed-form*, so it's computationally trivial for real-time risk on thousands of contracts. The trick experienced desks use is to *feed the market's own skew back in*: rather than trusting one constant vol, we quote each strike at its own IV and read greeks off that surface — the model's flaws are absorbed by letting IV vary. So BSM survives not because it's right but because it's a universal, invertible, differentiable framework that everyone agrees on. I'd tell the interviewer: I trust BSM's *structure* (greeks, parity relationships) and distrust its *constant-vol assumption*, which I override with the observed surface.
- *Say it crisply:* We use BSM not as truth but as a translation layer — it turns prices into comparable implied vols and gives consistent greeks, and we patch its constant-vol flaw by quoting each strike at its own IV.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "So what do you actually trust from BSM?" The relationships and greeks structure, and parity — not the assumption that one vol prices the whole chain.

**Q3. What is BSM genuinely good for, despite its flaws?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* balance — you criticized it, now show you value it.
- *Deep answer:* Three concrete things. **One, greeks for hedging and aggregation** — BSM gives me delta to hedge, gamma to gauge stability, vega to size vol risk, theta to know my carry; even approximate, they let me manage a thousand-line book coherently. **Two, the implied-vol lens** — inverting BSM turns every option price into an IV, which is the only way to compare a 24,000 weekly call with a 25,000 monthly put or with a Bank Nifty option; the entire vol-trading business runs on this. **Three, relative-value and parity checks** — BSM and its parity relationships tell me what's *internally consistent*, so I can spot when a call, put and future don't line up (an arb) or when one strike's IV is out of line with its neighbors (skew mispricing). Where I *don't* lean on it: pricing deep tails or event jumps precisely, or assuming my delta-hedge is costless and continuous. It's the scaffolding of the whole options market — imperfect load-bearing beams that everyone has agreed to build on. A trader who says "BSM is useless" is as naive as one who believes it literally.
- *Say it crisply:* BSM is invaluable for the greeks that let me hedge and aggregate a book, for the implied-vol lens that makes options comparable, and for the parity/consistency checks that flag mispricing — just not for pricing tails or jumps exactly.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Best single use?" The IV translation — without it there's no vol surface, no skew trading, no way to compare options at all.

**Q4. When would you reach for a binomial model instead of BSM?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* knowing the right tool — early exercise and discrete events.
- *Deep answer:* Binomial (Cox-Ross-Rubinstein) discretizes the underlying into an up/down lattice and prices by working *backwards* from expiry, checking value at each node. Its edge over closed-form BSM is that it can handle **early exercise** and **discrete events** node-by-node. The main reasons to use it: (1) **American options** — at every node you compare "hold" vs "exercise now" and take the max, which BSM's closed form can't do; this matters for single-stock American-style options and anywhere early exercise has value. (2) **Discrete dividends** — you can drop the stock price by the dividend at the specific ex-date node, which is more honest than BSM's continuous-yield fudge, and it's exactly when a large discrete dividend makes early exercise of an American call rational. (3) **Path-dependent tweaks** and intuition-building — the lattice makes the hedging/replication argument visible. Downside: it's slower and less precise than closed-form for vanilla Europeans, so for a large real-time index book I use BSM/analytics; I reach for binomial (or a tree/PDE/Monte Carlo) specifically when early exercise or a discrete cash event makes the closed form wrong. In the Indian *index* context most of what I trade is European and cash-settled, so binomial is more relevant for single-stock American-style and dividend-sensitive names.
- *Say it crisply:* I reach for a binomial tree when early exercise or a discrete dividend matters — American options and dividend-paying names — because a backward lattice can check exercise at every node, which BSM's closed form can't.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "When is early exercise of an American call ever optimal?" Essentially only just before a large dividend (to capture it), since otherwise you'd rather sell the option and keep the time value.

**Q5. Are Indian index options American or European, and why does it matter for pricing?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* product knowledge feeding into model choice.
- *Deep answer:* Indian **index options (Nifty, Bank Nifty) are European** — exercisable only at expiry — and **cash-settled**. **Single-stock options are European in style but physically settled** (Indian stock options are European exercise, settled by delivery if ITM at expiry — verify current NSE spec). Why it matters: European style means **no early-exercise premium**, so BSM's closed form is directly applicable to the index chain — I don't need a lattice to check early exercise, which keeps real-time risk fast and clean. It also means put-call parity holds in its *exact* European form (C − P = S − K·e^(−rT)), giving me a clean arbitrage/consistency check on the index chain. The physical-settlement wrinkle on single stocks doesn't change the *exercise style* but changes the *expiry-day economics* — assignment into shares, delivery margins, and STT on notional — which is a settlement issue, not a pricing-model issue. So the punchline: because our index options are European and cash-settled, BSM and exact parity apply cleanly; the complications in India are on the *settlement and tax* side (physical delivery, STT-on-exercise), not on the exercise-style side that would force a binomial model.
- *Say it crisply:* Indian index options are European and cash-settled, so BSM and exact put-call parity apply directly with no early-exercise adjustment — the Indian complications are in physical settlement and STT, not in exercise style.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "So do you ever need a binomial model for Nifty options?" Not for exercise-style reasons — they're European; you'd only use trees/PDE for exotic or dividend-path reasons, which don't arise on the cash index.

**Q6. State put-call parity exactly and explain what it really says.**
- *Interviewer is testing:* precision and conceptual grasp of the most important no-arb relation.
- *Deep answer:* For European options on a non-dividend underlying: **C − P = S − K·e^(−rT)**, where C and P are call and put prices at the same strike K and expiry T, S is spot, r the risk-free rate. With dividends (yield q) it's C − P = S·e^(−qT) − K·e^(−rT); in futures terms, C − P = (F − K)·e^(−rT) where F is the forward/futures price. What it *really says*: a **long call + short put at the same strike is a synthetic forward** — it has exactly the payoff of being long the underlying at K, so it must cost the same as the actual forward, discounted. It's a **model-free** relationship — it doesn't assume BSM, lognormality, or any vol; it's pure no-arbitrage from replication, so it holds regardless of what you think vol is. That's why it's the bedrock check: if the observed C − P doesn't equal S − K·e^(−rT), there's a risk-free arbitrage (a conversion/reversal). It also pins down that a call and put at the same strike share the *same implied vol* — if they didn't, parity would be violated. So parity is the skeleton the whole chain hangs on, independent of any pricing model.
- *Say it crisply:* C − P = S − K·e^(−rT): a call minus a put at the same strike is a synthetic forward, and because it's model-free no-arbitrage, any deviation is a pure arbitrage and it forces calls and puts at a strike to share one implied vol.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Does it need BSM?" No — it's pure replication/no-arbitrage, which is exactly why it's a check *on* models rather than a product of one.

**Q7. Build me every synthetic position from the parity relationship.**
- *Interviewer is testing:* fluency in synthetics — the trader's algebra.
- *Deep answer:* Rearranging C − P = S − K·e^(−rT) gives the full synthetic toolkit (same strike/expiry throughout): **Synthetic long stock/forward = long call + short put** (C − P). **Synthetic short stock = short call + long put.** **Synthetic long call = long stock + long put** (a protective put *is* a synthetic call). **Synthetic long put = short stock + long call.** **Synthetic short put = long stock + short call** (a covered call *is* a synthetic short put — which surprises people who think covered calls are 'safe' and short puts are 'risky'; they're the same payoff). Why this matters on a desk: (1) it lets me construct or unwind exposure through whichever leg is cheapest/most liquid — if the future is rich, I build synthetic long stock via options; (2) it's the basis of **conversion/reversal arbitrage** — if the synthetic and the real underlying/future diverge, I lock the difference risk-free; (3) it clarifies risk — realizing that a covered call and a naked short put are identical payoffs stops people mis-framing their risk. In India, since index futures and options are both liquid, I watch the synthetic-vs-future basis for parity arbs, netting out carry and STT.
- *Say it crisply:* From parity: call minus put is synthetic long stock, stock plus put is a synthetic call, and a covered call equals a short put — so I can build any exposure through the cheapest legs and arb the synthetic against the real underlying.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Why does covered-call = short-put surprise people?" Because they price the two very differently in their heads, but parity proves the payoffs are identical — same risk, different framing.

**Q8. Walk me through a concrete put-call parity arbitrage with numbers.**
- *Interviewer is testing:* can you actually spot and execute a parity arb, net of costs.
- *Deep answer:* Take Nifty spot S = 24,000, strike K = 24,000, T ≈ 30 days (0.082 yr), r = 7%. Fair forward F = S·e^(rT) = 24,000 × e^(0.07×0.082) ≈ 24,000 × 1.00575 ≈ 24,138. Parity says C − P should ≈ (F − K)·e^(−rT) = S − K·e^(−rT) = 24,000 − 24,000×e^(−0.07×0.082) = 24,000 − 23,862 ≈ **138**. So the call should be ~₹138 richer than the put. Suppose the market shows C = ₹300, P = ₹100 → C − P = 200, which is *more* than the fair 138. The call is overpriced relative to the put → **execute a conversion**: sell the call (+300), buy the put (−100), buy the underlying/future (−24,000-equivalent). This synthetic-short (short call + long put = short forward at 24,000) plus long actual future locks in the 200 − 138 = **₹62 mispricing per unit**, risk-free, held to expiry. The discipline: I only trade it if ₹62 comfortably exceeds **all** frictions — STT (and the ITM-exercise STT if it settles ITM), brokerage, exchange fees, GST, the bid-ask on three legs, and the financing/margin cost — which in practice eats most retail-visible parity gaps. Real parity arbs in liquid Nifty are tiny and fleeting, captured by low-cost desks; the value in the interview is proving I know the exact fair basis and how carry and STT set the true threshold.
- *Say it crisply:* Fair C − P here is ~₹138; if the market shows 200 the call's rich, so I sell call, buy put, buy the future — a conversion locking ₹62 risk-free — but only if that beats STT, fees and carry, which usually closes the gap.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "What kills most parity arbs in India?" Transaction costs — STT (especially on ITM exercise), three-legged bid-ask, and financing — so the visible gap has to clear all of that before it's real.

**Q9. What is a box spread and how does it relate to parity and interest rates?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* advanced structure — synthetic lending/borrowing.
- *Deep answer:* A box spread combines a **bull call spread** (long call K1, short call K2) with a **bear put spread** (long put K2, short put K1) at the same two strikes — which, by parity, is a **synthetic loan**: its payoff at expiry is *fixed* at (K2 − K1) regardless of where the underlying lands. Because the payoff is certain, the box's price today must equal the present value of (K2 − K1), i.e. (K2 − K1)·e^(−rT). So a box is effectively **lending or borrowing money at the rate implied by option prices** — buy the box (pay less than K2−K1 today, receive K2−K1 at expiry) and you've *lent* money; sell it and you've *borrowed*. Traders use it to (1) extract the market-implied financing rate and compare it to the actual repo/funding rate — a relative-value/rate arb; (2) as synthetic funding. The famous caution: boxes are only "risk-free" for *European, cash-settled* options — with American exercise or physical settlement, early assignment can break the fixed payoff (this is what blew up retail "risk-free box" traders on US brokers). In India, since index options are European and cash-settled, the box is clean on the index; on physically-settled stocks it carries assignment/delivery risk. And, as always, STT and fees set whether the implied rate edge is actually capturable.
- *Say it crisply:* A box is a bull call spread plus a bear put spread that locks a fixed K2−K1 payoff, so it's synthetic lending at the option-implied rate — clean on European cash-settled index options, but not "risk-free" where physical settlement or early assignment can break the payoff.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Why did boxes blow people up abroad?" American-style short legs got assigned early, breaking the guaranteed payoff — the 'risk-free' label only holds for European cash-settled options like our index.

**Q10. How do you use put-call parity to spot mispricing or back out an implied borrow/funding rate?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* parity as a practical relative-value radar, not just theory.
- *Deep answer:* Parity is my model-free consistency scanner. Rearranging C − P = S − K·e^(−rT), I can solve for whichever input the market is implying. **Implied funding/rate:** given observed C, P, S and K, I back out the r that makes parity hold — that's the market's implied financing rate, which I compare to actual repo/MIBOR; a divergence flags either a rate arb or a supply/demand distortion. **Implied forward / basis:** C − P directly gives (F − K)·e^(−rT), so it pins the option-implied forward, which I cross-check against the actual futures price — if the option-implied forward and the future disagree beyond costs, that's a synthetic-vs-future arb. **Implied borrow/dividend:** for single stocks, deviations in parity reveal the market-implied dividend or **stock borrow cost** — hard-to-borrow names show up as a persistent parity skew because shorting the stock leg is expensive, so puts get bid relative to calls. That last one is genuinely useful: parity turns the options market into a *sensor* for borrow costs and dividend expectations I can't see directly. Operationally I don't expect free arbs in liquid Nifty — I use parity to sanity-check my marks, detect data errors, and read the implied carry, rather than to print money.
- *Say it crisply:* I invert parity to back out the market-implied forward, funding rate, dividend, or stock-borrow cost from C, P and S — so parity is less a money-printer in liquid names and more a sensor for carry, borrow and mispriced marks.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "What does a persistent parity skew on a single stock tell you?" Usually an expensive/hard-to-borrow short — puts richen relative to calls because the stock leg of the arb is costly to short, so parity reads out the borrow cost.
