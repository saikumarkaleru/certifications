# Strategy Selection & Construction

Anyone can name the payoff diagrams. What a desk head wants is the reasoning that maps a VIEW — direction × volatility × time — onto the right structure, with strikes and expiry chosen deliberately. This chapter is about that translation, with worked construction on Nifty/Bank Nifty.

**Q1. Walk me through how you pick a structure from a view. What's your framework?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* whether you have a repeatable view-to-structure map, not a favorite trade you force everywhere.
- *Deep answer:* I decompose every view into three axes and let them pick the structure. (1) Direction: bullish, bearish, neutral, or "no strong view." (2) Volatility: do I think IV is rich (sell premium) or cheap (buy premium), and will realized exceed or lag implied. (3) Time/path: sharp move soon, slow grind, or range-bound into an event. Then: bullish + rich IV → sell a put spread (credit); bullish + cheap IV → buy a call spread (debit); neutral + rich IV, range expected → iron condor or short strangle; neutral + cheap IV, big move expected → long straddle/strangle; pin-to-a-level → butterfly; view on TIMING/term structure → calendar/diagonal. The vol axis decides debit vs credit — I don't buy premium when IV percentile is high, and I don't sell premium into a pending event or backwardation. On Nifty specifically the weekly theta and the STT/cost structure push me toward defined-risk credit spreads over naked options for retail-size books. The discipline: the structure must express ALL three axes, and if any axis is "I don't know," I size down or don't trade.
- *Say it crisply:* I split the view into direction, vol (rich vs cheap), and time/path — direction picks calls vs puts, the vol axis picks debit vs credit, and the path picks the exact structure.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Bullish but IV is high — call spread or put spread?" Sell the put spread; high IV means I want to be a net premium seller, collecting the rich vol rather than paying it in a debit call spread.

**Q2. Debit vertical vs credit vertical — same directional view, when do you use which?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* that you choose based on IV and probability, not habit.
- *Deep answer:* They express the same direction but opposite vol posture. A bull call spread (debit) buys the lower call, sells the higher — you PAY, you're modestly long vega, max profit if it rallies to the short strike, defined risk = debit. A bull put spread (credit) sells the higher put, buys the lower — you RECEIVE, short vega, max profit if it just stays above the short put, defined risk = width − credit. Decision rule: sell the credit spread when IV is rich (high IV percentile) because you're a net vol seller and you win even on a flat-to-up tape via theta; buy the debit spread when IV is cheap and you expect a directional pop, because you don't want to pay rich vol. Probability nuance: the credit put spread usually has higher POP (you win if it's flat or up), the debit call spread has lower POP but bigger payoff-per-rupee-risk if the move comes. Example, Nifty at 24,000, bullish: rich IV → sell 23,800/23,600 put spread for say ₹60 credit, risk ₹140 (×lot); cheap IV → buy 24,000/24,200 call spread for say ₹80, risk ₹80, better if a sharp rally comes. Same view, chosen by the vol regime and whether I want POP or payoff.
- *Say it crisply:* Same direction, opposite vol bet — sell the credit spread when IV's rich and I want high POP via theta; buy the debit spread when IV's cheap and I want a sharp directional payoff.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Which has higher probability of profit?" Usually the credit spread, because you also win on a flat tape — but its payoff-to-risk is worse.

**Q3. Straddle vs strangle — construction, and when each earns its keep.**
- *Interviewer is testing:* long/short vol expression and the cost-vs-probability tradeoff.
- *Deep answer:* A straddle is same-strike call + put (usually ATM); a strangle is OTM call + OTM put. Long either = long vol/gamma, betting realized > implied or a big move; short either = short vol/theta, betting the market stays range-bound. Straddle costs more (both ATM, max premium, max gamma) but has tighter breakevens and more sensitivity; strangle is cheaper, wider breakevens, needs a bigger move to pay but bleeds less theta when short. Long straddle before an event you think is under-priced (cheap IV, big catalyst); but beware IV crush — if IV is already bid, the crush can kill a long straddle even on a real move. Short strangle is the classic premium-harvest in a calm, contango, high-IV-percentile regime — sell the 16-delta call and put on a Nifty weekly, collect theta, win if it stays inside the wings. Worked: Nifty 24,000, sell 24,400 call + 23,600 put for ₹70+₹80 = ₹150 credit; breakevens ~23,450 and 24,550; profit if it expires in that band. The risk is undefined — a gap through a wing hurts — so I'd often turn it into an iron condor by buying further wings, or size it as a fraction of capital and manage at a delta/loss threshold.
- *Say it crisply:* Straddle is same-strike, costly, high-gamma; strangle is OTM, cheaper, wider. Long for a big move with cheap IV; short strangle to harvest theta in a calm, rich-IV range — sized for the tail or wrapped into a condor.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Short strangle risk?" Undefined on both sides plus short vega — a gap or vol spike hurts twice; I cap it with wings (condor) or manage at a hard delta/loss stop.

**Q4. Construct an iron condor on Bank Nifty and tell me exactly how you'd pick the strikes.**
- *Interviewer is testing:* delta-based strike selection and defined-risk premium harvesting.
- *Deep answer:* An iron condor is a short strangle with protective wings: sell an OTM put spread AND an OTM call spread — four legs, net credit, defined risk, short vega/long theta, wins if the underlying stays in a range. Strike selection is delta-based, not arbitrary: I sell the short strikes around 15–20 delta (roughly a 1-standard-deviation band for the expiry, so ~80–85% theoretical POP) and buy the wings a fixed width out to cap risk. Worked, Bank Nifty at 52,000, weekly: sell 52,800 call (~16Δ) / buy 53,200 call; sell 51,200 put (~16Δ) / buy 50,800 put. Say I collect ₹90 net credit, width 400, so max risk = 400 − 90 = ₹310 × lot each side (only one side can lose). Breakevens ~51,110 and 52,890. I pick the short-strike delta by how much cushion I want vs how much credit, and I widen wings for more credit but more risk. Management: I define profit target (~50% of max credit) and a mental stop (e.g., short strike touched or loss = 1.5–2× credit), and I watch which side is getting tested to roll or close early. The condor earns its keep in a calm, high-IV-percentile, contango regime with no event inside the week.
- *Say it crisply:* Iron condor = short strangle plus wings; I sell the ~16-delta strikes for a 1-sigma band and buy fixed-width wings, collect the credit, target 50% profit and cut if a short strike is breached.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Why 16 delta?" It's roughly the 1-sigma edge for the expiry, giving ~84% theoretical POP — a standard balance of credit collected against probability of staying in range.

**Q5. When is a butterfly the right structure, and how do you build one?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* pin-risk/low-cost targeted bets and cheap tail expressions.
- *Deep answer:* A long butterfly (buy 1 lower, sell 2 middle, buy 1 upper, equidistant) is a cheap, defined-risk bet that the underlying PINS near the middle strike at expiry. Max profit at the body, small defined loss = the debit paid, very low cost, positive theta near expiry if you're near the body. It shines when I have a strong "expires around here" view — e.g., an expiry-day pin near a big open-interest strike on Nifty — or as a cheap way to express a target with tiny risk. Worked: Nifty 24,000, buy 23,800 call / sell 2× 24,000 calls / buy 24,200 call for a small debit, say ₹30; max payoff ₹170 if it pins 24,000, max loss ₹30. Broken-wing variants skew the payoff and can be done for a credit to lean directional with no upside risk on one side. The nuance: butterflies are expiry-day/near-expiry instruments — the profit tent only sharpens as gamma concentrates, so entering a fly too early just means paying to wait. I use it when I want a high payoff-to-risk ratio on a precise level, accepting a low probability of hitting the sweet spot.
- *Say it crisply:* A long butterfly is a cheap, defined-risk bet on the underlying pinning a specific strike — buy-1/sell-2/buy-1 — best used near expiry when I have a strong 'it settles right here' view.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Butterfly vs condor?" The fly targets a point (higher payoff, lower probability); the condor targets a range (lower payoff, higher probability) — I pick by how precise my view is.

**Q6. Explain a calendar spread and what view it actually expresses.**
- *Interviewer is testing:* term-structure trading, not just another neutral strategy.
- *Deep answer:* A calendar (horizontal) spread sells a near-dated option and buys a longer-dated option at the SAME strike. It's fundamentally a term-structure/vol trade: you're short the fast-decaying front theta and long the slower back-month, and you're long vega net (the back has more vega than the front). It profits when the underlying sits near the strike so the front decays faster than the back, AND/OR when back-month IV rises relative to front. So the ideal setup is CONTANGO (front cheap, back richer) with the underlying expected to hover near the strike, and preferably a view that longer-dated vol will firm up. Worked: Nifty at 24,000, sell this week's 24,000 call, buy next week's/monthly 24,000 call for a net debit; if Nifty pins 24,000 the front expires worthless and I keep the back which retains value. The danger is a big move away from the strike (both legs go deep ITM/OTM and the spread compresses) or the back-month IV collapsing. I use calendars specifically when I have a term-structure view — front vol overpriced vs back — not as a generic "neutral" trade, and I avoid them in backwardation.
- *Say it crisply:* A calendar sells the fast-decaying front and buys the slower back at one strike — it's a long-vega, term-structure trade that wants the underlying to pin the strike in a contango curve.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Diagonal vs calendar?" A diagonal uses different strikes too, adding a directional lean on top of the calendar's term-structure/theta bet.

**Q7. What's a ratio spread or backspread, and when would you put one on?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* asymmetric/convex structures and skew awareness.
- *Deep answer:* A ratio spread sells more options than it buys (e.g., buy 1 call, sell 2 higher calls) — usually a credit, short extra vega/gamma, with an UNHEDGED tail if the move runs past the short strikes; you use it when you expect a move to but not beyond a level. A backspread flips it — sell 1 nearer, buy 2 further (net long options) — long gamma/vega with a convex tail, financed by the one you sold, often near-zero cost. The put backspread is the classic crash trade: sell 1 near put, buy 2 further OTM puts, so if the index gaps down and skew steepens you have explosive long-tail profit, and if nothing happens you lose little (sometimes it's a credit). I put on a call ratio when I'm mildly bullish but think upside is capped and want to finance it; a put backspread when I want cheap, convex downside protection or to bet on a volatility explosion with limited bleed. Skew matters: backspreads buy the expensive wing, so I want to enter when skew is NOT already extreme; ratios sell the wing, better when skew is rich. The discipline is respecting the naked tail on ratio spreads — that's where they blow up.
- *Say it crisply:* Ratio spreads sell more than they buy (credit, tail risk, for a capped move); backspreads buy more than they sell (convex, long-tail, cheap) — I use the put backspread as a low-cost crash bet and ratios for a move-to-a-level.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Ratio spread's danger?" The extra short option is naked beyond the short strike — an unlimited/large tail if the move overshoots, so I size it small or convert if tested.

**Q8. How do you actually select strikes — delta, breakevens, or something else?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* disciplined, probabilistic strike selection.
- *Deep answer:* Delta is my primary lens because it doubles as an approximate probability of expiring ITM — a 16-delta short strike is ~1-sigma out with ~84% chance of staying OTM. For premium selling I anchor short strikes to a target delta (15–20Δ for condors/strangles) so I'm systematically selling a consistent probability band, then adjust for IV (higher IV pushes the same delta further out in points, giving more cushion). For debit/directional trades I pick strikes off the expected move and breakevens — where does the structure start making money and is that inside a realistic move for the expiry. I compute breakevens explicitly (credit spread BE = short strike ± credit; straddle BE = strike ± total premium) and sanity-check them against the expected move (≈ ATM straddle price, or IV×√(t/365)×spot). POP vs payoff is the tradeoff I balance at every strike choice: selling closer = more credit and lower POP, further = less credit, higher POP. I never pick strikes on "looks about right" — it's delta for probability, breakevens vs expected move for reality, and the credit/width ratio for whether the risk-reward justifies it.
- *Say it crisply:* Delta for probability (short strikes ~16Δ ≈ 1-sigma), breakevens checked against the expected move for reality, and the credit-to-width ratio for reward — never eyeballed.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Quick expected-move estimate?" The ATM straddle price is the market's expected move for that expiry, or spot × IV × √(days/365).

**Q9. Defined-risk vs undefined-risk strategies — how do you decide?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* risk-management maturity and capital/margin awareness.
- *Deep answer:* Undefined-risk (naked strangles/straddles, ratio spreads) collects more premium and has higher capital efficiency in a calm tape, but exposes you to gap/tail risk that can dwarf the credit — one Bank Nifty gap can exceed weeks of harvested premium. Defined-risk (verticals, condors, flies) caps the loss to a known number for a smaller credit, and crucially caps your MARGIN. My decision hinges on: (1) account size and whether one tail can materially impair capital — smaller books must be defined-risk; (2) regime — I'll only run undefined risk in benign, contango, no-event conditions and even then sized small; (3) event calendar — never naked into a fat event; (4) margin/SPAN — in India naked index options carry heavy SPAN+exposure margin, so defined-risk condors are far more margin-efficient for the same view. My default as a disciplined trader is defined-risk, and I "earn" the right to run undefined risk only when the regime is clearly benign and the size is a small fraction of capital. The question I always ask: "if the worst realistic gap happens overnight, does this trade hurt or does it end me?"
- *Say it crisply:* Default defined-risk for capped loss and margin; I only run undefined risk in a benign regime, small, with no event pending — because one gap can exceed months of premium.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Margin angle?" Naked index options carry heavy SPAN+exposure margin in India; buying wings to define risk slashes the margin, so condors are far more capital-efficient than naked strangles.

**Q10. Give me a full worked construction: you're mildly bearish Nifty into next week with elevated IV.**
- *Interviewer is testing:* end-to-end reasoning producing an actual trade with strikes.
- *Deep answer:* View decomposed: direction = mildly bearish; vol = IV elevated (high IV percentile), so I want to SELL premium not buy it; time = one-week horizon, no fat event I'm scared of. Mildly bearish + rich IV → a bear CALL credit spread (sell the call spread above the market): I profit if Nifty stays flat, drifts down, or even rises modestly to my short strike, and I'm a net vol seller collecting the elevated IV. Construction, Nifty at 24,000: sell 24,200 call (~30Δ) / buy 24,400 call, collect say ₹75 credit, width 200, max risk = 200 − 75 = ₹125 × lot. Breakeven 24,275; max profit ₹75 if Nifty expires below 24,200; max loss ₹125 above 24,400. Why not a debit put spread? IV is rich — I don't want to pay inflated vol; the credit call spread lets me win on flat/down/mildly-up AND harvest the elevated IV via theta and eventual IV normalization. Management: target ~50% of credit to close early, cut if 24,200 is breached and delta runs, and I'd avoid this if the weekly IV were in backwardation ahead of an event. If I were MORE bearish and IV cheap, I'd flip to a debit put spread for bigger payoff.
- *Say it crisply:* Mildly bearish plus rich IV means sell a bear call credit spread — e.g. sell 24,200 / buy 24,400 for ~₹75 — winning on flat-to-down while harvesting the elevated vol, rather than paying up for a debit put spread.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Why not just sell a naked call?" Undefined risk and heavy margin; the bought 24,400 wing caps my loss and margin for a small give-up in credit.

**Q11. When is the best trade NO trade? How do you know?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* discipline and edge-awareness — the mark of an experienced trader.
- *Deep answer:* The best trade is no trade whenever I can't clearly articulate an edge on all three axes. Concretely, I stand down when: (1) direction, vol, and path don't align into a coherent structure — e.g., I'm bearish but IV is cheap AND an event could crush it, giving conflicting signals; (2) vol is neither clearly rich nor cheap — selling cheap or buying expensive premium is negative-edge by construction; (3) a fat event sits inside the horizon and I have no view on it — that's gambling on a coin-flip while paying the crush; (4) the setup requires me to force a favorite structure onto a view it doesn't fit; (5) I'm revenge-trading or bored — the worst reason to have a position. Costs matter too: with STT (rising to ~0.15% on option premium from Apr 2026 — verify current), brokerage, and bid-ask, a thin-edge trade is negative after frictions. Experienced traders lose money not from missing trades but from taking marginal ones; capital preserved is optionality kept. The tell that I should pass: if I can't say in one sentence WHY this specific structure has positive expected value right now, I don't put it on.
- *Say it crisply:* No trade whenever I can't state the edge in one sentence — conflicting axes, vol that's neither rich nor cheap, an event I have no view on, or forcing a favorite structure. Preserved capital is kept optionality.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "How does cost affect this?" Rising STT (~0.15% on premium from Apr 2026, verify), brokerage and spread turn thin-edge trades negative after frictions — the edge must clear costs before it's a trade.

**Q12. How does the India cost/settlement structure change your strategy choice?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* India-specific practicality that separates a real desk trader from a textbook one.
- *Deep answer:* Several India frictions actively shape structure. (1) STT and the ITM-exercise trap: STT on exercised long ITM options is charged on the full SETTLEMENT/intrinsic value, not premium — historically much higher, so letting a long ITM option auto-exercise can cost far more than squaring off in the market; I square off ITM longs before expiry rather than exercise. STT on option SELLS is on premium and rises to ~0.15% from Apr 2026 (verify current), which taxes high-churn selling. (2) Physical settlement of STOCK options: in-the-money single-stock options settle by physical delivery, so an ITM position at expiry means taking/giving actual shares and a big delivery obligation/margin — I close or roll stock-option positions before expiry unless I genuinely want delivery, and this is why I mostly express views in cash-settled INDEX options. (3) SPAN + exposure margin makes naked/undefined structures expensive, pushing me to defined-risk spreads. (4) Weekly-expiry dynamics and recent SEBI changes to expiry days, lot sizes and contract specs (revised through 2024–26 — verify current lot sizes and the single-weekly-expiry-per-exchange rules) change gamma/theta timing and position sizing. Net effect: I favor cash-settled index options, defined-risk spreads, and squaring off before expiry to dodge the exercise-STT and physical-delivery traps.
- *Say it crisply:* India frictions push me to cash-settled index options and defined-risk spreads, and to square off ITM positions before expiry — to dodge the ITM-exercise STT trap, physical stock-option delivery, and heavy SPAN margin.
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "The ITM-exercise STT trap in one line?" STT on exercised long ITM options is levied on full intrinsic value, so auto-exercise can cost far more than simply selling the option in the market before expiry — always square off.
