# Event & Directional Trading with Options

Events are where options traders make or lose their year — and where amateurs learn the hard way that being right on direction can still lose money on a long option. This chapter is about expressing views through the vol run-up and crush around the Budget, RBI, Fed and results, on Nifty and Bank Nifty. Numbers are illustrative and as of 2026 — **verify current** strikes, VIX and rules.

**Q1. Explain the pre-event IV run-up and post-event crush. Why can you be right on direction and still lose on a long option?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* the single most important event-trading lesson — vol crush beats direction.
- *Deep answer:* Ahead of a known event (Budget, RBI policy, big-name results), uncertainty is high, so implied vol on the expiries covering the event gets bid up — India VIX and the front-month/weekly IV climb in the days before. That inflated IV is baked into option *premiums*: an ATM straddle prices in a big expected move. The instant the event passes and uncertainty resolves, IV collapses — the "crush." So if you bought a call because you were bullish, and the market gaps up but by *less than the priced-in move*, your call's *intrinsic* gain is more than offset by the *vega* loss from IV falling. Worked example: Nifty 24,000, budget tomorrow, VIX 22. The ATM call trades ₹280 (fat, event-loaded). Budget day Nifty rises 250 points to 24,250 — you were *right*. But VIX crushes to 13, so the call reprices to maybe ₹300–310 despite +250 intrinsic — you barely make money, and if the move were smaller you'd lose outright. That's the trap: you paid for a move that had to *exceed* the priced-in move for a naked long to win. The fix is either express the view with structures that neutralise vega, or demand a move bigger than what's priced.
- *Say it crisply:* "IV inflates before an event and crushes right after, so a naked long option can lose to vega even when direction is right — you need the move to beat what's already priced in, not just to be correct."
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "How do you know what move is priced in?" → The ATM straddle premium ≈ the market's expected move; if you don't beat it, the long loses.

**Q2. How do you extract the priced-in expected move from the option chain before an event?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* a concrete, quantitative pre-event workflow.
- *Deep answer:* Two quick reads. First, the **ATM straddle**: sum the ATM call and put premiums of the expiry covering the event — that's roughly the market's expected absolute move by that expiry (breakeven of a long straddle). If Nifty 24,000's covering-weekly ATM straddle = ₹360, the market's pricing ~±360 points (~1.5%) by expiry. Second, **VIX-implied daily move**: VIX/√252 × spot. At VIX 22, that's ~1.39%/day ≈ ±333 points — consistent with the straddle. I compare that priced-in move to my *own* view of the likely move. If I think the Budget will move Nifty 500 points and the chain only prices 360, long optionality (or a directional structure) has edge. If I think it'll be a damp squib and only 150 points, the vol is *rich* and I'm a seller of premium (fading the event). I also look at the **IV term structure** — the covering weekly's IV vs the next weekly/monthly; a steep front hump quantifies the event premium I'd sell or the crush I'd suffer as a buyer.
- *Say it crisply:* "I read the ATM straddle of the covering expiry as the priced-in move and cross-check it with VIX/√252 × spot, then trade the gap between that and my own view of the move."
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Straddle says 360, you think 200 — what do you do?" → Vol's rich; I'm a net premium seller into the event, hedged/defined-risk.

**Q3. Long option vs vertical spread vs future — how do you choose to express a directional view?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* the core structuring decision and its trade-offs.
- *Deep answer:* Three tools, three profiles. A **long option** is long delta *and* long vega *and* short theta — great if you expect a big move AND rising/holding vol, terrible into a vol crush. A **vertical spread** (e.g. bull call spread) sells a further strike to fund the long — it caps upside but slashes cost, cuts vega (you're long one vol, short another, so it's far less vega-sensitive) and cuts theta bleed; it's the right tool for a *directional-but-vol-crush* event view. A **future** is pure delta — no vega, no theta, symmetric payoff, cheapest way to be directional if you're confident and want no vol exposure, but unlimited downside and full margin. My rule: strong conviction + want vol crush protection + capped move expectation → **spread**. Strong conviction + expect a *big* move + vol not rich → **long option** (or long a slightly OTM to cheapen). Pure directional, no vol view, deep conviction → **future** (or synthetic via options). Into a budget where IV is fat, I almost never buy a naked long — the vega tax is too high; I use a spread or a future so I'm not paying for the crush.
- *Say it crisply:* "Long option = delta+vega+theta, good only for a big move with vol not rich; spread caps the move but kills vega and theta, ideal into a vol crush; future is pure delta with no vol exposure — I pick based on how much of the move is already priced."
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Why not always use the future?" → Unlimited loss and full margin; the spread gives defined risk and often better risk/reward when I expect a bounded move.

**Q4. Walk me through a full worked trade: how would you trade Nifty into the Union Budget?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* end-to-end event structuring with real numbers and risk management.
- *Deep answer:* Setup (illustrative, Feb 2026 — verify): Nifty 24,000, budget on a Saturday special session, VIX elevated at 20 (up from 12), covering weekly ATM straddle ~₹340, so market prices ~±340 (1.4%). My view: mildly bullish (capex-heavy budget likely) but I *know* IV will crush Monday. A naked long call is a trap — I'd pay ₹200 for a strike that needs +200 just to break even against a crush. Instead I put on a **bull call spread**: buy 24,000 call ~₹190, sell 24,300 call ~₹80, net debit ₹110, max value ₹300 at/above 24,300, max profit ₹190, defined risk ₹110. Because it's a spread, the vega is largely netted — Monday's crush hurts both legs similarly, so I keep my directional edge without eating the full crush. If I were *neutral* and thought the market over-priced the move, I'd instead **sell** the straddle or an iron condor to harvest the crush, defined-risk, sized small. Risk management: I size the spread to a fixed % of capital as max loss, I set a mental stop if the thesis breaks (e.g. budget turns fiscally loose and market sells off through 23,700), and I plan to exit into Monday's liquidity/crush rather than holding to expiry. The discipline is: pick the structure that isolates the edge (direction) and neutralises the tax (vega crush).
- *Say it crisply:* "Bullish into the budget with VIX fat, I'd buy a 24,000/24,300 bull call spread for ~₹110 debit — it keeps my directional edge, nets out most of the Monday vol crush, and caps risk — rather than buy a naked call that the crush would gut."
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "What if you had *no* directional view?" → Then I'd fade the priced-in move with a defined-risk iron condor/short straddle sized small, to harvest the crush.

**Q5. Explain using a calendar spread around an event. When does it work and when does it hurt?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* understanding of term-structure vol, not just directional bets.
- *Deep answer:* A calendar (sell near-dated, buy longer-dated, same strike) is a bet on **term structure and the crush timing**. Into an event, the near expiry's IV is pumped more than the far, so the near option is relatively expensive — selling it and buying the cheaper far leg means after the event the near crushes hard (you gain on the short) while the far holds more of its vol (you keep value). It profits if spot stays near the strike (max value at the strike at near-expiry) and if the front IV crush exceeds the back's. It *hurts* if spot makes a big directional move away from the strike (both legs move but the short near-leg's gamma bites), or if the whole vol surface collapses uniformly, or if you mis-time and the far leg crushes too. So a calendar is really a "big priced-in move that I think won't happen, pinned near this strike, with front vol richer than back" trade. I use it when the event premium is concentrated in the front weekly and I expect a damp move — I'm selling the overpriced front crush while staying long some vega on the back in case I'm wrong on magnitude.
- *Say it crisply:* "A calendar sells the event-inflated near expiry and buys the cheaper far one — it profits when spot pins near the strike and the front IV crushes harder than the back, and it hurts on a big directional move or a uniform vol collapse."
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Directional risk?" → Yes — it's short gamma on the front, so a big move away from the strike is the main loss driver; size and strike selection manage it.

**Q6. What's a backspread and why might you use one into an event?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* knowledge of convex, long-gamma event structures.
- *Deep answer:* A ratio backspread (e.g. call backspread: sell 1 near ATM call, buy 2 further-OTM calls) is *net long options* — long gamma and vega — often structured for zero or small net debit/credit. Into an event you'd use a call backspread if you expect a *big* upside move (bigger than priced-in) but want to cheapen the long optionality by financing it with the short ATM. Payoff: you lose a little in the middle if the market stalls near the short strike (the danger zone), but if it rockets you have net-positive convexity — the two long OTM calls run away. It's the structure for "I think the event triggers a violent move and vol will *spike further*, not crush" — e.g. a shock RBI hike or a tail-risk political event. The risk is the reverse of a naked long: if the move is medium (lands in the short-strike zone) you take the max loss there. So backspreads are for *convexity/fat-tail* event views where you expect the priced-in move to be *exceeded* and vol to expand — the opposite regime from the typical crush. I size them small; they're a lottery-ticket-with-financing, not a bread-and-butter trade.
- *Say it crisply:* "A backspread is net long gamma/vega, financed by a short ATM — I use it when I expect the event to trigger a move *bigger* than priced and vol to expand, accepting a loss zone around the short strike if the move is only medium."
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Crush vs backspread?" → Backspread wins if the move exceeds priced-in and vol spikes; it's the wrong trade if you expect the normal post-event crush.

**Q7. Trading RBI policy vs the Fed vs results — how do the vol dynamics differ?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* nuance across event types, especially Bank Nifty sensitivity.
- *Deep answer:* **RBI policy** hits Bank Nifty hardest (rate-sensitive financials) — the move is often in *rates guidance/stance* not just the repo number, so a "no change but hawkish" can still whip financials. I trade RBI mostly through Bank Nifty, and the crush is sharp because it's a scheduled, binary-ish event. **The Fed (FOMC)** is an *overnight/gap* event for India — it happens after our close, so the reaction is a gap open, meaning intraday hedging is impossible and the risk is a jump; I size for gap risk and lean on defined-risk structures or hold delta-one hedges overnight. **Corporate results** (e.g. HDFC Bank, Reliance) are single-stock events with physically-settled stock options — huge stock-specific IV run-up and crush, plus assignment/delivery risk into expiry; and index impact is weighted (a big HDFC Bank miss drags Bank Nifty/Nifty). Across all three the crush pattern holds, but the *hedgeability* differs: RBI is intraday and hedgeable, Fed is a gap you can't hedge live, results are single-name with delivery mechanics. I match structure to hedgeability — spreads/defined-risk for the un-hedgeable gaps.
- *Say it crisply:* "RBI is a hedgeable intraday event best played via Bank Nifty, the Fed is an un-hedgeable overnight gap so I use defined-risk/held hedges, and results are single-name crushes with delivery risk — same crush, different hedgeability."
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Which has the biggest Bank Nifty crush?" → RBI policy — it's the most bank-specific scheduled catalyst.

**Q8. Explain a gamma-heavy expiry-day directional play on Nifty. What's the edge and the danger?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* understanding of expiry-day gamma dynamics.
- *Deep answer:* On Nifty expiry day (the surviving weekly), near-ATM options have enormous gamma and almost no time value left, so they behave like cheap, high-leverage directional bets — a 30-point Nifty move can double a well-chosen ATM/slightly-OTM option bought for ₹10–20. The *edge* case: if you have a strong intraday directional read (breakout, trend day, momentum after an open) and buy a near-ATM option, the gamma means your delta ramps *with* you — the position accelerates in your favour. The *danger*: theta is brutal and gamma cuts both ways — if the market pins or reverses, that ₹15 option decays to near zero within hours, and "pinning" near a high-OI strike (dealers hedging short gamma pull spot toward the max-pain strike) can trap you. So expiry-day long-gamma directional is a *timing* trade, not a hold: I only take it with a clear intraday catalyst, size it as risk capital I can lose entirely, set a tight time-and-price stop, and I respect max-pain/high-OI strikes as magnets. The alternative expiry play is being the *seller* of that decay — but then I'm short the gamma and one clean trend move hurts. It's the purest risk/reward asymmetry on the board, which is exactly why it's dangerous.
- *Say it crisply:* "Expiry-day near-ATM options are cheap, high-gamma directional lottery tickets — great with a clear intraday catalyst because delta ramps in your favour, but theta and pinning gut them fast, so it's a tightly-stopped timing trade, not a hold."
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "What's max pain?" → The strike where total option-holder losses are maximised; spot often gravitates there near expiry as dealers hedge, so I treat high-OI strikes as magnets.

**Q9. How do you manage a directional options position that's working — and one that isn't — through an event?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* live risk management and discipline, not just entry.
- *Deep answer:* Working: if my directional spread/option is in profit post-event, I don't get greedy against the crush — I take profit into the post-event liquidity, or roll to a further-OTM structure to lock gains and keep a cheaper runner. On a long option that's worked on direction, I'm acutely aware vega is now against me, so I harvest rather than hope. Not working: I pre-define the invalidation *before* the event — a price level where my thesis is wrong (e.g. budget-day Nifty breaks 23,700 on a bullish trade). Because event moves gap, a hard stop can slip, so for un-hedgeable gaps I prefer *defined-risk structures* (spreads) where my max loss is the debit — I can't be stopped worse than that. I never average down into a losing event trade (throwing good money at a crushed long). And I size so that the *worst case* — full debit loss, or the spread's max loss — is a tolerable fraction of capital decided at entry. The meta-skill is that event trades are decided at *entry*: structure and size chosen so that both the win and the loss are things I can live with, because you can't reliably manage a gapping event intraday.
- *Say it crisply:* "I decide the structure and worst-case size at entry so a gap can't hurt me beyond plan, take profits into the crush rather than hoping, pre-set my invalidation level, and never average down a losing event long."
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Why prefer spreads for stops?" → Because event gaps blow through hard stops; a spread's max loss is capped by design, so the gap can't exceed it.

**Q10. Give me your general framework for deciding whether to be a net buyer or net seller of options into any event.**
- *Interviewer is testing:* a repeatable decision process, the sign of an experienced trader.
- *Deep answer:* It comes down to comparing **priced-in move vs my expected move**, and **direction conviction vs uncertainty**. Step 1: extract priced-in (ATM straddle / VIX-implied). Step 2: form my own expected move and direction. Step 3: (a) If I expect a move *bigger* than priced AND have a direction → net *buyer*, via a spread if vol's rich (to dodge crush) or long option/backspread if I expect vol to expand. (b) If I expect a move *smaller* than priced (event is over-hyped) → net *seller*, harvesting the crush via defined-risk short straddle/iron condor/calendar. (c) If I expect a big move but *no* direction → long straddle/strangle only if it's cheap vs my move estimate, else stand aside (the crush usually punishes naked long straddles). Step 4: overlay hedgeability — un-hedgeable gaps (Fed) push me to defined-risk. Step 5: size to worst-case-as-%-of-capital. The discipline: I'm not "bullish/bearish," I'm trading the *gap between what's priced and what I expect*, and I let vol richness and hedgeability pick the structure. Most retail loses at events by being a naive naked buyer of over-priced, about-to-crush options.
- *Say it crisply:* "I compare the priced-in move to my expected move: bigger-and-directional → buy (spread if vol's rich), smaller → sell the crush defined-risk, big-but-no-direction → only a cheap straddle — then let hedgeability and size discipline finish the decision."
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Default bias into most Indian events?" → Slight seller of the crush via defined-risk, because event IV is usually rich relative to the realized move — but only when I have no strong directional edge.
