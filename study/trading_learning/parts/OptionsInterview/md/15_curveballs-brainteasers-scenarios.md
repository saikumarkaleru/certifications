# Curveballs, Brain-Teasers & "Trade This Scenario"

This is where the interview stops being a viva and becomes a live test of your reflexes: mental math, quick Greeks, expected-value puzzles, and "the market is here, VIX is X — trade it." They don't want a perfect number; they want to see fast, structured reasoning delivered out loud without freezing. Below are the classics with the shortcut logic to say in real time. Numbers are illustrative for 2026 NSE F&O (verify current lot sizes, STT, and expiry structure at interview time — the STT-on-options hike to ~0.15% of premium from Apr 2026 is a favourite "do you know the frictions" check).

**Q1. An ATM straddle costs roughly what percentage move?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* Whether you can instantly link straddle price to the market's expected move — core options intuition.
- *Deep answer:* The shortcut every desk trader carries: an ATM straddle price ≈ the market's expected (1-standard-deviation) move over its life. So if Nifty is at 24,000 and the weekly ATM straddle (call + put) trades at ~₹360, the market is pricing roughly a ±360-point move, i.e. ±1.5%, by expiry. The cleaner rule: **ATM straddle ≈ 0.8 × S × σ × √T** (σ = annualised IV, T in years). Turn it around to sanity-check IV against VIX: with India VIX at ~14% and 5 calendar days to expiry, √T = √(5/365) ≈ 0.117, so expected move ≈ 24,000 × 0.14 × 0.117 ≈ **₹393**, ~1.6%. If the straddle is trading richer than that, IV > VIX-implied on this expiry (weekly event premium); cheaper, and realised has room to disappoint the seller. Out loud you say the mapping, then the number. The follow-through insight: the straddle *is* the expected move, so if you think the actual move will be smaller you sell it, larger you buy it — that's the whole trade.
- *Say it crisply:* "ATM straddle ≈ the 1-sigma expected move — roughly 0.8 × S × IV × √T. At Nifty 24,000, ~14 VIX, weekly, that's about ±₹390, ~1.6%, and if the straddle's richer than that I'm a seller."
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "So what's a rough IV from a straddle?" → "IV ≈ straddle ÷ (0.8 × S × √T) — invert the same formula."

**Q2. ATM delta is about 0.5 — so what?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* Whether you understand delta as both hedge ratio and rough probability, and can extend it.
- *Deep answer:* Three readings of "ATM delta ≈ 0.5" you fire off in order. **Hedge ratio:** the ATM call moves ~₹0.50 per ₹1 in the underlying, so to hedge one ATM option's delta on Nifty (lot 25, say) I need ~half a lot of futures equivalent — i.e. delta-neutralise with ~12–13 units of underlying delta. **Probability proxy:** delta ≈ risk-neutral probability of finishing ITM, so ATM ≈ 50/50, a 0.30-delta OTM call ≈ ~30% chance of expiring ITM — which is how I pick strikes for a target probability-of-profit. **Straddle delta:** long ATM call (+0.5) plus long ATM put (−0.5) ≈ 0 net delta — that's why a straddle is a pure vol/gamma bet at inception, direction-neutral. The nuance a senior trader adds: delta drifts. As the underlying moves or time passes, that 0.5 doesn't stay 0.5 — gamma is highest ATM near expiry, so a "delta-neutral" short straddle on expiry morning can be dangerously long or short delta by lunch. So "delta 0.5, so what?" — so I hedge it, I read it as ~50% ITM odds, and I respect that it won't stay 0.5 for long.
- *Say it crisply:* "0.5 means half a unit of hedge per option, ~50% odds of finishing ITM, and a straddle is delta-neutral at entry — but gamma means it won't stay 0.5, so I re-hedge as it drifts."
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "0.30-delta strike — probability ITM?" → "Roughly 30%, so a short 0.30-delta option has ~70% chance of expiring worthless, before costs."

**Q3. Nifty 24,000, you buy the 24,200 call for ₹80 — breakeven and max loss?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* Clean payoff mechanics under time pressure, including that you don't forget costs.
- *Deep answer:* Breakeven = strike + premium = 24,200 + 80 = **24,280** at expiry (index must rise ~1.17% just to break even — that's the theta/OTM cost you're paying). Max loss = premium paid = **₹80 per unit**, ×lot 25 = **₹2,000** per lot, and that's the *whole* risk, which is the beauty of a long option. Max profit = unlimited above breakeven. The experienced-trader add-ons said out loud: (1) breakeven is at expiry — before expiry the option can be profitable well below 24,280 if IV rises or time hasn't decayed, so I don't need to reach breakeven to make money if I'm trading it, not holding to expiry. (2) Costs bite: STT on the buy premium, brokerage, and if I let it expire ITM the exercise-side STT is charged on *settlement value*, not premium — the classic trap that can make a marginally-ITM held-to-expiry option a net loser. So I'd square off before expiry rather than exercise. (3) Probability check: 24,200 is OTM, delta maybe ~0.35, so ~35% chance of finishing ITM — I'm paying ₹80 for a ~1-in-3 shot, which only makes sense if I think IV is cheap or the move is bigger than priced.
- *Say it crisply:* "Breakeven 24,280 at expiry, max loss the ₹80 premium (₹2,000/lot), unlimited up — but I'd square off rather than exercise to dodge the ITM-exercise STT trap."
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Why not hold to expiry if it's ITM by ₹50?" → "Because exercise STT on settlement value could exceed the ₹50 intrinsic — squaring off in the market avoids that."

**Q4. You flip a fair coin; heads you win ₹2, tails you lose ₹1. Do you play, and how much?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* Expected value plus bet sizing (Kelly) — the mental model behind position sizing.
- *Deep answer:* EV per flip = 0.5(+2) + 0.5(−1) = **+₹0.50**, positive, so yes I play — repeatedly. But "how much" is the real question and the answer is Kelly, not "all-in". Kelly fraction for this bet: f = edge/odds = (p·b − q)/b where b = 2 (win 2 per 1 risked), p = 0.5, q = 0.5, so f = (0.5×2 − 0.5)/2 = 0.5/2 = **25% of bankroll** at full Kelly. Then the senior-trader caveat: full Kelly is too aggressive in the real world because I never know the true edge and drawdowns at full Kelly are brutal, so I'd bet **a fraction of that — quarter-to-half Kelly, ~6–12%.** The trading translation, which is why they ask: this coin is a metaphor for a positive-expectancy strategy. Positive EV says *trade it*; Kelly says *size it to the edge*; fractional Kelly says *stay alive through variance because ruin is permanent and a good edge only pays if you're still in the game.* If they make it lose ₹1 on tails but only win ₹0.90 on heads, EV flips negative and I don't play at any size.
- *Say it crisply:* "EV is +₹0.50, so yes — full Kelly says 25% of bankroll, but I'd bet a quarter of that to survive variance. Positive EV says trade, Kelly says size to edge, fractional Kelly keeps me alive."
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Why not full Kelly?" → "Because I don't know my true edge and full-Kelly drawdowns can hit 50%+ — fractional Kelly gives up a little growth for a lot of survivability."

**Q5. Market's at Nifty 24,000, India VIX is 11 — historically low. How do you position?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* Whether you translate a vol regime into a coherent structure, not just "buy calls".
- *Deep answer:* Low VIX means options are cheap in absolute terms and vol is likelier to mean-revert *up* than fall much further — so the asymmetric play is to be a *net buyer of vol/gamma*, not a seller. First I check IV percentile/rank, not just the level: 11 might be the 5th percentile of the last year, which strengthens the "own vol" case. Structures I'd voice: long a cheap straddle/strangle if I expect a regime break or have an event catalyst; or, if I want to be direction-agnostic and pay less theta, a calendar or a long-gamma position where cheap vol works for me. Critically, I'd *avoid* being a naked premium seller here — selling an 11-VIX weekly strangle collects tiny premium for full tail risk, terrible risk/reward, and one vol spike wipes weeks of it. The nuance: cheap vol can stay cheap (theta bleeds you while you wait), so I'd size the long-vol position small, tie it to a catalyst or a tight time window, and treat it as a cheap option on disorder rather than a conviction bet. Contrast this with the mirror question — VIX at 25 — where I'd flip to being a disciplined, defined-risk *seller* of rich premium.
- *Say it crisply:* "Vol's cheap and mean-reverts up, so I'm a net buyer of gamma — long straddle/calendar tied to a catalyst, small size for the theta bleed — and I refuse to sell tail risk for pennies at 11 VIX."
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "And if VIX were 25?" → "Flip it — disciplined defined-risk premium selling like an iron condor, sized for the fat tail, because now I'm being paid richly for vol that's likelier to fall."

**Q6. You think Bank Nifty stays in a range for the next two weeks. Trade it.**
- *Interviewer is testing:* Structure selection, strike placement, and — critically — how you'd manage a range trade that breaks.
- *Deep answer:* A range view is a *short-vol/short-gamma* view, so I sell premium and collect theta. Cleanest defined-risk expression: an **iron condor** — sell an OTM call spread and an OTM put spread around the expected range, wings capping the tail. Strike placement is the skill: I'd anchor short strikes near the edges of the expected move (roughly ±1 sigma from the straddle, or the range's technical bounds — say short 51,000 call / 49,000 put if Bank Nifty's at 50,000), collect the premium, and buy wings ~500–1,000 points further out to cap loss. Why not a naked short strangle? Because Bank Nifty gaps, and undefined risk on a gap is how books die — the condor's defined loss is worth the smaller credit. Sizing: max loss (width minus credit) sized to ~1% of book. Management, which is what they're really probing: I take profit early (~50% of max credit) rather than milking the last rupee near expiry where gamma explodes; I have a mechanical adjustment/exit if the underlying tags a short strike (roll the tested side out, or just take the defined loss); and I avoid holding through a known event inside the window. The India kicker: with 2026's higher STT and expiry changes, I'd lean monthly-ish or the longer weekly rather than churning multiple weeklies, so costs don't eat the thin range premium.
- *Say it crisply:* "Range = short vol, so a defined-risk iron condor with shorts near the ±1-sigma edges and wings for the gap — take profit at ~50%, roll or cut the tested side, and never naked-short the tail on Bank Nifty."
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Why condor not short strangle?" → "Same view, but the wings cap the gap risk — on Bank Nifty an undefined short is a blowup waiting for a bad open."

**Q7. You're short a Bank Nifty strangle and the market gaps 4% overnight. What now?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* Crisis management under a live tail event — the single most revealing scenario question.
- *Deep answer:* First, no panic and no ego — the decision is risk, not "being right." A 4% gap on a short strangle means my tested (say, put) side is now near/ITM, delta has blown out against me, and gamma means it's getting worse fast as spot moves. Step one: **assess the real exposure** — what's my current max loss and net delta right now, not what it was yesterday. Step two: **cut or hedge the delta immediately** — the killer in a gap is the runaway directional exposure, so I'd buy futures/deltas to neutralise the position's delta and stop the bleeding, *then* think. Step three: decide on the structure — if my thesis is dead I close the tested side and take the defined loss cleanly; I do **not** average down / sell more premium to "reduce basis" — that's doubling into a moving tail and how strangle sellers blow up. If IV has spiked hard, the untested (call) side has cheapened and I can buy it back cheap to remove that tail. Step four: check the whole book for correlated shorts — a 4% gap likely hit every short-vol position at once, so the portfolio delta/vega is the real risk. The meta-point they want: I had this planned *before* the trade — defined risk via wings, and a pre-set "on a gap I hedge delta first, question later" rule — so I'm executing a plan, not improvising in fear.
- *Say it crisply:* "Hedge the runaway delta with futures first to stop the bleed, then close the tested side and take the defined loss — never average down into the gap — and check the whole book for correlated short-vol risk."
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Why hedge delta before deciding?" → "Because gamma makes the loss accelerate while I deliberate — neutralising delta buys me time to think without the position running further away."

**Q8. Two options, same everything, but one strike's implied vol is higher. What does that tell you, and can you arb it?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* Understanding of the volatility skew/smile and whether you know it's a feature, not a free lunch.
- *Deep answer:* Different IVs across strikes is the **volatility skew** — in index options like Nifty, OTM puts almost always carry higher IV than OTM calls because of crash-risk demand (people pay up for downside protection) and the leverage effect. So a higher IV on the low strike isn't a mispricing to arbitrage — it's the market pricing fatter left-tail risk, and it's persistent and rational. Can I "arb" it? Not risklessly — selling the expensive-IV put and buying the cheap-IV call (a risk-reversal) leaves me with directional and tail exposure, not a locked profit; the skew exists precisely because that tail risk is real. What I *can* do is trade the skew as a relative-value view: if I think the skew is steeper than justified (crash premium overpriced), I can lean short the rich puts within a defined-risk structure; if I think it's too flat before a risky event, I own downside. The senior signal: I distinguish *statistical* mispricing (tradeable edge) from *risk premium* (compensation for real risk you must bear). Skew is mostly the latter. And I'd sanity-check it's not stale quotes or a wide book on an illiquid strike before assuming it's meaningful at all.
- *Say it crisply:* "That's the skew — OTM puts price fatter crash risk, and it's a risk premium, not a free arb. I can trade it as relative value with defined risk, but there's no riskless lock — the tail it's pricing is real."
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "So skew is never mispriced?" → "It can be over/under-priced versus fair, and that's a real RV trade — but it's never a riskless arbitrage, you're always bearing tail risk."

**Q9. Quick: a call and a put, same strike and expiry — how are their prices related?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* Put-call parity, the most fundamental no-arbitrage relationship in options.
- *Deep answer:* Put-call parity: **C − P = S − K·e^(−rT)** (for European options, ignoring dividends). In plain desk terms: a call minus a put at the same strike equals a (discounted) forward position in the underlying — long call + short put = synthetic long the underlying at strike K. So if I know the call, the put, and the underlying, the fourth is pinned; any deviation beyond costs is an arbitrage (a conversion/reversal). The practical uses I'd name: (1) **synthetics** — I can build a long/short underlying, or replace an expensive/illiquid leg, using the other three; if the ATM call is liquid but the put isn't, I price the put off parity. (2) **arbitrage check** — if C − P strays from S − PV(K), a market-maker locks it with a box/conversion. (3) It tells you calls and puts at a strike share the *same* implied vol in theory — so an IV gap between a call and put at the identical strike signals either the skew reference, a dividend/rate assumption, or a stale quote. Indian index options are European (Nifty/Bank Nifty), so parity holds cleanly; stock options being American and physically settled introduces early-exercise and settlement nuance. Say the formula, then "call minus put is synthetic underlying" — that's the intuition they want.
- *Say it crisply:* "Put-call parity: C − P = S − PV(K). Long call plus short put is synthetic long the underlying, so the fourth price is pinned — any real gap is a conversion arbitrage."
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Does it hold for Indian stock options?" → "Less cleanly — they're American and physically settled, so early exercise and settlement mechanics break strict parity; index options being European hold it tightly."

**Q10. Estimate: Nifty at 24,000, 30 days to expiry, 15% IV. What's the ATM straddle worth, no calculator?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* Fast mental approximation of option value — the ability to price in your head, roughly.
- *Deep answer:* Use the ATM approximation **straddle ≈ 0.8 × S × σ × √T**. Plug in: S = 24,000, σ = 0.15, T = 30/365 ≈ 0.082, so √T ≈ 0.286. Straddle ≈ 0.8 × 24,000 × 0.15 × 0.286. Chunk it: 24,000 × 0.15 = 3,600; × 0.286 ≈ 1,030; × 0.8 ≈ **₹825**. So the ATM straddle's worth roughly **₹800–850**, meaning each leg (call, put) ≈ ~₹410–420. Sanity check via expected move: 0.15 × √(30/365) ≈ 0.15 × 0.286 ≈ 4.3% monthly move, × 24,000 ≈ ₹1,030 for the 1-sigma move, and the straddle at ~0.8× that ≈ ₹825 — consistent. The bits that make you sound seasoned: (1) I quote a *range*, not a false-precise number, because I'm approximating. (2) √T is the workhorse — halve the days, price drops by ~30% (1/√2), not 50%; that non-linear time decay is the intuition. (3) Real straddle will differ for skew, rates, and the actual IV surface, so I'd treat ₹825 as the anchor and check it against the live mid. The whole point is producing a defensible number in ten seconds and knowing *why* it's roughly right.
- *Say it crisply:* "Straddle ≈ 0.8 × S × IV × √T = 0.8 × 24,000 × 0.15 × 0.29 ≈ ₹825, so ~₹410 a leg — and I'd quote it as a range because it's an approximation."
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Weekly instead of monthly?" → "√T shrinks by ~√4 = 2, so roughly half the monthly straddle — about ₹410 — for the same IV."

**Q11. A stock's equally likely to go up 20% or down 20% each year. Expected value after two years?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* Volatility drag / geometric-vs-arithmetic mean — a subtle trap most people get wrong.
- *Deep answer:* The trap: arithmetic intuition says "±20% averages to 0%, so flat." Wrong for compounding. Track the paths from ₹100: up-up = 100×1.2×1.2 = 144; up-down or down-up = 100×1.2×0.8 = 96; down-down = 100×0.8×0.8 = 64. Each of the four paths is equally likely (¼), so **expected value = (144 + 96 + 96 + 64)/4 = 400/4 = ₹100** — the arithmetic *expectation* is genuinely flat at ₹100. BUT the *median/typical* outcome is ₹96, a loss, because three of four paths end at or below 100 and the mean is dragged up by the single 144 outlier. That gap is **volatility drag**: the geometric (compounded) return is negative even though the arithmetic mean is zero — √(1.2×0.8) = √0.96 ≈ 0.98, so ~−2% per year typical. Why an options interviewer loves this: it's *why* long volatility/gamma has value (you profit from the dispersion of paths, not the mean) and why leveraged/compounding products bleed in choppy markets. Say both numbers: "Expected value ₹100, but the typical path lands at ₹96 — that's vol drag, and it's the whole reason gamma is worth paying for."
- *Say it crisply:* "Expected value is ₹100 — but the median path is ₹96 because up-down doesn't cancel when you compound. That gap is volatility drag, and it's exactly why long gamma has value."
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "So arithmetic mean lies?" → "For compounding returns, yes — the geometric mean is what you actually earn, and it's always ≤ arithmetic, the gap growing with volatility."

**Q12. It's expiry day, 3:15pm, Nifty is sitting exactly at 24,000 and you're short the 24,000 straddle. What are you thinking?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* Deep understanding of pin risk, expiry-day gamma, and settlement mechanics under pressure.
- *Deep answer:* Both my short legs are ATM with 15 minutes left — this is **peak gamma, peak pin risk**, the most dangerous spot on the board. My position delta is a hair-trigger: a small tick up and I'm suddenly short a real call delta; a tick down and I'm short a put delta — and near expiry that delta swings violently for tiny spot moves, so I can't cleanly hedge (I'd be whipsawed buying high/selling low). Best case, if Nifty pins *exactly* at 24,000 both legs expire worthless and I keep full premium — but "exactly" is a knife-edge and the settlement price is the average of the last 30 minutes (weighted), not the 3:29 tick, so it's not fully in my control. The real risks I'm managing: (1) a late move puts one leg ITM and Indian index options are cash-settled so I owe intrinsic — manageable but I'd rather square off. (2) **Pin/whipsaw** — I'd generally just buy back the straddle for cheap (a few rupees each) rather than gamble on the exact pin; paying ₹5–10 to remove tail risk in the last minutes is cheap insurance. (3) If these were *stock* options I'd be far more careful — physical settlement plus the ITM-exercise STT on full contract value is a genuine trap, so I'd never let a stock option sit ATM into settlement. The mature answer: at 3:15 the edge is mostly earned and the remaining premium isn't worth the gamma lottery — I close and bank it.
- *Say it crisply:* "Peak gamma and pin risk — the delta's a hair-trigger I can't hedge cleanly, so I'd just buy the straddle back for a few rupees and bank the premium rather than gamble on an exact pin, especially anything physically settled."
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "But you'd give up the last ₹10 of profit?" → "Gladly — that ₹10 is a lottery ticket on the exact pin against real gamma and settlement tail risk; closing is the positive-expectancy move."
