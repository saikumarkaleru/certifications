# Advanced: Market-Making, Gamma Scalping, Dispersion & Vol-Arb

The senior/quant round. Here they're probing whether you understand options as a *volatility* business, not a directional one — capturing edge, rehedging gamma, trading the relationship between index and single-name vol, and knowing why even a "hedged" short-vol book can blow up. Answers assume India F&O context where relevant; concepts are universal. Numbers illustrative, 2026 — **verify current**.

**Q1. How does a delta-neutral options market-maker actually make money?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* whether you grasp that MM edge is the spread plus vol edge, harvested through hedging, not directional bets.
- *Deep answer:* A market-maker quotes a two-sided bid/ask and earns the **spread** when they buy at bid and sell at ask — that's the raw edge. But they end up warehousing risk (net long or short options), so they immediately **delta-hedge** with the underlying/future to strip out directional exposure, leaving them running a *vol* position. Their real business is capturing the **difference between the implied vol they trade at and the realized vol they hedge against**, plus the bid/ask edge, minus hedging costs. If a MM sells options (collecting rich IV) they're short gamma — they make theta but must rehedge, and profit if realized vol comes in below the implied they sold. If they're forced long options, they gamma-scalp (below). They manage inventory skew — if they're getting hit on one side, they lean their quotes to attract offsetting flow. In India the edge sources are the same but the game is dominated by tight-spread near-ATM weekly Nifty strikes where competition is fierce and edge is thin; the far strikes have wide spreads but little flow. So MM P&L = spread capture + (implied − realized) vol edge − hedging/slippage/financing costs, run continuously delta-neutral.
- *Say it crisply:* "A market-maker earns the bid/ask spread, delta-hedges away direction, and is then really trading implied vs realized vol — profiting when the vol they trade at beats the vol they actually hedge against, net of hedging costs."
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "What kills a MM?" → Adverse selection (being picked off by informed flow) and a realized-vol spike blowing past the implied they sold while short gamma.

**Q2. Explain gamma scalping. What determines its P&L?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* the mechanical link between hedging a long-gamma book and the realized-vs-implied vol payoff.
- *Deep answer:* If you're **long options (long gamma)** and delta-hedge continuously, every time spot moves your delta changes in your favour — a long-gamma position gets longer as spot rises and shorter as it falls — so you **sell high and buy low** when you rehedge back to neutral, banking small scalps. Those scalps are your gross gamma P&L. Against that, you *pay theta* every day for holding the long options. The net result: **long gamma P&L ≈ realized vol − implied vol** (times a dollar-gamma scaling). You win if the underlying actually moves (realizes vol) more than the implied vol you paid for the options; you lose if it sits still (theta bleeds, no scalps). Worked intuition: buy a Nifty straddle pricing 12% implied; if Nifty then chops around realizing 16%, your rehedging scalps out-earn the theta and you profit; if it pins and realizes 8%, theta wins and you lose. Practical levers: **how often you rehedge** (continuous captures more but pays more transaction cost/slippage; discrete bands trade off scalp capture vs cost), and **where spot is relative to strike** (gamma is highest ATM near expiry, so scalping is richest there but theta is also brutal). Gamma scalping is literally *monetising the gap between realized and implied volatility.*
- *Say it crisply:* "Long a gamma-hedged book, each spot move lets you rehedge by selling high/buying low — those scalps pay you realized vol while theta charges you implied, so gamma-scalp P&L ≈ realized minus implied vol."
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "How often do you rehedge?" → A trade-off: tighter bands capture more scalps but pay more slippage/STT; I widen bands when transaction costs are high and tighten when vol and gamma are high.

**Q3. If gamma-scalp P&L is realized minus implied, what's the point of the greeks — just watch vol?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* depth — that the path, dollar-gamma and vega/vanna/volga all matter, not a single number.
- *Deep answer:* The realized-minus-implied result is the *clean* theory under continuous hedging with constant vol. Reality diverges, and the greeks tell you how. **Dollar-gamma** scales the payoff — the same vol move earns more when your gamma is large (ATM, near expiry), so *when and where* the move happens matters, not just the annualised realized number: a move that happens while you're ATM-heavy pays more than the same move when you're OTM. **Path dependence**: realized vol measured over the period can equal implied yet you lose if the moves came when your gamma was low. **Vega** captures P&L from the *level* of implied vol repricing (a MtM hit if IV moves), separate from the hedging scalps. **Vanna/volga** (skew and vol-of-vol sensitivities) matter when you hold a skewed book and the surface shifts. So an experienced vol trader watches: dollar-gamma profile across strikes/time, vega by expiry (term structure exposure), and the skew greeks — because the "realized − implied" headline hides *where* on the surface and *when* in the path the P&L is coming from. That granularity is the difference between a book that behaves and one that surprises you.
- *Say it crisply:* "Realized-minus-implied is the clean theory, but dollar-gamma scales it, path decides when it's earned, and vega/vanna/volga capture surface repricing — so I watch the whole greek profile, not one vol number."
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Give an example of same realized vol, different P&L." → If the big moves happen when I'm far OTM (low gamma) I capture little; the same annualised vol delivered while I'm ATM pays much more.

**Q4. What is dispersion trading?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* understanding of index vol vs single-name vol and correlation.
- *Deep answer:* Index variance is a function of the constituents' variances *and their correlations*: an index moves less than the average of its stocks because they don't all move together. So **index implied vol is cheap relative to the average single-name implied vol when implied correlation is high**, and vice versa. Dispersion trades that: classically you **sell index options (short index vol) and buy options on the constituents (long single-name vol)** — you're short correlation, betting the stocks will move around individually more than the index (i.e., realized correlation comes in below the implied correlation embedded in index vol). If the names decorrelate (idiosyncratic, stock-specific moves — think an earnings season where each bank reacts to its own results), the single-name legs pay while the index leg stays calm. You lose in a **correlation-1 crash** where everything sells off together — the index leg (short) blows out while the single names don't diversify. In India this is harder: liquid single-stock options are limited to the big F&O names, Bank Nifty is only ~12 names (so a Bank Nifty vs constituent-banks dispersion is actually feasible and is the cleanest local version), and borrow/liquidity constraints bite. It's a **relative-value vol / short-correlation** strategy, not a directional one.
- *Say it crisply:* "Dispersion sells index vol and buys constituent vol — a short-correlation bet that single names move more idiosyncratically than the index implies; it pays when stocks decorrelate and loses in a correlated crash."
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Local version?" → Bank Nifty vs its ~12 constituent banks' options is the cleanest Indian dispersion, since the index is concentrated and those names have liquid options.

**Q5. What is implied correlation and how does it sit inside index vol?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* the math intuition behind dispersion and correlation risk.
- *Deep answer:* For an index, variance ≈ Σ wᵢ²σᵢ² + ΣΣ wᵢwⱼσᵢσⱼρᵢⱼ — the weighted sum of constituent variances plus the covariance (correlation) terms. Given the index implied vol and the constituents' implied vols and weights, you can back out the **implied correlation** the market is pricing — a single number roughly between 0 and 1 telling you how together the market expects the names to move. High implied correlation (near 1) means index vol is *expensive* relative to the components (dispersion is attractive to put on — sell index, buy names); low implied correlation means the opposite. Implied correlation spikes in crises (everything moves together → index vol rich) and falls in stock-picker/earnings regimes. Traders watch implied correlation as its *own* tradable variable — a "dispersion index." The risk in being short correlation (long dispersion) is exactly a correlation spike: a market crash drives ρ toward 1, index vol explodes against your short index leg, and the long single-name legs don't diversify enough to save you. So implied correlation is both the signal (when to put dispersion on) and the risk factor (what blows it up).
- *Say it crisply:* "Index variance is component variances plus correlation terms, so you can back out the implied correlation priced into index vol — high implied correlation makes index vol rich vs the names (dispersion signal), and a correlation spike toward 1 is what blows a short-correlation book up."
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "So dispersion is short what, exactly?" → Short realized correlation — you profit if realized correlation is below the implied correlation you sold via the index leg.

**Q6. Explain a skew relative-value trade. When is skew "too steep"?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* whether you trade the shape of the vol surface, not just its level.
- *Deep answer:* Equity index skew is persistently negative — OTM puts trade at higher IV than OTM calls (crash fear, hedging demand). A skew RV trade bets the *shape* is mispriced relative to history or to realized dynamics. Example: a **risk reversal** (sell the expensive OTM put, buy the cheaper OTM call, delta-hedged) is short skew — you put it on when skew looks too steep vs its typical range and you expect it to flatten, i.e. put IV to come down relative to call IV. Or a **put spread vs call spread** to isolate the smile's slope. "Too steep" is judged relative to: the skew's own history (percentile), the level of vol (skew and level co-move — skew steepens as vol rises), and realized skew (does the market actually move down faster than up by as much as priced?). The risk: skew is steep *for a reason* — it's the price of crash insurance, and being short skew (short the put wing) means you're short the tail; a crash both steepens skew further and hurts your short put. So skew RV is a *carry-with-tail-risk* trade: you collect when the surface mean-reverts and pay badly when the tail hits. I size it small and often keep a cheap deep-tail hedge so I'm short the "belly" of the skew, not the extreme wing.
- *Say it crisply:* "Skew RV trades the surface's slope — e.g. a risk reversal short an over-steep put skew expecting it to flatten — judged against skew's own history and vol level, with the catch that steep skew is crash insurance, so you're short the tail and size it small."
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Why does skew steepen when vol rises?" → Fear drives put demand and spot-vol correlation is negative, so down-moves come with vol spikes — the surface prices that leverage into a steeper put skew.

**Q7. Variance swaps and vol swaps — what are they and how do they differ from trading options for vol?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* knowledge of pure vol instruments and the convexity distinction.
- *Deep answer:* A **variance swap** pays the difference between **realized variance** and a strike (variance), i.e. it's a *pure* bet on realized vol² with no delta and no need to gamma-hedge — you get clean exposure to realized variance. A **vol swap** pays on realized *vol* (not variance) linearly. The key distinction: variance swaps are **convex in vol** (payoff in variance = vol²), so they're worth more than a vol swap when vol is uncertain — that convexity premium is why a variance swap strike sits slightly above the ATM implied (it's priced off the *whole* option strip, via the log-contract replication: a static portfolio of options across all strikes weighted 1/K² plus a dynamic future hedge). Versus trading options for vol: with options you get *messy* vol exposure — your vega changes with spot, you must gamma-scalp and rehedge, and your realized-vs-implied capture is path-dependent and concentrated near your strikes. A variance swap gives you the *clean* realized-variance exposure that a perfectly hedged, continuously-rehedged strip of options *approximates* — it removes the path/dollar-gamma noise. In India these OTC vol products aren't exchange-traded for retail, so the *concept* matters (it's how you think about "pure vol" and why an option book's vol P&L is lumpy), even if you actually express vol via listed options.
- *Say it crisply:* "A variance swap is a clean bet on realized variance (no delta, no gamma-hedging), convex in vol so it prices above ATM implied and replicates via a 1/K²-weighted option strip; a vol swap is the linear version — both give the pure vol exposure that a hedged option book only approximates messily."
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Why does a variance swap replicate with 1/K² weights?" → That weighting across all strikes makes the portfolio's aggregate gamma constant in dollar terms, which is exactly what gives exposure to variance independent of where spot goes.

**Q8. Why does a hedged short-vol book still carry fat-tail risk even when it's delta-neutral and gamma-managed?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* the central humility of the vol-selling business — the thing that blows up funds.
- *Deep answer:* Delta-neutral removes *direction*; it does nothing about **gap risk, jumps and the vol-of-vol.** A short-vol book is short gamma (loses on big moves) and short vega (loses when IV spikes) — and those two hit *together and non-linearly* in a crash. Continuous delta-hedging assumes you can rehedge in small increments; a **gap** (overnight, or a circuit-breaker, or a Fed shock while India's shut) jumps spot past your hedge, and short gamma means the loss grows *quadratically* with the move — a 5-sigma gap doesn't cost 5x a 1-sigma, it costs ~25x on the gamma. Simultaneously **IV explodes** (vega loss) and **skew steepens** (your short put wing reprices against you) and **correlation goes to 1** (if it's a dispersion/index book). And **SPAN margin gets hiked mid-crisis**, forcing you to cover shorts at the worst prices — a liquidity/forced-liquidation spiral. So the book that quietly collects theta for months has a **short, fat left tail**: many small wins, rare enormous losses (short-vol payoff is "picking up pennies in front of a steamroller"). Managing it means: cap gross short gamma/vega, always own some cheap deep-OTM tail hedges (convert naked shorts to defined-risk spreads), stress-test to jumps not just 1-sigma moves, keep a margin cushion, and size so a 5–8 sigma gap is survivable, not fatal.
- *Say it crisply:* "Delta-neutral only removes direction — a short-vol book is short gamma and vega, so a gap makes losses grow quadratically while IV spikes, skew steepens, correlation goes to 1 and margin gets hiked all at once; it's pennies-in-front-of-a-steamroller, so I cap size, keep tail hedges, and stress to jumps."
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "How do you convert it to survivable?" → Buy cheap deep-OTM wings so every short is a defined-risk spread, cap net short vega/gamma, and size to survive a multi-sigma overnight gap with margin cushion.

**Q9. How do you decide what implied vol is "fair" — what's your fair-value process for vol?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* a structured vol-valuation framework, the core of a vol trader's edge.
- *Deep answer:* I triangulate. (1) **Realized vs implied**: compute recent realized vol (close-to-close and range-based like Parkinson/Garman-Klass) over relevant windows and compare to the implied I'd trade at — is the market paying a premium (usual) or discount to recent realized? (2) **Vol risk premium**: implied structurally sits above realized (sellers demand a premium), so I judge whether the current premium is rich or thin vs its history. (3) **Term structure**: is the front cheap/rich vs back (contango/backwardation)? Event humps? (4) **Events calendar**: what known catalysts (budget, RBI, Fed, results) justify or don't justify the level — is IV high because of a real event or just fear? (5) **Skew/surface**: is the wing I'm trading fair vs the smile? (6) **Regime**: VIX level and its percentile, macro backdrop. Fair value isn't a point, it's "given recent realized ~11%, a normal ~2-point premium, no event this week, and VIX in the 30th percentile, 13% implied looks slightly rich → lean short, defined-risk." The edge is disciplined comparison, not a model spitting one number — and always cross-checked against *what could make me wrong* (an un-priced catalyst).
- *Say it crisply:* "I triangulate implied against recent realized, the historical vol-risk-premium, the term structure and event calendar, the skew, and the VIX regime/percentile — fair value is a lean (rich/cheap) with a reason, not a single model number."
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Implied always above realized — so always sell?" → No — the premium compensates for exactly the fat-tail/gap risk in Q8; you only sell when the premium is *rich* relative to that risk, sized to survive the tail.

**Q10. As a desk lead, how would you build and risk-manage a relative-value vol book across Nifty/Bank Nifty?**
- *Interviewer is testing:* synthesis and risk-management maturity at a portfolio level.
- *Deep answer:* I'd frame the book around a few *independent* vol edges rather than one big directional-vol bet: (a) **term-structure RV** (front vs back Nifty weeklies/monthlies), (b) **index vs constituent / Bank Nifty dispersion** (short-correlation, sized for the crash tail), (c) **skew RV** (risk reversals on over-steep wings, tail-hedged), and (d) opportunistic **event vol** (selling rich crush, buying cheap pre-event convexity). Each is delta-hedged so the book is directionally flat and lives on vol/correlation/skew. Risk management is the job: I run aggregate greeks — net vega by expiry, net dollar-gamma, skew (vanna) and vol-of-vol (volga) exposure — and cap each. Critically I **stress the book to jumps and correlation-to-1**, not just 1-sigma, because the edges (short vol, short correlation, short skew) all lose *together* in a crash — so I keep them small enough and tail-hedged enough that a systemic gap is survivable. I budget **SPAN under a stressed-parameter scenario** (assuming SPAN gets hiked) and run margin at ~60–70% utilisation. I size each strategy to a fraction of capital-at-risk and require them to be as *uncorrelated* as I can make them — the whole point is diversified vol edge, so a bad month in dispersion isn't a bad month everywhere. And I keep a permanent cheap deep-tail hedge as portfolio insurance. The philosophy: harvest multiple small vol edges, hedge direction, and spend real money and discipline making sure the shared left tail can't end the book.
- *Say it crisply:* "I'd run several independent, delta-hedged vol edges — term-structure, dispersion, skew, event crush — cap aggregate vega/gamma/skew greeks, stress to jumps-and-correlation-to-1 (since they all lose together in a crash), keep a permanent tail hedge, and size to a stressed-SPAN margin so a systemic gap is survivable."
- *Follow-up they may ask:* "Biggest hidden risk in that book?" → Correlation *between the strategies* — they look diversified in calm markets but all short the tail, so in a crash they converge and lose together; that's what I stress and hedge for.
