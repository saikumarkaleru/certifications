# Combining TA with Fundamentals & Flows

## The false war between chartists and fundamentalists

Walk into any Indian trading community and you'll hear the tribal chant: "Charts are astrology," say the value investors; "Fundamentals are already in the price," reply the technicians. Both camps are half-right and wholly incomplete. Fundamentals tell you **what** to own and **why** it should be worth more over time — the earnings power, the balance sheet, the moat, the management. Technicals and flows tell you **when** the market is willing to agree with you, and **when** it has changed its mind before the fundamentals have caught up. The two operate on different clocks. A stock can be cheap for three years and expensive in three weeks. If you only read fundamentals you buy early and bleed; if you only read charts you buy strength with no idea whether you're riding a durable re-rating or the last gasp of a pump.

The professional synthesis is neither dogma. It is a **layered decision process**: fundamentals define the universe and the direction of the long-term bias, flows tell you who is actually moving the price and whether smart money agrees, and technicals give you the precise, risk-defined entry, stop and exit. This chapter is about weaving those three strands into one rope for the Indian market — where the flow data is unusually rich and public (FII/DII daily prints, bulk/block deals, promoter pledging, mutual-fund holdings, F&O positioning) and where ignoring it is a genuine, avoidable mistake.

## The three clocks: valuation, positioning, price

Think of any stock as running three clocks at once.

The **valuation clock** is slow. It ticks with quarterly results, guidance, sector cycles, interest rates. Reliance's move into new energy, HDFC Bank's post-merger deposit grind, a PSU bank's asset-quality turn — these are multi-quarter to multi-year stories. Valuation tells you the *terminal* direction and roughly how much upside exists if the thesis plays out. It is nearly useless for timing.

The **positioning/flow clock** is medium-speed. It ticks daily to weekly: FIIs turning net buyers of Indian equities, a mutual fund building a position across a month, promoters pledging or releasing shares, a spike in delivery volume, F&O open-interest build-up. Flow is the bridge between the slow fundamental story and the fast price — it's the footprint of large players acting on (or ahead of) the fundamentals.

The **price/technical clock** is fast. It ticks intraday to weekly: support and resistance, moving averages, breakouts, momentum, volume, VWAP. Price is the only clock you can transact on with precision, and it's the only one that gives you a stop-loss.

Winning trades are usually those where **all three clocks point the same way**: a fundamentally improving business (valuation clock up), that FIIs and funds are accumulating (flow clock up), breaking out of a base on volume (price clock up). Losing trades are clock-conflicts you ignored: a great story with distribution flow and a breaking-down chart (you were early and stubborn), or a beautiful breakout in a fundamentally rotten, promoter-pledged small-cap (you were the exit liquidity).

## Reading Indian flow data

India gives retail traders flow visibility that traders in many markets would envy. Learn to read it.

**FII/DII daily activity.** Every evening the exchanges publish Foreign Institutional Investor and Domestic Institutional Investor net buy/sell in cash and their F&O positioning. FIIs are the marginal price-setter for large-caps and the index; when FIIs turn sustained net sellers (say ₹3,000–₹5,000 crore/day for several sessions), index rallies struggle and even good charts fail. DIIs — mutual funds and insurers fed by relentless SIP inflows — have become the great absorbing force since roughly 2020, often buying the dips FIIs sell. The interplay matters: a market where FIIs sell but DIIs absorb tends to grind sideways-to-down slowly; a market where both buy is a powerful tailwind. Layer this on your index charts: a Nifty breakout with FIIs turning net buyers in cash *and* adding index longs in F&O is a far higher-quality signal than the same breakout on a day FIIs dumped ₹4,000 crore.

**FII F&O positioning — the long/short ratio and index futures.** Beyond cash, the FII long-short ratio in index futures is a positioning gauge. Extremely low ratios (heavy net shorts) at support often precede short-covering rallies; extremely high ratios at resistance warn of crowded longs. This is sentiment you can quantify, and it pairs beautifully with technical extremes.

**Delivery percentage.** For a cash-market stock, the delivery percentage (shares actually taken to demat vs total traded) distinguishes conviction from churn. A breakout on 70% delivery is investors accumulating; the same breakout on 25% delivery is intraday speculation that fades. Rising delivery volume on up-days plus a technical base is the footprint of quiet accumulation.

**Bulk and block deals.** Exchanges publish bulk deals (≥0.5% of shares) and block deals (large negotiated trades) daily. When a respected FII or domestic fund appears as a buyer in bulk deals in a name that's also basing technically, that's independent confirmation of the flow thesis.

**Promoter pledging and holding changes.** Rising promoter pledge is a red flag — it can force selling and signals promoter cash stress. Falling pledge, promoter buying from the open market, or rising institutional holding across quarters are structural positives. These are fundamentals-adjacent flow signals; a chart breakout in a name where promoters are *releasing* pledged shares and FIIs are adding is a much cleaner setup than one where pledge is climbing.

**F&O open interest and option chain.** Covered elsewhere in depth, but in the fusion context: OI tells you where positioning is concentrated and defended. A technical resistance that coincides with heavy call writing is a wall; a support with heavy put writing is a floor. Flow (who's writing/buying) plus price (the level) equals conviction.

## The fusion framework: a checklist that layers the clocks

Here is a practical, repeatable process for a swing or positional trade in an Indian stock.

**Layer 1 — Fundamental gate (is this ownable?).** Is the business fundamentally acceptable for the timeframe I'm trading? For a multi-week positional trade I want: growing or stable earnings, no accounting red flags, manageable debt, low/falling promoter pledge, and ideally a sector with tailwinds. This is a *filter*, not a precision tool — it removes the traps (pledged small-caps, serial diluters, structurally declining businesses) so my chart signals occur on ownable names. I don't need a full DCF; I need "is this a real business or a manipulation vehicle?"

**Layer 2 — Flow confirmation (is smart money agreeing?).** Are institutions accumulating? Check: rising FII/DII interest at the index level for market context; rising institutional holding quarter-on-quarter; healthy delivery percentage; supportive bulk/block deals; benign or falling pledge; constructive F&O positioning. Flow answers "am I early and alone, or is size moving with me?"

**Layer 3 — Technical trigger (when and where exactly?).** Only now do I go to the chart for the precise trade: a base breakout, a pullback to a rising moving average, a support hold with a reversal candle, a momentum thrust. The chart gives me the entry, the stop (invalidation), and the targets. This is the only layer that produces a transactable, risk-defined order.

The order matters. Fundamentals and flow are *filters and context*; technicals are the *trigger and risk*. Reversing the order — trading a chart pattern first and rationalising fundamentals later — is how you end up long the prettiest chart in a fraud.

## A worked India example: the fusion trade

Let me reconstruct a plausible fusion setup (levels approximate, to verify on your own charts). Imagine a large private bank — call it a proxy for a leading lender — through a difficult phase. Fundamentally the story is improving: deposit growth reaccelerating, net interest margin bottoming, asset quality stable, valuation compressed to the low end of its historical price-to-book band. That's the **valuation clock** turning up, but valuation alone has been a value trap for two quarters.

Then the **flow clock** shifts. Over three weeks, quarterly shareholding shows FII stake ticking up; daily FII cash data turns net-positive on Indian financials; delivery percentage in the stock rises on up-days from the mid-30s to the high-50s; a large domestic fund appears as a bulk-deal buyer. In F&O, put writing builds at a strike just below the current price — writers are defining a floor. Smart money is quietly accumulating a fundamentally cheap, improving business.

Finally the **price clock** confirms. The stock, which had been basing in a 1,540–1,620 range for six weeks, closes an hourly and then daily candle above 1,620 on volume 1.6× its 20-day average, with the daily RSI pushing above 60 and price reclaiming a flattening 50-DMA. *Now* you have all three clocks aligned.

The trade: enter on the daily breakout close near 1,628, stop below the breakout structure and the put-writing floor at ~1,588 (roughly 40 points / 2.5% risk), targets at the prior swing high ~1,720 (T1) and a measured-move / re-rating extension toward 1,820 (T2). Position sized so the 40-point risk is 1% of capital. The flow context lets you hold through noise: as long as FIIs keep buying financials and the stock holds its rising 20/50-DMA, a wobble to 1,600 is a hold, not a panic. Suppose it reaches T1 1,720 over two weeks and T2 1,820 over the following month as the re-rating plays out. The chart gave the entry and stop; the flow gave the conviction to hold; the fundamentals gave the reason the move had legs beyond a two-day pop.

Now the counter-example that teaches more. Same beautiful breakout candle, but the flow clock says the opposite: FIIs net sellers of financials all week, delivery percentage *falling* on the up-day (intraday churn, not accumulation), promoter pledge creeping up in a related group entity, and heavy *call* writing capping the very level you're breaking. That "breakout" is far likelier to be a bull trap — a technical pattern with no flow behind it. Same chart, opposite decision, because the other two clocks vetoed it.

## When the clocks disagree — reading the conflict

Conflicts are not noise; they are information. Learn the common patterns.

**Great fundamentals, bad chart, distribution flow.** The market knows something, or the story is a consensus long that's now being distributed to retail. Respect the tape. "The chart is wrong" is the most expensive sentence in investing. Wait for price to confirm the fundamental thesis before committing size.

**Bad/expensive fundamentals, strong chart, strong momentum flow.** A momentum move that can run far in the short term — think of a hot theme or a stock in a speculative frenzy. Tradeable for a technician with a tight stop, but *not ownable* — you're renting momentum, not investing, and you must be ruthless with the exit. Never let a momentum trade in a fundamentally weak name become a "long-term investment" because it went against you.

**Cheap fundamentals, basing chart, accumulation flow, but no trigger yet.** The setup is forming. This is the watchlist zone: put an alert (see the automation chapter) on the breakout level and wait. Anticipation without a trigger is just hoping.

**Everything aligned but the index/market regime is against you.** Even a perfect single-stock fusion setup fights gravity in a market where FIIs are dumping and Nifty is below its 200-DMA. Regime is the master filter: reduce size, tighten stops, or stand aside in hostile markets regardless of how good the individual name looks. The index flow context sits above every single-stock decision.

## Event-driven fusion: earnings, RBI, budget, results season

Some of the sharpest fusion opportunities are event-driven, where a fundamental catalyst meets a technical setup and a flow shift in a compressed window.

**Earnings.** A stock coiled in a tight technical base into results, with rising delivery and institutional accumulation, that then reports a genuine beat and gaps up on volume — the gap is the fundamental catalyst releasing the technical energy that flow had been storing. The fusion trader was positioned or ready, not surprised. Conversely, a stock at technical resistance with crowded longs and stretched valuation into results is asymmetric downside — a miss gaps it down through supports.

**Macro events — RBI policy, Union Budget, Fed.** These reprice whole sectors. A rate-cut cycle turning is a fundamental tailwind for rate-sensitives (banks, autos, real estate); the fusion trader watches for the sector index to confirm technically (e.g., Bank Nifty reclaiming a key moving average) *and* for FII flows to rotate in, then trades the leaders. The budget's capex push shows up first in flow (FIIs rotating into capital goods/infra) and then in the charts of the sector leaders.

**Results season rotation.** Track which sectors are beating and getting bought (flow), map their index charts (technical), and concentrate on names where the fundamental surprise, the flow rotation, and a clean chart converge. This top-down fusion — sector flow first, then stock chart — is how many professional Indian PMS and fund managers actually operate.

## Practical tools and where to get the data

You don't need a Bloomberg terminal. The Indian retail stack: NSE/BSE websites publish FII/DII activity, bulk/block deals, delivery percentages, and shareholding patterns. Moneycontrol, Trendlyne, StockEdge, and Screener.in aggregate fundamentals, holdings, pledging and flow into readable dashboards. StockEdge in particular surfaces FII/DII, delivery, and deal data usefully; Screener.in is the go-to for quick fundamental health and shareholding trends; Trendlyne overlays some flow and technical signals. For F&O positioning, the exchange participant-wise OI data and broker option-chain tools. The workflow: fundamentals and flow research in the evening/weekend on these platforms to build and prune a watchlist, then TradingView/broker charts intraday for the technical trigger and execution.

## Pitfalls

- **Rationalising the chart with fundamentals after entry.** Decide the layers in order — fundamentals/flow as filter, chart as trigger. Don't invent a story to justify a trade you took on a whim.
- **Treating flow data as real-time.** FII/DII cash data is end-of-day; shareholding is quarterly. It's context, not a timing tool — the chart still times.
- **Ignoring regime.** A great single-stock setup in a market where FIIs are dumping and the index is below its 200-DMA is a low-probability fight. Size for the regime.
- **Confusing momentum with investment.** A strong chart in a weak business is a rental with a tight stop, never a hold-forever position.
- **Stale fundamentals.** Yesterday's cheap stock can be today's value trap after a guidance cut. Refresh the fundamental gate around results.
- **Data-mining flow into a story.** One bulk deal is not accumulation; falling pledge in a fraud is still a fraud. Look for a *convergence* of flow signals, not a single cherry-picked one.
- **Over-anchoring to FII flows in small-caps.** FII/DII data is most meaningful for large-caps and the index; small-caps are driven more by delivery, deals, and operator activity.

## Interview-ready summary

Technicals and fundamentals are not rivals; they run on different clocks and answer different questions. Fundamentals (the slow valuation clock) tell you *what* to own and the long-term direction; flows — India's unusually rich public data: FII/DII daily activity, delivery percentages, bulk/block deals, promoter pledging, institutional holding changes, and F&O positioning — are the medium-speed clock that reveals whether smart money agrees; technicals (the fast price clock) give the precise, risk-defined entry, stop and target. The fusion framework layers them in order: a fundamental gate to define an ownable universe and remove traps, flow confirmation that institutions are accumulating and the regime is supportive, and only then a technical trigger for the exact transactable trade. Winning trades usually have all three clocks aligned — an improving business, accumulation flow, and a volume breakout; the sharpest losses come from ignoring a clock conflict, such as a gorgeous chart in a pledged small-cap with distribution flow, where you become the exit liquidity. Regime (index-level FII flow and the 200-DMA) is the master filter above every single-stock call. Fundamentals and flow give you conviction to hold; the chart gives you the stop that keeps you alive.
