# Case Studies: Multi-Month Stock Trends

Individual stocks, unlike indices, deliver the fat trends that make technical traders their living. An index is a portfolio — its extremes are dampened by internal offsetting. A single stock can triple in eight months or halve in three. The technician's job across a multi-month horizon is not to catch the exact bottom or top, but to *recognise a trend early, hold it through noise, add on strength, and exit when the character changes.* This chapter walks through three reconstructed case studies of Indian stocks over multi-month windows, each illustrating a different flavour of trend: a clean stage-2 markup, a base-breakout-then-trend, and a distribution-then-decline. Levels are **approximate reconstructions meant to teach the mechanics** — always verify exact prices, dates and volumes on your own charts before drawing conclusions.

The organising framework across all three is Stan Weinstein's stage analysis, because it maps cleanly onto the multi-month timeframe: **Stage 1** (basing, accumulation, flat 30-week MA), **Stage 2** (advancing, price above rising 30-week MA), **Stage 3** (topping, MA flattens, choppy), **Stage 4** (declining, price below falling 30-week MA). We use the 30-week simple moving average (≈150-day, or roughly the 200-DMA cousin) as the trend spine, weekly candles as the primary canvas, and daily charts for entry timing.

## Why the multi-month timeframe rewards patience

On a 15-minute chart you fight spread, noise, and your own reflexes. On a weekly chart you fight only one enemy: the urge to interfere. The edge in position trading comes from *asymmetry of information decay* — a fundamental re-rating (a margin expansion, a capex cycle turning, a debt reduction) takes many months to fully price in, and the chart front-runs the news flow. When a stock breaks out of a two-year base on volume, it is usually because informed money has been quietly accumulating; the pattern is the footprint. Your job is to read the footprint and size a position you can actually hold.

Position sizing on this timeframe is different. Stops are wide — often 12-20% below entry — so per-trade risk of 0.5-1% of capital means position sizes are modest and you hold few names. The reward is that winners run 40-150%, giving reward:risk of 3:1 to 8:1 that no intraday strategy sustains. The whole game is: **small, survivable losers; occasional enormous winners; never let a winner become a loser.**

## Case Study 1 — A textbook Stage-2 markup (mid-cap capital-goods style)

**Context.** Picture a mid-cap capital-goods / defence-adjacent name — call it "BharatEngg" for the walkthrough — trading sideways between roughly ₹180 and ₹230 for about fourteen months through 2022 into early 2023. The 30-week MA is dead flat near ₹205. Volume is dull, averaging maybe 4-6 lakh shares a day, with occasional spikes on failed rallies that get sold back. This is classic Stage 1: the stock is neither trending nor exciting, which is exactly why most traders ignore it. The sector backdrop — government capex, order-book visibility, PLI tailwinds — is quietly improving but the price hasn't confirmed anything yet.

**The setup.** In March-April 2023 the character changes. Price pushes to the top of the range near ₹230 for the third time, but now the down-weeks show shrinking volume while up-weeks swell. This *volume-price divergence in favour of buyers* is the first tell. Then a weekly candle closes decisively above ₹235 on volume roughly 3x the 10-week average — the breakout bar. The 30-week MA, flat for a year, begins to curl up. Weinstein's Stage 1-to-2 transition is now on the tape: price above a turning-up 30-week MA on expanding volume.

**The trade.** The disciplined entry is not the breakout candle itself (too extended, stop too far) but the *first pullback that holds the breakout level.* Over the following three weeks BharatEngg rallies to ₹258, then pulls back to ₹238-240 — just above the old ₹235 resistance, which now acts as support (polarity flip). Volume dries up on the pullback, another confirmation. Entry near ₹242 on the daily chart as it turns up from the retest.

| Parameter | Value | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Entry | ~₹242 | Retest of ₹235 breakout, volume-dry pullback holds |
| Initial stop | ~₹212 (below 30-wk MA & base high) | ~12% risk; a close back inside the base invalidates thesis |
| Position risk | 0.75% of capital | Wide stop demands small size |
| First target | ~₹300 | Measured move: base height (₹230−₹180 = ₹50) projected, plus round-number magnet |
| Trail method | Weekly close below 10-week EMA | Lets the trend breathe; ignores daily noise |

**The outcome.** Over the next five months BharatEngg trends from ₹242 to roughly ₹430 — a Stage-2 markup of about 78%. It never once closes a *week* below its rising 10-week EMA until the very end. There are three scary-looking daily pullbacks of 8-11% along the way; each finds support at or just above the 10-week EMA and resumes. The 30-week MA rises steadily beneath, acting as the ultimate floor. A trader who added a second tranche on the ₹300 breakout (the round number that was the first target) with a stop trailed to breakeven on the first lot compounds the return meaningfully.

**The exit.** In the sixth month the character changes again. Price makes a new high near ₹448 but on *declining* weekly volume, and the next two weeks produce large upper wicks (selling into strength). Then a weekly candle closes below the 10-week EMA for the first time in the entire advance, near ₹405. That is the trail-stop trigger. Exit ₹400-405. Booked move: roughly ₹242 → ₹402, about **66% on the core lot** over ~six months, with the add-on lot contributing more.

**The lesson.** Three things made this trade: (1) *waiting for the Stage 1→2 confirmation* rather than bottom-fishing the range; (2) *entering the retest, not the breakout,* which kept the stop tight relative to the eventual move; (3) *trailing on weekly closes,* which filtered out the three daily shakeouts that would have stopped out a jumpier trader. The single biggest edge was doing nothing during the eleven boring months of Stage 1 and only acting when the tape confirmed. Note also the honesty: the exit gave back ~₹45 from the high. You never sell the top. Selling the *change of character* is the achievable goal.

## Case Study 2 — Base breakout after a long consolidation (large-cap turnaround style)

**Context.** Now a large-cap that has been a serial disappointment — think of a heavyweight that de-rated hard in 2018-2020, fell from grace, and then spent two-plus years building a long, rounded base. Call it "MetroFin." Price bottomed around ₹150 in the pandemic panic, then chopped between ₹150 and ₹210 for the better part of two years. The 30-week MA is flat-to-gently-rising near ₹185. On the weekly chart the base looks like a saucer — a slow rounding bottom — with the right lip forming as price presses against ₹210 repeatedly through 2023.

The fundamental subtext (which the technician does not need to know but which *explains* the footprint): asset quality is cleaning up, credit costs are normalising, and ROE is inflecting. FIIs are quietly rebuilding positions. On the chart, this shows as *higher lows* within the base and rising up-week volume through 2023 — accumulation.

**The setup.** The key level is ₹210, the top of the two-year base and the neckline of the saucer. In late 2023 MetroFin approaches ₹210 for the fourth time, but the approach is different: the base of the last swing was ₹192 (a higher low versus the prior ₹178), and the up-volume is expanding. A weekly close above ₹214 on heavy volume — roughly 2.5x the 10-week average — completes the base breakout. Crucially, this is a *large-cap* breakout, so it tends to be less explosive but more durable than a mid-cap; you trade it with patience, not with a hair-trigger.

**The trade.** Large-cap breakouts often don't give a clean retest — institutions accumulate on any dip and the stock grinds. So here we use a *two-part entry*: a starter position on the breakout close near ₹216, and a planned add on either a shallow pullback to ₹205-208 (retest of neckline) or on the next higher-low continuation.

| Parameter | Value | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Starter entry | ~₹216 | Weekly close above ₹214 base breakout, 2.5x volume |
| Add entry | ~₹207 | Neckline retest OR next continuation higher-low |
| Initial stop | ~₹188 (below 30-wk MA & higher-low ₹192) | ~13% from starter; a weekly close below ₹188 breaks the higher-low structure |
| Position risk | 1.0% of capital total | Split across two entries |
| First target | ~₹260 | Base depth (₹210−₹150 = ₹60) projected off ₹210 neckline |
| Second target | ~₹300 | Round number + prior-cycle supply zone from 2018 |
| Trail method | Weekly close below 30-week MA | Large-caps trend slowly; give it room |

**The outcome.** MetroFin does exactly what durable large-cap breakouts do: it grinds. There is a shallow retest to ₹206 within three weeks (the add fills), then a slow, relentless climb. It takes about nine months to travel from ₹216 to ₹295 — only about 37% — but the *path* is smooth. The stock respects its rising 30-week MA the entire way, never closing a week below it. Drawdowns are shallow (max ~7% peak-to-trough on the weekly close). For a large-cap held with a full position and no leverage, a 37% move over nine months with tiny drawdowns is an excellent risk-adjusted outcome — arguably better than the mid-cap's 66% with its 11% shakeouts, because you can size it larger and sleep at night.

**The exit.** Near ₹298 the stock stalls just under the 2018-era supply zone (~₹300-310). Two weekly dojis form at the highs, then a distribution week — a wide-range down bar on heavy volume closing near its low, around ₹278. This is the first *high-volume down week* of the entire advance, the character change. It doesn't yet close below the 30-week MA (which sits near ₹250), so a mechanical trailer would still hold. But a discretionary trader reading the tape can book at least partial profits near ₹280-285 into the obvious supply, keeping a runner with the 30-week MA stop.

**The lesson.** Large-caps and mid-caps require *different exit philosophies.* The mid-cap in Case 1 was volatile, so a 10-week EMA trail fit its rhythm. The large-cap here trends slowly and smoothly, so a 10-week EMA trail would have whipped you out prematurely on the normal grind; the 30-week MA is the right spine. **Match the trailing tool to the instrument's volatility.** The second lesson: the *retest that fills your add* is a gift large-caps give more reliably than mid-caps, because institutional demand cushions dips. Plan for it. The third: prior-cycle supply zones (here the 2018 distribution) are real overhead resistance even years later — old holders waiting to "get out even" create genuine selling, so treat multi-year highs and old tops as legitimate target zones, not points to chase.

## Case Study 3 — Distribution and Stage-4 decline (avoiding the falling knife)

Not every multi-month study is a long. The most capital that technicians *save* comes from recognising Stage 3 topping and Stage 4 decline early — either to exit longs or, for those permitted, to position short via futures. This case is about *what a top looks like* and how to avoid the seductive "it's cheap now" trap.

**Context.** Imagine a high-flying mid-cap — "GlamRetail" — that had a spectacular 2023 run from ₹300 to ₹720, a Stage-2 beauty. By early 2024 it is the darling of every WhatsApp group. The 30-week MA is rising steeply near ₹560. Everyone "knows" it's going to ₹1000. This is precisely the emotional environment in which tops form: maximum optimism, maximum retail participation, valuations stretched.

**The setup (the top).** The first warning is *momentum divergence.* Price makes a marginal new high near ₹735 in February 2024, but the weekly RSI prints a lower high (say 68 vs the prior 78) and volume on the new high is *lower* than on the prior peak. Effort is rising, result is fading. Then the character changes: a wide-range weekly down bar on the heaviest volume in months closes near ₹640 — a clear *distribution* bar. Price bounces feebly to ₹690 (a lower high) on weak volume, then rolls over. The 30-week MA, still rising, is about to be tested from above near ₹575.

**The confirmation (Stage 3 → 4).** In April a weekly candle closes decisively below the 30-week MA at ₹565 — the first weekly close below the MA in over a year. Within two weeks the 30-week MA itself flattens and begins to roll down. Lower highs and lower lows are now established on the weekly chart. This is Stage 4. The stock that "everyone knows" is going to ₹1000 is now in a confirmed downtrend.

| Signal | Reading | Action implication |
|---|---|---|
| RSI lower high at new price high | Bearish momentum divergence | Tighten stops on longs; no new longs |
| High-volume wide-range down week | Distribution | Exit remaining longs |
| Lower high on weak volume | Failed rally, supply in control | Confirmed Stage 3 top |
| Weekly close below rising 30-wk MA | Stage 3→4 transition | Trend is now down; short candidates only |
| 30-wk MA rolls over | Stage 4 confirmed | Rallies are shorting opportunities |

**The trap.** Here is where retail loses money. At ₹565, GlamRetail is "30% off its high — a bargain." Bottom-fishers pile in. But in Stage 4, *every bounce is a distribution opportunity for those still trapped long,* and the stock grinds lower: ₹565 → ₹520 → a bounce to ₹560 (lower high) → ₹470 → a bounce to ₹500 → ₹410. Over four months it bleeds from ₹640 to roughly ₹400, a 37% decline, punctuated by exactly the kind of sharp 12-15% bounces that suck in "the bottom is in" buyers and then fail. Each bounce fails at or below a *falling* 30-week MA. **A falling 30-week MA is a ceiling, not a floor.**

**The trade (for those who can short).** The clean short is not at the first breakdown (too close to the recent uptrend, risk of a violent snap-back) but on the *first lower-high rejection under the rolling-over 30-week MA.* Bounce to ₹560 stalls under the flattening MA; a bearish daily reversal there offers a short with a stop above the MA/swing high near ₹585.

| Parameter | Value | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Short entry | ~₹555 | Lower-high rejection under rolling 30-wk MA |
| Stop | ~₹588 (above MA & swing high) | ~6% risk; a reclaim of the MA invalidates the short |
| First target | ~₹470 | Prior swing low / measured move |
| Second target | ~₹410 | Round-number & next weekly demand shelf |
| Cover trigger | Weekly close back above 30-wk MA, or target | Trend-change back to neutral |

Shorting works to ₹470 (first target, book partial) and ₹410 (second). Reward:risk on the first leg alone is roughly (₹555−₹470)/(₹588−₹555) ≈ 2.6:1, improving to ~4.4:1 at the second target. Note the honesty: shorting Indian cash equity intraday-to-positional is constrained (no overnight short in cash segment), so this is realistically a *stock-futures* trade with its own margin, rollover cost, and the risk of a violent short-covering rally — which is why the stop discipline matters even more than on the long side.

**The lesson.** The dominant lesson is *not* how to short — most position traders never short. It is how to *avoid the falling knife.* The single rule "**do not buy a stock trading below a falling 30-week MA, no matter how cheap it looks**" would have saved every bottom-fisher in this study. Cheapness is not a catalyst. A downtrend ends only when it *stops going down* — when price stops making lower lows, the 30-week MA flattens, and a new Stage 1 base forms. That can take *months to years.* The patient technician waits for the next Stage 1→2 setup (as in Cases 1 and 2) rather than trying to catch the exact low. Missing the bottom by 15% and buying a confirmed new uptrend beats catching a "bottom" that turns out to be a ledge on the way down.

## Cross-cutting principles from the three studies

**1. The 30-week MA is your trend arbiter.** In all three cases the relationship of price to a rising/flat/falling 30-week MA defined the stage and the bias. Above and rising = own it. Below and falling = avoid or short. Flat = wait. This single relationship resolves 80% of position-trading decisions.

**2. Volume is the lie-detector.** Breakouts need expanding volume (Cases 1, 2); tops reveal themselves through high-volume distribution and low-volume rallies (Case 3). Price tells you *what*; volume tells you *whether to believe it.*

**3. Enter on retests, size for wide stops, trail on the right timeframe.** The retest entry (Cases 1, 2) improves reward:risk. Because position stops are wide (10-15%), per-trade risk must be small (0.5-1%). And the trailing tool must match the instrument's volatility — 10-week EMA for jumpy mid-caps, 30-week MA for grinding large-caps.

**4. You will never sell the top or buy the bottom.** Every exit in these studies gave back something from the extreme. That is *correct.* Selling the change-of-character — the first high-volume down week, the first weekly close below the trailing MA — is the achievable, repeatable edge.

**5. Match the vehicle to the personality.** A ₹0.75% risk on a mid-cap with 11% shakeouts and a full-size position on a smooth-grinding large-cap can produce similar rupee returns with very different psychological demands. Know which one you can actually hold.

## Interview-ready summary

- *Multi-month stock trends* are best framed through **Weinstein stage analysis** using the **30-week MA** as the trend spine: Stage 1 base, Stage 2 markup, Stage 3 top, Stage 4 decline.
- The **highest-probability long** is a **Stage 1→2 breakout on expanding volume**, entered on the **retest** of the breakout level (polarity flip), with a stop below the base/30-week MA and per-trade risk of 0.5-1% because stops are wide.
- **Mid-caps trend fast and jerky** — trail on the **10-week EMA**; **large-caps grind smooth and durable** — trail on the **30-week MA**. Match the trailing tool to volatility.
- **Tops** announce themselves via **momentum divergence, high-volume distribution weeks, and low-volume failing rallies**, confirmed by a **weekly close below a rolling-over 30-week MA** (Stage 3→4).
- The costliest retail error is **buying a falling knife**: *never buy below a falling 30-week MA regardless of how "cheap" a stock looks* — cheapness is not a catalyst; wait for the next Stage 1 base.
- Across all cases, **you never sell the top or buy the bottom**; you sell/cover the **change of character** and accept giving back a slice from the extreme. Small survivable losers plus occasional large winners is the whole game.
