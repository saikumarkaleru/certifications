# V-Patterns, Pipe Tops/Bottoms & Spikes

Most reversals are polite. They give you a head-and-shoulders that takes three weeks to form, a double bottom you can see coming from a mile away, a rounding base that lets you accumulate at leisure. And then there are the reversals that slap you in the face — the ones with no right shoulder, no second test, no handle. Price is falling hard, and then in a single session or two it simply stops and rockets the other way. No base. No consolidation. Just a violent hinge.

These are V-patterns, pipe tops and bottoms, and spikes. They are the reversals of panic and euphoria, and in the Indian market — where retail leverage, F&O expiry mechanics, and news-driven gaps are everywhere — they are far more common than the textbooks admit. Bank Nifty in particular is a serial producer of V-bottoms. If you only know how to trade rounded, patient reversals, you will miss half the turns the index actually makes.

This chapter is about the fast reversals: what they are, why they form, how to trade the second chance (because the first move is almost always ungrabbable), and how to avoid the trap of buying a "V" that turns out to be the first leg of a much bigger fall.

## What they are and why they work

A **V-bottom** is a sharp decline followed by an equally sharp advance, forming the shape of the letter V. There is no accumulation base — the low is a single point (or a two-to-three bar cluster) rather than a zone. A **V-top** (inverted V, sometimes called an "A" or spike top) is the mirror: a steep run-up that reverses just as steeply.

A **pipe bottom** is a specific two-bar version, usually seen on weekly charts (Thomas Bulkowski's original definition used weeklies). Two adjacent tall down-then-up spikes with long lower shadows, side by side, resembling a pair of pipes or a tuning fork. The two long tails mark a decisive rejection of lower prices across two periods. A **pipe top** is the inverted twin — two tall adjacent up-spikes with long upper shadows.

A **spike** (or "extended spike") is the single-bar cousin — one bar with an abnormally long tail that pokes far beyond the recent range and closes back near the opposite end. A hammer or shooting star on steroids, usually created by an intraday flush or a stop-run.

Why do these work when they work? Because they are the signature of **exhaustion, not distribution**. A rounded top forms when smart money quietly distributes over weeks. A V-top forms when the last buyers pile in all at once — an emotional blow-off — and then there is simply no one left to buy. The fuel runs out instantly, so the reversal is instant. Same on the downside: a V-bottom is a **capitulation** low. Forced sellers (margin calls, stop-losses, panicked retail, algo liquidation) dump everything into a vacuum. Once the forced selling is exhausted, there is no natural selling pressure left, and price snaps back to where value buyers sit.

The key intuition: **the sharper the move in, the sharper the potential move out — because sharp moves are driven by forced participants, and forced participants finish quickly.** A slow grind down has real sellers who will keep selling on bounces. A vertical crash has margin calls, and margin calls end.

## Mechanics: how to identify each variant

**V-bottom — the checklist:**

1. A steep, near-vertical decline into the low, typically covering more ground in a few sessions than the prior several weeks.
2. A single low (or 2–3 bar cluster) — no lateral base.
3. A reversal bar at the low: a hammer, bullish engulfing, or a long-tailed spike closing in the upper third.
4. An equally steep advance off the low, ideally reclaiming a prior support-turned-resistance level fast.
5. **Volume climax** at the low — the highest volume bar of the recent decline prints at or near the bottom. This is the tell that separates a real capitulation from just another down day.

**Pipe bottom — the checklist (weekly, but works on daily too):**

1. Two adjacent bars, both with long lower shadows extending well below the surrounding price.
2. The two lows are roughly equal (within a small band).
3. Both bars poke below the prior consolidation — they are *spikes*, standing out from neighbours.
4. Confirmation: price closes above the higher of the two pipe tops in the following period.
5. High volume on at least one of the two pipe bars.

**Spike (single bar):**

1. One bar with a tail at least 2x the length of its body, poking beyond recent range.
2. Close in the opposite third from the tail (long lower tail → close in upper third).
3. Often coincides with a round number, a prior swing low, or a VWAP/anchored level being stabbed and rejected.

The common thread is the **long tail = rejection**. The market went there, found no acceptance, and left violently. In Wyckoff terms, a V-bottom is often a **spring** (a stab below support that immediately reverses), and a V-top is often an **upthrust** (a stab above resistance that immediately fails). That framing is useful — it tells you the level that was violated is the level to watch on the retest.

## Worked India example: the Bank Nifty V-bottom (₹ and levels)

Picture Bank Nifty in a fast correction — the kind that happens two or three times a year. Say the index has been sliding from around 48,500 and over five sessions it accelerates: 48,200, 47,600, 47,100, then a gap-down open and an intraday flush to **45,850** on a day when the news wires are full of global-risk-off headlines and the India VIX spikes from 13 to 19.

That flush session is the tell. The index opens near 46,600, sells straight down to 45,850 by 10:30, and then — instead of drifting lower into the close as it had every prior day — it reverses. By 15:15 it closes at **46,720**, back above the open, printing a bullish engulfing on the daily and by far the highest volume (and highest futures turnover) of the entire decline. That is a **capitulation bar**: forced sellers dumped into the morning, the flush cleared them, and value buyers (and short-covering) took it straight back up.

The next session it gaps up and runs to 47,300. Within four sessions it's back at 48,000 — the V is complete, and it retraced the entire five-day decline in four days. That asymmetry (down slow-ish, up fast) is classic.

Now — **how do you actually trade this without trying to catch the falling knife at 45,850?** You don't buy the low. You buy the **confirmation**: the reclaim of a reference level. In this example, 47,100 was the prior swing that broke on the way down. When price gaps up and reclaims 47,100 with the momentum intact, that reclaim — support-broken-then-reclaimed — is a far higher-probability entry than the tail itself. You give up the first ~1,200 points of the bounce but you trade with confirmation instead of hope.

For the option trader: the V-bottom is where you *sell* the fear. As VIX spiked from 13 to 19, put premiums ballooned. A trader reading the capitulation bar could sell out-of-the-money puts (say the 45,500 puts) or run a bull put spread, collecting the inflated premium. When the V confirms and VIX collapses back toward 14, the volatility crush alone hands you a large chunk of the profit — you win on direction *and* on vega. This is one of the cleanest edges around a V-bottom: the pattern and the volatility regime line up.

## Worked India example: the pipe top in a single stock

Take a mid-cap that's had a euphoric run — say a defence or railway PSU that doubled in three months on order-book excitement. On the weekly chart it prints two adjacent weeks with long upper shadows: week one rallies to an intraweek high of ~₹840 but closes back at ₹770; week two again pokes to ~₹835 but closes ₹760. Two pipes, two rejections of the ₹835–840 zone, both on huge weekly volume.

That is distribution disguised as strength. Twice the crowd tried to push it through ₹840 and twice it got hammered back. The confirmation comes when the following week closes below ₹760 (the lower of the two pipe bodies). From there the stock rolls over — and because there was no base built on the way up (it was a vertical run), there is no support on the way down either. It can retrace to the ₹600 breakout shelf quickly. Pipe tops on parabolic stocks are among the most reliable "get out now" signals in the Indian small/mid-cap space, where these vertical runs and vertical unwinds are a way of life.

## How to trade it: entry, stop, target

The golden rule for all fast reversals: **trade the second chance, not the first move.** The tail itself is un-tradeable in real time — you don't know it's a tail until the bar closes, and by then price has moved. So you have two clean, rule-based entries:

**Entry method A — the confirmation reclaim.** After the spike/V, wait for price to reclaim the nearest broken reference (a prior swing low for a V-bottom, prior swing high for a V-top). Enter on the reclaim close. Stop goes below the spike low (V-bottom) or above the spike high (V-top) plus a small buffer. Target: the origin of the sharp move (V's tend to fully retrace their own decline) and then the next structural level.

**Entry method B — the retest of the tail.** Sometimes price doesn't gap away — it bounces off the V, pulls back, and retests the spike low without breaking it (a higher low). This second, gentler touch is a lower-risk entry: your stop is tight (just below the tested low) and you get confirmation that the low is holding. This is the Wyckoff "test of the spring." It's the highest-quality entry when you get it, but you don't always get it — strong V's just run.

**Sizing the stop.** These patterns give you a *natural, tight stop* — the extreme of the spike. That's the gift of a V: the invalidation is crystal clear. If price closes back below the V-bottom low, the pattern has failed; you're out, no debate. Because the stop is tight and well-defined, V-patterns can offer excellent risk-reward even though the entry is "late."

| Element | V-bottom (long) | V-top / pipe top (short) |
|---|---|---|
| Trigger | Reclaim of prior broken swing OR successful retest of the spike low | Loss of prior swing OR failed retest of the spike high |
| Stop | Below the spike low + buffer | Above the spike high + buffer |
| Target 1 | Origin of the decline (full V retrace) | Origin of the rally |
| Target 2 | Next structural resistance / measured move | Next structural support |
| Timeframe | Daily entries; 15-min for intraday V's | Daily / weekly for pipes |
| Best regime | After a fast, news-driven flush with volume climax | After a parabolic, exhausted run |
| Confluence | VIX spike + reversal, oversold RSI, put-side max pain | VIX complacency, overbought RSI, call writers stacking |

## Confluence — including OI and options

A V-pattern in isolation is a coin-flip-plus. Stacked with confluence it becomes a genuine edge. Look for:

- **Volume climax** — non-negotiable. The best V-bottoms print the highest volume of the whole decline *at* the low. No volume spike = suspect the move is just a pause, not a capitulation.
- **VIX behaviour** — for index V-bottoms, a VIX that spikes hard and then reverses down alongside price is powerful confirmation. Fear peaked and is now unwinding. A V-bottom on a *falling* VIX is less trustworthy.
- **RSI / momentum divergence** — the low often prints on a higher RSI low than the prior swing (bullish divergence). Momentum was already fading even as price made a new low.
- **Option chain / OI (index & F&O stocks):**
  - At a genuine capitulation low, look for **put writers stepping in** — a build-up of open interest on the put side at strikes just below the spike low tells you institutions are willing to sell insurance there; they think the bottom will hold. If puts are being *bought* aggressively (OI up on rising premium) into the low, the fear isn't done.
  - **Max pain** shifting up over the next session or two after the spike confirms the market is re-anchoring higher.
  - For a pipe/V *top*, watch **call writers piling into** the strike that got rejected. Heavy call OI stacking at the pipe high, combined with the rejection, says the smart money is capping the move there.
- **Level confluence** — a V that bottoms exactly at a prior breakout shelf, a 200-DMA, a big round number (Nifty at a round 22,000; Bank Nifty at 46,000), or a weekly demand zone is far more trustworthy than one that bottoms in mid-air.

The mental model: the pattern gives you the *shape*, the volume/VIX gives you the *emotion*, and the OI gives you the *positioning of the people on the other side*. When all three agree, you have a trade.

## Pitfalls — where V-traders get destroyed

**1. Catching the knife.** The single biggest killer. A steep decline *looks* like it must be near a bottom — until it isn't. There is no rule that says a fast fall reverses; sometimes it's just the first leg of a crash. Never buy inside the falling spike. Wait for the reversal bar to *close*, then wait for confirmation. "It's fallen so much it has to bounce" is not a thesis; it's a prayer.

**2. The dead-cat bounce dressed as a V.** A sharp fall often produces a sharp *bounce* that is not a reversal at all — it's a relief rally inside a downtrend that then rolls over to new lows (covered in depth in the next chapter). The distinguishing test: a true V-bottom *reclaims* structure and holds; a dead-cat bounce stalls at the underside of broken support and fails. If your "V" can't get back above the level it broke, be very suspicious.

**3. No volume, no capitulation.** A V-shape on limp volume is usually just noise — a wiggle that will be overrun. Demand the climax.

**4. Pipe tops on the wrong timeframe.** Pipes are a weekly/higher-timeframe pattern. Two long-shadow bars on a 5-minute chart during a choppy afternoon mean nothing. Respect the timeframe the pattern was defined for.

**5. Expiry distortion (India-specific).** On Bank Nifty/Nifty expiry days, intraday spikes and V's are routinely manufactured by option-writing mechanics and gamma pins, not by genuine directional conviction. A vicious V-bottom at 2pm on expiry may simply be a squeeze to a max-pain level, fully reversing the next day. Discount intraday spikes on expiry; they carry far less predictive weight than a spike on a normal trending session.

**6. Chasing the fourth day.** By the time the V is obvious to everyone, most of the move is done. If you missed the reclaim, don't chase three days later into resistance. Wait for the next setup — a pullback, a base, a fresh trigger.

**7. Widening the stop after entry.** The V's gift is a tight, clear invalidation. The moment you "give it room" and move your stop below the spike low, you've thrown away the only edge the pattern offered. If it breaks the low, the pattern is void — respect it.

## Interview-ready summary

A **V-pattern** is a sharp, base-less reversal — a steep decline (or advance) that hinges violently the other way, forming the letter V. It is the signature of **exhaustion**: forced sellers (margin calls, stops, panic) or forced buyers (blow-off euphoria) finish all at once, leaving a vacuum that price snaps back through. A **pipe bottom/top** is the two-bar, long-shadow version, best read on weekly charts; a **spike** is the single-bar version — a hammer or shooting star that stabs beyond range and gets rejected.

They work because **the sharper the move in, the sharper the potential move out** — vertical moves are driven by participants who *must* act, and forced participants exhaust quickly. The essential confirmations are a **volume climax at the extreme**, a **VIX spike-and-reverse** for index turns, momentum divergence, and supportive option-chain positioning (put writers defending a V-bottom; call writers capping a V-top).

You never trade the tail. You trade the **second chance**: the reclaim of broken structure, or the successful retest of the spike. The pattern's beauty is a **tight, unambiguous stop** at the spike's extreme — invalidation is clean. Its danger is the **falling knife** and the **dead-cat bounce** that masquerades as a bottom. In the Indian market, Bank Nifty is the archetypal V-machine, mid-cap PSUs are pipe-top factories, and expiry-day spikes must be heavily discounted. Respect volume, respect the level, respect the stop — and let the confirmation, not the hope, put you in the trade.
