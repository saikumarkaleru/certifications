# Setups: Reversals (5 setups)

Reversal trading is the most seductive and the most punishing style in the book. Nothing feels smarter than buying the exact low or shorting the exact high, and nothing empties an account faster than repeatedly "catching the falling knife" on a trend that refuses to die. The professional attitude toward reversals is therefore paradoxical: you must be simultaneously eager and sceptical. Eager, because the best risk-reward trades in the market are located at the moment a crowd has committed hard in one direction and is about to be proven wrong. Sceptical, because 70-80% of "reversal" signals in an ongoing trend are just pauses that resolve back the original way.

The unifying principle across all five setups below is this: **a reversal is not a price event, it is an exhaustion event.** Price making a new low is nothing. Price making a new low on collapsing momentum, shrinking participation, a failed breakdown, or a climactic volume spike that gets absorbed — that is something. We are not buying "cheap." We are buying the point at which the sellers have run out of ammunition and the last weak longs have already been flushed. Every rule below is engineered to make you wait for evidence of exhaustion rather than pay for the hope of it.

A word on regime before the rules. Reversal setups have the highest edge when the broader regime is choppy, range-bound, or late-stage-trending, and the *lowest* edge in the first two-thirds of a strong trend. In a runaway Nifty bull leg with the index above a rising 20-DEMA and India VIX under 13, fading strength is a donation. Save these setups for extended moves, for tests of major support/resistance, and for divergent conditions. Position size on reversals should be smaller than on continuation trades — half a unit is sensible — because your stop, by definition, sits against a crowd that may not be done yet.

## Setup 1 — The Failed Breakdown (Spring Reversal)

This is the highest-quality reversal pattern in the entire toolkit because it uses the crowd's own stop-losses as fuel. A stock or index grinds sideways in a defined range for days or weeks. Everyone can see the support line. Breakout-sellers stack shorts just below it; longs place stops just below it. Price then pierces support — triggering the shorts' entries and the longs' stops — and *immediately* reverses back above the broken level. The breakdown has failed. All the traders who sold the breakdown are now trapped offside, and their covering becomes rocket fuel for the reversal.

Wyckoff called the bullish version a "spring" and the bearish version an "upthrust." The logic is identical: a false move beyond an obvious level that snaps back. The false move is not a failure of the pattern; it is the pattern. The deeper and faster the reclaim, the more trapped traders there are and the more violent the reversal.

**Worked India example.** Imagine Tata Motors basing between roughly ₹960 and ₹1,000 for three weeks in a corrective market. On a weak morning it gaps and slides to ₹948, breaking the ₹960 floor that every chart-watcher had drawn. Volume spikes as stops trigger. But within the same session buyers absorb the supply, and price closes back at ₹972 — above the broken support, forming a long lower wick. That daily candle is the spring. The reclaim of ₹960 confirms the failed breakdown.

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| Trigger | Price breaks below defined range support, then closes back *above* the support level within 1-2 candles |
| Entry | On the close that reclaims support (₹972), or on a next-day push above the reversal-candle high |
| Stop | Below the low of the false break (below ₹948), the swing extreme |
| Target | T1 = top of the range (₹1,000); T2 = measured-move = range height projected up (₹1,000 + 40 = ₹1,040) |
| Timeframe | Daily for swing; 15-min for intraday springs off VWAP/PDL |
| Regime | Range-bound or late in a down-leg near major support; avoid in free-fall |

**Confluence.** The spring is strongest when the false break coincides with a higher-timeframe support (a 200-DEMA, a previous major swing low, a round number). Volume behaviour is diagnostic: ideal is a volume *spike on the break* (stops firing) followed by *strong volume on the reclaim* (buyers stepping in). In F&O names, check the option chain — if the failed breakdown coincides with heavy Put writing appearing at the strike just below support (say the 950 PE seeing large OI addition as price reclaims), that is smart money marking the floor. Falling India VIX during the reclaim adds conviction.

**Pitfalls.** The killer is the *slow bleed* that never reclaims — you buy the "spring" but price closes below support and keeps going. That is not a spring, it is a breakdown, and your discipline is the stop below the false low. Never anticipate the reclaim; wait for the close back inside. Second pitfall: springs in strong downtrends have low odds. A failed breakdown at the third lower-low of a brutal bear leg is often just a bounce. Demand that the range be genuinely established (multiple touches) before trusting the spring.

## Setup 2 — Momentum Divergence Reversal (RSI/Price)

Price and momentum should agree. When price makes a higher high but the RSI makes a lower high, the second push up is being achieved with less internal force than the first — buyers are getting tired even as the tape looks strong. This is bearish divergence. The mirror image, price making a lower low while RSI makes a higher low, is bullish divergence and warns that selling is exhausting. Divergence does not *cause* reversals; it *describes* the fading fuel that precedes them. That distinction matters enormously for how you trade it.

The cardinal rule: **divergence is a warning, not a trigger.** A stock can print bearish divergence and keep grinding higher for two weeks. You never short on divergence alone. You wait for divergence *plus* a price-structure trigger — a break of a short-term trendline, a lower high confirmed, a swing low taken out. Divergence tells you *where* to look; price tells you *when* to act.

**Worked India example.** Bank Nifty rallies into a major top. It makes a swing high at 52,400 with the 14-period daily RSI at 74. A week later it grinds to a marginally higher high of 52,650 but RSI only reaches 66. Price up, momentum down — classic bearish divergence at an extended level. You do nothing yet. The trigger comes when price breaks the intervening swing low at 51,800. Now momentum and structure agree, and you short.

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| Trigger | Divergence forms (price HH / RSI LH for shorts; price LL / RSI HL for longs) AND price breaks the intervening swing pivot |
| Entry | On the break of the swing pivot (51,800 short) with a confirming candle |
| Stop | Above the divergent high (52,650 + buffer) for shorts; below the divergent low for longs |
| Target | T1 = prior swing low / structural support; T2 = 1.5-2x the risk, or the next HTF level |
| Timeframe | Daily and 1-hour for swings; use 5/15-min for intraday index reversals |
| Regime | Extended/late-stage trends; near major S/R; NOT in early trend |

**Confluence.** The most reliable divergences occur at higher-timeframe resistance or support, not in the middle of nowhere. Bank Nifty diverging into a prior all-time-high zone is far more actionable than diverging mid-range. Stack it with candlestick exhaustion (a shooting star or bearish engulfing at the divergent high) and with breadth: if the index makes a new high but the advance-decline and the percentage of stocks above their 20-DMA are deteriorating, that is divergence at the market-internals level confirming divergence at the price level. On the option chain, watch for aggressive Call writing appearing at and just above the divergent high — writers are betting the top is in.

**Pitfalls.** In powerful trends, momentum "diverges" repeatedly and every signal fails — a strong uptrend routinely prints three or four bearish divergences before it actually tops. This is why the standalone divergence has such a poor reputation. The fix is non-negotiable: require the price-structure break, keep size small, and honour the stop above the divergent extreme. Also beware "hidden" versus "regular" divergence confusion — regular divergence signals reversal; hidden divergence (price HL / RSI LL in an uptrend) signals *continuation*. Know which one you are looking at.

## Setup 3 — Climax Reversal (Volume/Range Exhaustion)

A selling climax is the moment panic peaks. After an extended decline, price gaps down or accelerates lower into a vertical drop, and volume explodes to a multiple of the average — the last frightened holders are dumping at any price and margin calls are firing. Precisely because *everyone who was going to sell has now sold*, supply dries up and price snaps back hard. The buying climax is the bullish-market mirror: a euphoric vertical spike on gigantic volume that marks the top when the last greedy buyer has bought.

The tell is the combination of three things at once: an extended prior move, a range-expansion candle (the day's range far larger than recent days), and a volume spike far above average, followed by an immediate rejection wick or reversal candle. One without the others is not a climax. A big volume day in the middle of a calm range is nothing; a big-range down day on huge volume after a month-long slide, closing well off its lows, is a potential selling climax.

**Worked India example.** Take a beaten-down midcap — say a stock that has fallen from ₹640 to ₹430 over six weeks. On result-day disappointment it gaps to ₹412 and cascades to ₹388 intraday on volume 5x its 20-day average — a capitulation. Then buyers appear; it closes at ₹431, printing a huge lower wick and a bullish outside day. That is a selling climax. The reversal entry is a break above the climax candle's high.

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| Trigger | Extended move + range-expansion candle + volume ≥ 3x average + immediate rejection (long wick / reversal close) |
| Entry | Break above the climax candle high (for bottoms); break below climax high (for tops) |
| Stop | Beyond the climax extreme (below ₹388 for the long) |
| Target | T1 = first supply zone / prior breakdown level; scale out — climaxes give sharp but often incomplete recoveries |
| Timeframe | Daily for the classic pattern; works on 5-min for intraday flush-and-reverse |
| Regime | End of extended trends, panic events, event-driven gaps |

**Confluence.** India VIX behaviour is the single best confirmer of a market-level selling climax. A capitulation low in Nifty typically coincides with VIX spiking to an extreme (say 22-25+ in a normal regime) and then reversing lower — falling VIST off a spike as price holds is textbook fear-exhaustion. On individual stocks, look for the climax to occur at a major long-term support or a round number. In the option chain, a selling climax often coincides with maximum Put OI and extremely negative sentiment — the point of peak fear. Delivery-percentage jumping on the reversal day (real buyers taking stock, not just intraday churn) strengthens the case.

**Pitfalls.** The great danger is that the first bounce off a climax is frequently *not* the final low. Climaxes relieve the panic and often produce a secondary test days later at or slightly below the climax low on much lighter volume — that retest is frequently the higher-odds entry than the climax itself. Trade climaxes with reduced size, take partial profits into the sharp initial recovery, and do not marry the position expecting a V-shaped moonshot. Also, "3x volume" alone is not a climax without the extended prior move — volume spikes at the *start* of moves signal continuation, not reversal.

## Setup 4 — Double Top / Double Bottom Confirmation

The double top and double bottom are the most widely recognised reversal patterns, which is both their strength (self-fulfilling) and their weakness (front-run and faded). The pattern is a rejection of a level twice: price rallies to resistance, pulls back to a "neckline," rallies again to the *same* resistance, fails again, and then breaks the neckline. The two failed attempts to break higher demonstrate that supply overwhelms demand at that price, and the neckline break confirms the sellers have won.

The disciplined edge here comes entirely from *waiting for the neckline break.* Amateurs short the second top on hope; professionals wait for the neckline to give way, because until it does, the pattern is just a range that could resolve either way. The pattern is invalid — and often a bull-trap — if price makes a *higher* high on the second attempt without failing.

**Worked India example.** Reliance Industries rallies to ₹1,540, pulls back to a neckline around ₹1,470, rallies again to ₹1,538 (essentially the same high), and stalls. The twin peaks at ~₹1,540 with a trough at ₹1,470 form a double top. The trade triggers when price closes below the ₹1,470 neckline. Measured target = pattern height (1,540 − 1,470 = 70) projected down from the neckline → ₹1,400.

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| Trigger | Two peaks (or troughs) at approximately the same level, then a close beyond the neckline |
| Entry | On the neckline break close (below ₹1,470), or on a retest of the broken neckline from below |
| Stop | Above the second peak (above ₹1,540) for the top; below the second trough for the bottom |
| Target | Neckline − pattern height (₹1,400); scale at prior support levels along the way |
| Timeframe | Daily/weekly for reliable patterns; intraday versions on 15-min are noisier |
| Regime | After an extended trend, at major S/R; the higher the timeframe, the better |

**Confluence.** The best double tops carry bearish momentum divergence into the second peak — price equal, RSI lower — confirming the second push is weaker. Volume typically shrinks on the second peak (less conviction) and expands on the neckline break (sellers commit). A double top that forms right at a prior all-time high or a major supply zone is worth far more than one in mid-range. The retest entry — waiting for price to break the neckline, bounce back up to it, and fail — offers a tighter stop and filters out false breaks, at the cost of sometimes missing the move entirely.

**Pitfalls.** The most common error is calling a double top before the neckline breaks — many "double tops" simply base and break *out* to new highs, trapping early shorts. Discipline: no neckline break, no trade. The second common error is the second peak that overshoots the first by a rupee or two, triggers breakout-buyers, and *then* reverses — this is actually a more powerful reversal (a failed-breakout double top) but it shakes out premature shorts first. Keep your stop above the *highest* of the two peaks. Finally, in strong trends these patterns fail often; demand an extended prior move and higher-timeframe context.

## Setup 5 — Trendline Break + Retest Reversal

Trends travel inside channels and along trendlines. A well-established trendline — one touched three or more times — represents the agreed pace of the trend. When price decisively breaks that trendline, the character of the move has changed: the trend's rhythm is broken. But the *break* alone is a weak signal because trendlines are subjective and false breaks are common. The high-probability trade is the **break followed by a failed retest** — price breaks the line, pulls back to kiss the underside (former support now resistance), fails to reclaim, and rolls over. The retest converts former support into confirmed resistance and gives you a precise, tight-stop entry.

**Worked India example.** Nifty has been climbing along a rising trendline for two months, with the line currently near 24,600. Price breaks below it, closing at 24,450. Two days later it bounces back up to 24,590 — right at the underside of the broken line — stalls, prints a bearish rejection candle, and turns down. That failed retest is the short trigger; the broken trendline is now resistance overhead.

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| Trigger | Break of an established (3+ touch) trendline, then a retest that fails to reclaim the line |
| Entry | On the failed retest (rejection candle at the underside of the line, ~24,590) |
| Stop | Above the retest high / back above the trendline (above 24,650) |
| Target | T1 = prior swing low or nearest support; T2 = measured by the trend's own prior swings |
| Timeframe | Works on all; cleanest on daily and 1-hour |
| Regime | End of extended trends; combine with structure (lower highs forming) |

**Confluence.** The trendline break is most meaningful when it coincides with a break of horizontal structure too — a trendline break that *also* takes out the last swing low is a genuine change of trend, not just a channel exit. Momentum divergence into the final high before the break, deteriorating breadth, and a moving-average cross (price losing the 20/50-DEMA) all stack the odds. On Bank Nifty and Nifty, watch the option chain at the retest: if Call writers pile into the strike just above the retest level, the market is confirming the ceiling.

**Pitfalls.** Trendlines are the most abused tool in technical analysis because you can draw them to say almost anything — steep lines break constantly and mean nothing. Use lines with at least three clean touches and reasonable slope; discard near-vertical lines. The second pitfall is that many trendline breaks *do* retest and then *reclaim* the line, resuming the trend — that reclaim is your stop-out and your signal that the trend is intact. Respect it. Do not add to a losing reversal hoping the retest will eventually fail.

## Interview-ready summary

Reversal trading succeeds when you stop trying to predict tops and bottoms and start reacting to *evidence of exhaustion*. The five setups form a coherent toolkit: the **Failed Breakdown / Spring** weaponises trapped traders' stop-losses; **Momentum Divergence** reads fading internal fuel but demands a price trigger before acting; the **Climax Reversal** trades the exhaustion of panic on volume and range extremes; the **Double Top/Bottom** requires the neckline break to confirm twice-rejected supply or demand; and the **Trendline Break + Retest** waits for former support to prove itself as resistance. Across all five, four disciplines are constant. First, context filters everything — reversals need an *extended* prior move and a *major* level; fading mid-trend is a losing game. Second, wait for the trigger — the reclaim, the neckline break, the failed retest — never anticipate. Third, the stop is structural and non-negotiable, placed beyond the exhaustion extreme, and it must be small relative to the target for the maths to work. Fourth, size down — reversals are inherently lower-probability than continuation, so trade half units and take partial profits into the sharp initial move. Layer in India-specific confluence — India VIX spiking and reversing off a low, option-chain Put/Call writing marking the floor or ceiling, and breadth divergence — and you convert a gambler's instinct to catch the exact low into a disciplined, repeatable, risk-defined process.
