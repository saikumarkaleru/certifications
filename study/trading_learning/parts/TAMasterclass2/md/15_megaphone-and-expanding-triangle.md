# Megaphone & Expanding Triangle

Every trader learns to draw contracting triangles — the coil, the pennant, the symmetrical squeeze where two lines converge toward an apex and price eventually breaks out. They are comfortable. They imply a compression of energy that will be released. But there is a rarer, nastier structure that does the exact opposite: two lines *diverging* apart, each swing bigger than the last, the range expanding instead of contracting. This is the **megaphone**, also called the **broadening formation** or **expanding triangle**.

If a contracting triangle is a spring being compressed, a megaphone is a rope being frayed. It is the chart of a market losing its own anchor — volatility rising, participants disagreeing more violently at every turn, stops getting hunted on both sides. For most traders the megaphone is pure pain: they buy the higher high (it fails), they short the lower low (it fails), and they get chopped to pieces. But once you understand *why* it forms and *how* to trade its edges rather than its middle, the megaphone becomes one of the more honest patterns on the chart — it tells you plainly that the crowd has lost the plot, and it hands you clean, wide levels to trade against.

This chapter covers the broadening formation in its main variants, why it appears at emotional extremes, how to trade the reversals at its expanding boundaries, and — crucially — how to survive the version that resolves into a genuine trend rather than a reversal.

## What it is and why it works

A **megaphone** is a five-point (or more) pattern bounded by two diverging trendlines: an **upper line sloping up** (connecting higher highs) and a **lower line sloping down** (connecting lower lows). Each successive swing is *wider* than the one before — higher highs and lower lows in alternation. Drawn out, it looks like a megaphone or a reverse triangle, wide mouth pointing to the right.

Why does price behave this way? Because a megaphone is the geometry of **escalating disagreement and rising volatility**. In a normal market, buyers and sellers roughly agree on a fair value and trade around it. In a broadening formation, they violently *dis*agree, and the disagreement is *growing*. Every rally overshoots further than the last as greed stretches; every decline overshoots further as fear stretches. There is no consensus, no equilibrium — the market is swinging harder each time it tries to find one.

This typically happens in three situations:

1. **At a major top, after a long bull run.** This is the classic and most important context. Late in a bull market, the crowd is euphoric but nervous. News flow whipsaws sentiment. Smart money is distributing into the higher highs while the public panics at the lower lows. The result is a broadening top — often a warning that the trend is exhausting. The famous "Bump-and-Run" and the "broadening top" of classical charting both live here.
2. **Around a high-uncertainty event** — a Budget, an election result, a Fed decision, a war headline — where the market genuinely doesn't know how to price the outcome and lurches wider with each new rumour.
3. **In illiquid, over-leveraged names** where stop-hunting on both sides mechanically produces expanding swings.

The reason it "works" as a tradable pattern is that the diverging lines, though widening, still act as **support and resistance at the moment of touch**. Price reaching the upper line is stretched, over-extended, and meeting resistance — a place to fade. Price reaching the lower line is capitulated, over-sold, and meeting support — a place to buy. You trade the *boundaries*, not the middle. And because the boundaries are far apart, when a reversal off one line works, the target (the opposite line, or at least the midline) is a long way away — big reward for a well-placed fade.

## Variants and mechanics

**1. The broadening top (right-angled and ascending/descending).** The pure five-point megaphone with both lines diverging. Points 1, 3, 5 are the highs (each higher), points 2, 4 are the lows (each lower). A reversal is signalled when, after point 5, price fails to make a new high and instead breaks *below* point 4's level.

**2. The right-angled broadening formation.** One line is horizontal (flat), the other diverges. If the *top* is flat and the bottom drops away (lower lows against a flat resistance), it's a broadening formation with a descending base — often bearish. If the *bottom* is flat and the highs rise (higher highs against a flat support), it's the ascending version — often a distribution top that eventually breaks the flat support downward.

**3. The broadening wedge (expanding, tilted).** Both lines diverge but the whole structure tilts up or down. An expanding *falling* wedge (both lines down, diverging) often resolves *up*; an expanding *rising* wedge often resolves *down*. Direction of the tilt hints at the eventual resolution.

**Key mechanics to respect:**

- You need **at least five touch points** (three on one line, two on the other) to legitimately call a megaphone. Fewer, and you're drawing lines through noise.
- **Volume typically rises** through a broadening top — the opposite of a healthy contracting triangle, where volume dries up. Expanding volume into expanding range is the fingerprint of a distribution / panic structure.
- The pattern has **no apex and no clean measured target** the way a symmetrical triangle does — the lines diverge, so there's no convergence point to project from. Targets come from the *opposite boundary* and from prior structure, not from an apex projection.
- A megaphone is **inherently unstable**. It cannot expand forever. It resolves either by (a) a reversal off the fifth point, or (b) a decisive breakout *through* one line that ends the pattern and starts a trend. Knowing which is happening is the whole game.

## Worked India example (levels & ₹)

Consider Nifty in a late-stage rally that's getting jittery ahead of a Union Budget. Over roughly six to eight weeks it traces a broadening structure on the daily chart:

- **Point 1 (high):** ~22,100
- **Point 2 (low):** ~21,600
- **Point 3 (higher high):** ~22,450
- **Point 4 (lower low):** ~21,300
- **Point 5 (higher high):** ~22,750 — a marginal new high on *fading* breadth (fewer stocks participating, RSI making a lower high: a bearish divergence).

Each swing is wider than the last: swing 1→2 was 500 points, 3→4 was 1,150 points, and the up-legs are stretching too. The India VIX, which sat near 11 at Point 1, is now oscillating between 14 and 18 — volatility is *rising* as the pattern broadens, exactly as theory predicts.

The tradable moment is at **Point 5**. Price stabs to 22,750 (the upper diverging line), prints a shooting-star / bearish engulfing on the daily, on heavy volume, with RSI diverging. That is your **fade at the upper boundary**. Short trigger: a close back below 22,600. Stop: above 22,800 (just beyond the point-5 spike — a tight, defined invalidation). First target: the *midline* of the megaphone (~22,000), then Point 4's level (~21,300) as the pattern-confirmation target.

The megaphone is *confirmed as a top* when price breaks below Point 4 (21,300). Because the structure has no base — it's all wide swings, no accumulation — the break below 21,300 can run fast toward the origin of the whole move. On the options side, the rising VIX through the pattern meant put premiums were already fat; the alert trader shorting at Point 5 could buy slightly OTM puts or run a bear put spread, and as the top confirmed and the market fell, both direction *and* the further VIX expansion paid off.

The mirror case — a **broadening bottom** — shows up after a long decline: widening swings near a market low, VIX elevated and lurching, and a fifth point that stabs to a marginal new low on a *higher* RSI low (bullish divergence) before reversing. You'd fade the *lower* boundary long, stop below the point-5 spike, targeting the midline and then the upper line.

## How to trade it: entry, stop, target

The cardinal rule: **trade the edges, never the middle.** The interior of a megaphone is a chop-machine that will stop you out repeatedly. All the money is made fading the diverging boundaries, or catching the eventual break.

**Setup A — Fade the boundary (the primary play).**

| Element | At the upper line (short) | At the lower line (long) |
|---|---|---|
| Trigger | Reversal candle (shooting star / bearish engulfing) at/above the upper diverging line, then a close back inside | Reversal candle (hammer / bullish engulfing) at/below the lower diverging line, then a close back inside |
| Stop | Above the reversal spike + buffer | Below the reversal spike + buffer |
| Target 1 | Midline of the megaphone | Midline of the megaphone |
| Target 2 | Opposite (lower) diverging line | Opposite (upper) diverging line |
| Timeframe | Daily for indices/stocks; 15-min for intraday broadenings | Same |
| Best regime | Rising-VIX, late-trend, high-uncertainty | Rising-VIX, capitulation zone |

The beauty of the boundary fade is the **tight stop against a wide target**. You're risking the distance to the spike's extreme and aiming for the opposite line — often a 1:3 or better payoff. The ugliness is that you *will* be wrong sometimes (the boundary breaks instead of holds), so position size must respect that this is a fade against momentum.

**Setup B — Trade the breakout (the escape play).** A megaphone does not last forever. Eventually price breaks *through* one line and doesn't come back. The safest way to trade the megaphone is often to *wait* for this: let price break the boundary, retest it from the outside, and then trade in the breakout direction with the pattern behind you. A break below the lower line of a broadening top, retested and rejected, is a high-conviction short into a trend. This gives up the fade profits but avoids the chop entirely — a valid choice for traders who hate whipsaws.

**Partial rule of thumb (classical charting):** in a broadening top, a common cue is that the reversal is confirmed when, after a fifth-point high that fails, price closes below the most recent *minor low* (Point 4). Use that as your "pattern is now a top" line and manage the trade to the downside from there.

## Confluence — including OI and options

A megaphone screams "rising volatility," so the confluence tools that matter most are the *volatility and positioning* tools:

- **India VIX rising through the pattern** is near-mandatory confirmation. A broadening formation on a *falling* VIX is suspect — the disagreement it implies should show up in the volatility index. Rising VIX validates the structure.
- **Breadth divergence at the fifth point.** In a broadening top, the marginal new high at Point 5 should come on *deteriorating* breadth — advance-decline weakening, fewer stocks above their 200-DMA, the Nifty making a high that the broad market doesn't confirm. That divergence is what turns "another higher high" into "the last higher high."
- **RSI / momentum divergence** at Point 5 (lower RSI high against higher price high for a top; higher RSI low against lower price low for a bottom).
- **Volume expanding** into the swings — distribution, not accumulation.
- **Option chain / OI:**
  - At the **upper boundary of a broadening top**, look for **call writers stacking OI** at the point-5 strike — institutions selling calls into the euphoric high, betting it caps. Combined with the reversal candle, that's your green light to fade short.
  - Watch **put OI build-up below** as the top confirms — smart money positioning for the downside.
  - The **wide, unstable swings inflate implied volatility**, so option *sellers* face whipsaw risk; this is a pattern where *buying* options (defined risk) at the boundaries, or running spreads, often beats naked selling — the very expansion that defines the pattern is dangerous to short-vol strategies.
  - **Max pain drifting away from price** in the direction of the expected reversal adds confluence.

## Pitfalls — where megaphone traders get hurt

**1. Trading the middle.** Repeat it until it's reflex: the interior is a shredder. Every entry that isn't near a boundary is a coin-flip inside expanding chop. Most losses in this pattern come from impatience in the middle.

**2. Assuming it must reverse.** A megaphone is *not* automatically a reversal pattern. Roughly as often, it's a **continuation** — a violent, volatile pause that resolves in the direction of the prior trend. Don't marry the "it's a top" thesis. Let the boundary break tell you. If a broadening formation in an *uptrend* breaks *up* through the upper line and holds, it was a continuation, and shorting it will cost you dearly.

**3. Overfitting the lines.** Because the swings are wide and irregular, it's dangerously easy to draw megaphone lines through any messy period and convince yourself a pattern exists. Demand five clean touch points. If you're forcing the lines, there's no pattern.

**4. Too-tight stops inside a volatile structure.** The whole point of a megaphone is *rising* volatility. A stop that would be fine in a quiet market gets vaporised here. Place stops beyond the spike extreme, size *down* to compensate for the wider stop, and never normal-size a megaphone trade.

**5. Ignoring the event catalyst (India-specific).** Many Indian megaphones form around a *known* binary event — Budget, election counting day, RBI policy, a major earnings/verdict. If you're fading the upper boundary the day before the Budget, understand that the event can gap price straight through your stop. Either trade it *after* the event resolves the uncertainty, or size for gap risk.

**6. Expecting a clean measured target.** Unlike a flag or symmetrical triangle, the megaphone gives you no apex-based projection. Traders who wait for a "textbook target" get confused. Your targets are the opposite boundary and prior structure — nothing more precise exists.

**7. Fatigue and over-trading.** Broadening formations can persist for weeks, offering several boundary touches. Traders get worn down, start chasing, and give back the boundary-fade profits in the middle. Trade the edges, then *sit on your hands* until the next edge.

## Interview-ready summary

A **megaphone** (broadening formation / expanding triangle) is a five-point structure bounded by two *diverging* trendlines — an upper line sloping up and a lower line sloping down — with each swing wider than the last. It is the geometry of **escalating disagreement and rising volatility**, the mirror image of a contracting triangle. Where a symmetrical triangle coils energy toward an apex, a megaphone *frays* — the market is losing its anchor, overshooting further with every attempt to find fair value.

It appears most importantly at **major tops after a long bull run** (distribution into higher highs, panic into lower lows) and around **high-uncertainty events** (Budget, elections, policy). Its fingerprints are **rising volume and rising VIX** into the pattern, **breadth and momentum divergence** at the fifth point, and — in the options market — **call writers capping the upper boundary** of a broadening top.

You trade the **edges, never the middle**: fade the upper diverging line short (reversal candle, stop above the spike, target the midline then the opposite line) or the lower line long, aiming for a wide reward against a tight, spike-defined stop. Alternatively, wait for the **boundary break and retest** to trade the trend that ends the pattern. The confirmation of a broadening *top* is a close below the prior minor low (Point 4); because there's no base beneath, the resulting fall can be fast.

The killers are trading the volatile **middle**, assuming it *must* reverse (it can just as easily be a continuation), overfitting the lines through noise, using stops too tight for the expanding range, and getting gapped by the very event that created the uncertainty. Respect the boundaries, size down for the volatility, and let the pattern — not your bias — tell you whether it's a top or a pause.
