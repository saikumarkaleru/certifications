# Case Studies: Sector Rotation & RRG

Money in a market is never evenly distributed — it flows *between* sectors in slow, repeating tides. When IT is leading, autos may be basing; when banks catch a bid, metals may be topping. The trader who reads this rotation buys strength that is *accelerating* and avoids strength that is *fading*, and does it a few weeks before the crowd names the new leadership. The Relative Rotation Graph (RRG) is the single best tool for seeing this at a glance: it plots every sector's *relative strength* and its *relative momentum* against a benchmark on one quadrant chart, so you can literally watch capital rotate clockwise from Leading → Weakening → Lagging → Improving → back to Leading.

This chapter is a set of worked reconstructions of Indian sector rotations read through the RRG lens — the calls that paid, the ones that failed, and the discipline that separates them. Levels and RRG readings are **approximate reconstructions** for teaching; verify current positions on your own RRG (TradingView, StockCharts, or the RRG feature on Indian platforms) before acting.

## What the RRG actually plots — and why it works

The RRG has two axes. The **x-axis is the JdK RS-Ratio** — a smoothed, normalised measure of a sector's *relative strength* versus the benchmark (say the Nifty 500 or Nifty 50). Right of the 100 centre line = *outperforming*; left = *underperforming*. The **y-axis is the JdK RS-Momentum** — the *rate of change* of that relative strength. Above 100 = relative strength is *accelerating*; below = *decelerating*. Together they create four quadrants, and a sector's *tail* (its path over recent weeks) shows its trajectory:

- **Leading (top-right):** strong *and* accelerating — current leadership. Right axis, upper half.
- **Weakening (bottom-right):** still strong but *decelerating* — leadership tiring, momentum rolling over. Right axis, lower half.
- **Lagging (bottom-left):** weak *and* still decelerating — the laggards. Left axis, lower half.
- **Improving (top-left):** still weak but *accelerating* — the early turn, tomorrow's leaders. Left axis, upper half.

The powerful insight is the *direction of travel*: sectors rotate **clockwise** — Leading → Weakening → Lagging → Improving → Leading. Momentum (y-axis) *leads* strength (x-axis), so a sector rolls over on momentum (drops from Leading into Weakening) *before* it loses relative strength, and it accelerates (rises from Lagging into Improving) *before* it becomes an outright leader. That is why RRG can front-run rotation: it reads the *second derivative* (momentum of relative strength) that turns first.

It works because institutional money moves in size and slowly — you cannot rotate ₹10,000 crore out of banks and into IT in a day. That gradual reallocation shows up as smooth, persistent tails on the RRG *weeks* before it is obvious in headline index levels. And it is honest about uncertainty: RRG shows *probabilities and trajectories*, not guarantees — tails can curl back (a sector in Improving can fall back into Lagging rather than completing the rotation to Leading), which is exactly why you confirm with price.

## How to read tails, and the rules of engagement

A *long, smooth tail* pointing into the upper-right (toward or through Leading) at a healthy distance from the centre is a strong, established trend — high conviction. A *short, choppy tail* near the 100/100 centre is noise — low conviction; sectors near the centre whip between quadrants and should mostly be ignored. The highest-value, *earliest* signal is a sector in the **Improving** quadrant with a tail turning *up and to the right* — that is the pre-leadership setup. The most important *defensive* signal is a Leading sector whose tail *curls down* into **Weakening** — book profits and tighten stops on your longs there, because leadership is tiring.

Rules of engagement: (1) **Buy strength that is accelerating** — Improving-heading-to-Leading, or freshly Leading with a rising tail. (2) **Trim/exit strength that is fading** — Leading curling into Weakening. (3) **Avoid the Lagging quadrant** unless a tail is clearly curling up into Improving. (4) **Always confirm with price** — RRG tells you *where* to hunt; the sector's own chart and the leading stocks within it tell you *when* to pull the trigger. (5) **Use the right benchmark and timeframe** — weekly RRG vs Nifty 500 for the medium-term rotation picture; don't over-trade the daily RRG's noise.

## Case Study 1 — The winning rotation: IT from Improving to Leading

**Context.** Picture a period where Nifty IT had underperformed for months — the sector sat deep in the **Lagging** quadrant (left-bottom) of the weekly RRG versus the Nifty 500, while PSU Banks and Metals occupied **Leading**. Sentiment on IT was poor: rupee strength, soft US demand commentary, the usual. This is precisely when RRG earns its keep — spotting the turn *before* the narrative changes.

**The RRG signal.** Over three to four weeks, the Nifty IT tail curls *up* — RS-Momentum crosses back above 100 while RS-Ratio is still below 100. IT has moved from **Lagging into Improving** (top-left), with a smooth tail pointing up-and-to-the-right. Meanwhile, PSU Banks' tail in Leading *curls down* into Weakening, and Metals rolls from Leading toward Weakening too — the incumbents are tiring. The RRG is showing a clean rotation *out of* cyclicals *into* IT before it is visible in headline returns.

**Confirming with price.** RRG says "hunt IT"; now you go to the charts. The Nifty IT index is basing and prints a breakout above a multi-week down-trendline on rising volume. Within it, the leading stock — the largest-cap IT name — breaks a ₹1,650 resistance on 1.8x volume with Nifty IT tailwind (the exact kind of *sector-backed* breakout that has an edge). The *sector RRG position plus the stock breakout* is the combined trigger.

**The trade.** Rather than one stock, express the rotation across the *two strongest names* in the improving sector (the RRG identifies the sector; relative strength within the sector identifies the leaders). Buy the large-cap IT breakout at ₹1,678, stop ₹1,590, and a second mid-cap IT leader on its own breakout, each risking ~0.5% of capital. Simultaneously *exit or trim* any PSU Bank / Metal longs, since their tails are curling into Weakening — the RRG manages *both* sides of the book.

**Outcome.** Over the next six to eight weeks, Nifty IT rotates fully into the **Leading** quadrant (RS-Ratio crosses above 100 with RS-Momentum still elevated), and the large-cap name runs from ₹1,678 to ₹1,880 (~12%) while the mid-cap leader does better. PSU Banks and Metals, meanwhile, drift from Weakening into Lagging and *underperform* — trimming them was as valuable as buying IT. The rotation call captured the leadership handoff early.

**Lesson.** The winning rotation had the full RRG story: a *smooth tail* curling from Lagging into Improving (early signal), incumbents *curling out* of Leading (confirming the handoff), and — crucially — *price confirmation* via a sector and stock breakout. RRG found the sector weeks early; price timing and within-sector relative strength found the *when* and the *what*. And it improved the whole book, not just one trade, by flagging what to *sell* as clearly as what to buy.

## Case Study 2 — The FAILURE: chasing a Leading sector into its top

**Context.** This is the classic RRG mistake. Suppose Nifty Metals had been the standout leader — deep in the **Leading** quadrant, far from centre, after a huge run driven by a commodity spike. Everyone can see it; the financial press has crowned Metals; retail is piling in. A trader, seeing Metals "clearly the strongest sector," buys the top metal stock at ₹720 near the highs, reasoning "trade strength."

**Why it fails.** The RRG was already *warning*: for two to three weeks, the Metals tail had been *curling downward* — RS-Momentum rolling over, the tail bending from Leading toward **Weakening**. Momentum (y-axis) turns *before* strength (x-axis), so the sector was *decelerating* relative to the market even while still nominally "strong." The trader read the *quadrant* (Leading) but ignored the *tail direction* (down). On the stock chart, the metal name showed a *bearish divergence* (higher price high, lower RSI) and *fresh call writing* at the ₹720 strike — corroborating exhaustion. Buying a Leading sector whose tail is curling down is buying the *end* of leadership.

**The outcome.** The commodity cools, Metals' tail completes the roll into Weakening and then Lagging over the next month, and the stock falls from ₹720 to ₹610 (~15%). The trader who "traded strength" bought the rotation *out*. Meanwhile the money left Metals and showed up in the Improving quadrant elsewhere (say FMCG turning defensive as the market got nervous) — where the disciplined RRG reader was already hunting.

**What discipline would have done.** The rule *trim/exit strength that is fading* (Leading curling into Weakening) would have flagged Metals as a *sell/avoid*, not a buy. The correct read of a Leading-but-curling-down sector is "book profits on existing longs, do not initiate new ones." The trader confused a *quadrant* with a *trajectory* — the single most common RRG error.

**Lesson.** *Leading is not automatically a buy.* A sector in Leading with a tail curling *down* is late-stage leadership rotating toward Weakening — the crowd's favourite is often the professional's exit. Read the *tail direction*, not just the quadrant. The safest buys are in *Improving* (early, before the crowd) and in *fresh, rising-tail Leading*; the most dangerous buys are in *Weakening-bound Leading* that the whole market is celebrating.

## Case Study 3 — The defensive rotation: risk-off into FMCG/Pharma

**Context.** Now a *risk-off* rotation, which RRG reads beautifully. Suppose the broad Nifty enters a corrective, nervous phase — VIX rising, high-beta sectors (PSU Banks, Realty, Metals) rolling from Leading into Weakening and Lagging *together*. On the weekly RRG versus Nifty 500, the *defensive* sectors — FMCG, Pharma, and sometimes IT — begin curling *up* from Lagging into **Improving**. This is the market's classic defensive rotation: when participants get cautious, money hides in low-beta, earnings-stable sectors, and RRG shows it as defensives improving while cyclicals decay.

**The RRG signal and the read.** The tell is *breadth of the rotation*: it is not one sector but the *whole cyclical complex* curling down-left while the *whole defensive complex* curls up-left-to-up-right. That coordinated pattern is a macro risk-off signal in itself — often a warning that the *index* is topping or entering a correction, useful even to a pure index trader for reducing net long exposure. FMCG's tail moves cleanly into Improving and heads toward Leading; Pharma follows.

**The trade.** Rotate the book: reduce/exit high-beta longs (their sectors are in Weakening/Lagging), and add the *strongest defensive leaders* on price confirmation — e.g., a large-cap FMCG name breaking a base with the Nifty FMCG index turning up, stop under the base, ~0.5% risk each. Simultaneously, this RRG picture argues for *smaller overall position size and more hedges* (protective Nifty puts), because the coordinated risk-off rotation says the environment is fragile.

**Outcome.** Over the correction, FMCG and Pharma *outperform* — they fall less (or rise) while cyclicals drop hard — so the rotated book meaningfully outperforms a static, cyclical-heavy book. When the correction ends, the RRG shows the reverse: defensives curling back down from Leading into Weakening while cyclicals curl up from Lagging into Improving — the signal to rotate *back* into risk-on leadership.

**Lesson.** RRG is not only a stock-picking tool; the *coordinated* rotation of whole complexes (cyclicals vs defensives) is a *regime* signal. When defensives broadly move into Improving/Leading while cyclicals decay, treat it as risk-off: rotate to defensive leaders, cut beta, and hedge. When the reverse happens, lean back into risk. Reading the *whole board* — not one tail — turns RRG into a top-down allocation and even a market-timing aid.

## Building an RRG-driven workflow

Codify the cases into a repeatable weekly routine:

| Step | Action | Output |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Benchmark & timeframe | Weekly RRG of sector indices vs Nifty 500 | The rotation map |
| 2. Read the quadrants | Note who is Leading / Weakening / Lagging / Improving | Where the money is |
| 3. Read the *tails* | Direction & length — smooth long tails = conviction; near-centre = ignore | Trajectories, not snapshots |
| 4. Shortlist BUYS | Improving-heading-to-Leading; fresh rising-tail Leading | Sectors to hunt long |
| 5. Shortlist SELLS/AVOID | Leading curling into Weakening; anything in Lagging | Sectors to trim/avoid |
| 6. Within-sector RS | In each buy-sector, rank stocks by relative strength | The leaders to buy |
| 7. Confirm with price | Sector-index and stock breakouts, volume, OI | The *trigger* and timing |
| 8. Manage the book | Rotate: add to improving leaders, trim fading leaders, size to regime | Continuous reallocation |

The discipline that ties it together: **RRG tells you *where and which direction*; price tells you *when*.** RRG is a top-down filter that points your bottom-up chart work at the right sectors — it is not an entry signal on its own.

## Pitfalls specific to RRG

(1) **Quadrant-not-trajectory error** — buying Leading because it's "strong" while its tail curls down (Case 2). Always read tail *direction*. (2) **Trading the centre** — sectors near 100/100 whipsaw; ignore short, choppy tails. (3) **Wrong benchmark** — a sector can look "Leading" vs Nifty 50 but not vs Nifty 500; be consistent and know what you're measuring against. (4) **Over-trading the daily RRG** — daily tails are noisy; the medium-term rotation edge lives on the *weekly*. (5) **No price confirmation** — acting on the RRG alone, without the sector/stock chart agreeing, front-runs a rotation that may curl back (tails *do* reverse — Improving can fall back to Lagging). (6) **Ignoring the whole board** — missing the macro message when *complexes* rotate together (Case 3). (7) **Forgetting relative ≠ absolute** — a sector can be "Leading" on RRG while falling in absolute terms in a bear market (it's just falling *less*); RRG is *relative* strength, so in a downtrend "Leading" may still mean losing money on the long side — pair it with the absolute trend of the market.

## Interview-ready summary

The Relative Rotation Graph plots every sector's *relative strength* (RS-Ratio, x-axis) against its *relative momentum* (RS-Momentum, y-axis) versus a benchmark, creating four quadrants — Leading, Weakening, Lagging, Improving — through which capital rotates *clockwise*. It works because institutional reallocation is large and slow, so momentum (which turns first) reveals leadership handoffs *weeks* before headline returns do: sectors curling from Lagging into **Improving** are tomorrow's leaders (the earliest, highest-value buy signal), while Leading sectors curling into **Weakening** are tiring leadership to trim — the crowd's celebrated favourite is often the professional's exit. The three cardinal disciplines are: read the *tail direction*, not just the quadrant (the classic failure is chasing a Leading sector whose tail is curling down); *always confirm with price* (RRG tells you *where* to hunt, the sector and stock charts tell you *when* to trigger) and rank *within-sector relative strength* to pick the actual leaders; and read the *whole board*, because a coordinated rotation of cyclicals-out / defensives-in (or vice versa) is a *regime* signal that should adjust your net exposure and hedges, not just your stock list. Used this way — top-down filter plus bottom-up confirmation — RRG turns the invisible tides of sector money into an early, repeatable edge, while staying honest that tails can curl back and relative strength is not the same as making money in absolute terms.
