# Case Studies: Breakout Winners & Failures

A breakout is the market's most seductive setup. Price coils inside a range, volatility contracts, everyone watches the same line, and then price bursts through — and either runs for a clean trend or slams straight back into the range and traps the crowd who chased the candle. The difference between a breakout that pays 3R and a breakout that costs you 1R is rarely the pattern itself. It is the *context* around the pattern: the regime, the volume signature, the relationship to higher-timeframe structure, the option-chain positioning, and the retest behaviour. This chapter walks through worked reconstructions of real-feeling Indian-market breakouts — some clean winners, some ugly failures, and some in between — so you learn to read the tells *before* you commit capital, not after.

Every level here is an **approximate reconstruction** to illustrate mechanics. Treat the numbers as teaching scaffolding and re-verify the exact prints on your own charts (TradingView, Chartink screens) before trading anything resembling these.

## Why breakouts work — and why most fail

A breakout works because a horizontal or trend boundary is a *memory*. Sellers who defended ₹1,000 three times have resting supply there. Buyers who got trapped below ₹1,000 want out at breakeven. When price finally closes decisively above that shelf, three things happen at once: (1) resting supply is absorbed and exhausted, (2) trapped shorts cover, adding fuel, and (3) breakout-trend traders pile in on momentum. That triple-flow is what produces the "air pocket" — the fast, low-resistance move on the other side of a broken level.

Most breakouts fail because the level breaks on *thin participation*. If price pokes above ₹1,000 on average volume, with no short-covering pressure and no institutional sponsorship, there is nobody to absorb the profit-taking from the early longs. Price falls back in, and now the *breakout buyers* are the trapped crowd. In Indian indices and liquid stocks, the single most reliable failure tell is a breakout on **below-average volume into a resistance zone that has strong overhead OI (call writing)**. The best breakouts show the mirror image: expanding volume, unwinding of overhead call OI, and a higher-timeframe trend already pointing the same way.

The honest base rates matter. Across liquid Indian stocks and indices, a naked horizontal breakout without filters resolves into a sustained trend maybe 40–45% of the time. Add a volume filter, a retest, and higher-timeframe alignment, and you can push the win rate into the mid-50s to low-60s with a favourable payoff — but you will *never* get certainty, and the failures cluster in choppy, range-bound regimes. Sizing and stop discipline, not prediction, are what keep you solvent through the failure clusters.

## Case Study 1 — The clean winner: a stock breaking a multi-month base

**Context.** Imagine a large-cap capital-goods name — call it "L&T-type" for character — that spent roughly four months grinding sideways between ₹3,450 support and ₹3,620 resistance after a strong prior uptrend. The daily chart shows a classic *rectangle / horizontal base*: at least three touches of ₹3,620 rejected, and three defences of ₹3,450 held. The 200-DMA is rising and sits around ₹3,300, well below price — so the higher-timeframe trend is up and the base is a *continuation* pause, not a top. Volume during the base is quietly declining, the textbook "drying up" that precedes a resolution.

**The setup.** On the breakout day, price gaps up modestly and closes at ₹3,675 — a decisive daily close roughly 1.5% above the ₹3,620 ceiling — on volume about 2.1x the 20-day average. That volume expansion is the first green light. The second: the Nifty and the capital-goods sector index are both in uptrends and near their own highs, so there is *index tailwind* and *sector leadership*. The third, on the F&O side: the ₹3,600 and ₹3,700 strike calls, which had heavy open interest during the base (writers capping the range), show OI *unwinding* on the breakout day — call writers are covering, which is exactly the short-covering fuel a real breakout needs.

**The trade.** There are two clean ways to enter. The aggressive entry is on the breakout close itself at ₹3,675, with a stop below the breakout candle's low or below the old ceiling now acting as support — say ₹3,590 (just under ₹3,620). That is roughly an 85-point / ~2.3% risk. The patient entry — usually the better risk-adjusted one — is to *wait for the retest*: let price pull back toward the broken ₹3,620 shelf, and buy when it holds. In this reconstruction, price drifts back to ₹3,635 two sessions later, prints a bullish hammer on light volume (no aggressive selling), and resumes. The retest entry near ₹3,640 with a stop at ₹3,585 cuts the risk to ~55 points and dramatically improves the payoff geometry.

**Position sizing.** Suppose ₹10,00,000 capital and a 0.75% risk budget = ₹7,500 per trade. At the retest entry with a 55-point stop, that is roughly 136 shares. The initial target is a measured move: the base was ₹3,450–₹3,620, a height of ~170 points, projected above the breakout gives ₹3,620 + 170 ≈ ₹3,790 as target 1. A second, trend-following target rides a trailing stop under the rising 20-DMA.

**Outcome.** Price runs to ₹3,790 (target 1, ~+4% from retest entry, ~2.7R) within about two weeks, then trends to ₹3,880 over the next month as the trailing stop climbs. Target 1 books half; the trailer captures the rest. Net result: a multi-R winner.

**Lesson.** The winner had *five* independent confirmations stacked: horizontal base in an established uptrend, volume expansion on the break, index tailwind, sector leadership, and call-OI unwinding. The retest converted a 2.3% risk into a 1.5% risk without giving up the move. You did not need to predict — you needed to wait for the market to *prove* the breakout with a held retest, then let a measured move and a trailing stop do the work.

## Case Study 2 — The classic failure: the false breakout (bull trap)

**Context.** Now take a mid-cap chemicals stock — "Deepak-type" for character — trading around ₹680, in a *choppy, sideways* regime after a sharp earlier decline. The 200-DMA is flat-to-falling near ₹710, so price is *below* a non-rising long-term average. The stock has a resistance shelf at ₹700 that has capped it twice. The broader market (Nifty) is in a nervous, range-bound phase with elevated India VIX. Right there, the regime is already hostile: false breakouts cluster in flat/down higher-timeframe trends and choppy index conditions.

**The "setup."** On an up day for the index, the stock pushes to a daily close of ₹706 — just above ₹700 — but volume is *only about 0.9x* the 20-day average. There is no gap, no thrust; it is a limp poke above the line. On the option chain, ₹700 and ₹720 calls still show *rising* OI into the breakout — call writers are *adding*, betting the level holds, the opposite of the unwind you want. A breakout chaser buys the close at ₹706.

**The trap springs.** The next session opens flat and fades all day, closing back at ₹688 — *below* the ₹700 shelf. That single close-back-inside is the definitive false-breakout signature: price reclaimed by the range within one to two sessions on no follow-through. The day after, it gaps down to ₹672 as the trapped breakout longs bail and the emboldened call writers press. The ₹706 buyer with a naive "give it room" mental stop is now down ~5% and hoping.

**What discipline would have done.** A rules-based breakout trader has a hard stop under the breakout candle — say ₹684 — and is stopped out for a ~3% loss on the gap-down, no debate. Better still, the *filters would have vetoed the trade entirely*: below-average volume, price under a falling 200-DMA, rising overhead call OI, and a choppy index. Any one of those is a yellow flag; all four together is a "do not touch." The A+ breakout trader simply does not take this trade. The B trader takes it small and respects the stop. The trader who blows up takes it full size and averages down.

**Outcome.** Price grinds to ₹640 over the next two weeks — the failed breakout became a *short* setback. In fact, the cleaner trade was the *fade*: a false break above ₹700 that closes back inside is a high-quality short trigger, entry on the close-back-inside near ₹688, stop above the false-breakout high ₹710, target back to the range low near ₹650. That is a ~2R short in the opposite direction.

**Lesson.** Failures are not random — they are *predictable from context*. The failure tells (thin volume, hostile regime, adding call writers, sub-200-DMA) were all present *before* entry. And a false breakout is not just a loss to avoid; flipped, it is one of the highest-probability setups in the book: the *failure of the crowd's setup* is your setup.

## Case Study 3 — Bank Nifty intraday: the opening-range breakout that ran

**Context.** Bank Nifty is the intraday breakout instrument in India — high beta, deep options liquidity, and clean opening-range behaviour. On an expiry-week Tuesday, Bank Nifty opens around 48,200 after a strong global cue. The **opening range** (first 15 minutes, 9:15–9:30) sets a high of 48,340 and a low of 48,160 — a 180-point ORB box.

**The setup.** The **ORB long trigger** is a 5-minute close above the opening-range high of 48,340. It fires at about 9:50 with a 5-min close at 48,375 on strong volume. Confluence: the index is *above* the previous day's high and above VWAP (buyers in control from the open), and on the option chain the 48,300 call OI is *unwinding* while 48,000 puts are being *written* — put writers stepping in below is a bullish support signal, call writers covering above is fuel.

**The trade.** Entry on the ORB trigger at 48,375. Stop below VWAP / below the opening-range midpoint — say 48,240 (a ~135-point risk). Because this is intraday index, the clean expression is a *long via options*: buy a slightly ITM or ATM call, or a bull call spread to cap theta on expiry week. Target 1 is a measured move equal to the ORB box height (180 points) → 48,340 + 180 ≈ 48,520. Target 2 trails under rising 15-min swing lows.

**Outcome.** Bank Nifty trends up all morning, tags 48,520 by 10:40 (target 1), consolidates, then extends to 48,700 into the afternoon as put writers keep defending higher lows. The ORB long captures the bulk of a ~325-point day with a 135-point initial risk — roughly 2.4R on the runner. The expiry-week option position benefits from both direction and, on the trend day, favourable delta expansion.

**Lesson.** Intraday breakouts need *intraday* confluence: VWAP position, previous-day levels, and live OI shifts. The single best intraday breakout filter is "**above VWAP with put writing beneath**" — it tells you buyers are defending, which is what turns an ORB poke into a trend day. The failure mode (next case) is the ORB that fires *against* VWAP.

## Case Study 4 — The whipsaw: ORB failure and the reversal

**Context.** Same instrument, different day. Bank Nifty opens 47,900 on a flat cue, with India VIX low and the previous day an inside-day doji — a *balance* / low-conviction backdrop. Opening range: high 47,980, low 47,820.

**The trap.** At 10:05 a 5-min candle closes at 48,010 — a marginal ORB long trigger. But the tells are wrong: the break happens *below the previous day's high* (47,990 was resistance — the breakout is straight into overhead supply), volume on the trigger candle is unremarkable, and price is barely above a *flattish* VWAP. On the chain, 48,000 calls are being *written* aggressively — sellers capping exactly where price is trying to break. The chaser goes long at 48,010.

**The whipsaw.** Price stalls, prints a lower high at 48,030, then rolls over through VWAP. By 10:45 it breaks the *opening-range low* at 47,820 — a full failed-breakout reversal. The ORB long is stopped (stop was ~47,880, a clean ~130-point loss, 1R). The *reversal* is the real trade: the ORB-failure short triggers on the break of 47,820, stop above the failed high 48,030, target the day's lower distribution near 47,600.

**Outcome.** Bank Nifty sells off to 47,620 by early afternoon. The disciplined trader took a 1R loss on the long and, if playing both sides, a ~1.6R gain on the failure short. The undisciplined trader who "averaged the dip" on the long compounded the loss into the afternoon slide.

**Lesson.** The same pattern (ORB break) produced a winner in Case 3 and a loss in Case 4 — because the *context* differed. Winner: above previous-day high, above rising VWAP, put writing beneath. Loser: into previous-day resistance, flat VWAP, call writing at the strike. Read the environment, not just the line.

## Case Study 5 — The pullback that became a re-breakout (patience pays)

**Context.** A large-cap IT name — "Infy-type" — breaks a ₹1,650 descending-trendline / falling-resistance after a long downtrend, closing at ₹1,678 on 1.8x volume, with the Nifty IT index turning up. This is a *trend-change* breakout, statistically lower-probability than a continuation base-break, so scepticism is warranted.

**The setup and first "failure."** The aggressive breakout buyer at ₹1,678 sees price pull back hard to ₹1,648 — *just* below the broken trendline — three days later, and panics that it is a false break. But note: it holds ₹1,640 support on *declining* volume (no aggressive supply), the low is a *higher low* versus the pre-breakout swing, and put writers are appearing at ₹1,600. This is a *retest*, not a failure.

**The trade.** The disciplined re-entry is the *reclaim*: buy when price closes back above ₹1,660 after the retest holds, near ₹1,668, stop below the retest low ₹1,636 (~32-point risk). Target 1 the next supply shelf at ₹1,760; runner trails under the 20-DMA.

**Outcome.** Price bases for a week, then runs to ₹1,760 (target 1, ~5.5%, ~2.8R), then to ₹1,820 over the following month. The trader who bailed on the retest at ₹1,648 missed a multi-R trend; the trader who understood retest mechanics got a *lower-risk* entry than the original breakout.

**Lesson.** Not every pullback below a broken level is a failure. Distinguish a *retest that holds* (higher low, declining down-volume, support defended, put writers appearing) from a *close-back-inside failure* (price decisively reclaimed by the range, expanding down-volume, call writers adding). The first is an invitation; the second is an exit.

## Reading the tells: a comparison table

| Signal | Breakout likely to WORK | Breakout likely to FAIL |
|---|---|---|
| Volume on break | ≥1.5–2x 20-day avg, expanding | Below/at average, limp poke |
| Higher-TF trend | Price above rising 200-DMA | Below flat/falling 200-DMA |
| Regime | Trending, low-to-moderate VIX | Choppy/range, elevated VIX |
| Index/sector | Tailwind, sector leading | Headwind, sector lagging |
| Overhead call OI | Unwinding (writers covering) | Rising (writers adding) |
| Support beneath (options) | Put writing appearing | Puts unwinding / none |
| Retest behaviour | Higher low, holds on low volume | Closes back inside range fast |
| VWAP (intraday) | Break above rising VWAP | Break against flat VWAP |

Stack the left column and you have an A+ breakout worth full size. Stack the right column and you have a fade candidate, not a long. Most real setups are mixed — and the *number* of left-column boxes you can tick should scale your position size directly.

## A repeatable breakout checklist

Before you take any breakout, run this sequence: (1) **Structure** — is there a clean, multi-touch level and a base of adequate duration? (2) **Regime** — is the higher timeframe trending in the breakout's direction, and is VIX supportive? (3) **Volume** — did the break come with expansion? (4) **Sponsorship** — index/sector tailwind and, in F&O names, call-OI unwinding with put writing beneath? (5) **Entry choice** — aggressive on the close, or patient on the retest (usually better)? (6) **Invalidation** — where does the idea die, and does that stop give an acceptable R:R (≥1:2 to target 1)? (7) **Size** — fixed fractional risk (0.5–1% of capital), scaled by how many confirmations are present. If you cannot answer all seven crisply, you do not have a trade — you have a hope.

## Interview-ready summary

Breakouts pay when a remembered level breaks on *expanding participation*, in the *direction of the higher-timeframe trend*, with *sponsorship* (index/sector tailwind and, in F&O, overhead call-OI unwinding plus put writing beneath), and they *fail* on thin volume in choppy regimes into rising overhead call OI. The single highest-value refinement is to trade the **held retest** rather than the raw breakout candle — it converts the same move into a lower-risk, higher-payoff entry, and it lets the market prove the break before you commit. When a breakout *fails* — closes back inside the range within one to two sessions — do not average down; flip your thinking, because the failure of the crowd's breakout is frequently the cleanest trade on the chart. Above all, remember that the *pattern* is identical between winners and losers; it is the *context* — regime, volume, OI, VWAP, retest — that separates the 3R winner from the 1R stop. Predict nothing; read the tells, size to the confirmations, and let stops and measured-move targets manage the honest uncertainty that every breakout carries.
