# Quasimodo & Head-Fake Patterns

## What they are & why they work

The Quasimodo (QM) and the Head-Fake belong to a family of *liquidity-and-trap* patterns. They are not the polite, textbook reversals of the classic charting era — they are the modern, order-flow-aware descriptions of how markets *engineer* moves by triggering the stops and breakout orders of the crowd before travelling the other way. If you have ever bought a "breakout" that instantly reversed, or been stopped out at the exact high before the market dropped, you have been on the wrong side of a head-fake. These patterns teach you to be on the right side.

The **Head-Fake** (also "bull trap / bear trap," "false breakout," "stop-run") is the general phenomenon: price pushes just beyond an obvious level — a range high, a prior swing, a round number, a chart-pattern neckline — recruits the breakout traders and triggers the resting stops sitting beyond that level, then reverses back inside and travels in the opposite direction. It works because *obvious levels concentrate orders*. Every retail trader draws the same resistance line, so a cluster of buy-stops (breakout entries) and sell-stops (protective stops from shorts) piles up just above it. Larger participants need that liquidity to fill their size; the cleanest way to get it is to push price through the level, fill against the flood of triggered orders, and reverse. The "failed" breakout is not random — it is the market feeding on its own predictability.

The **Quasimodo** (QM, sometimes called "Over-and-Under" or a failed Head-and-Shoulders) is a *specific, structured* head-fake. It looks like a head-and-shoulders that "goes wrong": price makes a higher high (the head) but then breaks *below the prior swing low* (the left-shoulder low), signalling that the up-trend's structure has failed. The entry is placed at the *right shoulder* — a return to the level of the left shoulder — anticipating the reversal. The QM works because it marks the precise moment the trend's internal structure breaks: a higher-high followed by a lower-low is the mechanical definition of a trend change, and the QM front-runs the crowd who are still bullish at the right shoulder.

Both patterns encode the same truth: **the market seeks liquidity, and liquidity sits where everyone's orders are.** Trade *with* the trap, not into it.

## Mechanics of the Quasimodo

Take a **bearish QM** (reverses an up-move; the mirror applies for bullish). Label the swings left-to-right:

1. Price is trending up: it makes a **higher low (HL)** then a **higher high (HH)** — normal bullish structure so far.
2. From that HH it drops and breaks **below the prior higher-low**, printing a **lower low (LL)**. *This is the key event* — the break of structure (BOS). The up-trend's higher-low has been violated; the trend is mechanically broken.
3. Price then bounces back up into the region of the earlier **higher-low / left-shoulder level**. This bounce forms the **right shoulder**, ideally reaching roughly the same price as the left shoulder (often a 0.618–0.786 retracement of the down-leg).
4. The **QM entry** is a sell limit at that left-shoulder price level (the right-shoulder zone), betting the reversal resumes down.

So the anatomy is: **HL → HH (head) → break below HL (LL, break of structure) → retrace to left-shoulder level (right shoulder = entry).** The defining feature versus a normal head-and-shoulders is *sequence and entry*: QM enters at the right shoulder on a limit, using the break-of-structure as the confirmation that the trend has already failed, rather than waiting for a neckline break.

The **QM line** (the horizontal at the left-shoulder / higher-low that got broken) is the reference. Entry at that line, stop beyond the head.

## Mechanics of the Head-Fake / false breakout

The general head-fake has a simpler skeleton:

1. A well-defined **level** exists — range boundary, swing high/low, neckline, trendline, round number, or a chart pattern's breakout point.
2. Price **breaches** the level, often on a spike, sweeping the stops/breakout orders beyond it (the "liquidity grab" or "stop hunt").
3. Price **fails to hold** — it closes back inside the level (a "close-back-inside" is the confirmation) within a bar or two.
4. Price **reverses**, frequently travelling to the *opposite* side of the range with momentum, because trapped breakout traders now become fuel (they must exit at a loss, adding to the reversal).

A particularly clean sub-type is the **spring** (Wyckoff) at range lows and the **upthrust** at range highs: a brief poke beyond the range that immediately reverses, marking accumulation or distribution respectively.

## Settings and how to identify

- **Timeframes:** QM is popular on intraday charts (15-min, 1-hour) for Bank Nifty and Nifty, and on 4-hour/daily for swing trades. Head-fakes appear on every timeframe; the higher the timeframe of the failed level, the more significant the reversal.
- **Level selection:** Both patterns require an *obvious* level that many traders watch — that's where the liquidity is. Un-obvious levels have no stops to run.
- **Confirmation:** For head-fakes, demand a **close back inside** the level, not just a wick — though the wick itself (a long rejection tail poking beyond the level) is often the tell.
- **Tools:** Mark swing highs/lows (structure), draw horizontal QM lines at broken higher-lows/lower-highs, and watch volume on the breach.

## Worked India example — bearish QM on Bank Nifty (levels & ₹, approximate)

Bank Nifty, 1-hour chart. A teaching reconstruction — verify on your chart.

- Uptrend: a higher-low at **49,200**, rallying to a higher-high (**head**) at **50,000**.
- Price then sells off and breaks *below* the 49,200 higher-low, bottoming at **48,900** — a **lower low = break of structure.** The 1-hour up-trend is now mechanically dead.
- Price bounces back up toward the **49,200 QM line** (the old higher-low, now the right-shoulder level), tagging **49,180–49,250** on declining momentum. This is the **right shoulder.**
- **QM entry:** sell limit at **49,200**.
- **Stop:** above the head, **50,050** (about 850 points — wide, so size accordingly, or use the lower-timeframe structure to tighten).
- **Targets:** the LL at 48,900 first; then extension to **48,400** and **48,000** as the down-trend resumes. Reward far exceeds risk if you tighten the entry on a 5-min reversal candle at the QM line.

The logic: the crowd that bought the 49,200 "support" on the bounce is trapped when price rolls over; their stops below 48,900 become the fuel for the next leg down.

## Worked India example — bull-trap head-fake on a Nifty breakout (approximate)

Nifty daily has been ranging **24,000–24,500** for three weeks. Everyone's watching 24,500 as breakout resistance; buy-stops and breakout orders cluster just above it, and shorts' protective stops sit there too.

- Nifty spikes to **24,560** intraday on an opening gap — the "breakout." Breakout traders buy 24,510–24,560; media flashes "Nifty breaks out to new highs."
- But the day *closes back below 24,500 at 24,470* — a **close back inside the range = failed breakout / bull trap.**
- The next two sessions Nifty slides to 24,200, then 24,050 — a full traverse to the *other side* of the range. The 24,500 breakout buyers are trapped and their selling accelerates the drop.

**The trade (fade the fake):** short on the close-back-inside near 24,470, stop above the false-breakout high **24,600**, target the opposite range boundary **24,050** and the mid-range as a partial. Risk ~130 points for 400+ points reward.

## How to trade them (entry / stop / target)

| Element | Quasimodo (bearish) | Head-Fake / false breakout (bearish) |
|---|---|---|
| Setup | HH (head) + break of prior HL (BOS) + retrace to left-shoulder line | Price sweeps above an obvious level then closes back inside |
| Entry | Sell limit at QM line (right shoulder); tighten with 5-min reversal | Short on close-back-inside the level |
| Stop | Above the head | Above the false-breakout extreme (beyond the sweep wick) |
| Target 1 | The lower-low (BOS point) | Opposite side of the range / mid-range |
| Target 2 | Trend extension beyond the LL | Full range traverse or measured move |
| Timeframe | 15-min to daily | Any; higher TF = stronger |
| Regime | Trend-reversal / end of exhausted trend | Ranges, key resistance/support, chart-pattern necklines |

The bullish mirrors are identical, flipped: bullish QM = LL (head down) + break above prior lower-high + retrace to the level = buy; bear-trap = sweep below support then close back inside = long.

## Confluence (including OI)

- **Volume:** Genuine head-fakes often spike volume *on the breach* (stops triggering) then reverse — a volume climax at the extreme with no follow-through is the signature. On the QM, the right-shoulder bounce typically comes on *declining* volume (weak, no conviction), confirming the reversal.
- **Higher-timeframe level:** A QM line or failed-breakout level that coincides with a *higher-timeframe* structure (daily resistance under a 15-min QM) massively raises probability.
- **Fibonacci:** The right shoulder often lands at the 0.618–0.786 retrace of the break-of-structure leg — a Fib confluence with the QM line sharpens entry.
- **Round numbers:** Nifty 24,500, Bank Nifty 50,000 — psychological levels are stop-magnets and prime head-fake locations.
- **Open Interest (F&O):** This is where India-specific confluence shines. At a *bull-trap top*, watch for the breakout to occur while **call writers add positions** at the breakout strike (smart money selling calls into the "breakout" — they expect it to fail) and **long unwinding** in futures (price up, OI down = no real new longs backing the move). The reversal confirms when **put unwinding** flips to **put writing collapse** and call OI dominates. At a *bear-trap bottom*, the mirror: heavy **put writing** appears at the swept support strike (smart money selling puts, betting the low holds) and short-covering (price up, OI down) powers the reversal. A "breakout" on *falling* futures OI is a classic fake — no fresh conviction is funding it.
- **Divergence:** RSI/momentum failing to confirm the new high/low on the breach warns that the move is hollow.

The A+ setup: failed breakout of a round-number/higher-timeframe level + close back inside + volume climax with no follow-through + OI showing writers fading the move + a reversal candle. Multiple independent tells of a trap.

## Pitfalls

1. **Not every breakout is a fake.** Real breakouts exist and run for miles. The discriminator is *follow-through*: a genuine breakout holds above the level and extends; a fake closes back inside. If you fade every breakout you will get destroyed in trending markets. Demand the *close-back-inside* confirmation before shorting a "fake."

2. **Front-running the close.** Shooting a short the instant price pokes above resistance — before it fails — is guessing. Wait for the rejection. The wick is the evidence; the close seals it.

3. **QM limit orders with no confirmation.** A pure QM sell-limit at the right shoulder can get run over if the trend resumes up (the "QM" was just a pullback in a strong trend). Tighten by requiring a lower-timeframe reversal candle at the QM line rather than a blind limit, especially against a strong higher-timeframe trend.

4. **Stop placement too tight.** Stop hunts are *designed* to overshoot. If your stop sits just beyond the obvious level, you are exactly where the liquidity grab is aimed. Place stops beyond the *structural* extreme (the head, or the full sweep wick) with room for the overshoot — and size down to keep rupee-risk constant.

5. **India gap risk.** Nifty/Bank Nifty gaps on global cues (SGX/GIFT Nifty overnight, US close) can create *opening* head-fakes that reverse the gap, or gap *through* your fade. Be cautious fading right at the open; let the first 15–30 minutes establish the day's structure.

6. **Illiquid single stocks.** Head-fakes and QMs are cleanest on liquid indices and large-caps where the level actually holds order clusters. In thin small-caps, "breakouts" are often just a couple of ticks of noise, not engineered traps.

7. **Confirmation bias in labelling.** Because QM is drawn after the fact, it is tempting to see one everywhere. Insist on the strict sequence — HH, then a *genuine* break of the prior swing low, then retrace — before calling it a QM.

## Interview-ready summary

Quasimodo and Head-Fake patterns are *liquidity-trap* reversals built on the truth that markets hunt the stops and breakout orders clustered at obvious levels. The **Head-Fake / false breakout** is the general case: price pushes just past a watched level (range edge, neckline, round number), triggers the crowd's orders, closes *back inside*, and reverses to the opposite side — you fade it on the close-back-inside with a stop beyond the sweep. The **Quasimodo** is a structured version: a higher-high (head) followed by a break *below* the prior higher-low (break of structure) confirms the trend has failed, and you enter at the *right shoulder* — the retrace to the broken level — with a stop beyond the head and targets at the break-of-structure low and beyond. Both demand *confirmation, not anticipation* (the close-back-inside, or a lower-timeframe reversal at the QM line), stops placed beyond the structural extreme to survive the overshoot, and — in Indian F&O markets — OI confluence (writers fading the move, breakouts on falling futures OI) to expose the trap. Traded on liquid indices like Nifty and Bank Nifty around round numbers and higher-timeframe levels, they turn the frustrating experience of "getting faked out" into a repeatable edge: stop being the liquidity, and start trading with the hand that takes it.
