# Case Studies: Reversal Tops & Bottoms

Trend-following pays the rent, but the trades traders *remember* — and the ones that build reputations — are the great reversals: catching a major top before the crowd, or buying a generational bottom while the tape still bleeds fear. Reversals are also where most retail money dies, because the human urge to "call the top" runs far ahead of the market's willingness to actually turn. The discipline of reversal trading is therefore a paradox: you must be *early enough* to get a great price but *late enough* to have confirmation that the turn is real. This chapter works through three reconstructed reversals in Indian instruments — a major index top, a capitulation bottom in a heavyweight stock, and a commodity double-bottom on MCX — showing how to build a reversal thesis from evidence rather than hope, and how to trade it without being repeatedly stopped out by the fake turns that precede every real one.

All prices are **approximate reconstructions** for teaching, in the realistic zone of the periods described. Reversals especially must be verified on your own charts, because the entire edge lives in reading the specific structure of the specific turn.

## The anatomy of a real reversal

A genuine reversal is never a single candle. It is a *process* with a recognisable sequence, and the trader's job is to wait for enough of the sequence to complete before committing size. At a **top**, the sequence typically runs: (1) an extended, mature uptrend showing signs of exhaustion; (2) a *momentum divergence* — price makes a higher high while RSI/MACD make a lower high; (3) a *character change* — a distribution candle (shooting star, bearish engulfing) on heavy volume; (4) a *structure break* — the first lower low that violates the uptrend's higher-low sequence; and (5) a *retest* — a lower-high bounce that fails at broken support. At a **bottom**, the mirror: extended downtrend → bullish divergence → capitulation candle on climactic volume → structure break above the first lower high → higher-low retest that holds.

The single most important discipline: **you do not need step 1 or 2 to enter.** Divergence and exhaustion tell you to get *ready*; they are not entries, because a market can diverge for a long time and keep going. The *entry* comes at step 4 (structure break) with confirmation, or at the lower-risk step 5 (retest). Traders who enter on divergence alone are the ones who get run over. Traders who wait for structure and retest catch the same reversal with a fraction of the risk.

## Case Study 1 — The major index top (Nifty ~18,900 distribution top)

### Context

Set this at an all-time-high euphoria phase. Nifty had rallied for months into a marginal new high near 18,900. The surface looked triumphant, but underneath, three warning lights were flashing. First, **breadth divergence**: the index printed a new high while the advance-decline line and the percentage of stocks above their 200-DMA both made *lower* highs — fewer and fewer stocks were carrying the index. Second, **momentum divergence**: the daily RSI made a clear lower high (roughly 68 versus a prior 76) as price made its higher high. Third, **sentiment froth**: India VIX was pinned at multi-year lows near 11, retail F&O participation was at records, and option chains showed heavy, complacent call-writing far out of the money — the classic "nobody's hedged" setup.

### The setup

These signals put the trader on *alert*, not into a trade. The plan was to wait for the character change and the structure break. The distribution candle arrived as a bearish engulfing on above-average volume near the high, followed days later by the decisive signal: a close below the prior swing low at 18,400 — the *first lower low* in the entire uptrend.

|---|---|
| Instrument | Nifty 50 (short via futures or bear put spread) |
| Alert conditions | Breadth + RSI divergence at new high, VIX at lows |
| Trigger | Daily close below 18,400 (first lower low / structure break) |
| Stop | Daily close above 18,780 (the divergent lower high) |
| Initial risk | ~380 points |
| Target | Prior consolidation 17,400, then 17,000 |
| Regime | Distribution top, breadth diverging, VIX beginning to rise |

### The trade, bar by bar

Short triggered on the close below 18,400 at 18,360. The first drop was orderly, reaching 17,900. Then came the reversal-trade's defining test: the *retest*. Nifty bounced for three sessions back to 18,250, right into the underside of the broken 18,400 support (now resistance) and the declining 20-EMA. This bounce is where amateurs who shorted the high get stopped out and where amateurs who missed the top get lured into buying "the dip." The disciplined reversal trader saw it differently: 18,250 was a *lower high* (below 18,780 and below 18,900), it stalled exactly at broken support, and the daily RSI failed to reclaim its midline. That is textbook step 5 — the failed retest — and the single highest-conviction *add* point in the whole reversal. A second short tranche near 18,220 with a tight stop above 18,320 was justified.

From there the downtrend confirmed itself: 17,700, 17,400 (the first target, where a third of the position was covered), a weak bounce, then acceleration toward 17,000 as the earlier complacent call-writers were forced to cover and the VIX spiked toward 20.

### The outcome

The initial short plus the retest add captured roughly 18,300 (blended entry) down to a 17,150 cover into the eventual climax — on the order of **1,150 points** blended, against an initial risk of ~380 points, a clean multi-R positional reversal held over roughly five weeks.

### The lesson

**Divergence is the alarm clock, not the trade.** The breadth and RSI divergences at 18,900 told the trader a top was *possible*, but the entry waited for the structure break at 18,400 and the highest-conviction add waited for the failed retest at 18,250. A trader who shorted the 18,900 high on divergence alone would likely have been stopped as the index made its final marginal push; the trader who waited for structure caught the same move with a defined, smaller risk. **Sell the failed retest, not the euphoric high.**

## Case Study 2 — The capitulation bottom in a heavyweight (large private bank ~₹1,180 → ₹1,050 → ₹1,400)

### Context

Individual-stock bottoms are emotionally harder than index bottoms because a falling stock carries a *narrative* — an earnings miss, an asset-quality scare, a management concern — that makes buying it feel like catching a knife into bad news. Set this in a large-cap private-sector bank that had fallen from ₹1,180 to the ₹1,050 zone over several weeks on a sector-wide asset-quality worry, with the daily RSI deeply oversold near 22 and the financial press uniformly negative. The stock was down roughly 11% and every bounce had been sold.

### The setup

The reversal signal was a **capitulation candle plus bullish divergence**. On the final flush toward ₹1,048, volume spiked to the highest in months — capitulation, the point where the last frightened holders dump and the stock changes hands from weak to strong owners. Critically, this new price low was *not* confirmed by momentum: the daily RSI made a *higher* low (around 27 versus the prior 22 at a higher price) — bullish divergence, the market's way of showing that selling pressure was fading even as price probed lower. The candle itself was a long-tailed hammer, closing back near ₹1,075 after the intraday spike to ₹1,048.

| Element | Value (approx) |
|---|---|
| Instrument | Large private bank (positional long, cash or futures) |
| Alert conditions | Oversold RSI + bullish divergence + climactic volume hammer |
| Trigger | Daily close above the hammer's high (₹1,095) |
| Stop | Daily close below the capitulation low (₹1,040) |
| Initial risk | ~₹55 (~5%) |
| Target | ₹1,180 (prior breakdown), then ₹1,300, stretch ₹1,400 |
| Regime | Capitulation bottom, fear peaking |

### The trade, bar by bar

The trader did *not* buy the hammer (never catch the knife — wait for confirmation). The entry triggered two sessions later on a close above ₹1,095 at ₹1,102, with the ₹1,040 capitulation low as the hard invalidation. The first move was a grind back to ₹1,150, then the inevitable retest: the stock pulled back to ₹1,090 on lighter volume — crucially, a *higher low* than the ₹1,048 capitulation and holding above the entry-breakout zone. Lighter volume on the pullback confirmed sellers were exhausted. This higher-low retest that held was the confirmation the base was real, and a justified add.

From there the recovery gathered pace as the asset-quality fear proved overdone in the next results and the broader sector re-rated: ₹1,180 (prior breakdown level reclaimed — a powerful bullish tell), ₹1,250, ₹1,300, and a push toward ₹1,400 over the following weeks. The trailing rule was the rising 20-DMA on the daily; as long as pullbacks held it, the position rode.

### The outcome

Entry ₹1,102 (plus the ₹1,090 add), exit on a 20-DMA break near ₹1,380 after the run to ₹1,400 stalled. Roughly **₹280 per share** captured against a ~₹55 initial risk — a **~5R** positional reversal held for around eight weeks.

### The lesson

**Capitulation volume plus bullish divergence marks where a downtrend exhausts, but the entry still waits for confirmation and the conviction comes from the higher-low retest.** The narrative — "the sector has an asset-quality problem" — was precisely what made the price attractive; by the time the narrative turned positive, the stock was already 25% off its low. **Bottoms are bought when the news is worst and the divergence is clearest, not when the story improves.** The reclaim of the prior breakdown level (₹1,180) was the confirmation that the sellers were fully spent and the character had flipped.

## Case Study 3 — The commodity double-bottom (MCX Gold, reconstructed)

### Context

Commodities on MCX add the wrinkle of USDINR — the rupee price of gold is the dollar price times the exchange rate, so a falling dollar-gold can still be supported in rupee terms by a weakening rupee. Set this in MCX Gold (per 10 grams) that had sold off to a support zone near ₹58,200, bounced to ₹60,500, then sold off again to *almost exactly* the same ₹58,300 zone weeks later — the makings of a **double bottom**, one of the most reliable reversal patterns because it shows a support level being tested twice and holding, with the second test typically on *lower* volume and with bullish momentum divergence.

### The setup

The double bottom is not confirmed at the second low — it is confirmed at the *neckline break*. The two lows sat near ₹58,300; the intervening peak (the neckline) was ₹60,500. The pattern completes and triggers only on a decisive close above ₹60,500. The second low showed the confirming tells: lower volume than the first low (selling exhaustion) and a higher RSI low (bullish divergence). A supportive backdrop — a softening USDINR keeping rupee-gold underpinned even as global gold wobbled — added confluence.

| Element | Value (approx) |
|---|---|
| Instrument | MCX Gold (positional long, futures) |
| Pattern | Double bottom, lows ₹58,300, neckline ₹60,500 |
| Confirming tells | Lower volume + bullish RSI divergence on second low; softening USDINR |
| Trigger | Daily close above ₹60,550 (neckline break) |
| Stop | Daily close below ₹59,800 (below neckline retest zone) |
| Initial risk | ~₹750 |
| Target | Neckline break projection ≈ ₹62,700 (₹60,500 + [₹60,500 − ₹58,300]) |
| Regime | Double-bottom reversal, weak-rupee tailwind |

### The trade, bar by bar

The trader did not buy the second low (you cannot know it is a double bottom until the neckline breaks — until then it might just be a lower high before a fresh leg down). The entry triggered on the daily close above ₹60,550 at ₹60,620. The measured-move target was computed by projecting the pattern's height (₹60,500 − ₹58,300 = ₹2,200) above the neckline: ₹60,500 + ₹2,200 ≈ ₹62,700.

The classic post-breakout event followed: a **throwback** to the neckline. Price pulled back to ₹60,400 — briefly dipping just under the ₹60,550 breakout — testing the trader's nerve. But the stop was set below ₹59,800 for exactly this reason: a throwback to *retest a broken neckline* is normal and healthy, and setting the stop right at the breakout would have guaranteed a shakeout. The ₹60,400 retest held (the broken neckline acting as new support), volume dried up on the pullback, and the advance resumed. The rupee continued to soften, adding a tailwind to the rupee-gold price independent of global moves.

### The outcome

The measured move played out: gold pushed from the ₹60,400 retest through ₹61,500, ₹62,000, and tagged ₹62,750 — just past the projected target — where the position was scaled out. Roughly **₹2,100 per 10g** captured on a ~₹750 initial risk, a **~2.8R** trade, with the throwback retest offering an add point for those who took it.

### The lesson

**A double bottom is confirmed at the neckline break, not the second low, and the measured move gives a defined, objective target.** The two disciplines that mattered: (1) waiting for the neckline break rather than guessing the second low, and (2) placing the stop *below the retest zone* rather than at the breakout, so the normal, healthy throwback did not shake the position out. The USDINR confluence is the India-specific lesson — in MCX commodities, always separate the global-commodity view from the rupee view, because a weak rupee can carry a rupee-priced commodity higher even when the dollar price is flat.

## Cross-cutting principles for trading reversals

**1. Divergence and exhaustion are alarms, not entries.** Breadth divergence, RSI divergence, oversold/overbought extremes and climactic volume tell you a reversal is *possible* and to get ready. They do not tell you to enter, because markets can stay divergent far longer than you can stay solvent shorting or catching them.

**2. Enter on structure, confirm on retest.** The objective entry is the *structure break* — the first lower low at a top, the neckline or hammer-high break at a bottom. The highest-conviction add is the *failed retest* (lower high at a top) or *held retest* (higher low at a bottom). Across all three cases, the retest was where conviction and the best risk-reward lived.

**3. Never catch the knife; never call the exact high.** In every case the trader let the market prove the turn before committing — buying the confirmation candle, not the capitulation hammer; shorting the failed retest, not the euphoric high. Being slightly late with confirmation beats being early and repeatedly stopped.

**4. Place stops beyond the retest, not at the trigger.** Throwbacks and pullbacks to the broken level are normal features of real reversals. A stop set right at the breakout guarantees a shakeout; a stop beyond the retest zone (below the neckline, below the capitulation low, above the divergent high) survives the healthy noise.

**5. Stack confluence.** The best reversals had multiple independent confirmations: index top = breadth + RSI + VIX + distribution candle + structure break. Bank bottom = oversold + divergence + climactic volume + hammer + reclaim of prior breakdown. Gold = double bottom + volume + divergence + USDINR tailwind. One signal is a guess; four aligned signals is a thesis.

**6. Use options to define reversal risk.** Because reversals fail more often than trend-continuations, expressing them with defined-risk option structures — bear put spreads at tops, bull call spreads at bottoms — caps the cost of being wrong on a turn that hasn't fully completed, while still capturing most of the move if the reversal confirms.

## Interview-ready summary

Asked how you trade reversals, the professional answer: *"I treat a reversal as a five-step process, not a single candle. Divergence, exhaustion and climactic volume are my alarm clock — they tell me a turn is possible and to get ready, but they are never my entry, because markets stay divergent longer than I can stay solvent fighting them. I enter on the structure break — the first lower low at a top, the neckline or confirmation-candle break at a bottom — and I add the most size at the retest: the failed lower-high bounce at a top, or the held higher-low at a bottom. I place my stop beyond the retest zone so the normal throwback doesn't shake me out, and I stack confluence — breadth, momentum, volume, VIX, and for MCX the rupee — so I'm trading a thesis, not a guess. I never catch the knife and I never try to nail the exact high; being slightly late with confirmation beats being early and repeatedly stopped."*
