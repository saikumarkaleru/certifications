# Rising/Falling Three Methods & Mat Hold

Most candlestick education stops at the flashy reversal signals — the hammers, the engulfings, the morning stars. But the real money in trending markets is not made catching reversals; it is made *staying in* an existing trend through its inevitable pullbacks. The three-candle "continuation" family — the **Rising Three Methods**, the **Falling Three Methods**, and their higher-probability cousin the **Mat Hold** — are precisely the patterns that tell you a pause is just a pause, not a top or a bottom. For an Indian trader riding a Nifty leg or a strong momentum stock like a fresh breakout in an Adani or Trent, learning to read these correctly is the difference between panic-selling the first red candle and calmly holding for the next impulse leg.

## What it is & why it works

The Rising Three Methods is a five-candle bullish continuation pattern. It begins with a **long bullish (green) candle**, followed by a group of **three small-bodied corrective candles** — usually red, but the colour matters less than the fact that they are small and stay *within* the range of that first long green candle — and then a **second long green candle that closes above the high of the first**. Visually it looks like a strong up-move, a brief three-bar "rest", and then a decisive resumption.

The Falling Three Methods is the mirror image in a downtrend: a **long red candle**, three small rising candles that stay inside the first candle's range, and then a **long red candle that closes below the first candle's low**.

Why does it work? It is a compact story of *supply and demand exhaustion during a pullback*. The first long candle establishes that one side (buyers, in the rising version) is in firm control. The three small counter-candles represent profit-booking and short-term counter-trend traders trying to fade the move. Crucially, they *fail to make meaningful progress* — the pullback is shallow, orderly, and low-energy, staying inside the original range. That failure is the signal: the counter-trend crowd is weak. When the fifth candle blasts through the prior high (or low), it confirms that the dominant trend has absorbed all the profit-taking and is ready to extend. In market-structure language, it is a **flag or a shallow pullback** compressed into candlestick form. The three small candles are the flagpole's consolidation; the breakout candle is the resolution.

The **Mat Hold** is the aristocrat of this family. It is structurally similar but has a stricter, more bullish personality. In the classic Mat Hold, the first candle is a long green candle; the *second* candle often gaps up and then the small pullback candles drift down over the next two to three sessions, but — and this is the key distinction — the pullback stays **above the low, or even above the midpoint, of the first long candle**. The retracement is shallower than in a Rising Three Methods, showing even less willingness by sellers to give ground. The final candle again surges to new highs. Because the correction barely dents the first candle, the Mat Hold historically shows one of the *highest continuation reliabilities* of any candlestick pattern in back-tested studies — often quoted in the 70-78% range in Western equity data, and it behaves similarly on liquid NSE large-caps.

## Mechanics, formation rules & settings

There is no "formula" for a candlestick pattern, but there are precise formation conditions. Getting these right is what separates a genuine Rising Three Methods from three random red candles.

**Rising Three Methods — rules:**
1. Candle 1: a long green body, ideally with a range noticeably larger than the recent average (an above-average "trend" candle).
2. Candles 2, 3, 4: three small candles, each with a body smaller than candle 1, that collectively drift lower or move sideways. They should stay **within the high–low range of candle 1** (some purists allow minor spillover of the wicks, but bodies must stay inside).
3. Candle 5: a long green body that **closes above the close (and ideally the high) of candle 1**.
4. Context: it must appear *within an existing uptrend*, not at a random location. A continuation pattern needs a trend to continue.

**Falling Three Methods** — invert every condition: long red, three small rising candles inside the range, long red closing below candle 1's low, within a downtrend.

**Mat Hold — the stricter variant:**
- Candle 1: long green.
- Candle 2: small, often a small green or a gap-up doji, sitting near or above candle 1's high.
- Candles 3–4 (sometimes 3): small candles drifting down, but the pullback **holds above the midpoint or low of candle 1** — the "mat" is held, the trend doesn't fall through it.
- Final candle: strong green close to new highs.

**Practical settings for Indian charts.** Use these patterns primarily on the **daily** and **4-hour** timeframes for swing trades, and the **15-minute** chart for intraday index/futures trades. On the daily, a Rising Three Methods in Nifty typically spans a week of trading — one strong day, three quiet days, one strong day — a very common rhythm around monthly expiry cool-offs. Below the 15-minute timeframe, the pattern degrades into noise because the "three small candles" are often just microstructure and get invalidated by a single tick. On TradingView, no special indicator is needed; you simply mark the range of candle 1 with a horizontal ray to see whether the pullback candles respect it. Chartink cannot scan candlestick sequences well, so these are eyeballed patterns, not screener patterns.

## Worked India example (levels & ₹)

Consider a realistic reconstruction on **Reliance Industries** on the daily chart (verify exact dates on your own chart; levels are approximate and illustrative).

Reliance is in a clear uptrend, having moved from around ₹1,180 to ₹1,320 over several weeks. On a Monday, a strong earnings-driven session prints a long green candle: it opens near ₹1,300 and closes at ₹1,330, with a high of ₹1,333 — a ₹30 range candle, well above the recent ₹15–18 average daily range. This is **candle 1**.

Over the next three sessions, the stock does what strong trends do — it *rests without collapsing*. Tuesday it drifts to close ₹1,325, Wednesday ₹1,321, Thursday ₹1,323. Each of these is a small candle. None closes below ₹1,318, and all bodies stay comfortably inside the ₹1,300–₹1,333 range of candle 1. Volume on these three days is visibly *lower* than Monday's — the classic signature of a healthy pullback: price eases on declining participation, meaning sellers are not aggressive, only profit-takers are trimming.

On Friday, buyers return. The stock gaps up slightly to ₹1,328, runs all day, and closes at ₹1,345 with a high of ₹1,348 — a long green candle that closes **above candle 1's high of ₹1,333**. This is **candle 5**, the confirmation. The Rising Three Methods is complete.

Now the practical trade. The pattern completes on Friday's close at ₹1,345. Aggressive traders enter on the close of the confirmation candle at ₹1,345; more conservative traders wait for Monday and enter on a break above Friday's high of ₹1,348, or on any shallow re-test back toward ₹1,340. The logical stop sits **below the low of the three corrective candles** — the lowest point was roughly ₹1,318 during the pause. A stop at ₹1,314 (a rupee or two below that structural low) is defensible: if price falls back into and below the correction zone, the pattern has failed and the pause was actually distribution.

That gives a risk of about ₹31 per share from the ₹1,345 entry. A conservative first target uses the height of the prior impulse leg projected from the breakout — the leg from ₹1,300 to ₹1,333 was ₹33, so a measured-move target lands near ₹1,378, and a fuller trend-continuation target toward ₹1,400 (a round number and prior resistance). Even the conservative ₹1,378 target gives roughly ₹33 reward against ₹31 risk — a modest ~1:1 — while holding for ₹1,400 delivers a cleaner ~1.8:1. This illustrates an honest truth: continuation patterns often have *modest* individual reward-to-risk but *high hit-rates*, so their edge comes from frequency and from letting winners run past the first target with a trailing stop.

## How to trade it — entry, stop, target

**Entry.** Three tiers, in ascending order of safety and descending order of price:
- *Aggressive:* buy on the close of the confirmation (fifth) candle.
- *Standard:* buy on the next session's break above the confirmation candle's high, using a buy-stop order so you are only filled if momentum continues.
- *Conservative:* wait for a shallow re-test of the breakout zone (the top of candle 1's range) and buy the bounce, which gives a tighter stop and better entry but risks missing runaway moves.

**Stop-loss.** Place it a few ticks below the **lowest low of the three corrective candles** (for Rising Three; above the highest high for Falling Three). This is non-negotiable structural logic: the entire thesis is that the pullback held inside the first candle's range. If price violates the correction low, the thesis is dead. Never widen the stop to "give it room" — a broken continuation pattern often reverses hard because trapped trend-followers all bail together.

**Targets.** Use a layered approach: (1) measured move — project the height of candle 1 (or the prior impulse leg) from the breakout point; (2) the next horizontal resistance / round number; (3) trail the rest with a moving average (20-EMA on daily) or below each subsequent swing low, letting the trend pay you. Book partial profits at target 1 to lock the ~1:1, then let the remainder ride.

**Position sizing.** Because the stop is close (the correction is shallow by definition), these patterns allow *larger* position sizes for the same rupee risk. If you risk ₹5,000 per trade and the stop is ₹31 away, you can hold ~160 shares — a meaningful position. This capital efficiency is a quiet, underrated advantage of continuation patterns over reversal patterns, whose stops are usually wider.

## Confluence — including OI

A Rising Three Methods in isolation is decent; stacked with confluence it becomes a high-conviction trade. Look for:

- **Trend and moving-average alignment.** The pattern should form *above* a rising 20- and 50-EMA. A Rising Three that forms while price is below a falling 50-EMA is suspect — it may be a bear-flag in disguise within a larger downtrend.
- **The pullback holding a key level.** The best versions see the three corrective candles rest exactly on a prior breakout level, a Fibonacci 38.2% retracement of the last leg, or a rising trendline. Confluence of the pause with support dramatically improves odds.
- **Volume signature.** Falling volume during the three-candle pause and *rising* volume on the confirmation candle is the textbook footprint. Volume is your lie-detector: a confirmation candle on weak volume is a warning.
- **Broader-market alignment.** For a stock pattern, check that Nifty and the relevant sector index are also constructive. A bullish continuation in a stock while the Nifty is breaking down is fighting the tide.

**Option-chain / OI confluence (for index & F&O stocks).** In Bank Nifty or Nifty, if you spot a Rising Three Methods on the 15-minute or daily chart, cross-check the option chain. Bullish confirmation is strengthened when: (1) **Call OI at overhead strikes is being unwound / Call writers are covering** as price approaches, removing a resistance ceiling; and (2) **Put writing is building at the strike just below current price**, showing option sellers are confident of a floor near the pattern's support. If, for example, Nifty completes a Rising Three near 24,800 and you see aggressive Put writing at the 24,800 strike (rising Put OI) plus Call unwinding at 25,000, the option-sellers' positioning agrees with the candlestick — a powerful two-source confirmation. Conversely, if heavy Call writing is *building* right overhead, the candlestick breakout may stall into an OI wall, and you should temper targets.

## Pitfalls

1. **Trading it without a prior trend.** These are *continuation* patterns. Spotting the shape in a sideways, rangebound market — where there is no trend to continue — produces frequent whipsaws. Demand a clean, established trend first.
2. **Being loose about the range.** If the three corrective candles balloon outside candle 1's range — especially if one closes below its low — it is *not* a valid Rising Three. A deep pullback signals real supply, not a gentle rest. Discipline here filters out most failures.
3. **Ignoring volume.** A pause on *rising* volume is distribution, not consolidation. If the three small candles come on heavy volume, be very cautious; smart money may be quietly distributing into the strength.
4. **Chasing the confirmation candle.** Buying at the top of a long confirmation candle leaves your stop far away, wrecking your reward-to-risk. Prefer the break-of-high entry or a re-test; let the market come to you.
5. **Confusing Mat Hold with Rising Three.** The Mat Hold's shallower pullback is a *feature*, not a defect — it is a stronger signal. Don't dismiss a Mat Hold because "the correction was too small to be a real pause." That small correction is exactly why it works.
6. **Applying it on ultra-low timeframes.** On the 1- or 5-minute chart, these patterns fire constantly and mean little. Reserve them for 15-minute and higher.
7. **Event risk.** In Indian markets, a "three quiet candles" pause that straddles a results announcement, an RBI policy day, or a Budget can gap violently and blow through your stop. Know your calendar before trusting the pause.

## Interview-ready summary

The Rising Three Methods, Falling Three Methods and Mat Hold are five-candle **continuation** patterns: a strong trend candle, a shallow three-candle pause that stays *inside* the trend candle's range on lower volume, and a decisive breakout candle that resumes the trend. They work because the shallow, low-energy pullback reveals that counter-trend traders are weak, so the dominant side re-asserts control. Trade them only within an established trend; enter on the confirmation candle's close or the break of its high; place the stop just beyond the corrective-candle extreme (a naturally tight stop that permits larger size); and target a measured move plus a trailing exit. The **Mat Hold** is the highest-reliability member because its pullback barely dents the first candle. Stack with EMA-trend alignment, support/Fib confluence, a falling-volume pause with a rising-volume breakout, and — for index/F&O names — supportive option-chain positioning (Put writing below, Call unwinding above). The classic failure mode is a deep pullback that escapes the first candle's range; when that happens, respect the stop, because trapped trend-followers can turn a failed continuation into a sharp reversal.
