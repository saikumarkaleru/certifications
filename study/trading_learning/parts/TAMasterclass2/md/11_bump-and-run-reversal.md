# Bump-and-Run Reversal

## What it is & why it works

The Bump-and-Run Reversal (BARR) is a pattern of *speculative excess*. Thomas Bulkowski catalogued it in the 1990s after noticing that certain stocks did not top out gracefully — they accelerated into a near-vertical ramp fuelled by tips, momentum-chasers and leverage, then collapsed just as violently. The pattern is essentially a chart-picture of a bubble and its bursting, compressed into a few weeks. Because human greed and fear repeat, BARR shows up again and again in Indian small-caps, freshly-listed IPOs, "operator" stocks, and even index heavyweights during blow-off phases.

The pattern works because it captures a shift in *who owns the asset*. During the calm lead-in, a stock trends up at a sustainable slope — real buyers absorbing real supply. Then something changes: an F&O ban list entry, a viral WhatsApp tip, a sudden delivery-volume spike, an "order-win" announcement. Latecomers pile in with borrowed conviction. Price detaches from the trendline and goes parabolic. That final buyer, by definition, has no one left to sell to at a higher price. When the momentum crowd realises the move is over, everyone reaches for the same narrow exit simultaneously. The result is a decline that is faster and deeper than the bump that preceded it. BARR is the geometry of that round-trip.

There is a mirror version — the inverted or *bullish* BARR — where a stock is dumped in a panic capitulation (a downward bump) and then reverses up hard. It is rarer and noisier in Indian markets because downside blow-offs are often halted by circuit filters, but it does appear after forced deleveraging or margin-call cascades.

## Mechanics: the three phases

Bulkowski defined BARR with three distinct phases, and the discipline of measuring each phase is what separates a real BARR from any random spike.

**1. The Lead-in phase.** This is the reference period — typically at least a month of price data, ideally longer. You draw a *lead-in trendline* along the rising lows at a moderate slope, roughly 30 to 45 degrees on a normally-scaled chart. The gentleness matters: the lead-in should look orderly, not frantic. During this phase you also measure the *lead-in height* — the vertical distance from the trendline to the highs, taken at the midpoint of the lead-in. This height defines "normal" volatility for the stock and becomes your yardstick.

**2. The Bump phase.** Price breaks away from the lead-in trendline and rallies at a much steeper angle — Bulkowski specified roughly 45 degrees or steeper, and in practice the good ones go near-vertical. The rally is *valid as a bump* only if the peak rises to at least **twice the lead-in height** above the trendline. That "2x height" filter is the whole point — it confirms the move is genuinely anomalous, an emotional overshoot rather than trend continuation. Volume typically balloons during the bump; you often see the highest-volume candles of the entire chart here.

**3. The Run phase.** Price rolls over and falls back toward the lead-in trendline. The *sell trigger* is the break of that trendline. Once broken, the pattern projects a decline that frequently overshoots well below the trendline — often back to where the bump began, and in aggressive cases far beyond it. The run is where the money is made on the short side (or where the exit is made if you were long).

A clean BARR therefore reads left-to-right as: gentle ramp → parabolic spike to at least 2x normal height → rollover → trendline break → collapse.

## Settings and how to draw it

- **Timeframe:** Daily charts are the natural home for BARR in equities — the pattern usually plays out over 6-15 weeks. Weekly charts catch multi-month bubbles (think a small-cap that 4x'd over a quarter). Intraday BARRs exist on 5-/15-minute charts during single-session blow-offs but are lower-quality.
- **Scale:** Use *arithmetic* scale, not log, when judging the 45-degree slopes — Bulkowski's angle rules assume linear scaling. On log scale a parabolic move looks tamer than it is.
- **Trendline:** Anchor the lead-in trendline to two or more swing lows in the orderly phase. Do not let the bump's lows pull the line steeper — the line must reflect the *pre-bump* trend.
- **Height measurement:** Measure lead-in height at the lead-in midpoint, trendline-to-high. Multiply by two; the bump peak must clear that.

## Worked India example (levels & ₹)

Consider a mid-cap capital-goods stock — call the reconstruction *"BharatForge-type"* behaviour, levels approximate and to be verified on your own charts.

Through roughly March-April, the stock trends from ₹880 to ₹1,020 in an orderly grind. You draw the lead-in trendline along the rising lows; at the midpoint the trendline sits near ₹930 while highs poke to ₹975 — a **lead-in height of about ₹45**. Your 2x threshold is therefore ₹90 above the trendline.

In early May an order-book announcement hits. Delivery volumes triple. The stock rips from ₹1,020 to ₹1,240 in nine sessions — a near-vertical bump. Extend the lead-in trendline forward to that date and it reads around ₹1,010; the peak at ₹1,240 is **₹230 above the line — more than 2x the ₹45 height (5x, in fact).** This is a textbook bump: emotional, over-extended, volume-drenched.

Around the ₹1,240 peak you notice classic exhaustion: a gravestone-doji day on record volume, then a bearish engulfing. Price begins the run — ₹1,240 → ₹1,180 → ₹1,120. Extend the lead-in trendline to the current date; it now sits near ₹1,060. When price closes below ₹1,060 (the **trendline break**), the BARR sell signal is live.

The projection: BARRs commonly retrace the *entire* bump and overshoot. A conservative target is the bump's origin (~₹1,020); an aggressive target uses the lead-in height projected below the trendline (₹1,060 − ₹45 = ~₹1,015, or a full-height ₹90 giving ~₹970). Price ultimately bleeds to ₹945 over the next three weeks, undercutting the pattern start — a decline of nearly ₹300 (24%) from peak. The latecomers who bought the ₹1,200 breakout are trapped.

## How to trade it (entry / stop / target)

**Short side (the classic play):**

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| Setup | Confirmed bump (peak ≥ 2x lead-in height) followed by rollover |
| Entry | Close below the extended lead-in trendline; or a lower-high short after the peak with a stop above it |
| Stop | Just above the most recent lower swing high (in the ₹ example, above ~₹1,180) or above the bump peak for a wider stop |
| Target 1 | Bump origin (~₹1,020) — book 50-60% |
| Target 2 | Trendline minus one lead-in height (~₹1,015 / ₹970) — trail the rest |
| Timeframe | Daily; hold days-to-weeks |
| Regime | Works best in a broad market that is topping or choppy; dangerous to short in a raging bull tape |

**Long side (if you already hold):** treat the 2x-height bump as a *distribution alert*. The single best use of BARR for most investors is not shorting — it is **exiting a winner near the top.** When a stock you own goes parabolic to 2x its normal deviation and prints an exhaustion candle, book profits into the strength rather than riding the run down.

**Bullish BARR (mirror):** after a downward capitulation bump that overshoots 2x below a declining lead-in line, go long on the reclaim of the trendline, stop below the panic low, target the bump origin. Position smaller — these fire less reliably in circuit-filtered names.

## Confluence (including OI)

BARR is far more tradable when it lines up with other evidence:

- **Volume signature:** Genuine bumps peak on climactic volume, then the run begins as volume *stays elevated on down days*. Rising volume on the decline confirms distribution.
- **RSI / momentum divergence:** The bump peak often prints RSI above 80 with a *bearish divergence* — price makes a higher high while RSI makes a lower high. That divergence is your early warning even before the trendline breaks.
- **F&O and Open Interest:** In F&O stocks, a parabolic bump is frequently accompanied by the stock entering or approaching the **F&O ban period** (open interest crossing 95% of market-wide position limit). That is a red flag of speculative overheating. As the run begins, watch for **long unwinding** — price falling *with* falling OI signals longs bailing out (confirms the reversal), whereas price falling with *rising* OI signals fresh shorts (aggressive, can lead to a sharper down-move but also to violent short-covering bounces). At the peak, heavy **call writing** at the strike just above spot (rising call OI capping price) marks the ceiling; a break below the highest-put-OI strike opens the run.
- **Delivery percentage:** A collapsing delivery% during the bump (i.e., the rally is intraday/speculative, not investment buying) warns that the move lacks real hands.
- **Sector/index context:** If the broad index is itself topping (Nifty printing its own exhaustion), individual BARRs resolve faster and deeper.

The highest-conviction short is: 2x bump + RSI bearish divergence + climax volume + long-unwinding on the OI + a bearish reversal candle at the peak. That is four independent witnesses telling the same story.

## Pitfalls

1. **Shorting the bump too early.** Parabolas can extend far past what looks absurd — "the market can stay irrational longer than you can stay solvent." Never short into a rising bump. Wait for the *rollover and the trendline break*. Momentum blow-offs have carried stocks 30-50% beyond the point where the pattern "should" have topped.

2. **No hard 2x measurement.** Traders eyeball a spike and call it a BARR. Without measuring lead-in height and confirming the peak clears 2x, you are just guessing. Many "BARRs" are ordinary continuation rallies that resume upward.

3. **Log-scale distortion.** Judging slopes on a log chart makes bumps look normal. Use arithmetic scale for the angle test.

4. **Circuit filters (India-specific).** Small-caps hitting upper or lower circuits gap through your entry/stop. On the short side, a stock in a 5% lower circuit can trap you with no fill on the way down (good) — but on any bounce it can gap up through your stop. Size for gap risk; prefer names with liquid options so you can define risk with a put spread instead of a naked short.

5. **Borrow / short constraints.** In the Indian cash market you cannot casually hold short overnight (intraday only for most retail). The practical BARR short is executed via **futures or a bear put spread** in F&O names, or as an *exit* signal in cash holdings. Trying to force a cash short overnight is not possible.

6. **The dead-cat bounce.** After the trendline break, expect a sharp counter-rally (short-covering) that can retrace 30-50% of the initial run. This shakes out early shorts. Plan for it: keep the stop above structure, not on a tick basis, and consider re-entering on the failed bounce.

7. **Fundamental catalysts cut both ways.** If the parabolic move is driven by a genuine, durable re-rating (a real earnings step-change), the "run" may be shallow and the stock consolidates rather than collapses. BARR assumes *speculative* excess; distinguish a story-stock re-rating from a pure momentum bubble.

## Interview-ready summary

Bump-and-Run Reversal is a top (or bottom) pattern that mechanises the anatomy of a speculative blow-off. It has three phases: an orderly **lead-in** (moderate uptrend, along which you draw a trendline and measure a reference height), a **bump** (a steep, emotional over-extension that must exceed **twice** the lead-in height to qualify), and a **run** (the rollover and collapse, triggered by a close back below the lead-in trendline). You trade it by *exiting longs* into the parabolic strength or *shorting the trendline break* — never shorting into the rising bump — with a stop above the last lower-high and targets at the bump's origin and one lead-in height beyond. Confluence from climax volume, RSI bearish divergence, F&O ban-list overheating, and OI long-unwinding sharpens the edge. In Indian markets it is most useful as a *sell discipline for over-extended small-caps and momentum names*, executed short via futures or put spreads rather than cash shorts, and always sized for circuit-gap risk. The core insight it teaches: parabolas are round-trips, and the last buyer always pays.
