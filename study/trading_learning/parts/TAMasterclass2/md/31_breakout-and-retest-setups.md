# Setups: Breakout & Retest (5 setups)

A breakout is the moment price escapes a zone of balance — a range, a base, a trendline, a chart pattern — and begins a directional move because one side of the market has decisively overwhelmed the other. Breakout trading is seductive because the winners are often the biggest, fastest moves on the chart: the multi-week base that resolves into a 30% run, the Bank Nifty range that snaps and delivers 800 points in a session. But breakouts are also where retail traders lose the most, because the market is full of *false* breakouts — deliberate stop-hunts and failed pushes designed, in effect, to trap the eager. The professional's answer is the **retest**: instead of buying the breakout candle itself, you wait for price to come back and confirm the broken level as new support before committing. You trade fewer breakouts, later, with far higher odds and a much tighter stop.

This chapter gives you five named breakout-and-retest setups for Nifty, Bank Nifty and liquid NSE stocks — the horizontal range break, the trendline break, the volatility-contraction (VCP) break, the opening-range break, and the chart-pattern (triangle/flag) break — each with exact rules and an India-specific worked example. The unifying discipline: **a breakout is a hypothesis; the retest is the confirmation; volume is the referee.**

## Why breakouts work — and why so many fail

A breakout works when it marks a genuine transfer of control. Price consolidates because buyers and sellers are roughly balanced; the range's edges are where the disagreement is sharpest — a ceiling defended by sellers, a floor defended by buyers. When accumulated demand finally exhausts the sellers at the ceiling, their defence collapses, their stops trigger (buy orders that add fuel), and sidelined buyers rush in fearing they'll miss the move. That cascade — exhausted defenders, triggered stops, FOMO entries — is the engine of a real breakout, and it is why the best breakouts *accelerate* rather than drift.

Breakouts fail when the move lacks that fuel. A push above the range on thin volume, with no real exhaustion of the defenders, quickly runs out of buyers; sellers who stepped aside momentarily return, price falls back inside the range, and everyone who chased the breakout candle is trapped above the level, becoming forced sellers on the way down. This is the false breakout (or "bull trap"), and it is extraordinarily common — arguably the majority of first breakout attempts at any given level fail or stall. The retest exists precisely to filter these: a *true* breakout, on the pullback, finds the old ceiling now acting as a floor (buyers defending the level they just conquered); a *false* one slices back through the level because there were never enough committed buyers to defend it.

Two referees settle the question. **Volume:** a real breakout expands volume on the break (conviction) and contracts it on the retest (weak selling); a false one breaks on weak volume or reverses on heavy volume. **The close:** a decisive *close* beyond the level matters far more than an intraday wick that pokes through and retreats. Demand both.

## Setup 1 — Horizontal Range / Base Breakout & Retest

The cleanest breakout of all. A stock trades in a well-defined horizontal range for weeks — a resistance ceiling tested multiple times, a support floor beneath. When it closes decisively above the ceiling on volume, then pulls back to retest that ceiling (now support) and holds, you enter the resumption. The measured target projects the range's height above the breakout.

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| Regime | Multi-week horizontal range; ceiling tested 2+ times |
| Trigger | Decisive close above ceiling on rising volume, THEN pullback to ceiling holds + bullish candle; enter on break of that candle's high |
| Stop | Below the retest low / back inside the range |
| Target | Measured move = range height added to breakout; partial + trail |
| Timeframe | Daily swing ideal |
| Filter | Skip if breakout close was marginal or on falling volume |

**Worked example — Larsen & Toubro.** L&T ranges between ₹3,600 (floor) and ₹3,800 (ceiling) for six weeks — a ₹200 range. It closes decisively at ₹3,845 on volume 60% above average (the breakout). Rather than chase, you wait: three sessions later price eases back to ₹3,810, retesting the old ₹3,800 ceiling as support, and prints a bullish engulfing candle on light selling volume. You buy the break of its high at ₹3,838, stop below the retest low at ₹3,775 (back inside the range), risking ₹63/share. Measured target = ₹3,800 + ₹200 = ₹4,000; you book partial there and trail the runner. The retest gave you a better price than the breakout candle *and* a tighter, more logical stop — and it filtered out the risk that the initial break was false.

**Confluence:** in the option chain, watch the breakout strike flip from heavy Call OI (resistance) to Put writing (support) — writers migrating confirms the level's polarity has genuinely flipped. Sector strength and a Nifty tailwind add odds.

**Pitfalls:** not every breakout retests — the strongest ones run away without looking back, and waiting for a retest means occasionally missing the best movers. Accept that trade-off; the retest discipline sacrifices a few runners to avoid many traps, and over a large sample it wins.

## Setup 2 — Trendline Breakout & Retest

A trendline connecting successive swing highs (in a downtrend) or swing lows (in an uptrend) captures the trend's slope. When price breaks the line and then retests it from the other side, holding, it signals the old trend's control has broken and often begins a new move. This is the classic tool for catching the *turn* of a down-trend into an up-trend, or the acceleration of a decelerating one.

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| Regime | Clear trendline with 3+ touches |
| Trigger | Decisive close through the line, THEN retest of the line from the other side holds; enter on the reversal confirmation |
| Stop | Beyond the retest extreme / back across the line |
| Target | Prior structure / measured move; partial + trail |
| Timeframe | Daily swing; intraday for finer lines |
| Filter | The more touches the line has, the more meaningful its break |

**Worked example — Nifty downtrend break.** Nifty has been grinding lower, each rally capped by a descending trendline drawn across the falling highs, currently passing through ~22,400. Price closes above it at 22,480 on strong volume. Two days later it dips back to 22,410, retesting the broken trendline from above, and holds with a bullish hammer. You go long (index proxy or ATM call spread) on the break of the hammer high at 22,460, stop below the retest low at 22,300. Target the prior swing high near 22,900. The trendline retest is powerful because the line was, until moments ago, resistance for the whole market; its flip to support on the retest is a visible, widely-watched confirmation that the down-move has lost control.

**Confluence:** a trendline break aligns best with a broader structure shift — a break of the down-trend's last lower high, a bullish moving-average cross, or a falling India VIX. On individual stocks, rising volume through the line and light volume on the retest is the tell.

**Pitfalls:** trendlines are subjective — drawn steeply or through wicks they "break" constantly and mean nothing. Anchor to obvious swing points, prefer lines with three or more clean touches, and demand a decisive close through, not a wick.

## Setup 3 — Volatility-Contraction Pattern (VCP) Breakout

Popularised by Mark Minervini and adapted well to Indian momentum stocks, the VCP is a base in which each successive pullback is *shallower* than the last and volume steadily dries up — a coiling spring. The tightening range and vanishing supply mean sellers are exhausted; when price breaks the tight final contraction on a volume surge, the move can be explosive. It is the premier setup for catching leadership stocks early in a major run.

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| Regime | Leader/strong stock forming a base with 2-4 progressively tighter contractions |
| Trigger | Break above the final (tightest) contraction / pivot on a clear volume expansion |
| Stop | Below the pivot low / the last contraction low (tight) |
| Target | Trend continuation; trail on 10/20 EMA — VCPs are for riding, not scalping |
| Timeframe | Daily positional |
| Filter | Volume must dry up through the base and surge on the break; skip if volume is erratic |

**Worked example — a mid-cap leader.** A capital-goods leader bases after a big run: first pullback −18%, second −11%, third −6%, each on progressively lighter volume — the textbook VCP contraction. The final tight pivot sits at ₹1,180 with volume having gone nearly dead. Price then breaks ₹1,180 on volume triple the recent average. You buy the pivot break at ₹1,186, stop below the last contraction low at ₹1,150 — a tight ~3% risk. Because the base signalled that sellers were fully exhausted, the breakout runs to new highs; you trail beneath the 10 EMA and ride from ₹1,186 toward ₹1,450 over the following weeks, exiting only when the EMA finally breaks. The genius of the VCP is that the *tightness* of the final pivot lets you place a very close stop, producing enormous reward-to-risk when the move works.

**Confluence:** VCPs work best in true relative-strength leaders during healthy markets — buy them when the stock is near new highs and outperforming Nifty. A VCP that resolves *with* a strong sector and index tape has the best odds. The volume signature (dry base, surge break) is non-negotiable.

**Pitfalls:** in weak or corrective markets even perfect VCPs fail — the base is only as good as the environment. And the "retest" for a VCP is optional and often absent; the tight pivot stop substitutes for it, so you must take the break on the volume surge rather than always waiting for a pullback.

## Setup 4 — Opening-Range Breakout & Retest (intraday)

The first 15-30 minutes of the session establish an opening range — the high and low set as overnight orders and early positioning clear. A break of that range, in the direction of the broader trend, with a retest that holds, is the staple intraday breakout setup on Bank Nifty and momentum stocks. It works because the opening range represents the day's first agreed value; escaping it signals the day has chosen a direction.

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| Regime | Trending-day potential; align with prior-day/gap bias |
| Trigger | Break of the 15-min opening range high on volume, THEN retest of the range high holds; enter on the resumption |
| Stop | Below the retest low / back inside the opening range |
| Target | Measured move of the opening range; day's extremes; scale out |
| Timeframe | 5-min / 15-min intraday |
| Filter | Best on days with a directional gap or clear cue; avoid flat, newsless opens |

**Worked example — Bank Nifty opening-range break.** Bank Nifty's first 15 minutes carve a range of 48,700-48,900 (a 200-point ORB) on a mildly positive gap. At 9:45 a 5-min candle closes at 48,935 above the range high on a volume pop. You don't chase — you wait for the retest. At 10:05 price dips to 48,905, holding just above the old range high, and prints a bullish 5-min candle. You enter long on its break at 48,930 via a slightly ITM call or futures, stop below the retest low at 48,860 (risk ~70 points). Measured target = 48,900 + 200 = 49,100 first, then trail toward the day's high. The retest converts the ORB from a coin-flip chase into a defined-risk entry with the old range boundary as your line in the sand.

**Confluence:** ORB works far better *with* the higher-timeframe and gap bias — a break to the upside on a gap-up trend day beats a countertrend break. VWAP support beneath the retest and rising cumulative buying volume confirm. On expiry days, mind the whippy, theta-driven Bank Nifty tape — ORBs can be treacherous into the close.

**Pitfalls:** on rangebound, low-conviction days the opening range breaks in both directions and traps everyone — the dreaded "ORB chop." Trade it only when the day has a directional catalyst, and let the retest filter the fakeouts.

## Setup 5 — Chart-Pattern Breakout & Retest (triangle / rectangle / flag)

Classical chart patterns — ascending triangles, symmetrical triangles, rectangles, bull flags — are simply structured ranges with a bias baked into their shape. Their breakout-and-retest behaves like the horizontal range break but with a directional lean and a clean measured-move target derived from the pattern's dimensions. This setup packages pattern recognition into a repeatable breakout trade.

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| Regime | A recognised pattern (ascending/symmetrical triangle, rectangle, flag) on the trading timeframe |
| Trigger | Decisive close beyond the pattern boundary on volume, THEN retest of the boundary holds; enter on confirmation |
| Stop | Back inside the pattern / below the retest low |
| Target | Measured move = pattern height projected from the breakout point |
| Timeframe | Daily swing or intraday |
| Filter | Volume should contract into the pattern's apex and expand on the break |

**Worked example — ascending triangle on TCS.** TCS forms an ascending triangle: a flat resistance at ₹4,100 tested repeatedly, with rising higher lows beneath (₹3,980, ₹4,020, ₹4,055) squeezing price toward the ceiling — a bullish structure signalling buyers growing more aggressive. The pattern's height is ₹120 (₹3,980 to ₹4,100). Price closes at ₹4,135 on strong volume, then retests ₹4,105 (old resistance as support) with a bullish candle. You buy the break at ₹4,132, stop below the retest low at ₹4,075. Measured target = ₹4,100 + ₹120 = ₹4,220; partial there, trail the runner. The ascending triangle's rising lows told you buyers were willing to pay more each time — the breakout simply confirmed what the structure already implied.

**Confluence:** the pattern break aligns best with the prevailing trend (a bull flag in an uptrend is higher-odds than a countertrend break). Volume drying into the apex and surging on the break is the hallmark; option-chain OI shifting from Call resistance to Put support at the breakout strike confirms on F&O names.

**Pitfalls:** subjective pattern-drawing invites bias — you can "see" a triangle anywhere if you want a trade. Insist on clean, obvious boundaries with multiple touches, and let volume and the retest, not wishful geometry, make the decision.

## The retest discipline — timing, and when to skip it

The retest is the heart of this chapter, so be precise about it. A *valid* retest comes back to the broken level, kisses or slightly pierces it, and turns *without closing decisively back inside* — accompanied by contracting (weak) volume. An *invalid* retest slices straight back through the level on rising volume: that is the breakout failing, and it is your signal to stand aside (or, for the aggressive, to trade the failure the other way). Give the level a small buffer; markets routinely dip a touch under a broken ceiling to shake tight stops before resuming, so place your stop below the *noise*, not exactly at the line.

Know, too, when *not* to wait for a retest. The very strongest breakouts — high-momentum VCPs, gap-and-go leaders, index breaks on powerful cues — often never look back, and insisting on a retest means missing them entirely. The resolution is to size the decision to the setup: for VCP and opening-range breaks in strong tapes, taking the break on a decisive volume surge is legitimate (the tight pivot/range stop substitutes for the retest); for horizontal ranges, trendlines and chart patterns in ordinary conditions, the retest earns its keep by filtering the many false breaks. A common professional compromise is to take a *half* position on the breakout close and *add the other half* on a successful retest — capturing the runaways while still confirming the laggards.

## Risk, sizing and the F&O angle

Breakout-and-retest setups are prized for the same reason as pullbacks: the retest gives you a tight, logical stop just inside the broken level, so R-multiples on the measured-move target are generous. Fix risk at 1% of capital; let winners run to the measured move and trail the rest. Because false breakouts are frequent, *expect* a meaningful share of small stop-outs — the method's profit comes from the few breakouts that turn into large trends, not from a high hit-rate. Never widen a stop to "give the breakout room"; a breakout that returns decisively inside the base has failed by definition.

On F&O, breakouts pair naturally with defined-risk option structures. A confirmed range break with a near retest stop is well expressed by a bull-call spread (cheap, capped risk, benefits from the directional pop) or, on indices, by buying slightly ITM options / futures to avoid the theta bleed that punishes OTM weeklies while you wait for the retest. Always read the chain around the breakout level: heavy Call OI at the ceiling that *unwinds* (falling OI) as price breaks through is short-covering fuel; a migration of Put writing up to the broken level after the break confirms it as fresh support. On Bank Nifty especially, respect expiry-day mechanics — theta and pin-risk distort intraday breakouts near the close.

## Interview-ready summary

Breakout-and-retest setups trade price escaping a zone of balance — a range, trendline, base or chart pattern — while using the *retest* of the broken level to filter the market's abundant false breakouts. The five core setups are: **(1) the horizontal range/base break** — buy the retest of a broken multi-week ceiling now acting as support, targeting the range's measured move; **(2) the trendline break** — buy the retest of a broken trend line from the other side, catching a trend turn; **(3) the VCP break** — buy the pivot of a volatility-contraction base on a volume surge in a leadership stock, with a tight pivot stop; **(4) the opening-range break** — buy the intraday retest of a broken opening range in the direction of the day's bias; and **(5) the chart-pattern break** — buy the retest of a triangle/rectangle/flag boundary, projecting the pattern's height. Two referees govern every one: **volume** (expanding on the break, contracting on the retest) and a **decisive close** (not a wick) beyond the level. The retest supplies a tight, logical stop and filters traps — at the cost of occasionally missing runaway breakouts, which is why VCP and ORB setups may take the break directly on a volume surge. Risk stays at ~1% per trade; profit comes from the few breakouts that become big trends, not from a high win-rate.
