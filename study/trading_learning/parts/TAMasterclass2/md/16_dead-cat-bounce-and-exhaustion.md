# Dead-Cat Bounce & Exhaustion Patterns

There is an old, grim piece of trading-floor wisdom: "Even a dead cat will bounce if it falls from a great height." It is crude, but it captures a truth that costs retail traders more money than almost any other single misread — the difference between a **reversal** and a **bounce**. A stock crashes 30% on a fraud allegation, rallies 12% the next day, and half the retail crowd piles in convinced the bottom is in. Two weeks later it's 25% below where they bought. That rally was not a bottom. It was a **dead-cat bounce** — a temporary, mechanical relief rally inside an ongoing downtrend, the last exhale before the next leg down.

The flip side of the coin is **exhaustion** — the point where a trend genuinely runs out of fuel and reverses for real. Both a dead-cat bounce and a true bottom start the same way: a sharp fall, then a rally. The entire skill is in telling them apart *before* you commit capital. This chapter is about that discrimination. It covers the anatomy of the dead-cat bounce, the family of exhaustion signals (blow-off tops, selling climaxes, exhaustion gaps, volume and momentum exhaustion), the tests that separate a real reversal from a trap, and how to trade both — because you can trade the bounce itself *and* you can trade the failure of the bounce.

## What they are and why they work

A **dead-cat bounce (DCB)** is a temporary recovery in price during a sustained downtrend, which is then followed by a continuation of the decline to new lows. It is a *continuation* pattern wearing the costume of a reversal. The bounce is real — price genuinely rises, sometimes 10–20% — but it is driven not by fresh conviction buying but by mechanical, temporary forces:

- **Short covering.** After a big drop, shorts take profits, and their buying pushes price up. This buying is finite — once shorts have covered, it stops, and the underlying selling pressure reasserts.
- **Bargain-hunting reflexes.** Traders trained to "buy the dip" reflexively buy, providing a temporary bid that soon exhausts.
- **Oversold technical bounces.** Stretched-below-the-mean prices snap back toward moving averages regardless of fundamentals — a rubber-band effect, not a change in trend.
- **No fundamental repair.** Critically, *nothing has changed* about why the stock fell. The fraud is still a fraud, the guidance cut is still a cut, the sector is still de-rating. The reason for the decline is intact, so the decline resumes.

**Exhaustion**, by contrast, is when a trend genuinely runs out of participants. A downtrend exhausts when the last forced sellers have sold — there is literally no one left to sell, so even modest buying turns price up and *keeps* it up. An uptrend exhausts when the last greedy buyers have bought — a blow-off — and there's no one left to buy. Exhaustion produces a *real* reversal because the supply/demand balance has actually flipped, not just paused.

Why does the distinction "work" as an edge? Because the market gives *observable tells* that separate the two. Volume, the reclaim of structure, the behaviour on the retest, and the fundamental catalyst all leave fingerprints. Learn the fingerprints and you stop buying dead cats.

## The anatomy of a dead-cat bounce

A textbook DCB unfolds in four acts:

1. **The event drop.** A sharp, often gap-driven decline on a specific catalyst — bad results, a downgrade, a regulatory action, a promoter-pledge scare. Big volume, big range, price gaps down and stays down.
2. **The relief rally.** Over the next few sessions price bounces, often sharply — 8%, 12%, sometimes more. Volume on the bounce is typically *lower* than the volume on the drop (the first tell). It's short covering and reflex dip-buying, not accumulation.
3. **The stall.** The bounce runs into resistance — very often the *underside of the level that broke* (broken support becomes resistance), or a declining moving average (the 20-DMA / 50-DMA sloping down). It stalls there and can't reclaim the broken structure. This is the decisive tell: **a true bottom reclaims broken levels; a dead-cat bounce fails at them.**
4. **The resumption.** Sellers who missed the first exit use the bounce as their chance to get out. Supply overwhelms the exhausted short-covering bid, and price rolls over to new lows — often accelerating, because the trapped bounce-buyers now become forced sellers too.

The most useful single question to ask of any post-crash rally: **"Has it reclaimed what it broke, or is it stalling under it?"** Reclaim = possible reversal. Stall under = dead cat.

## The family of exhaustion patterns

Exhaustion is the *genuine* article — the tells that a trend is truly ending. The main members:

**1. Selling climax (capitulation).** The end of a downtrend often comes on the *highest* volume of the entire decline — a panic flush where everyone who was going to sell finally sells, all at once, into a vacuum. Price makes a sharp new low and then reverses hard, closing well off the low (a long-tailed spike / hammer). The huge volume is the tell: it's not more selling to fear, it's the *last* selling. This is the V-bottom's engine (see the V-patterns chapter). The distinguishing feature from a DCB setup is **volume climax + reclaim**, not just a bounce.

**2. Blow-off top (buying climax).** The mirror at market tops — a near-vertical, accelerating final advance on euphoric, climactic volume, then a sharp reversal. The last buyers pile in; there's no one left to buy; price collapses. Parabolic small-cap runs in India end this way routinely.

**3. Exhaustion gap.** A gap that occurs *near the end* of a trend rather than the start. An up-trend that's been running gaps up one more time on climactic volume — but instead of continuing, it fails and gets filled quickly. The gap represented the last burst of demand, not fresh demand. An exhaustion gap that fills fast (within days) and reverses is a powerful top/bottom signal. Contrast with a *breakaway* gap (starts a move) and a *runaway/measuring* gap (middle of a move) — the exhaustion gap is the *last* gap.

**4. Volume exhaustion / drying up.** A trend that advances on steadily *declining* volume is running out of participants. The move continues on momentum and inertia, but the fuel gauge is near empty. Combined with price stretching far from its moving average, this is a warning that a reversal or sharp pullback is near.

**5. Momentum exhaustion (divergence).** Price makes a new extreme but the oscillator (RSI, MACD histogram) makes a *lesser* extreme — a lower RSI high at a higher price high (bearish divergence at a top), or a higher RSI low at a lower price low (bullish divergence at a bottom). The move is losing internal strength even as price stretches. Multiple divergences stacked up (a "triple divergence") are especially potent exhaustion signals.

**6. Climactic candle signatures.** Long-tailed spikes (hammers at bottoms, shooting stars at tops), exhaustion "shooting-star into a doji" clusters, and outsized reversal bars closing against the trend — all mark the moment the trend's momentum failed.

## Worked India example (levels & ₹)

**The dead-cat bounce that traps.** Consider a mid-cap NBFC that gets hit by a rating downgrade and a governance concern. The stock, which had been trading around ₹520, gaps down and crashes over three sessions to **₹360** — a 30% collapse on enormous volume. On day four it *bounces*: it rallies from ₹360 to ₹405 (a 12.5% pop) as shorts cover and bargain-hunters step in. Retail message boards declare "the bottom is in — 30% off, oversold RSI, must-buy."

Here's the discrimination. The prior support that broke on the way down was ₹450 (an old base). The bounce to ₹405 stalls *well below* ₹450 — it can't even reach, let alone reclaim, the broken shelf. The 20-DMA (~₹460) is sloping down overhead. Bounce volume is *half* the crash volume. And crucially, *nothing has repaired the fundamental* — the downgrade stands. Every tell says **dead cat**: failed under broken support, weak volume, downtrend MAs overhead, catalyst intact. Two weeks later the stock breaks ₹360 and grinds to ₹300. The bounce-buyers at ₹405 are down 25%.

**How you'd trade the failure:** rather than buying the bounce, you *short the stall*. Trigger: a bearish reversal candle at ~₹400–405 (under the broken ₹450 shelf) with a close back below ₹385. Stop: above ₹420 (above the bounce high). Target: a retest of ₹360, then measured continuation toward ₹300. The DCB is one of the few patterns where the *failure* is the tradable event.

**The real bottom, for contrast.** Now the same stock, a month later at ₹300, prints a very different picture: a final flush to **₹285** on the *highest* volume yet (a selling climax), a long-tailed hammer closing back at ₹315, and over the next week it *reclaims* ₹330, then ₹360 (the level that had been resistance), holding above it on a retest — with RSI showing a higher low against the ₹285 print (bullish divergence). *That* reclaim-and-hold, on climax volume with divergence, is a genuine exhaustion bottom. The tell that separates it from the earlier dead cat: it **took out structure to the upside and held it**, on climactic (not weak) volume. You'd buy the reclaim of ₹330, stop below ₹285, targeting ₹360 then ₹400.

For the options trader: after a crash, implied volatility on the name is sky-high and put premiums are fat. Selling puts into a *confirmed* exhaustion bottom (climax + reclaim) harvests both direction and the IV crush. But selling puts into a mere dead-cat bounce — before reclaim — is how you get run over when the next leg down hits and IV expands further. The reclaim is the gatekeeper.

## How to trade it: entry, stop, target

**Setup A — Short the failed bounce (trading the DCB itself).**

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| Context | Sharp downtrend, event-driven crash, catalyst *unrepaired* |
| Trigger | Bounce stalls at broken support / declining MA and prints a bearish reversal candle; enter on close back below the bounce's midpoint |
| Stop | Above the bounce high (tight, defined) |
| Target 1 | Retest of the prior crash low |
| Target 2 | Measured continuation below the low (equal to the first leg) |
| Timeframe | Daily; intraday for fast-moving F&O names |
| Best regime | Down-trending, weak-volume bounce, MAs sloping down |

**Setup B — Buy the confirmed exhaustion bottom (trading the real reversal).**

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| Context | Selling climax on *highest* volume of the decline, long-tailed reversal bar |
| Trigger | Price *reclaims* a broken structural level and holds it on a retest; RSI shows bullish divergence |
| Stop | Below the climax low (the spike extreme) |
| Target 1 | Underside of the next resistance / prior broken support |
| Target 2 | Measured retrace of the decline |
| Timeframe | Daily |
| Best regime | Post-capitulation, VIX spiking-and-reversing (for indices) |

The governing principle: **let the reclaim decide.** Never pre-empt the bottom. If you must be early, keep size tiny and treat it as a lottery ticket, not a position. The disciplined edge is: short the stall under broken structure (DCB), or buy the reclaim of broken structure on climax volume (real bottom) — and let the level, not the P&L of the crowd on Twitter, make the call.

## Confluence — including OI and options

- **Volume is the master tell.** A crash-then-bounce on *declining* volume = dead cat. A crash-then-reversal on *climactic* (highest-of-the-decline) volume = potential exhaustion bottom. This single comparison resolves most cases.
- **The reclaim test.** Does the rally get back above the level that broke, and hold on a retest? Reclaim = reversal candidate. Stall beneath = dead cat. Non-negotiable.
- **Moving-average posture.** A bounce into a *down-sloping* 20/50-DMA that gets rejected is textbook DCB. A reversal that reclaims and starts *turning up* the short MA is more credible.
- **Momentum divergence** at the low (bullish RSI divergence) supports a real bottom; its absence supports a dead cat.
- **The catalyst.** Ask: *has the reason for the fall changed?* If the fraud/downgrade/guidance-cut still stands, the bounce is almost certainly a dead cat. Fundamentals gate the technicals here more than in most patterns.
- **Option chain / OI:**
  - Into a suspected bottom, watch for **put writers stepping in** — OI building on the put side at strikes below the low, on *rising* underlying, signals institutions willing to defend the level. That supports a real reversal.
  - If puts are still being *bought* aggressively (OI up on rising put premium) *into* the bounce, fear is not done — favour the dead-cat read.
  - **IV crush** confirms a real bottom: as genuine exhaustion sets in, panic subsides and implied vol collapses. A bounce where IV stays *elevated or rising* is fragile — the market still fears lower prices.
  - **Max pain drifting up** across the days after the low corroborates a re-anchoring; drifting down favours continuation lower.

## Pitfalls — where traders get destroyed

**1. Buying the bounce as a "bottom."** The cardinal sin. A 12% pop after a 30% crash *feels* like the bottom and is usually the dead cat. Never buy a post-crash rally until it has *reclaimed structure*. "It's fallen so far it must bounce for good" ignores that most sharp bounces in downtrends fail.

**2. Confusing oversold with reversal.** RSI at 20 does not mean "buy." In a strong downtrend RSI can sit oversold for weeks while price keeps falling. Oversold is a *condition*, not a *signal*. Wait for the reclaim.

**3. Ignoring the catalyst.** Technicals alone can't distinguish DCB from bottom when a live, unrepaired fundamental catalyst is dragging the stock. If the "why" of the fall is intact, distrust the bounce regardless of how pretty the candle looks.

**4. Shorting the exhaustion bottom.** The mirror error — staying short (or shorting fresh) *into* a genuine selling climax. Climactic volume + long tail + reclaim is where downtrends *end*; pressing shorts there gets you squeezed violently. Cover into climax; don't add.

**5. Fighting the trend with size.** Both plays here — shorting the bounce, buying the reversal — are counter-move trades that require *confirmation before size*. Committing full size before the reclaim/failure confirms is how the middle of the pattern eats you.

**6. India-specific: circuit filters and illiquidity.** Many crashing mid/small-caps hit *lower circuits*, where you can't exit at all — the "bounce" may be an artificial pop off a circuit, not real demand. And thinly traded names produce false reclaims on tiny volume. Trade DCB/exhaustion setups primarily in liquid, F&O-eligible names where the tape is real.

**7. Widening the stop on the failed bounce.** If you shorted the stall and it reclaims the broken level against you, the thesis is void — cover. Don't "give the dead cat room" and turn a defined-risk short into an open-ended loss when the bounce turns out to be the real thing.

## Interview-ready summary

A **dead-cat bounce** is a temporary relief rally *inside* a downtrend — a continuation pattern disguised as a reversal — driven by **short covering, reflex dip-buying and an oversold snapback**, none of which repair the underlying reason for the fall. It unfolds as event-drop → weak-volume bounce → **stall beneath broken support / a down-sloping MA** → resumption to new lows. **Exhaustion**, by contrast, is the genuine end of a trend: the **selling climax** (highest-volume capitulation flush + long-tailed reversal) at bottoms, the **blow-off top** (parabolic climactic advance) at tops, plus **exhaustion gaps**, **volume drying up**, and **momentum divergences** that mark a trend running out of participants.

The whole edge is discrimination, and it comes down to a few tests: **volume** (weak-volume bounce = dead cat; climax-volume reversal = real bottom), the **reclaim test** (a true bottom *reclaims and holds* broken structure; a dead cat *fails beneath* it), **moving-average posture**, **momentum divergence**, the **catalyst** (has the reason for the fall changed?), and **option-chain tells** (put writers defending and IV crushing = real bottom; puts still being bought and IV rising = dead cat).

You can trade *both* sides: **short the failed bounce** (stall under broken support, stop above the bounce high, target new lows) or **buy the confirmed exhaustion bottom** (reclaim of structure on climax volume with divergence, stop below the spike). The rule that keeps you solvent is **let the reclaim decide** — never pre-empt the bottom, never buy oversold as if it were a signal, never press shorts into a climax, and always ask whether the fundamental that caused the fall has actually been repaired. In India, insist on liquid, F&O-eligible names so the bounce and the reclaim are real, not a circuit-driven mirage.
