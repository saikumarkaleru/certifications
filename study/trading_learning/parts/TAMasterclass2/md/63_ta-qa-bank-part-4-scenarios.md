# TA Question Bank Part 4 (Scenarios & Chart Reading)

Part 3 tested whether you *know* the concepts. This part tests whether you can *apply* them under the ambiguity of a live chart. Each question below is a scenario — a described chart state or a decision point — and the answer models the reasoning a disciplined trader would voice: read the context, weigh the evidence, state the plan with entry/stop/target, and name what would prove you wrong. Levels are approximate reconstructions in the spirit of real NSE/MCX behaviour; verify them on your own charts. The recurring theme: there are rarely certainties, only better and worse odds with a defined risk.

---

## Q1. Nifty is at 22,480, ten points below a resistance zone of 22,500-22,530 that has rejected price three times this month. It arrives on rising volume with RSI at 62. Do you buy the breakout in advance, wait, or fade it?

You do not buy *in advance* — the level has rejected price three times, so the base case is another rejection until proven otherwise. Two clean plans exist:

- **Breakout-and-confirm**: wait for a *15-minute close above 22,530* on volume above the recent average, then buy the pullback/retest toward 22,500 (now flipped to support). Entry ~22,505 on the retest hold, stop below 22,460 (under the zone and prior structure), first target the measured move or the next resistance (say 22,700). This trades the *confirmed* break, not the hope.
- **Fade-the-rejection**: if a bearish reversal candle prints into 22,510-22,530 with a long upper wick, short toward the range, stop above 22,550.

RSI at 62 with rising volume tilts odds slightly toward a genuine breakout attempt, but a triple-tested level demands confirmation. The invalidation is explicit: entering early and getting trapped on the fourth rejection is exactly the mistake to avoid. Let price commit first.

## Q2. Bank Nifty gaps up 350 points at the open above yesterday's high. By 9:25 it has filled half the gap. What is your read and plan?

A gap up that immediately starts filling is a *warning* — the enthusiasm at the open is being sold into. The read hinges on where it stabilises relative to the **previous day's high (PDH)** and the day's VWAP:

- If it fills the *entire* gap and closes back below PDH, that is a **gap-and-fail / exhaustion gap**, often bearish for the session — trapped gap-up buyers become sellers.
- If it fills *half* and finds support at VWAP or PDH with buyers stepping in, the gap holds and the up-move can resume.

Plan: do not chase the open. Mark PDH and plot VWAP. Wait until ~9:30-9:45 for the opening range to form. If price reclaims and holds above VWAP with a higher low, go long toward the day's high, stop below VWAP. If price loses PDH and VWAP with momentum, favour shorts toward the gap's origin. The disciplined stance: the first 15 minutes of a gap are the highest-noise window; let the auction decide who is trapped.

## Q3. A stock you follow — say Tata Motors — has rallied 40% in six weeks and today prints a huge bullish candle on the highest volume in a year, closing near the high. Bullish continuation or warning?

This is genuinely ambiguous and the honest answer names both cases. A massive-volume, wide-range up candle after an extended run can be either:

- **A blow-off / climax top**: the last wave of FOMO buyers piling in, absorbed by institutions distributing — the very high volume is the tell that supply is meeting demand at scale. Confirmation would be a sharp reversal the next day or two (a bearish engulfing / long upper wick).
- **A breakout continuation**: if the candle broke a clear resistance or consolidation, the volume confirms the break and price may run further.

Decision framework: *context decides*. After a 40% six-week move (extended), the base case leans toward exhaustion-risk, so you tighten trailing stops on longs rather than adding. You do **not** short into strength on this candle alone — climax candles can be followed by more upside. Wait for confirmation: a failure to make a new high plus a reversal candle on the following session flips you cautious/short; a quiet pullback that holds and then a new high says continuation. Reduce risk, let the next one or two candles resolve the ambiguity.

## Q4. Nifty daily shows a higher high in price, but RSI and MACD histogram both made lower highs. Price is at 24,200. What does this tell you and what do you do?

This is a **momentum divergence** on two independent oscillators — a stronger warning than one alone. It says the new high was achieved on *weakening participation and decelerating momentum*: buyers are still winning but with less force. It is a caution flag, **not** a sell trigger. What divergence does *not* tell you is timing — trends can make several more divergent highs before rolling over.

Action: shift from offense to defense. Do not initiate fresh longs at the extended high; trail stops up on existing longs to protect gains (e.g., below the last swing low or the rising 20-EMA). Prepare a short *plan* but require a *trigger*: a break of the most recent swing low or an up-trendline, ideally with a reversal candle, before acting on the bearish thesis. If price instead makes another high and the divergence resolves (RSI/ MACD confirm), the warning lapses. Divergence tells you the trend is *tiring*, not that it is *dead* — respect it by managing risk, not by front-running the reversal.

## Q5. USDINR has been in a tight range of 83.00-83.40 for three weeks. Bollinger Bands have squeezed to their narrowest in months. How do you prepare?

The Bollinger squeeze plus a multi-week range signals **compressed volatility that is likely to expand** — energy is coiling. Critically, the squeeze tells you a big move is *coming* but **not its direction**. USDINR also has a regime quirk: RBI intervention often caps volatility, so ranges can persist longer than in equities, and breakouts can be sharp when they finally come.

Preparation, not prediction:

- Mark the range boundaries precisely (83.00 support, 83.40 resistance) as your trigger lines.
- Plan a **breakout trade both ways**: long on a decisive close above 83.40 targeting a measured move (~range height added on top), short on a break below 83.00.
- Size for the fact that the *first* move out of a squeeze can be a fake-out; consider waiting for a retest of the broken boundary rather than the initial break.
- Watch for external catalysts (Fed, RBI policy, oil, DXY) that typically trigger the expansion.

The mistake is picking a direction while price is still coiled. Stand ready on both sides, let the break confirm, and respect that USDINR breakouts can be muted by intervention.

## Q6. A stock breaks below a well-defined support at ₹1,500 on high volume, drops to ₹1,470, then two days later closes back above ₹1,505. What pattern is this and how is it traded?

This is a **bear trap / failed breakdown (spring)** — price broke support, triggered stop-losses and fresh shorts, then reclaimed the level, trapping the sellers. Reclaimed failed breakdowns are among the higher-probability reversal setups because the trapped shorts must buy to cover, adding fuel to the up-move. The Wyckoff term is a *spring*: a shakeout below support that fails.

Trade: the reclaim of ₹1,500-1,505 *is* the signal. Enter long on the close back above 1,505 (or on a small pullback that holds 1,500), stop below the trap low at ~1,465 (below 1,470 to allow noise). Target the top of the prior range or the next resistance, since trapped shorts often drive a swift move. Confluence that strengthens it: the reclaim happening on decent volume, a bullish candle, or a positive divergence at the 1,470 low. The invalidation is clean and tight — a break back below 1,465 says the reclaim failed and you are wrong. The key lesson: a failed break in one direction is often a strong signal in the other.

## Q7. On a 5-minute Bank Nifty chart, price is above a rising VWAP all morning, pulls back to VWAP at 47,850 for the third time, and prints a bullish pin bar. What is the trade?

This is a textbook **VWAP pullback continuation** in an intraday uptrend. Price holding above a *rising* VWAP signals intraday bullish control; each pullback to VWAP that holds is buyers defending fair value. The third tap with a bullish pin bar (rejection wick) is your entry signal.

Trade: long at the pin bar close, ~47,880, stop just below VWAP and the pin low, ~47,810 (a tight ~70-point risk). Target the day's high or the prior swing high; trail with VWAP or the 9-EMA. Position sizing off the tight stop lets you take a meaningful position for modest rupee risk. Two cautions: (1) *repeated* taps of VWAP weaken it — a third or fourth test is more likely to break than the first, so this is a "take it but respect the stop" trade, not a high-conviction one; (2) confirm the broader bias — if the 15-minute is bearish or a key resistance sits just overhead, skip it. The stop is the whole trade: below VWAP means the intraday control has flipped and you exit without debate.

## Q8. Nifty is trading at 23,000. The option chain shows heavy Call OI at 23,000 and 23,100, heavy Put OI at 22,800, and PCR of 0.75. Price is drifting up toward 23,000. How does OI change your read?

The OI map frames the near-term battlefield. Heavy **Call writing at 23,000-23,100** marks that zone as *resistance* — writers are betting price stays below and will defend it; heavy **Put OI at 22,800** marks support. A **PCR of 0.75** (more Call than Put OI) leans slightly bearish/cautious in positioning. So price drifting into 23,000 is drifting into a wall of call writers.

Read and plan: the base case is that 23,000-23,100 caps price unless call writers get *squeezed*. Two scenarios:

- **Rejection**: price stalls at 23,000, call writers hold, and it fades back toward 22,900/22,800 — a fade-the-resistance short with a stop above 23,120.
- **Breakout via short-covering**: if price *closes decisively above 23,100* and that Call OI starts *unwinding* (OI falling as writers cover), the removal of resistance can trigger a fast squeeze higher — then you flip long. The tell is watching whether call OI at 23,100 builds (bearish) or unwinds (bullish) as price approaches.

OI turns a plain "price near resistance" into a *positioning-aware* plan: know where the writers are, and let their behaviour (defend vs. cover) confirm the direction before committing.

## Q9. A stock forms a classic head-and-shoulders on the daily chart with a neckline at ₹800. It breaks the neckline on average volume, falls to ₹770, then rallies back to ₹800. Is the pattern still valid?

Yes — this is the **neckline retest**, a normal and often *confirming* part of a head-and-shoulders breakdown, not an invalidation. After breaking a neckline, price frequently pulls back to it; the neckline, formerly support, now acts as **resistance**. If the retest at ₹800 *rejects* price (a bearish candle, failure to reclaim), the pattern is not just valid — the retest offers a *lower-risk short entry* than the initial break.

Trade: short on the failed retest, entry ~₹795 as price rolls over from 800, stop above the neckline and any wick, ~₹812. Target the pattern's **measured move**: the height from head to neckline subtracted from the neckline (if head was at ₹880, height ₹80, target ~₹720). The caveat that *would* invalidate it: a decisive *close back above ₹800* (reclaiming the neckline) breaks the pattern — the breakdown failed and you stand aside or flip. Also note the initial break was on only *average* volume, which is a mild negative for reliability; the retest rejection becomes the more important confirmation. Distinguish a healthy retest (pullback to the line that fails) from a genuine reclaim (close back above).

## Q10. Gold on MCX is in a strong uptrend. Today it opens, spikes to a new high on huge volume, then reverses to close red below yesterday's close — a bearish engulfing / shooting star on high volume. Reverse your bias?

Not on a single candle, but treat it as a serious **exhaustion / reversal warning** at the end of a strong trend. A new-high spike that reverses to close red on huge volume is a *classic distribution signal* — buyers were trapped at the highs and sellers overwhelmed them (large effort, negative result). In a mature uptrend this is exactly what a top *can* look like.

Response, calibrated to evidence:

- **Do not blindly reverse to short** — one candle against a strong trend is a low-conviction reversal; trends often absorb one bad day. MCX gold is also sensitive to overnight international (COMEX) moves and USDINR, so the next session gaps can dominate.
- **Do reduce/protect longs**: tighten trailing stops (e.g., below today's low), book partial profit — the risk-reward of holding a fully-extended long just deteriorated.
- **Require confirmation for a short**: a *follow-through* down day closing below the reversal candle's low, or a break of the trend's rising support line. Then a short toward the last consolidation makes sense, with a stop above the reversal high.

The principle: a single reversal candle in a strong trend changes your *risk management* immediately but your *directional bias* only after confirmation. Respect the warning without over-reacting to one bar.

## Q11. You are long a stock from ₹500. It runs to ₹560, then starts consolidating in a tight ₹550-560 range for six sessions on declining volume. Is this bearish distribution or a bullish flag?

Declining volume in a tight range after an up-move is *more often* a **bullish flag / consolidation** than distribution — falling volume signals a pause where weak hands are shaken out but strong holders are not selling aggressively (which would show *rising* volume on down-days). The trend is innocent until proven guilty. Context supports it: a sharp rise (the "pole" from 500 to 560) followed by a shallow, low-volume drift is the archetypal bull flag, which typically resolves in the trend's direction.

Management: hold the long. Mark the flag boundaries (550 support, 560 resistance). The **trigger** is a breakout above 560 on *expanding* volume — add or hold with a measured-move target (flagpole height ₹60 projected from the breakout ≈ ₹620). Raise your protective stop to just below the flag low (~₹547) to define risk. What would flip the read to distribution: the range breaking *down* on *rising* volume, or a series of lower highs within the range — that signals supply and you exit. So: base case bullish flag, stop below the flag protects you if it is distribution in disguise, and the volume on the eventual break tells you which it was.

## Q12. Nifty and Bank Nifty are both up on the day, but the NSE advance-decline is negative (more stocks falling than rising) and midcaps are red. What is this breadth divergence telling you?

This is a **breadth divergence** — the headline indices are being propped up by a handful of heavyweights (a few large-cap names in Nifty/Bank Nifty) while the *broader* market is weak. Negative advance-decline and red midcaps on a green index day mean the *average* stock is falling; the rally is *narrow*, not broad-based. Narrow rallies are lower-quality and more fragile because they depend on a small group of leaders continuing to hold.

What it tells you and what to do: this is a *caution* signal about the sustainability of the index up-move, not an immediate sell. Broad participation confirms healthy trends; its absence warns that if the few leaders stumble, the index has little underneath to support it. Practically: be *selective* — favour the strong leaders that are actually driving the tape rather than buying the weak broad market; keep position sizes modest; watch for the divergence to either *resolve* (breadth catches up, rally broadens — bullish) or *worsen* (leaders finally roll over — the index follows the broad market down). Breadth is a context/quality filter: a green index on red breadth is a rally to *distrust* until participation broadens.

## Q13. On a 15-minute chart, price makes a sharp move up, then forms three successive candles each with small bodies and long upper wicks at the same resistance around ₹1,200. What is the message and the trade?

Three successive small-bodied candles with long *upper* wicks at the same resistance is **repeated rejection / supply absorption** — each time price probes above ₹1,200, sellers slam it back down, leaving upper wicks. The message: there is a persistent block of supply at ₹1,200 that buyers cannot clear. This is often a *short-term top* or at least a stall.

Trade: this favours a **short/fade** or, if you are long, an exit. Short entry on the break of the wicks' base (the low of the rejection cluster, say ₹1,188), stop above the wick highs (~₹1,205, above the ₹1,200 supply), target the prior support or a measured move down. Confluence that strengthens it: the resistance aligning with a higher-timeframe level, VWAP, or Call OI at that strike. The alternative scenario to respect: if a candle finally *closes decisively above ₹1,205* on strong volume, the supply has been absorbed/cleared and the setup flips bullish (a breakout after absorption can run hard). So the ₹1,205 close is both your short's invalidation and a potential long trigger. Repeated wicks at one level = a battle; trade the side that wins the close.

## Q14. A trader shows you a chart with a beautiful ascending trendline touched five times over three months. Price is now sitting on it for the sixth time at ₹2,400. Buy the bounce?

An ascending trendline with five prior touches is a *well-validated* line, so a sixth-touch bounce is a reasonable long *idea* — but two professional cautions apply. First, **the more times a trendline is tested, the weaker it becomes**: each touch consumes some of the demand that defends it, and heavily-tested lines break more readily than fresh ones. A sixth touch is *not* stronger than the second — it may be the one that fails. Second, you want *confirmation of the bounce*, not blind faith in the line.

Plan: do not buy merely because price is *at* ₹2,400. Wait for a **bounce signal** — a bullish reversal candle (hammer/engulfing) forming off the line, ideally with a pickup in volume, showing buyers are actually defending it. Then enter, stop *just below* the trendline (say ₹2,375) — this is the setup's beauty: a clean, tight invalidation. Target the recent swing high or the upper channel rail. Crucially, if price *closes below* the trendline instead, that break — after five holds — is itself a high-quality *short/exit* signal, as trapped trend-followers bail. So respect the line, but let price confirm the bounce, keep the stop tight beneath it, and be mentally ready for the break because well-worn trendlines eventually give way.

## Q15. Two stocks in the same sector: one is making new 52-week highs, the other is stuck 20% below its highs. The sector index breaks out. Which do you buy and why?

Buy the **relative-strength leader making new highs** — this is the *relative strength* principle, one of the most robust ideas in technical analysis. When a sector catches a bid, the stock *already* at new highs is the one institutions are accumulating; it has the least overhead supply (no trapped buyers waiting to sell at breakeven), so it can move freely. The laggard 20% below its highs faces *layers of resistance* — every prior buyer between here and the old high is a potential seller as price approaches breakeven. Strength begets strength; the leaders lead, and buying the strongest name in a strengthening sector stacks the odds.

Nuance and caveat: the tempting counter-argument is "the laggard is cheaper and has more room to catch up." Sometimes laggards *do* play catch-up late in a sector move — but that is a *lower-probability, mean-reversion* bet, not a momentum bet, and it often underperforms because there is a *reason* it lagged (weaker fundamentals or distribution). The disciplined default: in an early/mid sector breakout, buy the leader for a momentum continuation, entry on a pullback or breakout, stop below the breakout level. Consider the laggard only as a *later-stage rotation* trade, with confirmation that money is actually rotating into it (it starts outperforming). When in doubt, own strength.

---

### Scenario-reading principles distilled

- **Confirm before committing**: triple-tested levels, gaps, and breakouts demand a *close* plus volume, not an intrabar poke.
- **Divergence and single reversal candles change your risk management first, your direction only on confirmation.**
- **Failed breaks are strong signals the other way** — bear traps and reclaimed necklines trap participants who must reverse.
- **Volume and OI reveal conviction and positioning** that price alone hides — quiet breakouts and call-writer walls both warn.
- **Breadth and relative strength are quality filters** — distrust narrow rallies, and buy leaders over laggards.
- **Every plan names its invalidation**: the stop is not an afterthought, it is where your thesis is proven wrong.
