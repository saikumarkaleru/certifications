# Volume Spread Analysis (VSA) I

Volume Spread Analysis is a method of reading a chart bar-by-bar as a record of the ongoing fight between "smart money" — the large operators, institutions, market-makers and prop desks who move size — and everybody else. It descends directly from the Wyckoff tradition but sharpens it into a repeatable, three-variable diagnostic: for every single bar you read the **spread** (the range, high to low), the **close** (where price finished inside that range), and the **volume** (how much was transacted). VSA claims that when you combine those three, and place them in context, a bar stops being a random price wiggle and becomes a sentence in a story about who is accumulating and who is distributing.

This chapter (VSA I) builds the foundation: the philosophy, the exact definitions, the concept of effort versus result, and the first family of signals — the signs of weakness and strength that appear on individual bars. VSA II will assemble these bars into campaigns (accumulation and distribution phases, springs, upthrusts, tests) and give full India trade plans. Here we learn to read one bar at a time, because if you cannot read a single Nifty candle correctly, you cannot read fifty of them.

## What it is and the logic

The core belief of VSA is that markets are not random and not efficient in the textbook sense; they are actively engineered by professional money that must accumulate large positions without alerting the crowd, and later distribute those positions into the crowd's enthusiasm. Because a large operator cannot buy 5,00,000 shares of a mid-cap or 40,000 lots of Nifty in one click without spiking the price against himself, he must work his order over time, absorbing supply on down-moves and feeding stock out on up-moves. That activity leaves fingerprints in the volume and the spread. VSA is the craft of reading those fingerprints.

Tom Williams, a former syndicate trader in the 1960s–70s who later built the TradeGuider software, codified the modern VSA language, but the intellectual DNA is pure Richard Wyckoff: the market advances or declines because of an imbalance between supply and demand, and volume measures the *activity* behind price while the spread and close measure the *result*. The single most important VSA idea is:

> **Effort versus result.** Volume is effort. Price movement (spread and close) is result. When effort and result agree, the move is genuine. When effort and result disagree — huge volume but tiny result, or a big result on trivial volume — something is hidden, and that hidden thing is usually the professional operating against the obvious direction.

A classic example: Nifty is falling, and on one bar you see the widest down-spread of the week on the heaviest volume of the month, but the *next* bar cannot make a new low and closes up. The effort (enormous selling volume) produced no follow-through result. That failure is the signal — the selling was absorbed by buyers larger than the sellers. In VSA terms, professional money was buying into the panic. This is "no result from effort," and it is bullish precisely because it is counterintuitive to the crowd, who saw the big red bar and sold.

## Construction: the three variables and the rules

VSA needs no special indicator — it works on a plain OHLC or candlestick chart with a volume histogram. What matters is disciplined, relative reading. Here are the exact definitions.

**1. Spread (the range).** Spread = High − Low of the bar. Classify each bar's spread *relative to recent bars* (roughly the last 20–30):

| Spread class | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Wide spread | Range far larger than recent average — strong directional conviction on the bar |
| Average spread | Typical range — no special message alone |
| Narrow spread | Range far smaller than recent average — activity is being suppressed or dried up |

**2. Close position.** Where did price finish within the bar's range? Divide the range into thirds:

| Close location | Reading |
|---|---|
| Close in upper third | Buyers won the bar (demand) |
| Close in middle third | Balanced / indecisive |
| Close in lower third | Sellers won the bar (supply) |

**3. Volume.** Classify relative to the recent 20–30 bars:

| Volume class | Label |
|---|---|
| Well above average | Ultra-high / high volume |
| Around average | Average volume |
| Well below average | Low volume |

The reading is always a *combination* plus *context* (are we in an uptrend, downtrend, or range; is this at support/resistance; what did the prior bar do). A wide-spread up-bar closing on its high means nothing in isolation — its meaning flips depending on the volume and where it appears.

The two master principles that generate signals:

- **Weakness appears on up-bars.** The professionals sell into strength. So a sign of weakness is often a strong-looking green bar that is secretly being sold into.
- **Strength appears on down-bars.** The professionals buy into weakness. So a sign of strength is often an ugly red bar that is secretly being bought.

This inversion is what makes VSA hard for beginners and powerful for those who master it: the signal is usually the opposite of the emotion the bar provokes.

### The first-family VSA signals

**Signs of weakness (SOW) — expect a top / down-move:**

| Signal | Bar description | Volume | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Upthrust | Wide up-bar makes a new high then closes on/near the low, often engulfing | High | Price marked up to trap buyers and trigger stops, then dumped — distribution |
| No Demand | Narrow up-bar closing up (mid/high) | Low | Rise on no professional participation — buyers absent, up-move is hollow |
| Buying Climax | Very wide up-bar, new highs, close off the top | Ultra-high | Public buying frantically while professionals unload — the top |
| Supply coming in | Wide up-bar on high volume that fails to make progress next bar | High | Selling absorbing the up-move |

**Signs of strength (SOS) — expect a bottom / up-move:**

| Signal | Bar description | Volume | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Selling Climax | Very wide down-bar, new lows, close off the low | Ultra-high | Panic selling absorbed by professional buying — the bottom |
| Stopping Volume | Down-bar closing off its low | Very high | Buyers stepping in to arrest the decline |
| No Supply | Narrow down-bar closing down | Low | No professional selling left — sellers exhausted |
| Test | Narrow bar dips into old supply then closes up/mid | Low | Market probes for sellers, finds none — bullish confirmation |

Two definitions deserve special care because they are the workhorses.

**No Demand bar.** A bar in a rising or ranging market that closes higher than the previous close but on *narrow* spread and *lower* volume than the two prior bars. It says: price went up, but with no volume behind it — the professionals are not participating in this rise. If the smart money wanted higher prices they would be buying; their absence on an up-bar is a warning. No Demand is only meaningful in a context of prior weakness (e.g., after an upthrust). In a strong genuine uptrend, one low-volume up-bar means little.

**No Supply bar.** The mirror image: a bar that closes lower than the previous close on *narrow* spread and *low* volume. It says: price dipped, but nobody wanted to sell — supply has dried up. In a context of prior strength (e.g., after a selling climax and a test), No Supply is a green light.

## Worked India example (levels and ₹)

Take Reliance Industries on the daily chart through a hypothetical but realistic sequence around a ₹2,850 support zone (values illustrative, in the spirit of a 2025–26 tape).

**Day 1 — Selling Climax.** RIL had fallen from ₹3,050 to ₹2,880 over eight sessions on rising volume. On Day 1 it gaps down and trades to ₹2,842 intraday — a fresh swing low — on volume of 2.1 crore shares versus a 20-day average of 90 lakh. That is ultra-high volume. But the close is ₹2,896, in the *upper third* of a very wide bar (high ₹2,904, low ₹2,842). Read it: maximum effort of selling (huge volume, new low) produced a close near the high — the result contradicts the effort. Somebody with size bought everything the panic threw at them. This is a textbook **Selling Climax / Stopping Volume**. It is not yet a "buy" — it is a "stop looking to sell."

**Day 2 — automatic reaction and the setup.** Price drifts to ₹2,875 on average volume, closing mid-range. Normal. We are watching for a test.

**Day 3 — the Test.** RIL opens at ₹2,880, dips to ₹2,858 (back into the climax zone), but volume collapses to 55 lakh — well below average — and it closes at ₹2,889, in the upper third, a narrow-ish bar. This is a **No Supply / Test**: the market poked down into the region where sellers were previously active, and found almost none. Low volume on the dip confirms supply is exhausted.

**The trade.** The confluence is: ultra-high-volume climax with a strong close (Day 1) + successful low-volume test (Day 3). Entry on Day 4 as price takes out Day 3's high of ₹2,894, say a fill at ₹2,900. Stop below the test low with a buffer — ₹2,850 (below ₹2,858, under the climax low ₹2,842 would be safer but wider). Risk ₹50 per share on the ₹2,850 stop. First target the prior swing high / supply at ₹3,050 (reward ₹150, ~3R), scale out half there, trail the rest under rising swing lows. Over the next fifteen sessions RIL grinds back to ₹3,080. The climax marked the low; VSA let you read the low *as it formed* rather than three weeks later.

Now the bearish mirror, on **Bank Nifty index (spot/futures)**, illustrative levels near a 52,000 top.

**Bar A — Buying Climax.** Bank Nifty has rallied 1,800 points in six sessions. It prints a wide up-bar to a new high of 52,180, close 52,010 (off the top), on the heaviest futures volume in two months. Ultra-high volume, close off the high, new high made — classic **Buying Climax**. The public is chasing; professionals are feeding out.

**Bar B — No Demand.** Next session, a narrow up-bar: high 52,090, low 51,960, close 52,060 — up on the day but tiny spread, and volume is the lowest in ten sessions. **No Demand.** The rally has no professional fuel.

**Bar C — Upthrust confirmation.** Third session opens strong, spikes to 52,210 (marginal new high, stop-hunt), then reverses hard to close at 51,880 — a wide bar closing near its low on high volume. **Upthrust.** The trap is sprung.

**The short.** Enter on the break of Bar C's low, ~51,850, stop above the upthrust high 52,220 (risk ~370 points). Target the last consolidation floor near 50,600 (reward ~1,250, >3R). On Bank Nifty weekly options, this reads as the moment to buy puts or exit longs; the three-bar sequence (climax → no demand → upthrust) is far more reliable than any single bar.

## How to trade it — entry, stop, target, management

VSA is a *timing and context* tool, not a mechanical black box. The disciplined workflow:

1. **Establish context first.** Trend, and location relative to support/resistance. VSA signals near a major level (a prior high/low, a round number, VWAP, a big-volume node) are worth ten times a signal in no-man's-land.
2. **Wait for the pair, not the single bar.** The strongest trades come from a *climax* followed by a *test* (long) or a *climax* followed by *no demand / upthrust* (short). One bar is a hint; the confirming second bar is the trigger.
3. **Enter on confirmation of the second signal** — typically a break of the signal bar's extreme in the direction of the anticipated move, or the close of the test/no-demand bar.
4. **Stop placement** goes beyond the structural extreme: below the climax/test low for longs, above the upthrust/climax high for shorts, with an ATR-based buffer so a normal wick doesn't take you out.
5. **Targets** are prior structure — the last swing, the range they accumulated in, or a measured move from the trading range width.
6. **Management:** scale out at first structure, trail the balance. If a *contradicting* VSA signal appears (you are long and an upthrust prints into resistance), honour it and reduce.

## Confluence

VSA sharpens dramatically when stacked with:

- **Support/resistance and round numbers** — a selling climax exactly at ₹2,850 support is stronger than one in mid-air.
- **VWAP and volume profile** — a test that holds above the session VWAP or bounces off a High-Volume Node aligns "who is in control" between two methods.
- **Market breadth** — a Nifty selling climax on a day the advance-decline is washed out (say 40 advances, 10 declines... err, the opposite: 5 advances, 45 declines) is a genuine capitulation.
- **Options / OI in F&O** — a buying climax coinciding with peak call OI build-up and a spike in India VIX is a high-conviction distribution read.
- **Higher timeframe** — read the weekly for context, execute on the daily; read the daily for context, execute on the hourly.

Never trade a VSA bar *against* a hard higher-timeframe trend without a full multi-bar reversal sequence.

## Pitfalls

- **Trading single bars in isolation.** The commonest failure. A No Demand bar in a raging genuine bull trend is noise. Context and pairs matter.
- **Misjudging "high" and "low" volume.** Volume must be relative to the *recent* norm and adjusted for the day of the week and expiry effects. Indian F&O volumes balloon on expiry Thursdays and around monthly rollover — a "climax" volume on expiry day may just be settlement churn, not conviction.
- **Index vs stock volume.** Nifty and Bank Nifty *spot* have no real volume; you must read futures volume (and even that shifts across the rollover). Use the continuous futures or the near-month contract consistently.
- **Confirmation bias.** VSA is interpretive; it is fatally easy to see a "climax" wherever you already wanted to buy. Write the rule (ultra-high volume = top-decile of last 30 bars, close in upper/lower third) and hold yourself to it.
- **Gap distortion.** Indian stocks gap frequently on overnight news; a gap can create an artificial "wide spread" that is really an opening jump, not intrabar effort. Weigh gapped bars carefully.
- **Ignoring the news/event calendar.** A wide-spread high-volume bar on RBI policy day or a results day is driven by information, and the smart-money-absorption story may not apply cleanly.

## Interview-ready summary

Volume Spread Analysis reads each bar through three variables — spread, close, and volume — to infer whether professional money is accumulating or distributing. Its central principle is **effort versus result**: volume is effort, price movement is result, and a divergence between them (huge volume, no follow-through) exposes hidden professional activity. Because smart money sells into strength and buys into weakness, **signs of weakness appear on up-bars** (upthrust, no demand, buying climax) and **signs of strength appear on down-bars** (selling climax, stopping volume, no supply, test). The highest-probability trades come from *pairs* — a climax followed by a successful low-volume test (long) or a climax followed by no-demand/upthrust (short) — located at meaningful support/resistance, confirmed by breadth, VWAP or OI. In Indian markets, always read *futures* volume for indices, discount expiry-day and rollover volume distortions, and treat single bars as hints rather than commands. VSA does not predict; it tips the odds by revealing who is winning the fight on each bar.
