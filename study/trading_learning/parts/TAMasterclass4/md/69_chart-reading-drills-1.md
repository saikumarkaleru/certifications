# Chart-Reading Drills I

Reading a chart in a book is easy. Reading one live, with your own money on the line and the clock ticking, is a different skill entirely. The gap between "I understand support and resistance" and "I can act on it at 9:31 a.m. on a gap-up Bank Nifty candle" is bridged by one thing only: repetition under realistic conditions. This chapter is the first of three drill sets designed to build that muscle. You will not find new theory here — everything tested was covered in Volumes I through III. What you will find is twenty structured scenarios that force you to *decide*, then a detailed answer key that shows the reasoning, the probability, and the honest caveats.

## How to use these drills

Treat each scenario as a timed exercise. Read the setup, cover the answer, and write down — physically or in a notes app — three things: (1) your bias (long / short / no-trade), (2) your entry, stop, and first target in rupees, and (3) the single reason you would be wrong. Only then read the key. Score yourself not on whether you "got the direction right" but on whether your *process* matched. A trader who takes a valid setup that loses is correct; a trader who takes an invalid setup that wins is lucky, and luck is not a strategy you can repeat.

The scenarios below assume you are trading Nifty, Bank Nifty, and liquid NSE cash/F&O names on 5-minute, 15-minute, and daily charts, using TradingView or Chartink. Levels are realistic for the 2026 regime but the *method* is timeless — do not fixate on the exact numbers.

A reminder that runs through all three chapters: most retail traders lose money. SEBI's own studies on the F&O segment have repeatedly shown that a large majority of individual traders end the year in the red. These drills will not change that statistic for the impatient. They will help the disciplined minority who treat trading as a probabilistic craft.

## Drill Set A — Trend, structure, and location

### Q1. The gap and the prior day high
Nifty closes Monday at 24,180, having spent the afternoon coiling between 24,150 and 24,200. Tuesday opens at 24,310 — a gap up of 130 points — and the first 5-minute candle is a wide green bar closing near its high at 24,340. Prior day high was 24,225. What is your read, and what do you do in the first fifteen minutes?

### Q2. Lower high on the daily
Reliance has rallied from 2,760 to 2,980 over three weeks. The daily chart now prints a candle that pokes to 2,975, below the prior swing high of 2,980, and closes at 2,940 as a bearish engulfing bar on above-average volume. The 20-DMA is at 2,890 and rising. What structural signal has fired, and how do you frame the next few sessions?

### Q3. Bank Nifty at the round number
Bank Nifty grinds up all morning and stalls precisely at 52,000 — a round, psychologically loaded level. Price tests it four times between 11:00 and 13:00, each test making a marginally lower high (51,995, 51,988, 51,980, 51,975), while the 5-minute RSI drifts down from 68 to 61. Volume on each push is shrinking. What is this pattern, and what is the tell?

### Q4. The failed breakdown
Nifty breaks below a well-watched support at 24,000, trading as low as 23,955, then reverses hard and closes the 15-minute candle back at 24,030 — above the broken level — on a surge of volume. What is the name for this, and why is it often more powerful than a clean breakdown?

### Q5. Trend day recognition
By 10:15 a.m. Nifty has opened at 24,100, never traded below 24,090, and has climbed to 24,240 in a stair-step of higher highs and higher lows, each 5-minute pullback holding above the rising 9-EMA. There are no lower lows on the 5-minute at all. What kind of day is this likely to be, and how should it change your trade management?

### Q6. VWAP as the line in the sand
HDFC Bank opens weak, drops to 1,640, then recovers to sit exactly at the session VWAP of 1,658 by 11:30. It has been oscillating around VWAP for an hour — poking above, falling below, no conviction either way. What does price behaving this way around VWAP tell you about the intraday auction, and what is the professional's default action?

### Q7. The three-touch trendline
On the 15-minute Nifty chart you draw a rising trendline connecting the lows at 23,900 (Monday), 24,020 (Tuesday), and 24,110 (Wednesday). Price is now approaching that line from above for a fourth time at 24,150. What is the setup, and what confirmation would you demand before buying the touch?

## Drill Set B — Momentum, exhaustion, and divergence

### Q8. Bearish RSI divergence at a high
Tata Motors makes a new swing high at 1,020, up from a prior high of 1,005. But the daily RSI reads 64 at the new high versus 72 at the old high — a lower momentum reading against a higher price. What is this, how reliable is it alone, and what would confirm it?

### Q9. The exhaustion gap
After a five-day vertical run, Adani Ports gaps up 4% on the open, spikes another 2% in the first thirty minutes on massive volume, then reverses and closes red, below the open. What is the likely nature of this gap, and what does the intraday reversal imply?

### Q10. Volume climax
Nifty has been falling for six sessions. The seventh session opens with a panic gap-down, prints the widest-range red candle of the entire decline, on the highest volume of the move, and closes off its lows with a long lower wick. What is this candle likely marking, and why is "catch the falling knife" still dangerous here?

### Q11. Momentum thrust off a base
Infosys has traded flat between 1,880 and 1,920 for two weeks — a tight rectangle. Today it explodes through 1,920 on a wide-range candle, volume 3x the 20-day average, and closes at 1,955 near its high. MACD has just crossed up above its signal line from below zero. What is the confluence, and where is the logical stop?

### Q12. The second mouse
A stock breaks out of a base, runs 8%, then pulls back and *retests* the breakout level, holding it on lighter volume before turning up again. Traders call the retest buyer "the second mouse." What is the logic of the phrase, and why is the retest entry often superior to the breakout entry for a risk-conscious trader?

### Q13. Overbought is not a sell signal
Bank Nifty's daily RSI has been pinned above 70 for eleven straight sessions while price climbs from 50,000 to 53,500. A junior trader keeps shorting because "RSI is overbought." What is the flaw in his logic, and what does a persistently overbought RSI actually indicate about the trend?

## Drill Set C — Patterns and location together

### Q14. Head and shoulders — measured move
Nifty forms a head-and-shoulders top: left shoulder at 24,400, head at 24,600, right shoulder at 24,390, with a neckline at 24,200. Price breaks the neckline decisively. Calculate the measured-move target and state the two things that would invalidate the pattern.

### Q15. Flag in a strong trend
SBI runs from 620 to 680 in four sessions (the flagpole), then drifts sideways-down in a tight, orderly channel between 672 and 665 for three sessions on declining volume. What pattern is this, which direction does it resolve most often, and what is the entry trigger?

### Q16. The double bottom with a twist
Nifty bottoms at 23,600, bounces to 23,850, then falls again — but the second low prints at 23,640, slightly *above* the first low, before rallying. Is this a valid double bottom, and does the higher second low strengthen or weaken the case?

### Q17. Inside day compression
Bank Nifty prints a day whose entire range (high 52,100, low 51,700) sits within the prior day's range (high 52,300, low 51,500). The day after, an inside day forms again — an even tighter range. What is being signalled, and how do you trade the expansion?

### Q18. Support becomes resistance
Nifty had strong support at 24,000 for weeks. It finally broke below, fell to 23,700, then rallied back up to 24,000 — the old support — where it stalled and turned down again. What is this principle called, and why does it happen mechanically?

### Q19. The opening range breakout — India context
Reliance's first 15-minute candle (9:15–9:30) ranges from 2,910 to 2,935. At 9:47 price breaks above 2,935 on rising volume. State the classic ORB entry, stop, and one filter that dramatically improves the win rate in Indian markets.

### Q20. No-trade is a trade
Nifty is chopping in a 40-point range for two hours, VWAP is flat, ADX on the 15-minute is 12 and falling, and every breakout attempt has failed within two candles. You have taken two small losses already. What is the correct action, and why is recognising this the mark of a professional?

## Answer Key

### A1 — Gap-up above prior day high
**Bias: cautiously long, but no chasing.** A gap that clears the prior day high (24,225) and holds is a *breakaway* signal — the overnight order flow has repriced the instrument higher. The wide green first candle closing near its high says buyers are in control. But entering on the very first candle at 24,340 is chasing; your stop would be huge. The professional play is to let the first 15–30 minutes build, then buy a shallow pullback that *holds above 24,225* (old resistance now support) with a stop below it. If price falls back inside Monday's range (below 24,225), the gap is filling and the bullish thesis is dead. Honest note: roughly 40% of gaps fill the same day, so location and confirmation matter more than the gap itself.

### A2 — Lower high + bearish engulfing
**Signal: potential trend-change / distribution.** The failure to exceed 2,980 followed by a bearish engulfing on high volume is the first *lower high* attempt after a strong run — a warning, not yet a confirmed reversal. Frame the next sessions as "innocent until proven guilty": the trend is up while the 20-DMA (2,890) holds. Watch for a *lower low* — a break below the last swing low — to confirm the change of character. Until then this is a pullback-in-uptrend candidate, not a short. A disciplined trader tightens longs and waits; he does not flip short on one candle against a rising 20-DMA.

### A3 — Ascending exhaustion into a round number
**Pattern: a stalling rally / potential lower-high top under 52,000.** The four tests making marginally lower highs while RSI falls from 68 to 61 on *shrinking* volume is textbook momentum exhaustion — buyers are spending more effort for less price. The tell is the divergence between price (flat-to-lower highs) and the fading RSI plus volume. This is a *setup to watch for a short*, triggered only when price breaks the intraday rising support or the 5-min structure prints a lower low. Round numbers like 52,000 attract resting sell orders (options strikes, institutional targets), which is why rallies stall there.

### A4 — Bear trap / failed breakdown
**Name: a bear trap (failed breakdown, or "spring" in Wyckoff terms).** Price broke 24,000, triggered stop-losses and fresh shorts below it, then reversed and reclaimed the level on high volume. It is often *more* powerful than a clean breakdown because it traps two groups: longs who sold their stops and shorts who entered on the break. Both are now offside and must buy to cover, adding fuel to the reversal. Entry: long on the reclaim of 24,000 with a stop below the 23,955 low; the failed-breakdown low becomes your invalidation. These traps are among the highest-conviction reversal setups precisely because of the trapped-order dynamic.

### A5 — Trend day
**Read: a bullish trend day.** No lower lows on the 5-minute, every pullback holding the rising 9-EMA, an open near the low of day — these are the fingerprints of a trend day, where price closes at or near the extreme. Management must change: on a trend day you do *not* scalp small targets and you do *not* fade strength. You trail a stop under the 9-EMA (or under each higher low) and let the position run, adding on shallow pullbacks. The single biggest intraday mistake is treating a trend day like a range day and booking 20 points when 150 were available. The invalidation is a decisive break below the 9-EMA that fails to recover.

### A6 — Chop around VWAP = balance
**Read: the intraday auction is balanced / two-sided.** When price oscillates around VWAP with no conviction, neither buyers nor sellers control the session — it is a *balanced* auction. VWAP is the day's fair-value anchor and the reference institutions use for execution. The professional default in balance is to *reduce activity*: fade the extremes of the range back toward VWAP if you must trade, but ideally wait for price to break decisively away from VWAP and *hold* on one side (accepting value higher or lower) before committing directionally. Trading a balanced market with trend-day size is how accounts bleed.

### A7 — Trendline test, demand confirmation
**Setup: a fourth-touch buy on a rising trendline.** A trendline validated by three touches is tradable on the fourth, but the touch alone is not a signal — trendlines break. Demand *confirmation*: a bullish reversal candle at the line (hammer, bullish engulfing), a bounce with rising volume, or a hold above the line on a closing basis. Buy the confirmation, not the mere touch, with a stop just below the trendline / the confirmation candle's low. The more times a line is tested, the *weaker* it tends to become — supply/demand at that level is being consumed — so the fourth or fifth touch carries higher break risk than the second.

### A8 — Bearish divergence, needs confirmation
**This is a bearish RSI divergence** — price higher high, momentum lower high. On its own it is a *yellow flag, not a sell*: divergences can persist for a long time in strong trends and often "unwind" by price going sideways rather than reversing. Confirmation is a break of the recent swing structure (a lower low on the relevant timeframe) or a break of a short-term rising trendline. The mistake is shorting the divergence in isolation; the discipline is treating it as a reason to tighten longs and *prepare* a short, executed only on structural confirmation.

### A9 — Exhaustion gap
**Likely an exhaustion gap.** After a mature, extended run, a gap up on massive volume that *reverses* to close red below the open is classic exhaustion — the last buyers rushing in at the top, absorbed by sellers distributing. The intraday reversal (close below open, off the highs) signals that demand was overwhelmed. This often marks a short-term top. A conservative trader does not short the spike; he waits for a lower high and a break of the day's structure, then shorts with defined risk. The reversal candle's high is the natural stop reference.

### A10 — Selling climax
**This is likely a selling climax (capitulation).** Widest-range red candle, highest volume of the decline, panic gap, and a long lower wick with an off-the-lows close — this is the point of maximum fear where weak hands puke and value buyers step in. It often marks a tradeable low. But "catch the falling knife" is dangerous because a climax marks a *potential* low that usually needs *retesting* — price frequently makes a secondary low days later. The disciplined entry is not the climax candle itself but the successful retest that holds above (or near) the climax low on lighter volume, confirming supply is exhausted.

### A11 — Base breakout confluence
**Confluence:** a tight base (contracted volatility), a wide-range breakout candle, 3x volume expansion, a strong close near the high, and a MACD bullish cross from below zero. That is four independent confirmations aligning — a high-quality long. The logical stop is *below the breakout candle's low* or back inside the rectangle (below 1,920, or below the candle's low, whichever your risk allows). If price falls back inside the base, the breakout has failed. Volume is the key witness here: a breakout on average volume is far more prone to failure than one on 3x volume.

### A12 — The second mouse (retest logic)
**"The first mouse gets the trap; the second mouse gets the cheese."** The breakout buyer (first mouse) risks a failed breakout / bull trap. The retest buyer (second mouse) waits for price to come back to the breakout level and *prove* it as support before entering — the breakout has already demonstrated demand, and the retest offers a *much tighter stop* (just below the retested level) and therefore a better reward-to-risk ratio. The trade-off is that strong breakouts sometimes never retest, and the second mouse misses them. For a risk-conscious trader the improved R:R and lower failure rate usually justify occasionally missing the runaway move.

### A13 — Overbought in an uptrend
**The flaw:** RSI overbought (>70) is *not* a sell signal in a trending market — it is evidence of *strength*. In powerful uptrends RSI can remain overbought for many sessions while price keeps climbing; shorting each overbought reading is fighting the trend and gets run over. A persistently overbought RSI actually *confirms* trend strength. Oscillators like RSI are mean-reversion tools best used in *ranging* markets; in trends they are used for pullback entries (buying when RSI dips to 40–50 in an uptrend), not for fading strength. The junior trader is applying a range tool to a trend.

### A14 — Head and shoulders target
**Measured move:** height = head (24,600) − neckline (24,200) = 400 points. Projected down from the neckline break: 24,200 − 400 = **23,800 target.** Two invalidations: (1) price closing back *above the neckline* (24,200) after the break — a failed H&S, often a bullish trap-reversal; (2) price closing above the *right shoulder high* (24,390), which negates the topping structure entirely. Note: targets are guidelines, not guarantees; take partial profits into support levels on the way down and trail the rest.

### A15 — Bull flag
**Pattern: a bull flag** — a sharp flagpole (620→680) followed by an orderly, low-volume consolidation drifting slightly against the trend. Flags resolve in the *direction of the flagpole* (up) the majority of the time. Entry trigger: a break above the upper flag boundary (672) on expanding volume, stop below the lower flag boundary (665) or the flag's low. Measured target: add the flagpole height (~60 points) to the breakout point. The declining volume during consolidation is the confirming tell — it shows profit-taking is orderly, not a reversal of demand.

### A16 — Double bottom with a higher second low
**Yes, this is a valid double bottom, and the higher second low *strengthens* the case.** The classic double bottom needs two lows at roughly the same level; a second low that holds *above* the first (23,640 vs 23,600) shows sellers could not even reach the prior extreme — demand is stepping in earlier, a sign of *accumulation*. Confirmation is a break above the intervening peak (23,850) on volume, which completes the pattern. The higher low is effectively an early higher-low structure, adding conviction. Target: pattern height (23,850 − 23,600 = 250) added to the breakout: ~24,100.

### A17 — Inside-day compression
**Signal: volatility contraction — energy coiling.** Successive inside days (each range within the prior) mean the market is compressing, and compression precedes *expansion*. You do not predict direction from the inside days themselves; you trade the *break*. Place a buy-stop above the mother-bar high (52,300) and a sell-stop below its low (51,500), enter on whichever triggers, and stop on the opposite side (or the nearer structure). The tighter the coil, the more explosive the expansion tends to be. Beware false breaks: demanding a *close* beyond the range, or a volume surge on the break, filters many traps.

### A18 — Polarity (support-becomes-resistance)
**Principle: the principle of polarity** — broken support becomes resistance (and vice versa). Mechanically: traders who bought the old support at 24,000 and rode it down to 23,700 are now trapped and underwater; when price rallies back to 24,000 (their break-even), they sell to escape, creating fresh supply exactly at the old level. Add short-sellers targeting the retest and the level flips from floor to ceiling. This is why old support/resistance levels remain relevant long after they break — the *memory* of trapped participants persists.

### A19 — Opening range breakout (India)
**Classic ORB:** the first 15-minute candle (9:15–9:30) defines the opening range (2,910–2,935). Entry: buy-stop above the range high (2,935); stop below the range low (2,910) or a tighter level like the range midpoint; first target = one range-width projected up (~25 points), then trail. **The filter that dramatically improves win rate in India: trade the ORB only in the *direction of the gap and the broader index bias*, and demand volume expansion on the break.** ORBs against the index trend and on thin volume fail often. Adding a VWAP filter — only take long ORBs while price is above VWAP — screens out many false breaks in choppy sessions.

### A20 — The no-trade decision
**Correct action: stop trading and stand aside.** ADX at 12 and falling confirms a *non-trending, low-directional* environment; a flat VWAP and repeated failed breakouts confirm chop. This is a market designed to death-by-a-thousand-cuts a breakout trader. Recognising that "there is no trade here" and protecting capital is the hallmark of a professional — amateurs feel they *must* trade to justify screen time and give back gains in chop. Two small losses is the market telling you its character; the disciplined response is to reduce size to zero and wait for ADX to rise and a clean trend to establish. Preservation of capital *and* mental capital is itself a winning trade.

## Scoring and what to carry forward

Tally your process matches, not your directional guesses. If you scored 15+/20 on *process*, you have internalised the core reading skills. Below that, note *which category* you missed — trend/structure (A), momentum/exhaustion (B), or patterns/location (C) — and re-drill that weakness. The three recurring lessons in this set: **location beats signal** (the same candle means different things at support versus mid-range), **confirmation beats prediction** (wait for the break/hold, don't anticipate), and **no-trade is a position** (chop and balance are where accounts die). Drills II and III raise the difficulty with multi-timeframe conflicts, options-context reads, and full trade-management scenarios.
