# Agri-Commodities TA

Agricultural commodities are a different animal from the metals and energy contracts, and applying the same technical playbook to them without understanding their peculiar character is a fast way to lose money. Agri markets are seasonal, weather-driven, policy-shackled, and structurally less liquid — which means the charts behave differently, the levels mean different things, and the risks come from directions that never trouble a copper or crude trader. This chapter is a playbook for the Indian agri technician: the character and drivers of the complex, how NCDEX and MCX agri contracts behave, the setups that actually work, a worked guar-complex trade, and the risk notes that keep agri from quietly ruining you.

## The complex's character & drivers

In India, agricultural derivatives trade primarily on the **NCDEX** (National Commodity & Derivatives Exchange), with a few on MCX. The liquid names have shifted over the years as regulators have suspended and re-permitted contracts, but the perennial technical-tradeable agri names include the **guar complex** (Guar Seed and Guar Gum — India dominates world guar production and it feeds oilfield fracking demand), **cotton / Kapas**, **soybean and its derivatives** (soy oil, refined soy oil), **mustard/rapeseed (RM Seed)**, **castor seed**, **jeera (cumin)**, **dhaniya (coriander)**, **turmeric**, and **chana (chickpea)**. On MCX the standout agri contracts have been **cotton** and **CPO (crude palm oil)**, plus **mentha oil**, a uniquely Indian contract.

The defining features that make agri commodities technically distinct:

**Seasonality is the master driver.** Every agri commodity has a crop calendar: sowing, monsoon/growth, harvest, and lean season. Prices follow a repeating seasonal rhythm — typically weak into and just after harvest (peak supply) and firm in the lean pre-harvest months when stocks deplete. Kharif crops (soybean, cotton, guar, castor) are sown June-July with the monsoon and harvested October-November. Rabi crops (mustard, chana, jeera, coriander, wheat) are sown October-December and harvested February-April. A technician who ignores where the market is in the crop calendar is reading the chart blind.

**Weather and the monsoon are the volatility source.** The June-September Southwest monsoon determines kharif output; its timing, distribution and total (relative to the Long Period Average) drive the biggest agri moves. A deficient monsoon or a badly-timed dry spell during the flowering stage can send an oilseed or guar contract limit-up for days. IMD monsoon forecasts, sowing-progress data, and mandi (spot-market) arrival numbers are the fundamental inputs.

**Policy is an ever-present wildcard.** This is the risk that has no metals equivalent. The government intervenes constantly: Minimum Support Prices (MSP) put a floor under many crops; export bans and import-duty changes can be announced overnight (cotton, onions, wheat, sugar have all seen sudden export restrictions); stock limits under the Essential Commodities Act can be imposed to curb "hoarding"; and SEBI itself has suspended entire futures contracts (as happened to several agri contracts in 2021-2022). A perfect technical setup can be destroyed by a policy announcement that no chart could have predicted.

**Liquidity is thinner and lumpier.** Agri contracts trade far less volume than crude or copper. This means wider bid-ask spreads, more slippage, and charts that can show sharp candles on modest volume. It also means the daily price bands and circuit limits matter — agri contracts often hit the exchange-imposed limit-up/limit-down freeze, locking you in a losing position with no exit.

**Physical convergence and delivery.** Agri futures converge to physical spot at expiry, and quality/grade specifications matter. The basis between futures and the benchmark mandi spot price is a real signal — a persistent premium or discount tells you about carrying costs and expected supply.

## Key levels & behaviour

**Seasonal levels beat pure chart levels.** In agri, the most important "level" is often the seasonal turning point on the calendar, not a line on the chart. The pre-harvest lean-season high and the post-harvest glut low recur year after year. Overlaying a seasonal chart (average price by month over several years) on the current price is one of the most powerful agri techniques and has no real equivalent in metals trading.

**MSP as a soft floor.** For MSP-supported crops, the support price acts as a psychological and often practical floor — prices rarely spend long far below MSP because government procurement kicks in. Mark the MSP on your chart as a support reference.

**Circuit limits define the true risk.** Agri contracts have daily price limits (often 4% base, expandable to 6%). When a weather or policy shock hits, the contract can lock limit-up or limit-down and stay frozen for consecutive sessions. Your stop is worthless in a locked market — you cannot get out. This behaviour must shape your position sizing more than any support/resistance line.

**Round numbers and prior harvest highs/lows.** Agri contracts respect round numbers (Rs 5,000, Rs 10,000 on guar; Rs 55,000, Rs 60,000 on cotton per candy/bale references) and the prior season's harvest low and lean-season high as multi-month reference levels.

**Mandi arrival data as a leading indicator.** Rising daily mandi arrivals signal peak-harvest supply pressure (bearish); falling arrivals in the lean season signal tightening (bullish). This physical data leads the futures and is the agri technician's version of order flow.

## Best setups

**1. The seasonal trade.** The highest-conviction agri setup is aligning a technical entry with the seasonal tendency. In a crop with a reliable pattern — say buying an oilseed in the lean pre-harvest window when a technical base forms, or shorting into the harvest glut when a topping pattern appears — the seasonal tailwind stacks the odds. The technical pattern is the trigger; the seasonal bias is the reason. Never fight the season without a strong specific catalyst.

**2. The monsoon-shock momentum trade.** When the monsoon disappoints during a critical crop stage, the affected kharif commodity (guar, soybean, castor, cotton) can trend hard. The technical job is to join on the first pullback or first-day-limit-move breakout, not to fade it. These are the biggest agri moves of the year and they run because real supply is being destroyed.

**3. The post-harvest range fade.** After harvest, with supply abundant and demand steady, many agri contracts settle into a range for weeks or months. Buying the range floor (near MSP support) and selling the range ceiling, with tight risk, works during these quiet phases — until the seasonal lean-season demand or a weather event breaks the range.

**4. The basis / spread trade (advanced).** The guar complex offers a classic Indian spread: Guar Gum is processed from Guar Seed, so the gum-seed ratio and spread mean-revert around a processing-cost band. When the spread stretches unusually wide or narrow, a mean-reversion spread trade (long one, short the other) is a lower-directional-risk agri strategy favoured by professionals.

**5. The breakout from a seasonal base, confirmed by arrivals.** A technical breakout from a multi-week base is far more reliable when the physical data confirms it — a break higher backed by falling mandi arrivals (tightening supply) is a genuine signal, whereas a breakout on rising arrivals is suspect and often fails.

## A worked example

A realistic guar-complex trade. Guar is grown in the semi-arid regions of Rajasthan, Haryana and Gujarat, sown with the monsoon in July and harvested in October-November. Its price carries a double driver: the domestic monsoon (supply) and global oilfield fracking demand for guar gum (US shale activity). This makes it one of the more technically tradeable agri names.

Set the scene in a realistic 2025 monsoon season. Through July, the Southwest monsoon over Rajasthan ran below the Long Period Average, and by early August the IMD flagged a prolonged dry spell over the key guar-growing districts during the critical germination-to-flowering window. Sowing data showed guar acreage down year-on-year. NCDEX Guar Seed, which had based around Rs 5,200 per quintal through the quiet post-rabi period, began to firm as arrivals thinned and the crop-damage narrative built.

On the chart, Guar Seed broke out of a multi-week base above Rs 5,350 resistance on expanding volume, with mandi arrival data falling — physical confirmation. A disciplined trader waited for the first pullback. Two sessions later, the contract pulled back to retest the Rs 5,350 breakout level (now support), holding above the rising short-term moving average, and printed a bullish reversal candle at Rs 5,360.

The trade:
- **Entry:** Rs 5,380 on the reversal confirmation.
- **Stop:** Rs 5,220, below the breakout base and prior support. Risk = Rs 160/quintal.
- **Target 1:** Rs 5,650 (prior seasonal high), book half.
- **Target 2:** Rs 5,950, trailing under the rising moving average.

The confluence: seasonal lean-supply bias, monsoon-deficit supply shock, technical breakout-retest, and physical arrival confirmation — four independent reasons aligned. Over the following three weeks the dry spell persisted, crop-damage estimates worsened, and Guar Seed ran to Rs 5,980 before a late-monsoon revival triggered a pullback that took the trailing stop out around Rs 5,820. A strong multi-R outcome.

Now the essential honest counter-scenario, because it defines agri risk. Suppose that after entry, the government — worried about food/feed inflation — abruptly imposed stock limits on the guar complex and hinted at trading curbs, exactly the kind of intervention that has hit Indian agri markets before. Guar Seed could gap limit-down and lock limit-down for two consecutive sessions. The Rs 5,220 stop is meaningless in a locked-down market — the trader cannot exit at any price and watches the loss compound to Rs 400-500/quintal before the market finally trades freely. This is the agri-specific catastrophe: not a bad chart read, but a policy shock that no technical level can protect against, made unmanageable by circuit locks.

## Risk notes

**Size for the limit-lock, not the stop.** This is the cardinal agri rule. Because contracts can lock limit-up or limit-down for consecutive sessions, your stop may be un-executable. Assume a worst-case loss of 2-3x your intended stop and size the position so that even that outcome is survivable. If you cannot survive a two-day limit-lock against you, the position is too big.

**Respect the policy calendar and never bet the account on one agri position.** Export bans, stock limits, MSP changes and duty tweaks arrive without warning, usually against speculators. No single agri trade should risk more than 1% of capital, and total agri exposure should be modest.

**Trade only liquid contracts, and mind the spread.** Thin agri contracts have wide spreads and vicious slippage. Stick to the actively traded names (guar complex, cotton, soy complex, mustard, jeera, MCX cotton/CPO) and check open interest and volume before entering. Avoid the back months and near-dead contracts.

**Overlay seasonality on every setup.** Before taking any agri trade, ask where you are in the crop calendar. A long into peak harvest supply, or a short into the lean pre-harvest squeeze, is fighting the season — you need an exceptional specific catalyst to justify it.

**Watch the physical: arrivals, sowing, monsoon.** The agri technician's edge comes from reading the physical data (mandi arrivals, sowing progress, IMD monsoon updates) alongside the chart. When chart and physical agree, conviction is high; when they diverge, trust the physical and stand aside.

**Mind expiry and delivery.** Agri futures converge to physical and carry grade/quality delivery mechanics. Retail speculators should exit or roll well before expiry to avoid delivery obligations and the illiquidity of the dying contract.

The honest summary: agri-commodities can offer some of the cleanest seasonal edges in the Indian markets — the monsoon and crop calendar produce repeatable, tradeable patterns that a disciplined technician can exploit. But they carry a risk no metals or energy trader faces: the twin threat of circuit-limit locks and overnight policy intervention, either of which can turn a well-placed stop into a fiction. The traders who last in agri are the ones who size tiny, respect the season, read the physical data, and never, ever assume they can get out when the market locks limit-down. Most retail agri speculators lose money, and a disproportionate share of those losses come not from bad charting but from being trapped in a frozen market on the wrong side of a policy shock. Trade accordingly.
