# Mock Test I

This is the first full-length examination of the masterclass. It is built the way a real skills test — or a serious prop-desk interview — would be built: a spread of chart-reading, indicator-interpretation, decision-making, risk-arithmetic and psychology questions, every one of them India-specific with realistic NSE and MCX levels for 2026. Treat it seriously. Set a timer for 90 minutes, write your answer to each question on paper *before* you read the key, and score yourself honestly. The answer key is not just a letter or a level — it explains the reasoning, because a mock test that only tells you "C" teaches you nothing. Most retail traders who *think* they know technical analysis score below 60% on a test like this on the first attempt. That is the point: to find the gaps while it costs nothing.

## Instructions and scoring

There are 25 questions across five sections (5 marks each unless stated; total 100). Section A is multiple-choice concept checks. Section B is chart-reading and pattern recognition. Section C is decision-making (trade / no-trade with entry, stop, target). Section D is risk and money-management arithmetic. Section E is psychology and process. A score of 80+ is professional-grade; 65-79 is competent with gaps; below 65 means re-study the flagged volumes before risking real capital. Full answers with reasoning follow the questions.

# Answer Key — Mock Test I

## Section A

**Q1 — (b).** The 20-day EMA is a lagging trend-follower, but in an uptrend it acts as dynamic support (price pulls back to it and bounces) and its slope is a fast read on trend health. It is not leading or an oscillator. Full marks require noting *both* the support role and the health-proxy role.

**Q2 — (b).** Bearish divergence is a *warning* — momentum is weakening even as price makes new highs — but it is not an immediate sell and certainly not a guarantee of a top; trends run in divergence for weeks. The correct response is to reduce adds and tighten stops, not to short blindly. Answers (a) and (c) are the two classic errors (over-reacting or dismissing).

**Q3 — (b).** Volume is the fuel of a breakout. A 3x-average breakout shows genuine demand overwhelming supply, far more likely to hold than a low-volume break, which frequently fails and traps buyers.

**Q4 — (b).** HH/HL above a rising 20W EMA is a textbook uptrend; positional bias is long-only, with shorts permitted only as small counter-trend scalps. Fighting a clear weekly trend is a low-expectancy habit.

**Q5 — (b).** India VIX is the fear gauge. A spike from 12 to 22 alongside a falling Nifty signals rising fear and surging demand for put protection — volatility expansion. It does not guarantee a next-day bounce (though extreme VIX can precede reversals) and it makes options *more* expensive, not cheaper.

**Q6 — (b).** A failed breakout traps buyers who bought the break; when it fails they must sell, and that forced selling frequently drives price back across the whole range — which is why failed breakouts are among the most reliable reversal signals. It happens on all timeframes.

## Section B

**Q7.** A **bearish shooting star / exhaustion candle** at prior all-time-high resistance, on climax volume after an extended six-day run. It signals that buyers pushed to a new high intraday but sellers overwhelmed them by the close — likely short-term exhaustion. Implication: protect longs, tighten stops; a reversal or at least a pullback is probable. Not an automatic short, but the odds of continuation just dropped sharply.

**Q8.** A **rectangle / range breakdown** (a bearish continuation of the failed gap). The range height is 24,960 − 24,900 = 60 points; the projected move is roughly 60 points below the breakdown, i.e. a target near 24,840, with the gap-fill toward the prior close as a secondary objective. Expanding volume on the break confirms it. The gap-up that fails to hold and breaks its opening range is a classic intraday reversal.

**Q9.** A **double top / triple-top style range** with resistance at 152 and support at 141 (a "head" is absent, so best described as a rectangle with a critical support at 141). The critical level is 141: two prior bounces make it a tested support. A **decisive close below 141 on above-average volume** confirms the breakdown, projecting a measured move of the range height (152 − 141 = 11 points) below 141, toward ~130. Until 141 breaks, it remains a range.

**Q10.** A **base breakout (Stage 2 breakout)** from a year-long accumulation base. Measured target = breakout level + base depth = 1,600 + 250 = **1,850**. A decisive weekly close above the base on strong volume is a high-quality positional buy signal; the long base gives the move staying power.

**Q11.** A **bearish crossover / "death cross"** (20-day EMA crossing below the 50-day EMA) with price below both — confirmation of an established downtrend. Trend implication: bias is down; rallies to the falling EMAs are shorting opportunities, not buying opportunities.

**Q12.** A clean **uptrend structure** — a sequence of higher lows (1,650 → 1,662 → 1,671) and higher highs (1,668 → 1,679). Bias is bullish; the correct play is to buy pullbacks toward the rising structure/EMA, with invalidation on a break below the most recent higher low (1,671).

## Section C

**Q13 — TRADE, LONG (A+).** All three timeframes align: weekly up (permission), daily pullback to rising 20-EMA with a hammer (setup/location), hourly trendline break + higher low (trigger). Entry ~24,720, stop below the hammer low and 20-day EMA ~24,620 (risk ~100 pts), first target the prior swing high ~24,930 then continuation. R:R ~1:2+. Textbook buy-the-dip-in-an-uptrend.

**Q14 — NO-TRADE (positional).** The 15-min flag and daily bounce are tempting, but the weekly bias is DOWN and price is rallying straight into falling weekly resistance (51,000). This is a counter-trend bounce into resistance — a trap for longs. At most a tiny scalp targeting 51,000 with a tight 15-min stop; the professional play is to wait for a daily bearish reversal near 51,000 and short *with* the weekly.

**Q15 — NO-TRADE (or short scalp).** Daily is a range; you buy support and sell resistance, never buy near the top. Price at 638 is at range resistance (640) with an overbought, extended hourly. This is a place to take profits or watch for a short back toward the range middle — not to initiate a long. Fresh longs belong near 590-600.

**Q16 — TRADE, LONG.** MCX Gold in a strong daily uptrend, pulling back into confluence support (20-day EMA + prior breakout at ₹91,200) with a bullish engulfing. Entry ~₹91,600, stop below the engulfing low / confluence ~₹90,900 (risk ~₹700/10g), first target the prior high ₹92,400 then measured continuation. Buy-the-dip in a trending commodity — clean.

**Q17 — NO-TRADE.** Neutral daily, choppy hourly, 15-min already whipsawing, monthly expiry pinning near a round level. This is a non-tradable chop regime; trend-following gets shredded. Preserve capital. If forced, fade exact range edges with hard stops and quick profits. Recognising "no trade" is the correct answer.

**Q18 — TRADE, CAUTIOUS LONG (small size).** The 6% news gap-down halted precisely at multi-timeframe confluence (rising 20-week EMA + prior breakout at 1,335), and the hourly higher low shows sellers exhausting. Entry ~1,345, hard stop below the gap low / weekly EMA at 1,320 (a close below breaks the weekly uptrend — thesis dead), target the gap-fill toward 1,400. Small size because news gaps can extend; the confluence halt plus higher invalidation makes it a valid mean-reversion long.

## Section D

**Q19.** Risk per trade = 1% × ₹10,00,000 = **₹10,000**. Risk per share = 1,455 − 1,430 = ₹25. Shares = 10,000 ÷ 25 = **400 shares**. Capital deployed = 400 × 1,455 = ₹5,82,000 (within account). Rupee risk if stopped = 400 × 25 = ₹10,000, exactly 1%. Correct answer: **400 shares, ₹10,000 risk.**

**Q20.** Risk = 24,720 − 24,650 = 70 points. Reward = 24,930 − 24,720 = 210 points. R:R = 210 ÷ 70 = **1:3**. This **exceeds** the 1:2 minimum, so the trade is acceptable on reward-to-risk grounds.

**Q21.** Expectancy = (win% × avg win) − (loss% × avg loss) = (0.40 × 6,000) − (0.60 × 2,000) = 2,400 − 1,200 = **+₹1,200 per trade**. Over 20 trades: 20 × 1,200 = **+₹24,000 net**. The system is **profitable** despite a sub-50% win rate, because winners are 3x the size of losers — proof that win rate alone does not determine profitability; expectancy does.

**Q22.** After the 3-loss streak the rule halves size, so risk = 0.5% × ₹10,00,000 = **₹5,000** on the next trade (instead of ₹10,000). The rule exists to reduce risk during a losing streak — when you may be misreading the market or tilting emotionally — protecting capital and limiting drawdown until your edge re-asserts. It also blunts the psychological pressure to "make it back" with oversized bets.

## Section E

**Q23.** The underlying error is letting fear override the plan — an emotional exit, not a signal-based one. Process fix: **pre-define the exit before entering** (target and trailing-stop rules written in the trade plan) and *mechanically* execute them, optionally scaling out (book half at target 1, trail the rest) so fear is satisfied without abandoning the whole position. Journaling the "exited early vs plan" occurrences makes the leak visible and measurable.

**Q24.** This is **revenge trading** — an emotional impulse to recover a loss immediately, which usually produces an unplanned, oversized, low-quality trade and compounds the damage. The neutralising rule: a **mandatory cooling-off period** after a loss (e.g., no new trade for X minutes, or stop trading for the day after a defined daily-loss limit), plus the halve-size-after-streak rule. Structure removes the decision from the emotional moment.

**Q25.** A journal lets you **spot time-of-day performance patterns** by tagging each trade with the time and quality (planned vs unplanned) and reviewing the aggregate. Seeing that afternoon trades cluster your losses is evidence, not a feeling. A specific rule: **no new positions after 1:30 pm** (or only pre-planned setups after that time), and afternoons reserved for managing existing trades. The journal turns a vague suspicion into a hard, testable process change.
