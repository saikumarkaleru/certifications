# Building a Personal Trading Plan

A trading plan is the written constitution of your trading business. It converts a loose collection of setups, hunches, and rules-of-thumb into a repeatable, auditable process — one you can follow when you're tired, tilted, or staring at a P&L that's screaming at you to do something stupid. The difference between a trader with a plan and one without is the difference between a business and a gambling habit that occasionally pays. This chapter walks through building your own, section by section, with concrete Indian-market templates you can lift and adapt. The output is a living document you'll revise quarterly for the rest of your trading life.

## The principle: a plan exists to make decisions *before* you're emotional

Every rule in your plan should be written to answer one question: *what will I do when I don't feel like doing the right thing?* Markets are an environment engineered by evolution to trigger fear and greed at exactly the wrong moments. Your rational, pre-market self is a different person from your mid-drawdown, adrenaline-flooded self. The plan is a letter from the calm you to the panicked you. If a rule is vague ("cut losses quickly"), the panicked you will interpret it favourably and hold the loser. If it's specific ("exit if price closes below the 20-EMA on the 15-min chart, no exceptions"), there's nothing to negotiate. **Specificity is the whole point.** A plan full of aspirations is decoration; a plan full of if-then rules is armour.

## The eleven sections of a complete plan

A serious plan has these components. Write each one out — the act of writing forces the clarity.

### 1. Mission, goals, and timeframe

State *why* you trade and what "success" means, in measurable terms and with an honest horizon.

> **Example.** "I trade to build long-term wealth and eventually replace my salary. My goal for the next 12 months is not a rupee target but a *process* target: log 200+ trades, keep max drawdown under 15%, and finish the year with a positive, cost-adjusted expectancy. Rupee returns will follow process. I am in the *skill-building* phase and will not pressure the account to be income yet."

Notice the goal is a *process* goal, not "make ₹5 lakh." Beginners who set rupee targets force trades to hit numbers and blow up. Targets are outputs; you can only control inputs.

### 2. Markets and instruments

Define exactly *what* you trade and, importantly, what you *don't*.

> **Example.** "I trade: (a) Nifty and Bank Nifty index futures and simple option-buying setups; (b) the top-15 most-liquid F&O stocks (RELIANCE, HDFCBANK, ICICIBANK, SBIN, INFY, TCS, AXISBANK, etc.) in cash and futures. I do NOT trade: illiquid small-caps, options I can't model, commodities I don't understand, or any instrument on hearsay/tips. If it's not on my list, I don't touch it."

The exclusions matter as much as the inclusions. A defined universe stops FOMO-chasing whatever is trending on social media.

### 3. Timeframe and style

State your trading style and the exact charts you make decisions on. Multi-timeframe alignment: higher timeframe for bias, lower for entry.

> **Example.** "I am a swing/positional trader holding 2–15 days, with occasional intraday trades on high-conviction days. Bias timeframe: **daily** chart. Trigger timeframe: **1-hour**. Fine-tune entry: **15-min**. I do not scalp — I don't have the reflexes or the cost structure for it."

### 4. The setups (your edge, defined)

This is the heart of the plan: your specific, named setups with *exact* entry, stop, and target rules. Vague setups produce vague results. Write each as a checklist an outsider could follow.

> **Example — "Pullback-to-20EMA in an uptrend" (my A-setup):**
> - **Context:** Daily chart in a clear uptrend (price above rising 50-EMA, higher highs/higher lows).
> - **Trigger:** Price pulls back to the 20-EMA on the 1-hour chart and forms a bullish reversal candle (hammer / bullish engulfing) at or near the EMA.
> - **Entry:** Buy above the high of the trigger candle.
> - **Stop:** Below the low of the trigger candle (or below the 20-EMA swing low, whichever is structurally sound).
> - **Target:** T1 at the prior swing high (book 50%, move stop to breakeven); T2 trail the rest on the 20-EMA.
> - **Invalidation:** A daily close below the 50-EMA cancels the uptrend context — stand aside.

List your 2–4 highest-quality setups. Resist having twenty. A tight repertoire you know cold beats a sprawling one you half-remember. Grade them A/B/C and take full size only on A-setups.

### 5. Risk and position-sizing rules

Pull the money-management models into hard numbers (see the sizing chapter). This section is non-negotiable and should be the shortest to *read* and the hardest to *break*.

> **Example.**
> - Risk per trade: **1%** of current equity (0.5% on B-setups).
> - Position size = (equity × risk%) ÷ (entry − stop), rounded **down** to whole shares/lots.
> - Portfolio heat (total open risk) cap: **5%**. Per-sector risk cap: **3%**.
> - Max concurrent positions: **5**.
> - Drawdown throttle: cut risk to 0.5% at −10% from peak, 0.25% at −15%, **flat everything and stop for review at −20%**.
> - Daily loss limit: **−3% of equity in one day → stop trading, close the terminal.**

### 6. Entry and exit execution rules

How you actually place and manage orders — the operational detail that prevents costly fumbles.

> **Example.** "Enter with a stop-limit order above the trigger candle, never a naked market order into a fast move. Set the hard stop-loss order *the moment the entry fills* — a position without a live stop does not exist in my account. Scale out at pre-defined targets; never move a stop *away* from price to avoid being stopped (moving it *toward* price to lock profit is allowed). I do not average down, ever."

### 7. Trading hours and routine

Define *when* you trade. The Indian session has a rhythm: the 9:15–9:30 opening is volatile and gap-driven; ~9:45–11:00 and the 14:00–15:15 window tend to trend; the midday 11:30–13:30 lull chops. Decide where you play.

> **Example.** "I avoid taking fresh positions in the first 15 minutes (let the opening auction settle). I hunt entries 9:45–11:00 and 13:45–15:00. I don't initiate new intraday positions after 15:00. On expiry days (Thursday for Nifty/index in the 2026 schedule — verify current expiry days) I trade lighter or sit out, because gamma-driven whipsaws break my swing setups."

### 8. Pre-market, in-session, and post-market routines

The daily operating procedure — checklists that put you in the right state.

> **Pre-market (before 9:15):** Check global cues (US close, SGX Nifty/GIFT Nifty, crude, USDINR, Asian markets). Note the day's key events (RBI, Fed, results, macro data). Mark support/resistance on Nifty, Bank Nifty, and my watchlist. Write the day's bias in one line. Confirm I'm rested and calm — if not, trade half-size or not at all.
>
> **In-session:** Only take setups on the plan. Log every trade *as I take it* (entry, stop, size, reason). No P&L-watching between entries — I watch *charts*, not money.
>
> **Post-market:** Journal every trade — screenshot, setup grade, what I did well/badly, whether I followed the plan (process score) *independent* of whether it made money. Update equity, drawdown, and heat.

### 9. The trading journal spec

Define exactly what you log, because an un-journaled trader cannot improve — you can't fix what you don't measure.

| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| Date / instrument | 2026-07-14 / BANKNIFTY fut |
| Setup + grade | Pullback-to-20EMA / A |
| Entry / stop / target | 52,000 / 51,700 / 52,600 |
| Size / rupee risk | 1 lot / ₹4,500 |
| Exit / R-multiple | 52,450 / +1.5R |
| **Process score (1–5)** | 5 — followed plan exactly |
| Emotional state | Calm, patient |
| Lesson | Trailed too tight, left 0.8R on the table |

The **process score, judged separately from P&L**, is the most important column. A losing trade taken perfectly by the rules is a *good* trade (5/5 process). A winning trade taken against the rules is a *bad* trade (1/5) — because it rewards the behaviour that will eventually ruin you. Grade the process, not the outcome.

### 10. Rules for special conditions

Pre-decide your behaviour around the situations that wreck plans.

> - **Results/earnings:** No new positions in a stock within 2 days of its results (gap risk). Reduce or hedge existing ones.
> - **Big macro events (RBI policy, Fed, Union Budget, election results):** Trade half-size or flat; these are gap-and-whipsaw days.
> - **After 3 consecutive losses:** Stop for the day, review whether it's variance or a process breakdown.
> - **After a big win:** Beware euphoria; the trade after a big win is statistically where overconfidence causes errors. Same size, same rules.
> - **When flat/uncertain:** No position is a position. Cash is a valid, often correct, stance.

### 11. Review, metrics, and evolution

Define how and when you audit and improve the plan.

> **Weekly:** Review all trades, tally process-adherence %, note recurring errors.
> **Monthly:** Compute win-rate, average R, expectancy, max drawdown, and profit factor. Check which setups are working and which aren't.
> **Quarterly:** Revise the plan — retire dead setups, tighten rules I keep breaking, consider a risk-% step-up if eligible (new highs + sample size).

Key metrics to track:

```
Expectancy (per trade, in R) = (Win% × Avg Win R) − (Loss% × Avg Loss R)
Profit factor = Gross profit ÷ Gross loss   (aim > 1.5)
```

## A worked scenario: the plan under fire

It's 09:50. Bank Nifty is in a clean daily uptrend and pulls to its 1-hour 20-EMA at 51,900, printing a bullish engulfing candle. Your **A-setup** fires. You buy above the candle high at 51,960, stop below its low at 51,680 (280 pts). One lot risks 280 × 15 = ₹4,200 — under your ₹5,000 (1%) budget, so **1 lot**. Stop order goes in the instant you fill. You note it in the journal.

Price rallies to 52,400, then reverses hard on a surprise negative global headline and stops you at 51,680. Loss: −1R, ₹4,200. Here is the entire point of the plan: **this was a good trade.** You followed context, trigger, sizing, and stop-placement perfectly (process score 5/5). The loss was variance, not error. The plan-less trader, stopped out, would now *revenge-trade* — re-enter bigger to "get it back," breaking every rule, and turn a clean −1R into a −5R disaster. You, instead, log it, note "textbook stop, no error," and wait for the next A-setup. Over 200 such trades, your positive expectancy does its quiet work. **The plan didn't prevent the loss — it prevented the loss from becoming a catastrophe.** That is what plans do.

## Pitfalls in plan-building

- **Vagueness.** "Trade with the trend, cut losses" is not a plan — it's a poster. Every rule must be executable by a stranger.
- **Too many setups.** Twenty half-known setups is worse than three mastered ones.
- **No process/outcome separation.** Judging trades by P&L instead of adherence trains you to gamble.
- **A plan you don't actually follow.** An unfollowed plan is worse than none — it breeds contempt for your own rules. Start with rules you *can* keep, then tighten.
- **Never revising it.** Markets and your skill both evolve. A static plan calcifies. Revise quarterly.
- **Rupee targets instead of process targets.** They force trades and induce oversizing.
- **No written form.** A plan "in your head" is exactly the plan that dissolves under emotional pressure. Write it down.

## How to build it into your routine

1. **Draft it this week** — one page per section, in plain language, using the templates above. Done-and-usable beats perfect-and-unwritten.
2. **Print the one-page version** — universe, setups (with exact entry/stop/target), sizing, heat cap, daily loss limit, drawdown throttle — and tape it where you trade.
3. **Run the pre-market/post-market checklists daily** without exception; they're what put you in state and out of it.
4. **Journal every trade with a process score**, and in your weekly review compute your adherence %. Aim for 90%+ before worrying about anything else — a followed mediocre plan beats an ignored brilliant one.
5. **Revise quarterly**, driven by your own logged data, not by the last loud thing you read.

Your plan will not be right the first time, and it doesn't need to be. What it needs to be is *written, specific, and followed* — because a trading edge only exists if you can execute it consistently, and consistency is impossible without a constitution to hold you to.

## Interview-ready summary

A personal trading plan is the written constitution that converts scattered setups into a repeatable, auditable process, and its purpose is to make decisions *before* emotion arrives — every rule answers "what will I do when I don't feel like doing the right thing?", so specificity (executable if-then rules, not aspirations) is everything. A complete plan has eleven sections: **mission and process goals** (never rupee targets), **defined instrument universe** (with explicit exclusions), **timeframes/style**, **2–4 named setups with exact entry/stop/target/invalidation**, **hard risk-and-sizing rules** (1% per trade, 5% heat cap, daily loss limit, drawdown throttle), **execution rules** (stop set the instant you fill, never average down), **trading hours** matched to the NSE session rhythm, **pre/in/post-market routines**, a **journal spec whose most important field is a process score judged independently of P&L**, **rules for special conditions** (results, RBI/Fed events, loss streaks), and a **review-and-evolution cadence** (weekly adherence, monthly metrics — expectancy and profit factor — quarterly revision). The plan doesn't prevent losses; a textbook trade can still lose. It prevents a normal −1R loss from becoming a revenge-trading catastrophe. Write it down, tape the one-page version to your desk, follow it 90%+ of the time, and revise it quarterly from your own logged data — because an edge only exists if you can execute it consistently.
