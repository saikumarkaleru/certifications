# Routines & Rituals

Elite performers in every high-stakes field — surgeons, fighter pilots, professional cricketers taking guard — do not walk into the arena and improvise their mental state. They run a routine. The routine is not superstition; it is a deliberate technology for putting a fallible human brain into a repeatable, high-performing configuration on demand. Trading is uniquely hostile to consistency because the environment is random, the feedback is delayed and noisy, and money — the most emotionally loaded object humans handle — is on the line every minute. Against that, the only reliable defence is process that does not depend on how you feel. This chapter builds the daily architecture of a professional Indian trader: the pre-market routine that loads your bias, the in-session cadence that keeps you honest, and the post-market ritual that compounds learning. The principle throughout is simple and severe — **you cannot control outcomes, so you must control the process that surrounds every outcome.**

## The principle: routines make your best self the default

Your trading performance is not a flat line; it oscillates. There is a version of you that is calm, patient, and rule-following, and a version that is rushed, greedy, or vengeful. The market does not summon your best self — it randomly rewards and punishes, which tends to *pull out* your worst self. A routine is a scaffold that holds up your best self even when the market is trying to knock it down. It works through three mechanisms:

1. **State-loading:** The pre-market ritual physically and mentally shifts you from "person having coffee" to "trader ready to risk capital," the same way a batsman's guard-taking loads focus before the first ball.
2. **Decision-offloading:** By the time the bell rings, your levels, bias, and risk are already decided. You are executing a plan, not authoring one live. This preserves willpower for the one thing routines can't do for you: waiting.
3. **Compounding:** The post-market ritual converts today's random outcomes into structured lessons. Without it, you get 250 trading days of *experience* but zero *learning* — you repeat year one twenty times.

The honest limit: a routine is a container, not content. A beautiful routine around a losing system just loses beautifully. But no good system survives without a routine to execute it consistently, so this is necessary infrastructure regardless of your edge.

## The pre-market routine (before 9:15 IST)

The pre-market block is where the trading day is actually won or lost, long before any order. A professional Indian index or stock trader's sequence, roughly 45–60 minutes:

| Time | Ritual | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Wake + 20 min | Physical prep — no screens first | Regulate nervous system before market noise floods in |
| ~8:15 | Global scan: US close (S&P, Nasdaq), SGX/GIFT Nifty, crude, USDINR, Asian markets | Establish overnight context and gap risk |
| ~8:30 | News & events: RBI, Fed, results calendar, F&O expiry, global data | Know what can move the market today |
| ~8:40 | Mark levels: Nifty, Bank Nifty — yesterday's high/low/close, PDH/PDL, VWAP anchor, key round numbers, prior swing points | Pre-decide the map so you react to price, not invent levels live |
| ~8:55 | Write the bias: bullish / bearish / neutral above/below X, with the "if-then" scenarios | Convert analysis into a written plan |
| ~9:05 | Set risk: today's max loss, per-trade size table, "am I allowed to trade heavy today?" | Load the guardrails |
| ~9:10 | Mindset check-in: rate sleep, stress, mood 1–10; decide whether to trade at all | Screen out the days you shouldn't trade |

Two of these deserve emphasis.

**Marking levels in advance.** The difference between a professional and an amateur intraday is that the professional already knows that ₹48,150 is the level that matters on Bank Nifty *before* the market opens, and simply waits to see how price behaves there. The amateur discovers levels live, drawing lines to justify a position he already wants. Pre-marked levels are objective because you drew them when you had no position and no bias to protect.

**The mindset check-in (the go/no-go).** This is the most neglected and most valuable item. You rate three things — sleep quality, external stress (a fight at home, a sick child, a work crisis), and current emotional state — each 1 to 10. If two of three are below 5, you either trade at drastically reduced size or you don't trade at all. Pilots have a formal "IMSAFE" checklist for exactly this reason: a tired, stressed operator makes fatal errors. The trader who skips a bad-state day preserves the capital that the trader who "pushes through" hands to the market. Sitting out is a position — often the highest-expectancy one available on your worst days.

## The in-session cadence (9:15 a.m.–3:30 p.m.)

Once the market opens, the job flips from analysis to *self-management*. The dangerous instinct is to be glued to every tick, because constant screen-watching manufactures the urge to act. Professionals impose a cadence.

- **The opening 15 minutes (9:15–9:30):** Observe, don't trade. The open is maximum noise, widest spreads, and stop-hunts. Most plans forbid trading here. Watch how price interacts with your pre-marked levels; let the auction reveal intent.
- **Trade in windows, not continuously:** Your setups occur at specific moments (a breakout, a VWAP reclaim, a pullback into a level). Between setups there is nothing to do but wait, and waiting is the skill. Some traders use a physical timer — check the chart every 5–15 minutes rather than staring — precisely to break the tick-hypnosis that breeds overtrading.
- **The pre-trade pause:** Before every order, a literal breath and the checklist. Five seconds of "is this in my plan?" prevents most impulse trades.
- **The post-trade reset:** After any trade closes — win or loss — stand up, step away for two minutes. This severs the emotional carry-over. A win breeds overconfidence; a loss breeds revenge. The two-minute reset stops the previous trade from contaminating the next one. This single ritual prevents the classic "tilt cascade" where one bad trade becomes a bad day.
- **The lunch lull (roughly 11:30–1:30):** Indian markets often go dead in the midday session — low volume, choppy, whipsaw-heavy. Many professionals simply stop trading through it. Recognising a low-opportunity regime and stepping back is itself a routine.
- **The mid-day mental checkpoint:** Around 12:30, a 30-second self-audit: "Am I following my plan? What's my P&L and am I letting it drive me? Am I tilted?" Catching tilt at noon saves the afternoon.

The organising idea is **scheduled action inside enforced patience.** You are not trying to be in the market; you are trying to be *ready* for the few moments the market pays you, and calm during the many it doesn't.

## The post-market ritual (after 3:30 IST)

The bell does not end the trading day; the review does. This 20–30 minute ritual is where random outcomes become durable skill.

1. **Square the books:** Confirm all positions closed or accounted for; note final P&L but do not react to it yet.
2. **Log every trade:** Entry, exit, setup, size, R-multiple, and — critically — the emotion and the reason. (The deep mechanics of this belong to the journaling chapter; here it is one step in the ritual.)
3. **Grade the day on process, not profit:** Did I follow my plan? How many violations? Rate adherence 1–10. A green P&L day with three rule-breaks is a *bad* day; a red day with flawless execution is a *good* day. Internalising this is what separates a career from a gambling habit.
4. **One lesson:** Force yourself to write a single sentence — the one thing today taught you. Over a year that is 250 concrete lessons.
5. **Prep tomorrow's shell:** Note key levels and events already visible for the next session, so the morning routine starts warm.
6. **Physical shutdown ritual:** Close the terminal, close the journal, and consciously *end* the trading identity for the day — a walk, a workout, family time. Traders who never psychologically leave the market burn out within a year or two. The shutdown ritual protects the one non-renewable asset: your longevity in the game.

## Worked scenario: a full day in the life

**7:30 a.m.** — Priya wakes, no phone for 20 minutes, walks. **8:15** — GIFT Nifty up 60 points, US closed green, crude soft, USDINR stable. Gap-up context. **8:30** — Checks calendar: no RBI, no major result, but it's Bank Nifty weekly expiry — she notes elevated theta and whip risk. **8:40** — Marks levels: Nifty PDH 24,320, PDL 24,180; Bank Nifty resistance 51,600, support 51,150. **8:55** — Writes bias: "Gap-up into 24,320 resistance; if it holds above on retest, long toward 24,400; if it rejects, fade back to 24,250. Neutral until the open settles." **9:10** — Mindset check: slept well (8), low stress (7), calm (7). Cleared to trade normal size.

**9:15–9:30** — Gap-up opens right at 24,320, stalls. She watches, no trade. **9:45** — Price retests 24,320 from above, holds, 5-min closes strong on volume — her ORB/level-reclaim setup. Checklist clears. Long, risk ₹7,500, stop below the retest low. **10:30** — Hits first target +1.5R, trails the rest. Post-trade: stands up, two-minute reset. **11:30–1:30** — Recognises the lunch chop, no setups, does not force anything; reviews watchlist, waits. **12:30** — Mid-day audit: up 0.9%, plan followed, not tilted, good. **1:50** — A clean fade setup at 24,400 rejection; takes it, small win. **3:15** — Winds down, no fresh entries near the close.

**3:45 p.m.** — Post-market: logs both trades with emotions ("patient in the morning, slightly bored at lunch but held"), grades adherence 10/10, writes the day's lesson: *"Waiting through the lunch chop preserved my morning gains — the trade I didn't take was worth more than a trade I might have forced."* Notes tomorrow's levels. Closes the laptop, goes to the gym. The day is over.

Notice how little of that day was about market genius and how much was about *sequence*. The edge showed up in the twenty minutes of a real setup; the other five-plus hours were pure self-management held together by routine.

## Building your own routine

- **Start minimal, then harden:** Begin with three non-negotiables — pre-market level marking, a mindset go/no-go, and a post-market log. Add rituals only as you discover the specific failure they prevent.
- **Make it physical and cued:** Attach rituals to physical triggers (coffee = start scan; standing up = post-trade reset). Cues make routines automatic; automatic routines survive stressful days.
- **Protect the routine on bad days:** The days you least *feel* like doing the review are the days it matters most — after a big loss, when your brain wants to flee the screen. The ritual is precisely what converts that loss into a lesson instead of a wound.
- **Weekly and monthly zoom-out:** Beyond the daily loop, a weekend review (patterns across the week, rule-adherence score, what to change) and a monthly review (equity curve, expectancy, whether the system still works) keep the daily routine pointed at a real edge.

## Interview-ready summary

A trading routine is a technology for making your best self the default, structured as three loops: a **pre-market ritual** (global scan, event check, pre-marked levels, written bias, risk setup, and a mindset go/no-go that screens out bad-state days), an **in-session cadence** (observe the open, trade in windows not continuously, a pre-trade pause and a two-minute post-trade reset to prevent tilt cascades, and stepping back in low-opportunity regimes like the lunch chop), and a **post-market ritual** (log every trade, grade the day on process not profit, extract one lesson, prep tomorrow, and consciously shut down to protect longevity). The unifying principle is that outcomes are uncontrollable and random in the short run, so the professional controls the process surrounding every outcome. The routine doesn't create an edge — it is the container that lets a real edge express itself consistently and turns 250 random days into 250 compounding lessons.
