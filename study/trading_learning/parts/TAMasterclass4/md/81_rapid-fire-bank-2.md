# Rapid-Fire Question Bank II

This is the second rapid-fire drill set of Volume IV. Where the earlier bank tested single-concept recall, this one leans into **judgement under ambiguity** — the questions where two indicators disagree, where the "textbook" answer is a trap, and where an experienced trader's answer is "it depends, and here is on what." Each item is deliberately short so you can run twenty in fifteen minutes, flash-card style. Cover the answer, commit to a response out loud, then check. The reasoning matters more than the label; if you got the answer right for the wrong reason, mark it wrong.

All questions are India-first: Nifty, Bank Nifty, Fin Nifty, NSE cash stocks, MCX, USDINR, and the platforms (TradingView, Chartink, the NSE option chain) you actually use. Levels are realistic for 2026. Format: **Q**, then **A** with the *why*.

---

## Section A — Breadth and participation (Q1–Q6)

**Q1.** Nifty prints a new all-time high at 26,400 but the NSE advance-decline line has been making lower highs for three weeks. What is the single most likely interpretation, and what does it NOT tell you?

**A1.** It is a **breadth divergence** — the index is being carried by a shrinking number of heavyweight names (typically Reliance, HDFC Bank, ICICI, the top-5 by weight) while the median stock is already rolling over. It warns that the *quality* of the advance is deteriorating and that a correction, when it comes, can be sharp because there is little broad support underneath. What it does NOT tell you is **timing**. Breadth divergences can persist for weeks; a narrow market can grind higher for a month. Treat it as a reason to tighten trailing stops and stop adding fresh longs, not as a short signal on its own.

**Q2.** On a given session, Nifty is up 0.3% but only 12 of the 50 constituents are green. What is happening under the hood?

**A2.** A **handful of high-weight stocks** (or one giant like Reliance/HDFC Bank) is dragging the cap-weighted index up while the other 38 are red. The index return is misleading — an equal-weight view of Nifty would be *down* on the day. If you trade broad-market longs, this is a poor tape: your median stock is falling. Breadth like this often precedes a pullback in the index itself once the leaders pause.

**Q3.** The percentage of Nifty 500 stocks above their 200-DMA has fallen from 78% to 41% while the index is down only 4% from its high. Bullish or bearish, and why?

**A3.** **Bearish underneath a calm surface.** The index has barely corrected but *more than half the market* has already broken its long-term trend. This is classic late-cycle behaviour — the generals still standing, the soldiers already retreating. It argues for defense: reduce leverage, favour the strongest names only, and expect the index to eventually "catch down" to breadth.

**Q4.** Bank Nifty makes a higher high but the number of its 12 constituents making new highs drops from 7 to 2. Interpretation?

**A4.** Narrowing leadership within the bank index — likely HDFC Bank and ICICI carrying it while PSU banks and smaller privates lag. The move is **less robust** than the price suggests. In a sector index with only 12 names, two stocks making highs is thin; a stumble in either leader can reverse the whole index quickly.

**Q5.** Advance-decline ratio on NSE is 4:1 positive for the third straight day after a sharp fall. What does a breadth thrust like this usually signal?

**A5.** A **breadth thrust** — sudden, broad, powerful participation off a low — is one of the more reliable *bullish* signals in technical analysis. When the vast majority of stocks advance strongly together after a decline, it reflects a genuine shift in demand across the whole market, not just index cosmetics. Historically such thrusts mark the *early* stage of a new up-leg, not the end. Contrast this with Q1: narrowing breadth at highs is bearish; broadening breadth off lows is bullish.

**Q6.** Chartink scan shows 320 stocks hitting a 52-week high but the McClellan-style breadth oscillator you track is negative. Reconcile.

**A6.** The 52-week-high count is a **raw, price-level** statistic; the oscillator measures the **rate of change of net advances**. You can have many stocks *at* highs (a legacy of a past strong move) while daily *net* advances are deteriorating (momentum fading). The reconciliation: leadership is intact but momentum is cooling. Read it as "trend up, thrust weakening" — stay long, stop pressing.

## Section B — Sentiment and derivatives (Q7–Q12)

**Q7.** Nifty PCR (put-call ratio, OI-based) is 0.55. Contrarian read?

**A7.** A **low PCR** (well below 1) means far more call open interest than put — the crowd is heavily positioned for upside / complacent. Contrarians read very low PCR as **over-optimism**, a caution flag for longs. But note: PCR is noisy and regime-dependent. In a strong trending market, low PCR can persist. Use it as a *confirming* sentiment gauge, never a standalone reversal trigger. Extreme readings (below ~0.5 or above ~1.5) carry more signal than middling ones.

**Q8.** India VIX jumps from 12 to 21 in two sessions while Nifty falls 3%. What does the VIX spike tell you about the *stage* of the move?

**A8.** A sharp VIX spike alongside a fall signals **fear entering the market** — options are being bid for protection. Historically, VIX *spikes* (not high VIX per se) often coincide with or slightly precede **short-term bottoms**, because peak fear marks capitulation. The actionable nuance: don't short into a VIX spike; that's late. Watch for VIX to *stop rising and start falling* while price stabilises — that turn is the tradeable signal.

**Q9.** India VIX has been sitting at 10–11 for six weeks. Trading implication?

**A9.** **Complacency and cheap options.** Very low VIX means implied volatility is compressed — option premiums are small. Two implications: (a) it's a poor environment for premium sellers (little juice, and any shock hurts), and a good one for *buyers* of cheap optionality; (b) prolonged ultra-low VIX often precedes a volatility expansion — the "calm before." It doesn't time the break, but it tells you to size for a possible regime shift.

**Q10.** Max Pain for weekly Nifty expiry sits at 25,500; spot is 25,850 on Wednesday. What, honestly, is Max Pain worth here?

**A10.** Max Pain (the strike where the most option buyers lose) is a **weak, often overstated** tool. There is a mild tendency for price to gravitate toward heavy-OI strikes near expiry due to hedging flows, but it is *not* a law, and using it as a directional forecast is a rookie error. Honest answer: note that 25,500 has heavy OI (a magnet/support-resistance shelf), but don't build a trade around price "must" going there. Real drivers (news, global cues) override Max Pain routinely.

**Q11.** FII index futures long-short ratio drops to 25% long / 75% short. Bias?

**A11.** FIIs are **net heavily short index futures** — a bearish positioning signal since FIIs are the dominant directional force in Indian index derivatives. Confirm with cash-market FII flows (are they selling equities too?). If FIIs are short futures *and* selling cash, the bias is genuinely bearish. Caveat: extreme short positioning can also be a *fuel tank* for a short-covering rally on any positive trigger — so know whether you're trading with the trend or fading an extreme.

**Q12.** Retail (client category) is the most net-long it's been all year in stock futures while FIIs are net short. Who do you side with?

**A12.** **Side with FIIs, be wary of retail.** The empirical, honest pattern in Indian F&O: the client/retail category is the least-informed, most often wrong-at-extremes cohort, while FIIs and to some extent proprietary desks carry the smart-money read. Retail record-long + FII short is a classic distribution-risk configuration. This isn't a guarantee — it's a probability tilt.

## Section C — Intermarket and macro (Q13–Q17)

**Q13.** USDINR breaks above 88.50 to a new high while Nifty is near its own high. Which sectors do you rotate toward and away from?

**A13.** A weakening rupee **helps exporters** — IT (TCS, Infosys, HCLTech) and pharma earn in dollars — and **hurts importers** and dollar-borrowers: oil marketing companies, aviation, companies with large USD debt, and it pressures FII flows (a falling rupee erodes FII dollar returns, sometimes triggering selling). Rotate toward IT/pharma; be cautious on OMCs, aviation, and high-USD-debt names. Also watch whether rupee weakness is *orderly* (manageable) or *disorderly* (risk-off, sell everything).

**Q14.** Brent crude spikes 8% in a week. First-order Indian market effects?

**A14.** India imports ~85% of its crude, so a crude spike is a **macro headwind**: worsens the current account, pressures the rupee, stokes inflation (limiting RBI rate cuts), and hurts oil-sensitive sectors — **paints, tyres, aviation, OMCs** (marketing margins squeezed if they can't pass through), and broadly consumption. Beneficiaries: **upstream** names like ONGC and Oil India (higher realisations). Net index effect is usually negative if the spike is sharp.

**Q15.** US 10-year Treasury yield jumps sharply and the DXY (dollar index) rallies. What's the typical reflex in Indian equities and why?

**A15.** Typically **risk-off for Indian equities**: a stronger dollar and higher US yields make emerging-market assets relatively less attractive, often triggering **FII outflows** from Indian equities and debt, pressuring both the rupee and stocks. Rate-sensitive, high-valuation sectors (mid/small caps, richly-valued growth) feel it most. The transmission is *flows*, not fundamentals — nothing changed about Indian companies overnight, but the cost of global capital did.

**Q16.** Gold (MCX) and Nifty are both rising together over two months. Is that unusual, and what does it mean?

**A16.** Not as unusual as textbooks imply. Gold and equities can rise together when the common driver is **ample liquidity / a weak currency / inflation hedging** rather than pure risk-on-risk-off. In India specifically, a weakening rupee lifts MCX gold (priced via USD × INR) even if global gold is flat. So co-movement here often reflects **rupee weakness and liquidity**, not a contradiction. The intermarket signal to watch is when gold *outperforms sharply while equities wobble* — that's defensive rotation.

**Q17.** Nifty IT index is diverging up while USDINR is *falling* (rupee strengthening). Does that break the export-rupee logic?

**A17.** It complicates but doesn't break it. Rupee weakness *helps* IT margins, but IT stock performance is driven more by **global tech demand, US client spending, and deal wins** than by the currency on any given swing. A strong-rupee + strong-IT combination usually means the **demand/earnings narrative is dominating** the currency tailwind — for example, a US tech-spending upcycle. Lesson: intermarket relationships are tendencies, not gears; the dominant driver rotates.

## Section D — Sector and stock TA (Q18–Q23)

**Q18.** Bank Nifty is at 58,000 and its ratio chart vs Nifty (BankNifty/Nifty) just turned up after months of decline. What does the ratio turning up mean for how you trade the two?

**A18.** A **rising ratio** means Bank Nifty is starting to *outperform* Nifty — leadership rotating into banks. Practically: prefer long setups in banks over broad Nifty longs; in a pairs sense, banks are the stronger leg. Ratio (relative-strength line) analysis strips out the market beta and isolates *who is leading*. A turn after months of underperformance can mark the start of a multi-week rotation.

**Q19.** A cyclical stock (say a metal name, Tata Steel) and a defensive (an FMCG name, HUL) — which leads at a market *top*, which at a *bottom*?

**A19.** **Cyclicals/high-beta lead at bottoms** (metals, real estate, PSU banks rip first off a low as risk appetite returns) and **defensives lead into tops** (money rotates to FMCG, pharma, utilities as the smart crowd de-risks while the index still rises). So: metals outperforming = early-cycle bullish; FMCG/pharma quietly outperforming while the index grinds up = late-cycle caution. This rotation read is one of the highest-value intermarket skills.

**Q20.** A Nifty stock gaps up 6% on results, then spends the whole day in a tight range near the high on huge volume. Bullish or bearish continuation odds?

**A20.** Generally **bullish**. A gap that holds — price consolidating *near the high* rather than filling the gap — on heavy volume signals **acceptance** of the higher prices (buyers absorbing supply without needing lower prices). The failure to fill is the tell. Contrast: a gap up that immediately sells off and fills is an **exhaustion/trap** gap. The location of the day's range relative to the gap is the key diagnostic.

**Q21.** In a stock, RSI is 78 (overbought) but price keeps trending up strongly. Do you short the overbought reading?

**A21.** **No.** Overbought is not a sell signal in a strong trend — it's a sign of *strength*. RSI can stay pinned above 70 for the entire duration of a powerful move (this is "good overbought"). Shorting strength is how accounts die. The tradeable RSI signal in a trend is **divergence** (price higher high, RSI lower high) plus a *price* confirmation (break of a swing low or trendline), not the absolute level alone.

**Q22.** A stock breaks a well-watched resistance at ₹1,200 on strong volume, then two days later closes back below ₹1,200. Name it and the play.

**A22.** A **failed breakout / bull trap**. Breakouts that reverse back below the breakout level trap the momentum buyers who chased. The play: failed moves often lead to **fast moves in the opposite direction** (Wyckoff's "upthrust"). A close back below ₹1,200 is a short trigger with a stop above the failed-breakout high; targets back toward the prior range low. Failed breakouts are among the higher-probability reversal setups precisely because they trap real money.

**Q23.** Two stocks in the same sector: one is above its rising 50-DMA, the other below its falling 50-DMA. You want one long. Which, and what's the principle?

**A23.** **Long the one above its rising 50-DMA** — the principle is *relative strength: buy the strongest, not the "cheapest" laggard hoping it catches up.* In trending environments, strength persists and weakness persists; the leader keeps leading more often than the laggard reverts. Bargain-hunting the weak name in a sector uptrend is a common, costly instinct.

## Section E — Risk, sizing, and execution (Q24–Q30)

**Q24.** Your setup has a 45% win rate and average win = 2R, average loss = 1R. Is it worth trading?

**A24.** **Yes.** Expectancy = (0.45 × 2R) − (0.55 × 1R) = 0.9R − 0.55R = **+0.35R per trade**. A positive expectancy of a third of your risk per trade, run over hundreds of trades, is a genuine edge. This is the core honest lesson: you do *not* need a high win rate; you need positive expectancy and the discipline to take enough trades for the math to express itself.

**Q25.** You risk 2% per trade and take a run of 5 losers. Drawdown, and how many wins at 2R to recover?

**A25.** Five 2% losses ≈ **~9.6% drawdown** (compounding: 0.98^5 ≈ 0.904). To recover ~10% you need roughly **+10.6%** of gains. At 2R (4% gain per winning trade on 2% risk), that's about **~3 clean winners**. Lesson: losses compound against you asymmetrically, which is exactly why position sizing (keeping per-trade risk modest) matters more than being right.

**Q26.** A stock is ₹500, your stop is ₹485, and you'll risk ₹10,000 on the trade. Position size?

**A26.** Risk per share = ₹500 − ₹485 = **₹15**. Shares = ₹10,000 ÷ ₹15 = **~666 shares** (round down to 650–660 for margin/slippage comfort). Position value ≈ ₹3.3 lakh. This is the single most important calculation in trading: **stop distance and rupee-risk determine size**, not "how much I want to buy."

**Q27.** You're up 3R on an intraday Bank Nifty trade at 2:15 pm. Your rule set has no trailing stop defined. What's the professional error and fix?

**A27.** The error is **discretionary management of a well-performing trade** — deciding on the fly whether to hold or book, which invites emotion and inconsistency. The fix: have a **pre-defined exit plan** (trail below the last 5-min swing low, or scale out half at 3R and trail the rest, or exit-on-close). Consistency of *management* is as important as consistency of *entry*. A defined rule executed mechanically beats a smart decision made anxiously.

**Q28.** Overnight-gap risk: you're long a midcap into an event (RBI policy). What position-size adjustment does honest risk management demand?

**A28.** Because a **stop cannot protect against a gap** through your level, your *effective* risk is not the stop distance — it's the plausible gap size. Honest management: **reduce size** (or hedge with an option) so that a realistic adverse gap (say 5–8% on a midcap around a big event) still costs no more than your intended risk. Sizing to your stop while ignoring gap risk is a classic way to take a 5% loss on a "1% risk" trade.

**Q29.** Which is the bigger long-run threat to a retail account: a single 20% loss, or consistent 1R risk with occasional 2R slippage?

**A29.** The **single 20% loss** — and the *behaviour* that allows it (no stop, averaging into a loser, oversizing). A 20% drawdown needs +25% to recover; a 50% drawdown needs +100%. The honest, uncomfortable truth: most retail accounts don't die from small edges being too small — they die from **one or two catastrophic, undisciplined losses** that mathematically and psychologically break the account. Survival first.

**Q30.** Final honesty check: a friend says he made 40% in three months trading options and wants you to copy him. What's the technically-literate response?

**A30.** Ask about **process and drawdown, not returns.** A 40% gain in three months on options usually means **large position sizes and high leverage** — which implies the drawdown when the regime turns will be equally violent. Three months is far too short to distinguish skill from a favourable trend and luck. The right questions: What was your max drawdown? Your risk per trade? Your win rate and expectancy over *hundreds* of trades? Most such runs are variance, and copying the sizing that produced the up-move is how people blow up on the down-move. **Most retail option buyers lose money over time** — that's the base rate to respect.

## How to drill this bank

Run it in three passes. **Pass 1:** answer aloud, check reasoning, mark right/wrong. **Pass 2 (next day):** re-run only the ones you missed. **Pass 3 (weekly):** shuffle all 30 and time yourself — under 12 minutes for the full set means the concepts are becoming reflexive, which is the point. In a live market or an interview, you won't have time to reason from first principles; you need these to be pattern-recognition, retrieved in seconds. The recurring meta-lesson across all five sections: **indicators are probabilistic, context-dependent tools — the professional edge is knowing which one dominates in the current regime, and sizing so that being wrong is survivable.**
