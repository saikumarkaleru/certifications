# Mock Test IV

Mock Test III trained the *decision* under a moving tape. Mock Test IV raises the difficulty in two ways. First, several questions are deliberately built as **traps** — the "obvious" technical answer is wrong, and the mark is for the trader who sees the second-order effect. Second, this paper leans harder into **multi-instrument reasoning**: F&O positioning, expiry mechanics, intermarket chains and portfolio-level thinking, because that is where experienced Indian-market interviews actually probe. Everything remains India-specific and 2026-realistic.

**How to sit this paper.** 80 minutes, 20 questions, 2 marks each (1 for the call, 1 for the reason). Write your reason *first*, then your call — it forces you to reason rather than guess and back-fill. **34–40:** you are ready to trade the concepts, not just recite them. **26–33:** competent, gaps to close. **Below 26:** you are still answering from the surface of the chart; the debrief tells you where to dig.

## Section A — Sentiment, Positioning & Expiry (Q1–Q6)

**Q1.** It is Nifty weekly expiry Thursday. Max open interest on the call side is at 26,000 (huge OI), and max put OI is at 25,800. Nifty is trading at 25,910 at 1 pm. Explain the "max-pain / OI wall" reasoning and give the realistic expectation for the close — then state the one condition under which the walls *break* instead of hold.

**Q2.** Nifty futures are trading at a premium of 95 points to spot (spot 25,900, futures 25,995) with 12 days to expiry, versus a typical premium of ~30 points. Rollover to next series is high. What does an unusually high futures premium plus high rollover tell you about positioning, and what is the contrarian risk?

**Q3.** India VIX jumps from 12 to 19 in two sessions ahead of the RBI monetary policy and Union Budget week. A trader says "VIX spiked, sell the fear, short volatility." Identify why this is often a trap around scheduled events and what the professional does instead.

**Q4.** Bank Nifty options show call writers aggressively adding at 57,000 while put writers add at 56,000, compressing the expected range. Bank Nifty sits at 56,500 mid-week. What is this positioning called, what range does it imply, and what is the best *non-directional* structure to express it?

**Q5.** The Nifty PCR is 1.7 (high), India VIX is at a low 11.5, and the index is at an all-time high on narrowing breadth. A junior reads PCR as bullish and VIX as safe and goes max-long. List the three signals in conflict and give the correct synthesized bias.

**Q6.** On expiry day, a stock in the F&O ban list (crossed 95% of market-wide position limit) cannot take fresh positions. You spot a clean breakout on that stock intraday. What are the two specific execution/behavioral risks of trading a ban-period name, and what does the ban itself signal about crowding?

## Section B — Intermarket & Sector Chains (Q7–Q12)

**Q7.** Brent crude jumps from $78 to $91 in three weeks. Trace the intermarket chain to at least three Indian instruments/sectors and give the net directional bias for Nifty overall. (This is a chain question — partial credit for each correct link.)

**Q8.** US 10-year Treasury yield spikes from 4.1% to 4.7%. The dollar index (DXY) strengthens. Trace the effect on (a) FII flows into Indian equities, (b) USDINR, and (c) rate-sensitive Nifty sectors like Realty and Auto. Give the net bias for Nifty.

**Q9.** Nifty Metal is surging: Tata Steel, JSW Steel, Hindalco all breaking out. Simultaneously the Chinese stimulus news is dominating headlines and LME copper is at a 52-week high. Is this a *sustainable* sector trade or a *headline* pop, and what single confirmation separates the two?

**Q10.** Nifty FMCG (Hindustan Unilever, Nestlé, ITC) is outperforming a falling Nifty — the FMCG/Nifty ratio line is rising sharply. What macro regime does defensive-sector leadership typically mark, and how should it change your overall book posture?

**Q11.** Gold (MCX) and Nifty are *both* rising together over two months — unusual, since they often diverge. What two macro conditions can make gold and equities rise in tandem in India, and what does it tell you to watch for as the risk?

**Q12.** DXY is falling, USDINR is falling (rupee strong), crude is soft, and US yields are easing. Name the "risk-on for emerging markets" setup this describes, the Nifty sectors that benefit most, and the one Indian sector that is hurt.

## Section C — Risk, Sizing & Portfolio (Q13–Q17)

**Q13.** Your system wins 40% of trades with an average winner of 2.5R and average loser of 1R. Compute the expectancy per trade in R, and state whether this is a tradable edge. Then explain why a 40% win rate feels psychologically awful despite being profitable.

**Q14.** You want to risk 1% of a ₹20,00,000 account on a Bank Nifty futures trade. Entry 56,600, technical stop 56,200 (400 points). Bank Nifty lot size is 35, and the point value is ₹1 per point per unit. How many lots, and what is the margin-vs-risk distinction a beginner confuses here?

**Q15.** Two strategies: Strategy A returns 30% a year with a 40% max drawdown; Strategy B returns 18% a year with a 12% max drawdown. A trader picks A "because higher return." Using a risk-adjusted lens, explain why B may be the superior *tradable* system in practice, especially with leverage and human psychology involved.

**Q16.** You are up 8% on the month with three days left. You have two strong open positions and itch to add five more "to make it a great month." Name the specific behavioral trap, the account-level risk it creates, and the rule a disciplined trader applies to end-of-period euphoria.

**Q17.** A trader uses a fixed ₹5,000 stop on every trade regardless of the instrument's volatility — same rupee stop on a calm large-cap and on a wild Adani/PSU name. Explain the two things this breaks (position sizing logic and stop placement logic) and the volatility-based fix.

## Section D — Chart-Reading Traps (Q18–Q20)

**Q18. (Trap.)** SBI breaks below a well-defined support at ₹820 on a 5% down day, volume 3× average. A breakout-short trader shorts the breakdown. The next day SBI closes back above ₹820 on strong volume. Name the pattern, explain why the initial short was reasonable but the *reaction* is what matters, and give the trade the reversal now offers.

**Q19. (Trap.)** Infosys shows a "golden cross" — the 50-DMA crosses above the 200-DMA. A trader buys purely on the golden cross. Price is already 15% above the 200-DMA and RSI is 78. What is the timing flaw of acting on a moving-average cross in isolation, and when is a golden cross actually most useful?

**Q20. (Trap.)** Nifty prints a textbook bullish "hammer" candle at a support level — but it forms on a day when *volume was 40% below average* and it is the third hammer at the same level in two weeks, each of which failed. A trader buys the hammer by the book. What two contextual filters override the textbook signal, and what does repeated failure at one level foreshadow?

## ANSWER KEY — Section A

**A1.** The **OI-wall / max-pain** reasoning: heavy call writing at 26,000 and heavy put writing at 25,800 create "walls" — option sellers (who dominate volume and have the incentive and capital) tend to defend their strikes so that the maximum number of options expire worthless, pinning price toward the max-pain point between the walls. Realistic expectation: an expiry **close pinned inside 25,800–26,000**, often gravitating toward the higher-OI strike. The condition under which walls *break*: a **large exogenous shock or trending futures flow** (a news event, aggressive institutional directional buying) that overwhelms the writers and forces them to unwind/hedge, turning a pin into a squeeze. Walls are probabilities, not guarantees — they hold on quiet days and shatter on event days.

**A2.** An unusually high futures premium (95 vs typical 30) plus high rollover signals **aggressive, leveraged long positioning** — traders are so bullish they pay up to hold longs and roll them forward. It reflects crowded optimism. The contrarian risk: **crowded longs are fuel for a sharp unwind.** When everyone is leveraged-long, any disappointment triggers cascading long liquidation (margin-driven selling), and the premium can collapse toward or below spot violently. High premium = high complacency = elevated reversal risk. It is a caution flag on new longs, not a green light.

**A3.** It is a trap because **VIX rising before a scheduled event (RBI policy, Budget) is not irrational fear — it is correct pricing of a known binary risk.** Selling that volatility means you are short-vega and short-gamma into a scheduled catalyst that can gap the market hard; the "fat premium" is compensation for real event risk, and the payoff is asymmetric against you. The professional either (a) **stays out of short-vol until after the event** and sells the *post-event volatility crush*, or (b) uses **strictly defined-risk structures** (iron condor/fly) sized for a gap. You do not sell naked vol into a scheduled event because the market is "fearful."

**A4.** This is a **short strangle / range-compression by option writers** (call writers at 57,000, put writers at 56,000). It implies an **expected trading range of roughly 56,000–57,000** into expiry, i.e. writers are betting Bank Nifty stays boxed. The best non-directional structure to align with it: a **short iron condor** — sell the 57,000 call and 56,000 put, buy wings further out for defined risk — profiting from time decay if Bank Nifty stays in the box, with capped loss if it breaks out. You express "range-bound" without betting direction and without naked risk.

**A5.** The three conflicting signals: (1) **PCR 1.7** (high) reads superficially bullish/contrarian but at an all-time high can also mean heavy hedging; (2) **VIX 11.5** (low) = complacency, the *opposite* of a safe fear-washout; (3) **all-time high on narrowing breadth** = internal weakness. Synthesis: this is a **late-stage, complacent, narrow rally — a distribution-risk zone, not a max-long zone.** Correct bias: **cautious, trim longs, hedge, do not add leverage.** The junior mistook complacency (low VIX) for safety and a hedging-driven PCR for conviction. Two of the three signals are actually warnings.

**A6.** Two execution/behavioral risks of trading an **F&O-ban** name: (1) **you cannot add fresh futures/options positions — only reduce** — so your ability to manage, scale or hedge is crippled, and (2) **extreme volatility and gap risk**, because ban-period stocks are already at crowding extremes and unwind violently, with poor liquidity and wide spreads making stops unreliable. The ban itself signals **dangerous crowding** — market-wide open interest has hit 95% of the limit, meaning the trade is maximally consensus; consensus-maxed positions are precisely where sharp reversals originate. A "clean breakout" in a ban name is a low-quality, high-risk signal.

## ANSWER KEY — Section B

**A7.** The crude-up chain (India imports ~85% of its oil): (1) **OMCs — BPCL, HPCL, IOC — bearish** (marketing margins squeezed if pump prices are held); (2) **paints/tyres/aviation — Asian Paints, Berger, MRF, InterGlobe (IndiGo) — bearish** (crude derivatives / ATF are key input costs); (3) **ONGC / Oil India — bullish** (upstream producers gain on higher realizations); (4) **USDINR — up / rupee weak** (larger oil import bill widens the current-account deficit); (5) **inflation up → rate-cut hopes fade → rate-sensitives (Realty, Auto, Banks) pressured.** Net bias for **Nifty: negative** — India is a large net oil importer, so a crude spike is a macro headwind. Full credit for three or more correct links plus the net.

**A8.** (a) **FII flows: outflow** — higher US yields + strong dollar make US assets more attractive on a risk-adjusted basis, pulling foreign money out of emerging-market equities including India. (b) **USDINR: up (rupee weakens)** as FIIs sell rupee assets and buy dollars, and DXY strength adds pressure. (c) **Rate-sensitive sectors (Realty, Auto): bearish** — higher global yields feed domestic rate expectations and raise financing costs; high-duration, leverage-dependent sectors suffer most. Net **Nifty bias: negative** — the combination of FII outflows, a weaker rupee and rate pressure is a classic risk-off setup for Indian equities.

**A9.** This can be **either**, and the distinction matters. A headline pop on Chinese stimulus can spike metal stocks for days and fade; a sustainable trade needs the *commodity itself* to confirm. The single confirmation that separates them: **the underlying metal price (LME/MCX) making and holding higher highs alongside the stocks.** If Tata Steel and JSW are rising *and* LME steel/copper prices are in a confirmed uptrend with volume, it is a real cyclical move — trade it with trend rules. If the stocks are ripping but the underlying metal is flat or fading, it is a **headline pop to fade/avoid.** Metals stocks without metals-price confirmation are a trap.

**A10.** Defensive-sector (FMCG) leadership over a falling Nifty marks a **risk-off / late-cycle or corrective regime** — money rotates from cyclicals and high-beta into stable-cashflow, low-beta consumer staples for safety. A rising FMCG/Nifty ratio is a **flight-to-safety tell.** Book posture: **reduce high-beta and cyclical longs, raise cash, tilt toward defensives, tighten stops overall.** Defensive leadership is not a reason to be aggressively long the market — it is the market voting cautious, and you follow the vote.

**A11.** Gold and equities can rise together in India under: (1) **falling real interest rates / easy liquidity** — cheap money lifts *all* assets, gold and stocks together (a "liquidity tide"); and (2) **a weakening rupee** — since MCX gold is priced in rupees, a falling INR mechanically lifts domestic gold even at flat dollar-gold, while equities may rise for their own reasons. The risk to watch: **when the tide is liquidity-driven, both can also fall together** when liquidity tightens (a rate-hike surprise), removing gold's usual hedge benefit. Correlated up-moves warn that your "diversification" may be fake in a drawdown.

**A12.** This is the classic **"risk-on for emerging markets"** setup — weak dollar, strong rupee, soft crude, easing yields all pull global capital *into* EM equities like India. Sectors that benefit most: **Banks/Financials** (FII favorites, benefit from inflows and easing rates), **Realty and Auto** (rate-sensitive, love falling yields), and **capital-goods/infra** (risk-on cyclicals); a soft rupee-positive crude helps **OMCs and importers.** The one sector *hurt*: **Nifty IT** (and to a degree pharma) — a *strengthening* rupee erodes exporters' rupee revenue. Net **Nifty bias: bullish**, led by financials.

## ANSWER KEY — Section C

**A13.** Expectancy = (Win% × avg win) − (Loss% × avg loss) = (0.40 × 2.5R) − (0.60 × 1R) = **1.0R − 0.6R = +0.4R per trade.** That is a **strong, tradable edge** — you net 0.4R on average every trade; over 100 trades that is +40R. Why it *feels* awful: at a 40% win rate you **lose more often than you win** and will routinely string together 4–6 consecutive losers, so the emotional experience is dominated by frequent small losses punctuated by occasional big wins. Most traders cannot sit through the losing streaks and abandon a profitable system precisely because it is psychologically uncomfortable, not because it is mathematically weak.

**A14.** Risk budget = 1% × ₹20,00,000 = **₹20,000.** Risk per lot = 400 points × 35 (lot size) × ₹1 = **₹14,000 per lot.** Lots = ₹20,000 ÷ ₹14,000 = 1.43 → **1 lot** (round down). The margin-vs-risk confusion: a beginner thinks position size is limited by **margin** (how many lots the broker lets him fund, which for Bank Nifty futures might allow several lots on ₹20 lakh). But size must be governed by **risk** (the stop distance × point value), which here caps him at 1 lot. Margin tells you what you *can* buy; risk tells you what you *should* buy. Confusing the two is how leverage kills accounts.

**A15.** Risk-adjusted, **B is likely the superior tradable system.** Return-per-unit-of-drawdown (a MAR-style ratio): A = 30/40 = **0.75**; B = 18/12 = **1.5** — B is twice as efficient per unit of pain. In practice this matters more than headline return because: (1) a **40% drawdown requires a ~67% gain just to recover** (drawdowns are asymmetric), (2) with leverage a 40% drawdown risks a margin call / ruin before recovery, and (3) **humans abandon systems in deep drawdowns** — a trader is far more likely to actually *stick with* B through its shallow 12% dips and compound the 18%, than to survive A's gut-wrenching 40% pit. The best system is the one you can *execute*, not the one with the highest backtest CAGR.

**A16.** The trap is **house-money / end-of-period euphoria** — being up 8% makes gains feel like "the market's money," lowering risk discipline exactly when you should protect a good month. The account risk: **adding five positions multiplies exposure and correlation right when you're least vigilant**, and a single bad session can erase the month's edge and turn +8% into flat or negative. The disciplined rule: **your risk-per-trade and max concurrent risk do not change because you are up** — profits do not buy extra risk. Many pros actually *reduce* size after a strong run to protect the result. Protect the month; do not gamble it.

**A17.** A flat ₹5,000 stop across instruments of different volatility breaks two things. (1) **Position-sizing logic:** proper sizing derives the number of units from the *stop distance* and risk budget; a fixed rupee stop with a fixed size means you are risking wildly different *technical* amounts on each name. (2) **Stop-placement logic:** a stop must sit beyond the instrument's normal noise — a wild PSU/Adani name whips ₹5,000 in an hour and stops you out on noise, while on a calm large-cap ₹5,000 may be too wide and beyond any logical level. The fix: **volatility-based (ATR) stops** — place the stop at a multiple of the instrument's Average True Range (e.g. 2×ATR beyond the level), then *size the position* so that ATR-based stop equals your fixed risk budget. Stop distance adapts to the instrument; rupee risk stays constant.

## ANSWER KEY — Section D

**A18. (Trap.)** This is a **failed breakdown / bear trap (spring below support).** The initial short was reasonable — a 5% break of clear support on 3× volume is a textbook breakdown trigger. But the *reaction* is what carries information: a close back **above ₹820 on strong volume the next day** means the breakdown failed and trapped shorts, who must now cover, adding fuel. Failed moves lead to fast moves in the opposite direction. The trade the reversal offers: **go long on the reclaim of ₹820, stop below the breakdown-day low, target the top of the prior range** — because trapped shorts + reclaimed support is one of the highest-conviction long setups. The lesson: a breakout/breakdown is only as good as its *follow-through*; the reclaim invalidates the short and creates the opposite trade.

**A19. (Trap.)** The timing flaw: a **golden cross is a lagging signal built from 50- and 200-day averages** — by the time it triggers, price is often already far extended (here 15% above the 200-DMA, RSI 78), so buying *on the cross* means buying into an overbought, late move with a terrible entry and a distant stop. Moving-average crosses confirm a *trend that already began*; they are not timing tools. A golden cross is **most useful as a regime filter** — after a long downtrend/base, an *early* cross near the 200-DMA (price not yet extended) marks a genuine trend change, and as a *context* filter it tells you to favor longs. The right action here is to **wait for a pullback toward the rising averages**, not chase the cross at RSI 78.

**A20. (Trap.)** Two contextual filters override the textbook hammer: (1) **volume** — a reversal candle on 40%-below-average volume lacks the participation needed to confirm real demand; a hammer *needs* a volume surge to be trusted; and (2) **prior failures at the same level** — this is the *third* hammer that failed in two weeks, so the pattern has no predictive value here; the level keeps producing hammers that don't hold. Repeated failure of a support level foreshadows a **breakdown**: each retest that can't rally consumes buyers, and support that is tested too many times tends to *break* rather than hold (sellers are absorbing every bounce). Buying the third low-volume hammer at a failing level is trading the pattern's *form* while ignoring its *context* — the exact mistake this test is built to expose.

## Scoring & Debrief

Tally 40 marks. **34–40:** you are reading second-order effects and resisting the obvious wrong answer — trade-ready thinking. **26–33:** you have the tools but the traps caught you; note *which* trap type (positioning, intermarket chain, sizing, or chart context) and drill it. **Below 26:** you are still answering the surface pattern; the recurring lesson below is your homework.

**Interview-ready summary.** Mock IV's spine is **context beats pattern, and the crowd's positioning is a risk, not a comfort.** Every trap punished a trader who acted on the textbook signal while ignoring the surrounding reality: a golden cross at RSI 78, a hammer on dead volume at a thrice-failed level, a breakdown short with no follow-through, crowded longs at a fat futures premium, low VIX read as safety, a metals rip with no metal-price confirmation. The experienced Indian-market trader's edge is not knowing *more* patterns — it is asking, for every signal, "what is the volume, the breadth, the flow, the intermarket driver and the crowding *behind* this?" That single habit converts textbook knowledge into an actual edge.
