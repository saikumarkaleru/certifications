# Cognitive Biases Catalog II

Catalog I covered the eight biases that most directly touch the trigger finger — loss aversion, the disposition effect, confirmation, recency, anchoring, overconfidence, sunk cost and the gambler's fallacy. This second catalog covers a different, subtler class: biases that corrupt how you **learn** from the market, how you **perceive risk**, and how you're **influenced by the crowd**. These are quieter — they don't blow up an account in a single afternoon — but over months they warp your entire trading education, so that ten years of experience becomes one year repeated ten times.

We cover eight: availability bias, hindsight bias, the framing effect, herd behaviour, self-attribution bias, the endowment effect, the narrative fallacy, and the illusion of control. Same structure as before — mechanism, how it shows up on an Indian trading screen, a worked example, and a counter-protocol — followed by a section on how these biases interact and compound, because in the wild they never arrive alone.

## Bias 9 — Availability bias

**The mechanism.** We judge how likely something is by how easily examples come to mind. Vivid, recent, emotionally charged events feel more probable than they are, and boring statistical base rates feel less real than a single dramatic story.

**On the NSE screen.** After a stock gaps down 20% on a fraud disclosure, every trader suddenly "sees" fraud risk everywhere and avoids perfectly good setups. After one friend makes 10x on a weekly option lottery ticket, that vivid story makes far-OTM buying feel like a reasonable strategy — even though the base rate is that the vast majority of such options expire worthless. Availability replaces probability with memorability.

**Worked example.** In the week after a sharp gap-down in a large financial stock, you refuse three clean long setups in banking names because the *image* of that gap is so available. All three work. Meanwhile the vivid memory of a colleague's ₹5,000-to-₹2-lakh expiry-day trade pulls you into buying deep-OTM Bank Nifty calls every Thursday. You remember his one win; you don't "see" the twenty silent losses (his or yours), because losses that bleed ₹5,000 quietly aren't vivid. Over a quarter the lottery habit costs you ₹80,000 — the base rate always wins in the end.

**Counter-protocol.**
- **Trade base rates, not stories.** Backtest so you know the actual hit rate and expectancy of a setup, and let that number — not the last dramatic candle — drive your decision.
- **Keep a full log, including the silent losses.** The reason the lottery feels good is you only remember the wins. A complete journal makes the base rate available again.
- **Ask: "Is this vivid or is this likely?"** When an event feels scary or thrilling, that's the cue to check the statistics rather than the feeling.

## Bias 10 — Hindsight bias

**The mechanism.** After an outcome is known, the brain rewrites memory so it feels like we "knew it all along." The past looks more predictable than it was, which destroys our ability to learn honestly from it.

**On the NSE screen.** "It was obvious Nifty would fall from 25,000 — the divergence was right there." It was not obvious in real time; you're editing the memory now that you know the ending. Hindsight bias makes you overrate your foresight (feeding overconfidence) and, worse, makes you punish yourself for "missing the obvious," which erodes confidence and process trust.

**Worked example.** You take a valid long at Nifty 24,600 with a sound setup; it stops out for a planned ₹15,000 loss and then Nifty falls to 24,200. Reviewing that evening, hindsight whispers: "The lower high was so clear, I should have shorted, not gone long — how did I miss it?" But the lower high only became clear *after* the drop. You beat yourself up for a process-correct trade, and worse, you resolve to "trust your gut about reversals next time" — which means abandoning your system for hindsight-flattered intuition. That's how a good trader talks himself into a worse process.

**Counter-protocol.**
- **Journal the thesis at entry, before the outcome.** Written pre-trade notes are the only defence against memory rewriting. Review them and grade the *decision* against what you knew then, not what you know now.
- **Grade process, not outcome.** A well-taken trade that loses is a good trade; a reckless trade that wins is a bad trade. Score the decision quality independent of the P&L.
- **Ban the phrase "I knew it."** If you catch yourself saying it, ask: "Where did I write that down beforehand?" Usually nowhere.

## Bias 11 — The framing effect

**The mechanism.** The same fact produces different decisions depending on how it's worded. "90% of surgeries succeed" vs "10% die" are identical numbers, opposite feelings. Losses and gains framed differently trigger different risk appetites.

**On the NSE screen.** A position framed as "up ₹5,000" makes you risk-averse (grab the gain); the *same* position framed as "₹15,000 below my target" makes you patient. Brokers frame margin as "buying power" (a gain frame) to encourage leverage. A ₹2,000 brokerage-and-tax bill framed per-trade feels trivial; framed as "₹40,000 a month in costs" it changes your whole trade frequency.

**Worked example.** You're offered the same trade two ways in your own head. Frame A: "I can make ₹20,000 here." You size up, excited. Frame B: "I'm risking ₹15,000 to make ₹20,000, and my hit rate on this setup is 45%." Same trade — but Frame B, which surfaces the risk and base rate, leads you to correct sizing and sometimes to skipping a marginal setup. Traders who only ever hear Frame A in their heads systematically over-risk, because the reward frame is the one that feels good.

**Counter-protocol.**
- **Always frame in risk terms first.** State every trade as "risking ₹X to make ₹Y at Z% probability." The R-multiple frame is bias-resistant.
- **Convert costs to monthly/annual totals.** Reframe brokerage, STT and slippage as a running yearly number so they stop feeling free.
- **Standardise the frame.** A fixed pre-trade checklist forces the same neutral framing on every setup, removing the emotional wording.

## Bias 12 — Herd behaviour

**The mechanism.** Humans are wired to follow the crowd; safety in numbers was survival on the savanna. In markets the herd feels informed and its momentum feels like confirmation, but crowds are most unanimous exactly at tops and bottoms.

**On the NSE screen.** FinTwit euphoria, Telegram tip groups all screaming the same small-cap, "everyone" long the same expiry trade — herding pulls you in at the worst price, late, with a crowded stop that becomes fuel for the reversal. The Indian smallcap/SME manias and the operator-driven pump groups are herd behaviour weaponised against retail.

**Worked example.** A smallcap has run 40% in six sessions; three Telegram groups you follow are posting targets 30% higher. Herd instinct — plus FOMO and availability from remembering the last group win — pulls you in at ₹340. The move was the smart money distributing to the herd; the stock tops at ₹352 the next morning and slides to ₹250 over two weeks. You bought the unanimity. When a trade feels crowded and obvious and "everyone knows," you are usually the late money providing the exit liquidity for whoever's been holding since ₹200.

**Counter-protocol.**
- **Fade unanimity, or at least don't chase it.** When sentiment is uniformly one-sided, treat it as a risk signal, not confirmation.
- **Enter on your signal, not on the crowd's excitement.** If you missed the clean entry, the trade is gone — chasing is herding.
- **Mute tip groups during the trading day.** Make your decision from the chart and your plan before you look at what the crowd is doing.

## Bias 13 — Self-attribution bias

**The mechanism.** We attribute wins to our skill and losses to bad luck (or the market, or news, or SEBI). This one-sided accounting means we never learn from losses — they were "not our fault" — while overlearning from lucky wins.

**On the NSE screen.** A trade that wins on a random news pop becomes "my great read"; a well-reasoned trade that loses becomes "the market was manipulated / that gap-down was rigged." Over time self-attribution builds a false self-image of a skilled trader who's just been unlucky, immune to the feedback that would actually improve you.

**Worked example.** You buy a stock on a weak setup; a surprise upgrade the next morning gaps it up 4% and you book ₹30,000. You credit your "eye for value." A week later you take a textbook-perfect breakout that fails on a broad-market selloff for a ₹15,000 loss; you blame "FII selling, nothing I could do." Both attributions are wrong: the win was luck (bad process, good outcome) and the loss was fine process (good process, bad outcome). By crediting yourself for the lucky win and disowning the well-executed loss, you reinforce the weak setup and lose faith in the good one — learning exactly backwards.

**Counter-protocol.**
- **Separate process quality from outcome in your journal** — two independent scores. This is the single most powerful anti-attribution tool.
- **Do a "luck audit" on winners.** For each win ask: "Did this work because of my process, or did something random save me?" Honest wins and lucky wins get logged differently.
- **Own the losses that were process-correct.** Reframe them as evidence the process is being followed, not as failures — that protects both learning and confidence.

## Bias 14 — The endowment effect

**The mechanism.** We value things more simply because we own them. A stock in your portfolio feels more valuable and lower-risk than the identical stock you don't own, purely because of ownership.

**On the NSE screen.** You defend a holding you'd never buy today. Ask yourself "would I buy this now at this price?" and if the answer is no, the endowment effect is the only reason you still hold it. It's especially strong in long-term "investment" positions that quietly became bad trades but feel like part of your identity.

**Worked example.** You hold 300 shares of a PSU stock bought two years ago at ₹180, now ₹165, thesis long broken. A friend asks "would you buy it today?" — instantly, no. Yet you hold, because selling *your* shares feels like a loss in a way that simply not buying never would. The endowment effect keeps ₹49,500 of capital dead in a name you'd reject as a fresh buy, quarter after quarter, while better trends run without you.

**Counter-protocol.**
- **The ownership-neutral test:** "If I had this money in cash today, would I buy this position right now?" If no, sell. Ownership is not a reason to hold.
- **Review the portfolio as if you inherited it flat.** Imagine you woke up in cash and had to rebuild — which of your current holdings survive? Sell the rest.
- **Separate identity from positions.** You are not your trades. A stock is a vehicle, not a family member.

## Bias 15 — The narrative fallacy

**The mechanism.** The mind craves stories. We string random events into a causal tale because a coherent narrative feels like understanding — even when the events were noise. Markets generate endless data that we compulsively wrap in explanations.

**On the NSE screen.** "Nifty fell because of the US jobs data" — a story invented after the fact to explain a move that also had a dozen other inputs and plenty of randomness. Trading on narratives ("this sector is the next big theme, so it must go up") substitutes a satisfying story for probability and price.

**Worked example.** You build a compelling story: EV adoption is booming, so a particular auto-ancillary must rocket. The narrative is so satisfying you buy at ₹600 with no technical setup, ignoring that the stock is in a clear downtrend below all its moving averages. The story is true *and* the stock keeps falling to ₹480 — because a good macro narrative can coexist with terrible price action for a long time, and you paid for the story, not the chart. The narrative gave you conviction exactly where you needed doubt.

**Counter-protocol.**
- **Trade the chart, use the narrative only for context.** A story is not an entry signal; price and structure are. Never let a narrative override what the chart is doing.
- **Distrust the tidy explanation.** When the market move has one clean cause, be suspicious — reality is usually messier and more random.
- **Demand a technical trigger.** However good the story, no position without a valid setup, stop and target.

## Bias 16 — The illusion of control

**The mechanism.** We overestimate how much we influence random outcomes. Rolling dice harder, choosing our own lottery numbers, watching a position tick-by-tick — all create a false sense that our attention affects the result.

**On the NSE screen.** Staring at the chart every second doesn't change the outcome, but it *feels* like control — and it feeds overtrading, premature exits (you "see" every wiggle and react), and the fantasy that more screen time equals more influence. Excessive tinkering with stops and targets mid-trade is the illusion of control in action.

**Worked example.** You have a clean swing trade with a stop and a 5-day target. But watching it tick-by-tick, every red candle feels like something you must *do* something about. You tighten the stop (illusion: "managing risk"), get shaken out of a normal pullback for a ₹8,000 loss, and watch the trade go on to hit your original target without you. Your constant intervention — which felt like control — was the thing that cost you the winner. The market moved on its own; your fiddling only removed you from a good position.

**Counter-protocol.**
- **Set it and step away.** For swing trades, place the stop and target as orders and reduce screen time. You cannot influence the outcome by watching.
- **Distinguish what you control from what you don't.** You control entry, size, stop and exit rules. You do not control the outcome. Focus energy only on the former.
- **A rule against mid-trade tinkering.** No moving stops or targets once set, except by pre-defined trailing rules. This kills the "do something" itch.

## How these biases compound

In the wild, biases hunt in packs, and their combinations are more dangerous than any single one:

- **Overconfidence + self-attribution** creates the classic doomed arc: a lucky win (credited to skill) breeds overconfidence, which breeds oversizing, which breeds a blow-up — and the blow-up is then blamed on the market, so nothing is learned and the cycle repeats larger.
- **Availability + herding + narrative fallacy** is the anatomy of every retail mania: a vivid success story (availability) plus a compelling theme (narrative) plus everyone buying (herd) pulls you in at the top.
- **Loss aversion + sunk cost + endowment** is how a small planned loss becomes a portfolio-wrecking dead position: you can't take the loss (aversion), you've already committed so much (sunk cost), and it's *your* stock now (endowment).
- **Hindsight + recency** quietly rots your strategy discipline: recent losses feel decisive (recency) and the "obvious" thing you "should have done" (hindsight) tempts you to abandon a working system at the worst moment.

The defence against the pack is the same as against any single bias, only more so: **externalise your decisions into a written, rules-based process built when you are calm, and let that process — not your in-the-moment brain — execute.** A pre-written plan with defined entries, stops, targets, size and invalidations doesn't feel any of these biases. That is why the disciplined mechanical trader, with a mediocre edge, routinely beats the brilliant discretionary trader whose superior read is filtered through sixteen biases before it reaches the P&L.

## Interview-ready summary

- **Availability bias** trades memorable stories over statistical base rates; fix by logging silent losses and trading backtested hit rates.
- **Hindsight bias** rewrites memory into "I knew it all along," destroying honest learning; fix by journaling the thesis *before* the outcome and grading process, not P&L.
- **Framing effect** changes risk appetite based on wording; always frame trades as "risking ₹X to make ₹Y at Z% probability."
- **Herd behaviour** pulls you in at tops and bottoms when sentiment is unanimous; enter on your signal, not the crowd's excitement, and treat unanimity as a risk flag.
- **Self-attribution bias** credits wins to skill and losses to luck, so you never learn; separate process and outcome scores and run a luck audit on winners.
- **Endowment effect** makes you defend a holding you'd never buy today; use the "would I buy this now in cash?" test.
- **Narrative fallacy** substitutes a satisfying story for price; trade the chart, use the story only for context, and always demand a technical trigger.
- **Illusion of control** turns screen-watching into overtrading and shakeouts; set stop and target, then step away.
- **Biases compound in packs** — overconfidence+attribution, availability+herd+narrative, loss-aversion+sunk-cost+endowment — and the universal defence is a written, rules-based process built when calm and executed mechanically.
