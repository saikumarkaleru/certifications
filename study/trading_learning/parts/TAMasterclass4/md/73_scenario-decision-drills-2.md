# Scenario Decision Drills II

The first drill set trained your reflexes on single-instrument, single-decision moments. This second set raises the difficulty: multi-factor scenarios where breadth, sector rotation, intermarket signals, position management, and portfolio-level thinking all collide. Real trading is rarely one clean chart — it's a stock breaking out *while* the sector lags *while* the index is at resistance *while* you already hold two correlated positions. The right answer emerges only when you weigh several signals against each other and rank them.

These drills also press harder on the *management* side — what to do once you're already in a trade and the situation evolves. Most retail P&L is lost not on entries but on the messy middle: adds, trims, stop adjustments, and the moment a thesis quietly stops being true. As before: commit to a decision and a number *before* reading the key, and grade your reasoning, not just your outcome.

## Ground rules for set II

- **Weigh, don't just spot.** When two signals conflict, say which one wins and why. "Bullish stock, bearish index" needs a ranking, not a shrug.
- **Think in portfolio, not ticket.** Correlation, gross exposure, and how a new trade interacts with what you already hold now count.
- **Respect the Indian microstructure.** Circuit limits, expiry, F&O ban lists, liquidity gaps in midcaps, and STT on the sell side all shape the correct action.
- **A pass is 70%+ with clean reasoning.** The scenarios are deliberately grey; the skill is choosing the *higher-probability* action, not a mythical certainty.

## Drill 1 — Stock breaks out, index at resistance

**Scenario.** 10:15 AM. Titan prints a clean breakout above a multi-week base at 3,650 on 2x volume — genuinely strong. But at the same moment, Nifty is pressing into a major resistance at 24,450 that has capped it four times, and Nifty's own 15-min candle looks tired with a long upper wick. You want the Titan long. What do you do?

**Options:** (A) Take the Titan breakout at full size. (B) Take it at reduced size with a tight stop. (C) Skip it — the index overhead is a red flag. (D) Short Titan instead.

**Answer: B.** This is a *conflict* scenario, and the resolution is to respect the stronger, more specific signal while hedging against the weaker, broader one. Titan's breakout is stock-specific, confirmed by volume, and high quality — that's your edge. But **individual stocks rarely fight the index for long**: if Nifty rejects 24,450 and rolls over, even strong stocks get dragged down. So you take the trade — the setup is real — but at *reduced size* with a *tight stop* below the breakout level (3,635), accepting that index weakness could abort it. **A (full size)** ignores a legitimate market-wide headwind. **C (skipping)** is over-cautious — a stock-specific breakout can absolutely run even as the index stalls, and you'd forgo a real edge. **D (shorting the strongest chart on the screen)** is nonsensical trend-fighting. Reduced size is how you express "high-conviction setup, uncertain backdrop."

## Drill 2 — Breadth diverges from the index

**Scenario.** Nifty is up 0.4% and making a new intraday high at 24,500. But the market breadth is ugly: the NSE advance-decline is negative (roughly 18 advances vs 32 declines among Nifty 50), the Nifty Midcap and Smallcap indices are both *red*, and the up-move is being carried by just two heavyweight names (say Reliance and HDFC Bank). You're deciding whether to add fresh index longs. What do you do?

**Options:** (A) Go long the index; new highs are bullish. (B) Be cautious — narrow breadth warns of a weak rally; avoid fresh longs or tighten existing ones. (C) Short the index outright. (D) Buy midcaps since they're "cheap" now.

**Answer: B.** A new index high on *negative breadth* is a **narrow, low-quality rally** — the headline number is masking internal weakness because a couple of heavyweights are papering over broad selling. Historically, such divergences precede pullbacks: when the two generals leading the charge tire, there's no breadth beneath to hold the index up. The right posture is caution — don't chase fresh index longs into a hollow move, and consider tightening stops on existing longs. **A (buying the new high) takes the headline at face value** and ignores the single most important tell that the rally is fragile. **C (shorting outright)** over-commits — divergence is a warning, not yet a reversal *trigger*; you'd want price confirmation (a breakdown) before shorting. **D (buying red midcaps as "cheap")** is bottom-fishing the exact segment breadth says is under distribution — catching knives. Breadth is the market's honesty check on the index.

## Drill 3 — Sector rotation signal

**Scenario.** Over the last three sessions you notice Bank Nifty and the PSU Bank index are making fresh highs and leading, while the IT index and FMCG are quietly rolling over from resistance. Money is visibly rotating from defensives into financials. You have capital for one swing position. Where do you deploy?

**Options:** (A) Buy a leading PSU bank on a pullback. (B) Buy an IT name since it's "due for a bounce." (C) Short a bank since it's "overbought." (D) Split across a bank and an IT name.

**Answer: A.** **Trade with the rotation, not against it.** Sector rotation is one of the most reliable medium-term forces in equity markets: when institutional money moves *into* financials and *out of* IT/FMCG, the leaders tend to keep leading and the laggards keep lagging for weeks. The highest-expectancy deployment is to buy the *leading* sector — a strong PSU bank — on a pullback to support, riding the flow. **B (buying lagging IT for a bounce)** is fighting the rotation; "due for a bounce" is not a strategy, and money leaving a sector rarely reverses on schedule. **C (shorting a leader as "overbought")** is the classic error of shorting strength — leaders stay overbought precisely because they're being accumulated. **D (splitting into a leader and a laggard)** dilutes into the weaker idea and half-hedges away your edge. Concentrate in the sector that flow is favouring.

## Drill 4 — Intermarket: USDINR and IT

**Scenario.** USDINR spikes sharply from 83.20 to 83.90 over two sessions (rupee weakening). You trade Indian equities. Nifty IT is basing near support after a decline. How do you use the intermarket signal?

**Options:** (A) A weaker rupee is a tailwind for IT exporters — favour long setups in IT. (B) A weaker rupee is bearish for IT — avoid IT. (C) Ignore currency; it's irrelevant to stocks. (D) Short IT because the rupee is falling.

**Answer: A.** Indian IT majors (TCS, Infosys, Wipro, HCLTech) earn the bulk of revenue in **US dollars** and report in rupees, so a *weaker* rupee *inflates* their rupee earnings — it's a fundamental tailwind for the sector. When USDINR rises meaningfully and an IT index is *basing at support* (a technical setup), the intermarket signal and the chart align: favour long setups in quality IT names. This is exactly how intermarket analysis adds an edge — the currency move is a wind at the sector's back that a pure chart-reader would miss. **B and D invert the relationship** — they'd be correct for an *importer* or a company with dollar *costs*, not a dollar *earner*. **C (ignoring currency)** throws away a genuine, well-established macro linkage that Indian desks watch closely. The nuance: currency is a *bias* input, not a timing trigger — you still need the chart (the base at support) to time the entry.

## Drill 5 — Correlated positions, one signal

**Scenario.** You already hold longs in HDFC Bank and ICICI Bank (both leading private banks). A fresh, clean long setup now appears in Axis Bank — another private bank, highly correlated with the two you hold. Taking it would put ~75% of your capital in one sector. What do you do?

**Options:** (A) Take the Axis long at full size; it's a good setup. (B) Take it at reduced size, or skip it, to control sector concentration. (C) Take it at full size and add to HDFC too. (D) Sell HDFC and rotate fully into Axis.

**Answer: B.** The setup is fine in isolation, but you must think at the *portfolio* level. HDFC, ICICI, and Axis are **highly correlated** — they rise and fall together on banking news, RBI policy, and Bank Nifty moves. Piling a third private bank on top of two existing ones means you no longer have three trades; you have **one concentrated bet on private banks in disguise**, and a single adverse sector event (a bad RBI surprise, an asset-quality scare) hits all three at once. The disciplined move is to size Axis *down* to keep total banking exposure within your risk limit, or skip it entirely because you already have the sector's move. **A (full size) blows through concentration limits.** **C (full Axis + adding HDFC)** compounds the error into recklessness. **D (dumping a working HDFC long to chase Axis)** churns for no reason. Correlation is hidden leverage — manage it deliberately.

## Drill 6 — The winner you want to add to

**Scenario.** You're long a stock from 1,000; it's run cleanly to 1,080 (+8%) and is consolidating in a tight flag near the highs. You want to *pyramid* — add to the winner. Your original stop was 970. What's the correct way to add?

**Options:** (A) Add the same size as the original, keep the stop at 970. (B) Add a smaller size on the flag breakout, and raise the stop so the *combined* position risks no more than planned. (C) Double the position; it's clearly working. (D) Don't add — never add to a position.

**Answer: B.** Pyramiding into a winner is a legitimate, professional technique — *if done correctly*. The rules: **add a smaller tranche than the original** (so your average entry doesn't lurch up too far), add on a *continuation trigger* (the flag breakout), and — critically — **raise your stop** so that the *total* position's risk stays within your original per-trade risk budget. Done right, you increase exposure to a proven winner while your worst-case loss on the whole position stays capped, ideally at breakeven-or-better on the original lot. **A (same size, stale stop at 970)** is dangerous — it leaves the enlarged position risking far more than planned, because a drop to 970 now loses on a bigger base from a higher average. **C (doubling)** over-concentrates and violates risk sizing. **D ("never add")** is a myth — adding to winners (not losers) is exactly how trend-followers build large positions; the sin is adding to *losers*. The whole game is: add up, raise the stop, cap total risk.

## Drill 7 — Volatility regime shift mid-trade

**Scenario.** You hold an intraday Nifty long from 24,100, comfortably in profit at 24,180, stop trailed to 24,130. Suddenly India VIX jumps from 12 to 16 in twenty minutes and the tape gets violent — 40-point candles in both directions. Your position is fine for now. What do you do?

**Options:** (A) Do nothing; your stop is set. (B) Tighten the stop and/or reduce size to adapt to the higher volatility. (C) Add more to catch the bigger swings. (D) Widen the stop to survive the noise.

**Answer: B — adapt position management to the new regime.** A sharp VIX spike means the *character* of the market has changed: bigger, faster swings raise the odds of a violent reversal that round-trips your gains. Two sensible adaptations: **book part of the position** to lock profit and cut exposure to the whipsaw, and/or **tighten the trail** to protect the win — or if you keep full size, at least accept that your effective risk has risen. The principle is that **position size and stop discipline should scale with volatility** — the same rupee stop is now a much *looser* stop in ATR terms. **A (do nothing)** ignores a real regime shift; your trailed stop at 24,130 might now be one ordinary candle away. **C (adding into a VIX spike)** increases risk exactly when uncertainty explodes — wrong direction. **D (widening the stop)** hands back your profit cushion to noise; you never loosen risk to "survive" — you tighten or trim.

## Drill 8 — F&O ban and a stock setup

**Scenario.** A midcap you follow, say a metals name, has entered the **F&O ban period** (open interest crossed 95% of market-wide position limit). It shows a tempting bullish setup on the daily. You normally trade it via futures. What do you do?

**Options:** (A) Trade it via futures as usual. (B) Recognise that in a ban, only position *reduction* is allowed — you can't add fresh F&O positions — so trade the equity cash segment instead, or stand aside. (C) Ignore the ban and place the order anyway. (D) Buy deep OTM options as a workaround.

**Answer: B.** During an F&O ban, the exchange permits only *decreasing* existing positions — **you cannot initiate fresh futures or options positions**, and attempting to increase them attracts penalties. This is a hard microstructure constraint, not a suggestion. If you still like the setup, the correct route is the **cash/equity segment** (buy the underlying shares outright), where no ban applies, or simply stand aside. Also worth noting: stocks in ban are often there because of frothy, crowded positioning and elevated volatility — a reason for extra caution regardless. **A and C** would either be rejected or penalised. **D (deep OTM options as a "workaround")** still counts as initiating a fresh F&O position and is not a legal way around the ban — and deep OTM in a volatile ban-list name is a lottery ticket. Know the rules of the venue before you know the chart.

## Drill 9 — Gap and the overnight decision

**Scenario.** It's 3:20 PM. You hold an intraday long in a stock, up nicely, at 1,240 (bought 1,210). The chart looks strong into the close and you're tempted to *convert it to a swing* and hold overnight. But it's an intraday product (MIS) and there's a major event — the RBI policy decision — tomorrow morning. What do you do?

**Options:** (A) Convert to delivery (CNC) and hold overnight for more upside. (B) Book the intraday profit before 3:30 and go home flat, given the event risk. (C) Hold and add more before close. (D) Convert to delivery and place a wide stop.

**Answer: B (default) — with A acceptable only as a deliberate, sized event bet.** The clean answer for most traders: **you took an intraday trade, the plan worked, book it and go home flat** — especially with a binary event (RBI policy) tomorrow that can gap the stock either way past any stop. Overnight gap risk is *unmanageable* with a stop because price can leap over it at the open. Converting an intraday winner into an unplanned overnight event bet is **strategy drift** — you're changing the trade's entire risk profile on emotion (greed for "more upside"). **A is defensible only** if holding overnight into RBI was your *plan from the start*, sized for gap risk (smaller position, no stop-reliance) — not a spur-of-the-moment conversion. **C (adding before an event) doubles event exposure** at the worst time. **D (delivery + wide stop) misunderstands gaps** — a wide stop doesn't protect you when the stock gaps 5% through it. Book the intraday win; let tomorrow be a fresh, planned decision.

## Drill 10 — Two-thirds through a losing day

**Scenario.** It's 1:30 PM. You've taken three trades today and are down 2.2% of your account — nearly at your daily loss limit of 2.5%. You spot what looks like a genuinely good setup. What do you do?

**Options:** (A) Take it; a good setup is a good setup. (B) Take it at reduced size, respecting the remaining risk budget. (C) Stop trading for the day — you're at your loss limit. (D) Increase size to recover the day's losses.

**Answer: C (with B as a disciplined edge case).** The daily loss limit exists for exactly this moment. Being down 2.2% on a 2.5% limit means you have *almost nothing left to risk*, and — just as important — three losses in a row means you're likely **not seeing the tape clearly**; emotional capital is depleted, and "genuinely good setup" may be your tilted brain rationalising a revenge trade. The professional call is to **honour the limit and stop** — protect the account and your headspace, review the losing trades tonight, come back fresh. **B is acceptable** *only* if your rules explicitly allow one more trade sized to fit within the remaining 0.3% — a tiny position where a loss still keeps you under the limit — and only if you're genuinely clear-headed. **A (full size)** blows the limit on the next stop-out. **D (sizing up to recover)** is textbook revenge trading — the fastest route from a bad day to a blown account. The limit is not a suggestion; it's the circuit breaker that keeps you in business.

## Drill 11 — Conflicting timeframes

**Scenario.** You're a swing trader. On the *daily* chart, a stock is in a healthy uptrend, riding its 20-DMA. On the *weekly* chart, however, it's pressing into a major multi-year resistance that has rejected it twice before. A fresh daily buy signal fires today. What do you do?

**Options:** (A) Take the daily long at full size. (B) Take it at reduced size / with a nearer target, respecting the weekly resistance overhead. (C) Short it on the weekly resistance. (D) Ignore the weekly; only the daily matters for a swing trade.

**Answer: B.** **The higher timeframe sets the context; the lower timeframe times the trade** — and here the higher (weekly) timeframe flags a major overhead ceiling. The daily uptrend is real and tradeable, but a multi-year resistance that's rejected price twice is a genuine supply zone where the swing move is likely to *stall or reverse*. So take the long — the daily trend favours it — but respect the ceiling: *reduce size* and set a *conservative target just below the weekly resistance*, planning to book there rather than expecting a clean breakout on the first touch. **A (full size, open target)** ignores the ceiling and risks holding through the rejection. **C (shorting into a daily uptrend)** fights the immediate trend on nothing but "it's at resistance" — premature without a weekly breakdown. **D (ignoring the weekly)** discards the single most important piece of context for a swing trade. Timeframe alignment: trade the daily, but let the weekly cap your ambition.

## Drill 12 — The stock that won't hit your entry

**Scenario.** You planned to buy a stock on a pullback to 500 (a support level). Instead, it never dips — it holds 508 all morning, coils in a tight range above support, and now looks ready to break higher without ever giving you your 500 entry. What do you do?

**Options:** (A) Chase it — buy now at 508. (B) Adjust: enter on the tight-range breakout with a stop below the range, accepting a slightly worse price but a valid new trigger. (C) Stubbornly wait for 500; your plan said 500. (D) Give up on the trade entirely.

**Answer: B.** A stock that *refuses to pull back* to your buy level is often showing *strength* — the dip you wanted never comes because demand is too eager. Rigidly insisting on 500 (**C**) means you'll watch a good trade leave without you, a classic "anchoring to a price that no longer reflects reality" error. But the fix isn't to blindly chase (**A**), which means buying with an ill-defined stop. The professional adaptation is to **recognise the new structure** — a tight coil above support is itself a valid setup — and enter on the *range breakout* with a defined stop below the coil (say 503). You accept a marginally worse entry than your ideal 500 in exchange for a real, confirmed trigger and a tight stop. **D (abandoning)** over-reacts — the thesis (this stock goes higher) may still be perfectly valid; only the entry mechanism needs updating. Plans are hypotheses; update them when price reveals more information.

## Drill 13 — Distribution signs in a position you love

**Scenario.** You're swing-long a stock you're fundamentally fond of, up 30% over two months. Lately the chart shows warning signs: three consecutive sessions of high volume with *no upside progress* (churn at the highs), a lower high forming, and the stock now losing its 20-DMA on a closing basis. But you "love the story." What do you do?

**Options:** (A) Hold; the fundamentals are great. (B) Respect the technical distribution signals — book profits or tighten the stop hard. (C) Add more; it's a discount now. (D) Ignore the chart; you're a long-term investor.

**Answer: B.** This drill is about *separating your emotional attachment from the tape*. The signals are textbook **distribution**: heavy volume with no price progress means large sellers are unloading into buyers (churning), a lower high signals the uptrend structure is cracking, and losing the 20-DMA on a closing basis confirms momentum has turned. You have a large, real profit to protect. The disciplined move is to **honour the price action** — book at least a chunk and tighten the trailing stop hard — regardless of how much you *like* the company. **A and D ("I love the story," "I'm long-term")** are how traders round-trip a 30% gain back to zero: the story feels safest right at the top, when everyone agrees. **C (adding into distribution)** is throwing good money after a topping chart. The market doesn't care that you love the stock. *"Fall in love with the trade's thesis, never with the position"* — when the chart says distribution, the position no longer deserves your loyalty.

## Interview-ready summary

The meta-skills this second set drills:

1. **Rank conflicting signals.** Stock vs index, daily vs weekly, chart vs macro — name which dominates and size accordingly. When in doubt, respect the higher timeframe and the broader context, and express uncertainty through *reduced size*, not a coin-flip.
2. **Breadth and rotation are the market's truth-tellers.** A new high on bad breadth is a weak rally; money flowing into a sector keeps flowing. Trade with internals and rotation, not against them.
3. **Use intermarket bias correctly.** Weaker rupee helps IT exporters; currency and rates set *bias*, the chart sets *timing*. Know the direction of each linkage.
4. **Think in portfolio, not tickets.** Correlated positions are concentrated bets in disguise; manage total sector and gross exposure, not just the single trade in front of you.
5. **Add to winners the right way; never to losers.** Pyramid smaller, on a trigger, while raising the stop so total risk stays capped.
6. **Adapt to regime and respect hard limits.** Scale risk with volatility, honour the daily loss limit and F&O ban rules, and never let greed convert a planned intraday win into an unplanned overnight event bet.
7. **Divorce emotion from the position.** Distribution signals override how much you "love the story." The tape doesn't know your P&L or your feelings.

Re-run both sets weekly. When these weighings become automatic — when you *feel* the concentration risk or the narrow-breadth warning before you consciously reason it — you've internalised the difference between reading charts and trading them.
