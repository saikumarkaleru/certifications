# Top F&O Stocks Playbook II

This is the second half of the single-stock dossier begun in Volume Playbook I. Where the first set covered the index-heavy blue chips — Reliance, the two large private banks, Infosys and Tata Motors — this chapter profiles five more of the most-traded NSE F&O names, deliberately spanning different sectors and personalities so you build a rounded feel for how *different kinds* of stocks move: an FMCG defensive, an adani-cycle infrastructure name, a metals cyclical, a PSU heavyweight, and a high-beta broking/market-infra play. Same template throughout: **character & drivers → key levels & behaviour → best setup → risk notes.** Levels are realistic 2026-context teaching figures; re-anchor to live data before trading.

## ITC (ITC)

**Character & drivers.** ITC is the classic *defensive dividend compounder* — a cigarettes-plus-FMCG-plus-hotels-plus-agri conglomerate whose chart personality is low-beta, range-respecting and dividend-anchored. Drivers: cigarette-taxation policy (the Union Budget is the single biggest event — any change to GST/cess on cigarettes moves the stock hard), FMCG margin trends, the hotels-demerger value-unlocking story, and its role as a *defensive rotation destination*. When the market turns risk-off or when high-beta momentum names top out, money rotates into ITC for its yield and earnings stability, and the chart outperforms precisely when the broad market struggles.

**Key levels & behaviour.** ITC trends *slowly and grindingly* — it is not a stock that doubles in a quarter; it re-rates over quarters in measured steps. It respects the 200-DMA as a strong floor and tends to build long bases before stepping higher. Its dividend yield puts a soft floor under the price — when the yield gets high enough (price falls), income buyers step in. The Budget is its defining annual event: a "no bad news on cigarette tax" Budget often triggers a relief rally; a sharp tax hike gaps it down. Volume is generally steady rather than explosive, except around the Budget and demerger news.

**Best setup — the defensive-rotation relative-strength trade.** The highest-value ITC signal is *relative strength versus Nifty during a market wobble.* When the broad market is selling off but ITC is holding its ground or making higher lows (rising RS line), it is absorbing rotation flows — go long as a defensive position, with a stop below the recent structural low. This works because ITC's character is to *lead on the way down* (fall less) and hold up when momentum names crack. A second, event setup: the *post-Budget clarity* trade — once the Budget removes tax uncertainty favourably, ITC often trends for weeks as the overhang lifts.

**Risk notes.** ITC's low beta cuts both ways — it *won't* deliver momentum-style returns, so it is a wrong vehicle for aggressive traders expecting fast moves. The real tail risk is regulatory: a punitive cigarette-tax hike, or ESG-driven institutional divestment from tobacco, can de-rate it. Around the Budget, respect the two-sided gap risk. And don't chase ITC in a raging bull market — it typically *lags* when high-beta names are ripping, so its edge is defensive, not offensive.

## Adani Enterprises (ADANIENT) / the Adani complex

**Character & drivers.** The Adani-group flagship and incubator, and the archetype of a *high-beta, narrative-and-flow-driven* name. Its chart is dominated by group-level news (leverage, promoter pledging, index-inclusion/exclusion, MSCI weight changes, any regulatory or short-seller-report event), broad risk appetite, and FII/institutional positioning toward the group. The 2023 short-seller episode is the defining lesson: the entire complex gapped down catastrophically on a single report, and technicals were irrelevant against the fundamental/sentiment shock. Post-recovery it re-rated on de-leveraging and stake-sale news. This is a *pure sentiment-and-flow vehicle* — trade the momentum, never marry it.

**Key levels & behaviour.** ADANIENT is *violently trending* — huge directional moves, large gaps, and extreme sensitivity to headlines. It overshoots massively in both directions. Round levels and prior swing highs/lows matter, but the stock's defining feature is that *fundamentals and headlines override chart structure* far more than for a blue chip. It respects the 50-DMA only loosely in trends; in shocks it ignores every technical level and gaps through them. F&O volumes are enormous and the name is a favourite of aggressive momentum traders.

**Best setup — momentum breakout with hard risk control.** When the complex is in a positive-news phase (de-leveraging, stake sales, index inclusion) and price breaks out of a base on volume, trade the momentum long *with a strictly defined stop and reduced size.* The setup is a standard base breakout, but the *risk management* is the entire edge — because a headline can gap it 10-20% against you overnight, position size must be small enough that a gap-through-stop is survivable. Take profits into strength; do not hold hoping for more. This is a trade you rent, not own.

**Risk notes.** This is the single most important "respect the tail" name in the F&O universe. Stops can be jumped by double digits on a headline. *Never* concentrate the book here, never oversize, never hold through a known event without hedging. The 2023 episode should be tattooed on every trader's memory: no chart pattern protects you from a fundamental sentiment shock in a high-leverage, headline-sensitive name. Trade it small, trade the momentum, and get out when the momentum stalls.

## Tata Steel (TATASTEEL) / metals

**Character & drivers.** The bellwether metals cyclical and a *global-commodity, China-demand* play. Its chart is driven by (1) international steel prices and spreads, (2) China's demand and stimulus signals (China is the swing factor in global steel), (3) the rupee and global growth expectations, and (4) input costs (coking coal, iron ore). Metals are the *early-cyclical* group — they lead economic upturns and roll over early into slowdowns, so Tata Steel often turns ahead of the broad market at cyclical inflections. It is high-beta and moves with the global reflation/deflation narrative.

**Key levels & behaviour.** Tata Steel moves in *big cyclical swings* — long uptrends when the global metals cycle is expanding, long downtrends when it contracts, with sharp counter-trend rallies on China-stimulus headlines. It respects the 200-DMA as a cycle divider (above = cyclical bull, below = cyclical bear) more usefully than short-term averages. It gaps on results and on China policy news. Because it is a *proxy for global growth*, it often moves with global metals/mining peers and the broad commodity complex rather than purely on domestic factors.

**Best setup — the cyclical-turn trade with intermarket confirmation.** The best Tata Steel entries come at *cyclical inflections confirmed by intermarket signals.* When international steel prices bottom and turn up, China announces stimulus, and the stock reclaims its 200-DMA on volume — that confluence marks the start of a multi-month cyclical leg. Enter on the 200-DMA reclaim with the intermarket tailwind, stop below the recent low, hold positionally as the cycle plays out. The *intermarket confirmation* (steel prices, China signals, the global metals tape) is what distinguishes a real cyclical turn from a bear-market bounce — trade the turn only when the macro agrees.

**Risk notes.** Metals are *deeply cyclical and globally driven*, so a purely domestic chart read is insufficient — you must watch international steel prices and China. The danger is buying a "cheap" chart in a cyclical downturn (value trap) — a low P/E metals stock in a deteriorating cycle keeps getting cheaper. High beta demands smaller size. And China policy is a fat-tailed input — a stimulus announcement or a property-sector shock can gap the whole complex overnight.

## State Bank of India (SBIN) / PSU banks

**Character & drivers.** The PSU-bank bellwether and the largest public-sector lender. Its chart is driven by the *PSU-bank re-rating cycle* — asset-quality clean-up, credit growth, the government's PSU-reform and disinvestment narrative, and the broad rate/credit environment. PSU banks trade as a *cohort* (SBI, Bank of Baroda, PNB, Canara) that moves together, often with sharp catch-up rallies when the sector comes into favour after years of neglect. SBI is the lower-beta, higher-quality anchor of that cohort; the smaller PSUs are higher-beta versions of the same trade. Drivers: NIM, GNPA improvement, credit growth, and the PSU-Bank index's relative strength versus private banks.

**Key levels & behaviour.** SBI is a *base-and-breakout* name that spends long periods consolidating and then re-rates in bursts when the PSU-bank narrative turns. It respects round levels and the 200-DMA. Its results gaps are institutional and orderly. The key relationship to watch is *PSU Bank index vs Private Bank index (or Bank Nifty)* — when PSU banks are outperforming private banks, SBI is in a favourable regime; when private banks lead, SBI tends to lag. Because it is government-linked, it also reacts to Budget/disinvestment and any large capital-raising or policy news.

**Best setup — the PSU-cohort rotation breakout.** When the PSU-Bank index breaks out and starts outperforming Bank Nifty (rising RS), buy SBI on its own base breakout as the *quality anchor* of the rotation, or the smaller PSUs for higher beta if you want more torque. Enter on the volume breakout, stop below the base, hold as the cohort rotation plays out — these PSU re-ratings have historically run for months once they begin, as underowned institutions rebuild positions. The relative-strength cohort signal (PSU banks leading) is the timing key.

**Risk notes.** PSU banks carry *policy and governance risk* that private banks don't — government stake, potential for politically-driven lending, and disinvestment overhangs. They also have historically been asset-quality laggards, so a credit-cycle downturn hits them harder. The cohort moves together, so holding SBI + BoB + PNB is one levered PSU-bank bet, not three positions — aggregate the exposure. And SBI's insurance/AMC/cards subsidiaries add sum-of-the-parts complexity that can drive moves independent of the banking chart.

## Angel One (ANGELONE) / broking & market-infra

**Character & drivers.** The listed discount-broking and market-infrastructure play, and a *high-beta proxy for retail-trading volumes and demat-account growth.* Its chart is driven by (1) market cash and F&O turnover, (2) new demat-account additions and active-client growth, (3) — critically — *SEBI regulation of the F&O and broking business*, and (4) the broad market's health (retail participation booms in bull markets and collapses in bears). It is a levered bet on India's retail-investing boom, which makes it explosive on the upside and brutally exposed to regulatory and cyclical shocks on the downside.

**Key levels & behaviour.** Angel One and the broking/market-infra cohort (BSE, MCX, CDSL, Angel One) are among the *highest-beta movers* on the exchange — capable of huge percentage moves in short periods. They trend hard with rising retail-participation narratives and gap violently on SEBI circulars. Because the business model is directly in the regulator's crosshairs (F&O tightening, true-to-label charges, expiry-day rationalisation, increased lot sizes), the defining chart risk is the *regulatory gap*: a single SEBI announcement can re-rate the entire earnings model overnight. It respects moving averages loosely but is fundamentally a momentum-and-news vehicle.

**Best setup — momentum trend with a regulatory-event calendar.** In a rising-volume, bull-market regime, trade Angel One's momentum breakouts long with defined stops — the retail-boom tailwind can drive sustained trends. But the *edge and the survival* both come from keeping a SEBI-event calendar and *reducing or hedging exposure into known regulatory reviews.* The setup is a standard momentum breakout; the discipline is refusing to hold size through a scheduled SEBI decision, because the downside gaps are severe and one-directional. Take profits into strength; treat it as a rented momentum position, not a hold.

**Risk notes.** This is a *regulation-first* name — no technical setup survives an adverse SEBI circular, and the sector has repeatedly gapped double digits on volume-tightening measures. Position size must reflect that gap risk. It is also *deeply pro-cyclical*: in a bear market, retail volumes and account growth collapse together, and the whole cohort de-rates. Never treat Angel One as a defensive or buy-and-hold position — its beta and regulatory exposure make it a momentum vehicle only, to be traded small and watched closely.

## Reading these five as a set — and building the full book

Across both Playbook chapters you now have ten single-stock personalities spanning the market's engines. This second set deliberately broadened the factor coverage:

- **ITC** — low-beta defensive; leads on the way down, lags in bull runs; trade defensive rotation and the Budget.
- **Adani Enterprises** — high-beta, headline-driven; trade momentum small, respect the fat tail, rent-don't-own.
- **Tata Steel** — global cyclical; trade cyclical turns with intermarket (steel prices, China) confirmation.
- **SBI** — PSU-bank anchor; trade the cohort rotation when PSU banks outperform private banks.
- **Angel One** — high-beta retail-volume proxy; trade momentum but keep a SEBI-event calendar and size for gap risk.

The overarching professional lesson from all ten names is **match the setup to the personality and aggregate by risk factor.** A defensive like ITC demands a rotation/relative-strength approach; a cyclical like Tata Steel demands intermarket confirmation; high-beta headline names (Adani, Angel One) demand small size and event-awareness above all; trend-clean names (ICICI from Playbook I) reward buying pullbacks to moving averages. There is no single "right" setup — the right setup is the one that fits how *that specific instrument actually moves.*

And when you assemble these into a book, aggregate exposure by *underlying risk factor*, not by ticker count: banks (HDFC, ICICI, SBI) are largely one rate/credit-and-FII bet; the high-beta headline names (Adani, Angel One, Tata Motors) are one risk-appetite bet; ITC and Infosys are your genuine diversifiers. A book that looks like eight positions but is really three concentrated factor bets is the most common way retail F&O traders blow up in a correlated sell-off — everything they own is secretly the same trade. The dossier approach in these two chapters exists precisely so you know, position by position, what each name really *is* — and therefore what your book really *is*.
