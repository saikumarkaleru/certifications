# NISM-RA-Style Question Bank

If you want to publish research, issue technical calls to clients, or work at a broking/PMS/research desk in India, you will sit the **NISM Series XV: Research Analyst** certification. It is a SEBI-mandated exam, and while it covers fundamentals, economics, valuation and regulation, it also carries a meaningful **technical analysis** component — plus a strong emphasis on the *regulatory conduct* a Research Analyst (RA) must observe. This chapter is a bank of **exam-style questions** written in the NISM idiom: multiple-choice, single-best-answer, four options, with a detailed key that explains not just the right answer but *why the distractors are wrong*. The TA questions here are pitched at Volume-IV depth; the conduct/regulatory ones reflect the SEBI (Research Analysts) Regulations framework as an RA candidate must know it.

## How to use this bank

- These mirror the **NISM MCQ format**: one correct option, no partial credit, negative marking on the real exam (25% of marks per wrong answer on many NISM papers — so guessing blindly is penalised). Practise **eliminating distractors**, not just spotting the answer.
- The real Series-XV paper is 100 questions, 2 hours, 60% passing. Time discipline (~72 seconds/question) matters. Answer these under a loose clock.
- The regulatory answers below are written to the framework Indian RAs are examined on. **Always verify the current SEBI (Research Analysts) Regulations and latest circulars before acting professionally** — rules evolve, and this is study material, not compliance advice.

## Section A — Technical analysis concepts (Q1–Q12)

**Q1.** The core assumption underlying technical analysis is that:
- (a) Markets are always perfectly efficient
- (b) Price discounts all available information and moves in trends
- (c) Fundamental value is irrelevant to price
- (d) Past prices have no bearing on future prices

**Q2.** In Dow Theory, a *primary* trend is confirmed when:
- (a) The RSI crosses 50
- (b) Both the relevant averages (e.g., a broad index and a related sector/transport-type index) confirm the same direction
- (c) Volume falls on rallies
- (d) A single index makes a new high

**Q3.** A "head and shoulders top" pattern signals:
- (a) Trend continuation upward
- (b) A potential reversal from up to down
- (c) A neutral consolidation
- (d) An oversold bounce

**Q4.** Which statement about *volume* is correct in classical TA?
- (a) Volume should contract in the direction of the prevailing trend
- (b) Volume should ideally expand in the direction of the trend and contract on counter-trend moves
- (c) Volume is irrelevant once a trend is established
- (d) High volume always marks a top

**Q5.** The RSI (Relative Strength Index) is classified as a:
- (a) Trend-following indicator
- (b) Momentum oscillator
- (c) Volume indicator
- (d) Volatility band

**Q6.** A stock's 50-day moving average crosses *above* its 200-day moving average. This is commonly termed:
- (a) A death cross
- (b) A golden cross
- (c) A bearish divergence
- (d) An inside bar

**Q7.** Bollinger Bands widen sharply. This indicates:
- (a) Falling volatility
- (b) Rising volatility
- (c) A guaranteed breakout upward
- (d) The trend has ended

**Q8.** In a strong, healthy uptrend, RSI(14) staying above 70 for an extended period should be read as:
- (a) An immediate sell signal
- (b) A sign of strength ("good overbought"), not an automatic sell
- (c) Proof the trend has reversed
- (d) A data error

**Q9.** A "bullish divergence" occurs when:
- (a) Price makes a lower low but the momentum oscillator makes a higher low
- (b) Price and oscillator both make higher highs
- (c) Price makes a higher high but the oscillator makes a lower high
- (d) Volume rises with price

**Q10.** Support and resistance levels are best understood as:
- (a) Exact prices that can never be broken
- (b) Zones where supply/demand has historically shifted, which can break with follow-through
- (c) Only relevant on weekly charts
- (d) Determined solely by Fibonacci

**Q11.** A gap that occurs in the *middle* of a strong trending move, often used to estimate a target, is called a:
- (a) Breakaway gap
- (b) Exhaustion gap
- (c) Runaway (measuring) gap
- (d) Common gap

**Q12.** The primary limitation of technical analysis that an honest RA must disclose is:
- (a) It guarantees profits
- (b) It is subjective and probabilistic — signals can fail, and past patterns do not ensure future results
- (c) It works only for commodities
- (d) It replaces the need for risk management

## Section C — RA conduct, risk and regulation (Q23–Q30)

**Q23.** A SEBI-registered Research Analyst issuing a technical "buy" call on a stock in a public report must:
- (a) Guarantee the target will be achieved
- (b) Disclose any actual or potential conflict of interest, including personal holdings in the subject security
- (c) Hide their own position
- (d) Only recommend stocks they personally own

**Q24.** An RA who holds a personal position in a stock and then publishes a recommendation on it must, at minimum:
- (a) Do nothing special
- (b) Disclose the holding/interest in the research report
- (c) Sell before anyone reads it
- (d) Recommend the opposite of their position

**Q25.** "Front-running" — trading in one's own account ahead of a research recommendation that is expected to move the price — is:
- (a) A permitted RA practice if disclosed
- (b) A prohibited, unethical practice (a form of market abuse)
- (c) Encouraged for liquidity
- (d) Allowed for intraday only

**Q26.** A technical research report should present:
- (a) Only the bullish scenario to keep clients confident
- (b) A balanced view including the key risk/level at which the thesis is invalidated (the stop/invalidation)
- (c) Guaranteed returns
- (d) No price levels at all

**Q27.** The most honest and compliant way for an RA to frame a technical call's outcome is:
- (a) "This will definitely hit the target"
- (b) "Based on the setup, the probability favours X, with a defined invalidation at Y; results are not guaranteed"
- (c) "Risk-free trade"
- (d) "Cannot lose"

**Q28.** Regarding record-keeping, a SEBI-registered RA is generally expected to:
- (a) Keep no records
- (b) Maintain records of research reports, the rationale, and the basis of recommendations for the prescribed period
- (c) Delete reports after publishing
- (d) Keep records only if profitable

**Q29.** If a client asks an RA to "guarantee" 20% returns per month from technical trading, the correct professional response is:
- (a) Agree and promise it
- (b) Explain that no such guarantee is possible, that trading carries risk, and that most such expectations are unrealistic
- (c) Ignore the client
- (d) Recommend maximum leverage to try to achieve it

**Q30.** An honest RA's disclosure about technical analysis and retail outcomes should acknowledge that:
- (a) All retail traders make money
- (b) A majority of retail traders/derivative participants tend to lose money over time, and TA improves the *odds and discipline* but does not remove risk
- (c) TA eliminates the possibility of loss
- (d) Only fundamentals ever lose money

## Answer key with reasoning

**A1 — (b).** The three classical premises: *price discounts everything, prices move in trends, history tends to rhyme.* (a) is the efficient-market view TA partly rejects; (c) overstates — TA doesn't claim value is *irrelevant*, only that price already reflects it; (d) is the opposite of TA's premise.

**A2 — (b).** Dow Theory requires **mutual confirmation** by two related averages before a primary trend is trusted. (a)/(c)/(d) are single-signal shortcuts Dow specifically warns against; a lone index high isn't confirmation.

**A3 — (b).** Head and shoulders *top* is a **reversal** from up to down (its inverse is a bottom). (a) contradicts the pattern; (c)/(d) misclassify it.

**A4 — (b).** Volume should **expand with the trend and contract on pullbacks** — confirming conviction. (a) is backwards; (c) ignores volume's confirming role; (d) is false — high volume can mark tops *or* breakouts, so "always" is wrong.

**A5 — (b).** RSI is a bounded **momentum oscillator** (0–100). Not trend-following (that's MAs), not volume (that's OBV/volume), not a volatility band (that's Bollinger/ATR).

**A6 — (b).** 50-DMA crossing *above* 200-DMA = **golden cross** (bullish). The reverse is the death cross (a). (c)/(d) are unrelated concepts.

**A7 — (b).** Widening Bollinger Bands = **rising volatility** (bands are ±standard deviations). (a) is the opposite; (c) is false — widening says nothing about *direction*; (d) is unsupported.

**A8 — (b).** In strong trends RSI can stay overbought for a long time — **"good overbought," a sign of strength**, not an auto-sell. Shorting strength on the level alone (a)/(c) is a classic error; (d) is nonsense.

**A9 — (a).** Bullish divergence = **price lower low, oscillator higher low** (momentum improving as price falls). (c) describes *bearish* divergence; (b)/(d) are ordinary confirmations, not divergences.

**A10 — (b).** S/R are **zones of shifting supply/demand**, breakable with follow-through — not inviolable exact prices (a), not timeframe-exclusive (c), not Fibonacci-only (d).

**A11 — (c).** A **runaway/measuring gap** occurs mid-trend and can project a target (distance to the gap often repeats). Breakaway gaps start moves (a); exhaustion gaps end them (b); common gaps are noise in ranges (d).

**A12 — (b).** The honest limitation: TA is **subjective and probabilistic — signals fail, past patterns don't guarantee the future.** (a)/(d) are dangerous falsehoods; (c) is untrue (TA applies across asset classes).

**A13 — (b).** India VIX = expected **~30-day volatility implied by Nifty option prices** (the "fear gauge"). Not yield, flows, or breadth.

**A14 — (b).** A VIX **spike signals rising fear** and often coincides with **capitulation near short-term bottoms.** (a)/(d) describe *low* VIX; (c) overstates — a spike is not a confirmed long-term top.

**A15 — (b).** PCR is a **sentiment/contrarian** gauge (extremes flag over-optimism/pessimism), not trend-following (a), valuation (c), or a pure liquidity measure (d).

**A16 — (c).** A weak rupee **helps dollar-earning exporters (IT, pharma).** It *hurts* OMCs (a), aviation (b), and unhedged USD-debt firms (d) — those are the negatively-affected distractors.

**A17 — (b).** A **rising RS-ratio (BankNifty/Nifty) = Bank Nifty outperforming.** (a) is the falling-ratio case; (c)/(d) misread the tool.

**A18 — (b).** Deteriorating A-D line with index new highs = **breadth divergence / narrowing market** — a warning, *not* a precisely-timed crash signal (c overstates), and certainly not bullish (a) or irrelevant (d).

**A19 — (b).** **Defensives lead late-cycle / near tops** as money de-risks. Early bull markets are led by cyclicals/high-beta (a); (c)/(d) are wrong.

**A20 — (b).** A **breadth thrust is bullish**, often marking the *early* stage of a new advance (broad participation off a low). Not bearish (a), not noise (c); it's a reason to *lean long*, not short (d).

**A21 — (b).** India is a **large net crude importer**, so a crude spike is a **macro negative** (CAD, rupee, inflation). (a) is factually false; (c) understates; (d) is wrong — paints/aviation are *hurt* by high crude.

**A22 — (b).** Max Pain is a **weak, supplementary** read of heavy-OI strikes — never a precise forecast (a) or a recommendation basis (c) or a stop substitute (d). The disciplined RA treats it as minor context.

**A23 — (b).** An RA **must disclose conflicts of interest**, including personal holdings. (a) is impossible/prohibited (no guarantees); (c) is a violation; (d) is absurd.

**A24 — (b).** **Disclose the interest** in the report. Selling first (c) or recommending the opposite (d) doesn't cure the conflict; doing nothing (a) breaches conduct norms.

**A25 — (b).** **Front-running is prohibited market abuse** — trading ahead of your own price-moving research to profit personally. It is never permitted (a/c/d wrong).

**A26 — (b).** A proper report gives a **balanced view with the invalidation level** (where the thesis fails). One-sided bullishness (a), guarantees (c), or no levels (d) are poor/non-compliant practice.

**A27 — (b).** The honest framing is **probabilistic with a defined invalidation and a no-guarantee caveat.** "Definitely," "risk-free," "cannot lose" (a/c/d) are false and non-compliant.

**A28 — (b).** RAs must **maintain records** of reports, rationale and the basis of recommendations for the prescribed period — for accountability/audit. Deleting or not keeping records (a/c/d) breaches the framework.

**A29 — (b).** The correct response is to **refuse the guarantee, explain the risk, and reset unrealistic expectations.** Promising returns (a), max leverage (d), or ignoring the client (c) are all wrong — and guaranteeing returns is itself prohibited.

**A30 — (b).** Honest disclosure: **most retail/derivative participants lose over time; TA improves odds and discipline but doesn't remove risk.** (a)/(c) are false comfort; (d) is a straw man. This candour is both ethical and, increasingly, what SEBI's risk-disclosure emphasis expects.

## Exam strategy notes for Series-XV

A few tactics specific to how this paper is scored and worded. First, **watch for absolutes** — options containing "always," "never," "guaranteed," "risk-free," "cannot lose" are almost always the *wrong* answer in both the TA and the conduct sections; markets and regulators both deal in probabilities and disclosures, not certainties. Second, on **conflict-of-interest and market-abuse questions, the answer is virtually always "disclose / don't do it / act in the client's interest"** — when in doubt, pick the most transparent, most conservative, most client-protective option. Third, for **TA-mechanics questions, define the tool's category first** (trend vs momentum vs volatility vs volume vs breadth); half the distractors are just tools from the wrong category. Fourth, respect the **negative marking**: if you can eliminate two of four options, an educated guess is usually worth it; a blind guess among four is not.

Finally, internalise the *spirit* the exam is testing, because it's also the spirit of a durable career: a Research Analyst is a **fiduciary of attention and trust**, not a tipster. The technical skill lets you form a probabilistic view; the regulatory discipline forces you to state that view honestly, disclose your interest, define where you're wrong, and never promise what markets can't deliver. Master both halves and you pass the paper — and, more importantly, you become the kind of analyst clients and desks actually keep.
