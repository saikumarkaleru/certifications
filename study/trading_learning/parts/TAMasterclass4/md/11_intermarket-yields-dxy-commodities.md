# Intermarket: Yields, DXY & Commodities

No market trades in a vacuum. The Nifty you chart every morning is a downstream node in a global plumbing system whose upstream taps are the US 10-year Treasury yield, the US Dollar Index (DXY), crude oil, and gold. Intermarket analysis is the discipline of reading those upstream taps to anticipate the pressure that will hit Indian equities, the rupee, and rate-sensitive sectors *before* it shows up on the daily candle. This chapter builds the intermarket web specifically for an Indian trader in 2026 — a net oil importer, a heavy foreign-portfolio-flow market, and a currency that lives and dies by the dollar cycle.

## What it is & the logic

Intermarket analysis rests on a simple premise: capital is fluid and it chases relative return adjusted for risk and currency. Four global variables set the tide for Indian assets:

- **US 10-year Treasury yield (US10Y):** the world's risk-free discount rate. When it rises, the present value of every future cash flow falls, and money rotates *out* of long-duration risk assets (growth stocks, emerging markets) *into* the safety of US bonds. Rising US yields are a headwind for Nifty and a magnet pulling FPI money out of India.
- **US Dollar Index (DXY):** the dollar's value against a basket of major currencies. A strong dollar (rising DXY) tightens global financial conditions, pressures emerging-market currencies including the rupee, and typically drives FPI *outflows* from Indian equities. "Risk-on" globally usually means DXY down, EM up.
- **Crude oil (Brent / WTI, and MCX crude in rupees):** India imports ~85% of its crude. Rising oil is a direct tax on the economy — it widens the current-account and fiscal deficits, stokes inflation, weakens the rupee, and squeezes oil-marketing companies, paints, aviation, tyres, and logistics. Falling oil is a broad tailwind.
- **Gold (COMEX and MCX in rupees):** the fear-and-real-yield barometer. Gold rises when real yields fall or when systemic fear spikes; in India it also carries a currency component because a weaker rupee lifts MCX gold even when dollar gold is flat.

The Indian-specific transmission chain is the through-line of this whole chapter:

**US yields up → DXY up → rupee down → FPI sell Indian equities → Nifty pressured, especially high-PE/IT/rate-sensitive names.** And the reverse for the risk-on case. Oil and gold overlay their own sector-specific and inflation channels on top.

## Reading the relationships (correlations & signs)

Correlations here are *regime-dependent and unstable* — the honest starting point. But the sign of the typical relationship is a reliable prior:

| Upstream variable | Direction | Rupee (USDINR) | Nifty | Sectors that win / lose |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| US10Y | Rises | INR weakens | Headwind | Loses: IT, high-PE growth, REITs. Wins: none directly |
| DXY | Rises | INR weakens | Headwind (FPI out) | Loses: broad EM, rate-sensitives. Wins: exporters partly cushioned |
| Brent crude | Rises | INR weakens | Headwind | Loses: OMCs, paints, aviation, tyres, logistics. Wins: ONGC, Oil India (upstream) |
| Gold | Rises | (mixed) | Mixed | Wins: jewellers' inventory, gold financiers sentiment |

Two composite reads matter most for an Indian trader:

**1. The USDINR–Nifty coupling.** When the rupee weakens sharply (USDINR spiking above a round level like 84 or 85), it is usually a *symptom* of FPI outflow, and Nifty tends to be under simultaneous pressure. IT services (TCS, Infosys) are the natural hedge — they *earn* in dollars, so a weak rupee lifts their reported margins, which is why IT frequently outperforms on rupee-weakness days even as the broad index falls. This intra-index rotation is one of the most tradable intermarket signals in India.

**2. The oil–OMC–rupee triangle.** A crude spike hits three places at once: it weakens the rupee (import bill), it compresses OMC marketing margins if they cannot pass on prices (BPCL, HPCL, IOC), and it lifts upstream producers (ONGC). A trader who sees Brent gap up overnight already knows to be short OMCs, cautious on the rupee, and watchful of upstream longs — *before* the Indian market opens.

## Worked India example

Suppose the overnight tape reads like this on a Wednesday in 2026:

- US10Y jumps from 4.20% to 4.38% on a hot US CPI print.
- DXY rallies from 103.4 to 104.6.
- Brent climbs from $78 to $83 on a supply scare.
- Gold slips because the real-yield spike outweighs the fear bid.
- USDINR (offshore NDF) is quoting 85.20, up from 84.85 the previous close.

**The intermarket read, step by step:**

1. *Yields + dollar up together* = classic tightening shock. Global risk-off. FPIs will likely be net sellers of Indian equities today. Bias: Nifty gaps down and struggles to recover.
2. *Rupee at 85.20* confirms the outflow pressure. This is not a rupee-specific story; it is dollar strength, so it is broad EM weakness.
3. *Brent at $83* layers an oil shock on top. Now OMCs, paints, aviation, and tyres carry a *second* headwind independent of the index.
4. *The rotation trade:* IT should relatively outperform (weak rupee = margin tailwind), so a *long IT / short OMC or short high-PE midcap* pair expresses the whole macro picture with less directional index risk.

**The concrete plan:**

- Index bias: short-of-neutral. Fade rallies in Nifty into resistance rather than buying dips; the macro tide is out.
- Sector shorts: BPCL/HPCL/IOC (oil + rupee double hit), an airline, a paints name — these have *two* reasons to fall.
- Relative long: IT largecaps as the rupee hedge; ONGC/Oil India as the upstream oil beneficiary.
- Currency: expect USDINR to hold its gains; do not fade rupee weakness until DXY tops.
- Invalidations: if US10Y and DXY *reverse* intraday (yields fall back, dollar softens), the whole thesis flips — cover shorts, because the tide turned. Intermarket theses live and die by their upstream driver, so you watch DXY and US10Y in real time, not the Nifty candle.

By 9:15 am, the disciplined intermarket trader already has a ranked plan built from markets that closed hours ago in New York and are trading live in Asia.

## How to use it for bias & timing

- **Set the daily tide before the open.** Every morning, check US10Y, DXY, Brent (MCX crude proxy), gold, and the USDINR NDF alongside SGX/GIFT Nifty. This five-variable scan tells you whether you are trading with or against the global current.
- **Grade FPI-flow risk.** DXY up + US10Y up + rupee weak = high FPI-outflow probability = fade rallies, keep size small on longs. The reverse regime = FPI-inflow tailwind = buy dips with confidence.
- **Route sector selection.** Let oil pick your energy/consumption trades, let the rupee pick your IT-vs-domestic rotation, let yields pick your growth-vs-value tilt. Intermarket analysis is most powerful not as an index timing tool but as a *sector-rotation and pairs* engine.
- **Use divergences as early warnings.** When Nifty makes a new high but the rupee is quietly weakening and DXY is quietly strengthening, the rally is living on borrowed time — the upstream taps are turning off even as price still rises. These divergences precede many corrections.

## Pitfalls

- **Correlations break and even invert.** The "yields up = stocks down" relationship holds in inflation-shock regimes but *reverses* in growth-recovery regimes (yields up because the economy is strong = stocks up). You must know *why* yields are moving, not just that they are. Never apply the sign mechanically.
- **Lag and lead are inconsistent.** Sometimes Nifty front-runs the rupee; sometimes it lags a day. Intermarket signals are for *bias and probability*, not precise entry timing.
- **The rupee is managed.** The RBI actively intervenes in USDINR. A rupee that "should" be at 86 on pure flows may be pinned at 85 by RBI dollar sales, which distorts the currency signal and can trap traders who assume free-float dynamics.
- **Over-counting the same shock.** Yields, dollar, and oil often move *together* off a single US data print. Do not treat three correlated variables as three independent confirmations — that is one shock wearing three hats, and it inflates false conviction.
- **Ignoring the domestic offset.** Strong, sticky domestic SIP/mutual-fund inflows have repeatedly cushioned Indian equities against FPI selling in recent years. The global tide is one force; the domestic bid is another, and in 2020s India the domestic bid has often won. Weigh both.
- **Stale round-number anchors.** Levels like "84 on USDINR" or "$80 Brent" become psychological anchors that stop mattering once breached. Update your reference levels; do not fight a re-rated regime.

## Interview-ready summary

Intermarket analysis reads the global upstream drivers — the **US 10-year yield, DXY, crude oil, and gold** — to anticipate pressure on Indian equities and the rupee before it prints on the chart. The core Indian transmission chain is **US yields up → DXY up → rupee weakens → FPIs sell → Nifty pressured**, hitting high-PE and rate-sensitive names hardest, while a weak rupee simultaneously *helps* dollar-earning IT largecaps — the single most tradable intra-index rotation. Crude is a direct tax on an import-dependent India: rising oil weakens the rupee and squeezes OMCs, paints, aviation and tyres while lifting upstream ONGC/Oil India; gold reads real yields and fear with a rupee-driven overlay on MCX. Use the five-variable morning scan (US10Y, DXY, Brent, gold, USDINR NDF plus GIFT Nifty) to set the day's tide, grade FPI-outflow risk, and route sector and pairs trades — intermarket is strongest as a rotation engine, not an index-timing tool. The honest caveats: correlations are regime-dependent and can invert (you must know *why* yields move), the RBI actively manages the rupee, correlated variables off one shock are not independent confirmations, and sticky domestic SIP inflows have repeatedly offset FPI selling — so weigh the global tide against the domestic bid rather than trading the sign mechanically.
