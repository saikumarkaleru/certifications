# Answer Key — Mock Test II

## Section A

**Q1 — (b).** ADX measures trend *strength*, not direction. Below ~20 and falling means the market is choppy/rangey, and breakout entries in that regime fail frequently (false breaks, whipsaws). Being above the 20-EMA is necessary but not sufficient — without trend strength behind it, the breakout is low-probability. Trap answer (a) mistakes location for momentum.

**Q2 — (b).** The classic RSI trap. In a strong trend, RSI can pin above 70-80 for a long time; mechanically shorting every overbought reading in an uptrend is a proven way to lose. The professional use of an extreme reading is a *risk-management* prompt — trail stops, book partials, stop adding — not a reversal trigger. (a) is the trap.

**Q3 — (b).** A gap-up breakout that later fills the gap but *recovers* to close back above resistance is a healthy retest: broken resistance is being tested as new support and holding. This often offers a second, lower-risk entry. It is only a failed breakout (a) if price closes and *stays* back below resistance — the recovery is the key distinction.

**Q4 — (b).** Renko/line-break strip out time and minor noise, giving cleaner trend and reversal-of-brick signals — but the cost is lag (bricks only print after a threshold move) and loss of intrabar timing and true volume context. Every charting method trades responsiveness against clarity; claiming no trade-off (a/c) is the error.

**Q5 — (b).** A new index high on a deteriorating advance-decline line means the rally is being carried by a shrinking number of large stocks — narrowing breadth. It is a caution flag for durability, not an immediate crash signal (trends can persist on narrow breadth for a while), but it lowers conviction and warrants tighter risk.

## Section B

**Q6.** A **failed breakout / bull trap**: the range break was rejected and price closed back inside on higher volume, meaning breakout buyers are now trapped and will become sellers. Bias flips from bullish to bearish/neutral; a common follow-through is a move toward the *opposite* end of the range. Higher volume on the reversal day strengthens the signal.

**Q7.** A **symmetrical triangle / coil (pennant)** contracting into an apex on declining volume. Two resolutions: an upside breakout (continuation of the prior up-move) or a downside breakdown (reversal). Given the strong prior uptrend and the volume dry-up typical of a healthy pause, the **upside breakout (continuation) is more likely** — but you trade the actual break with volume, not the anticipation.

**Q8.** The middle candle is an **inside bar** (its range is entirely within the prior large candle — a harami-like consolidation). The breakout candle closing above the large candle's high is an **inside-bar breakout in the direction of the trend** — a bullish continuation trigger, with the inside-bar low as a tight stop.

**Q9.** A **rounding bottom / saucer** — a slow, accumulation-driven reversal pattern. Implication: a major trend change from down/sideways to up. Confirmation is a **decisive weekly close above the neckline (1,720) on rising volume**, projecting a target roughly equal to the depth of the saucer added to the neckline. It is a patient, high-quality positional signal.

**Q10.** The short-term structure changed from higher-highs to a **lower high (50,360 < 50,400)**, and the break below the prior swing low (50,200) confirms a **short-term trend reversal down** on the 15-min. Bias shifts from long to short intraday; the first target is the prior support and the move that built the morning rally. The lower high is the tell.

**Q11.** Price is holding the 200-day EMA support but making **descending lower highs on each bounce** — a **descending triangle against support**. This warns that buyers are getting weaker at each rally while the support is being tested repeatedly; repeated tests of a support tend to *weaken* it, and the falling highs suggest an eventual **breakdown of the 200-EMA** is increasingly likely. Reduce longs, watch 1,420 closely.

## Section C

**Q12.** **Book profit / tighten to a trailing stop just under the current hourly structure.** You're at 24,900, 50 points from a 24,950 target, and the environment has turned hostile (VIX spike + bearish hourly stalling at resistance). Don't be greedy for the last 50 points against deteriorating conditions — either take the profit now or move the stop up to protect the bulk of the gain (e.g., 24,850). Letting a near-target winner round-trip to break-even because you insisted on the exact target is a common, avoidable error.

**Q13.** **Consider letting the winner run beyond target 1 / scale the exit.** Your plan said book half at 640, and booking half there is disciplined and fine. But the *context has improved* — strong volume plus a sector-index breakout signals a possibly larger move. A reasonable professional adjustment: still book a portion to lock gains, but trail the remainder with a wider stop to capture continuation, rather than blindly exiting the full planned half into strength. Rules are a floor, not a cage — but only adjust *toward* letting winners run, never toward cutting them.

**Q14.** **Honour your stop discipline; the gap changes the picture.** You're short from 50,000 with a stop at 50,300. A bullish policy surprise gapping price to 49,900 (still below your stop and still in profit) is a warning that the down-move's driver may be gone. Options: book the open profit (you're up 100 pts from entry) rather than risk a continued short-squeeze back to your stop, OR if it opens above your stop, you're stopped out — accept it. The mistake is *moving the stop higher to avoid the loss*; against a fundamental surprise, take the profit or the planned loss, don't widen risk.

**Q15.** The concept is **lack of follow-through / a failing breakout that hasn't yet hit the stop** — a "time stop" situation. A breakout should work fairly quickly; two days of drifting on low volume with no continuation is evidence the trade thesis is weakening even though price hasn't hit 1,575. Action: consider a **time-based exit** near break-even (1,600) to free the capital and avoid the likely failed-breakout stop-out, or tighten the stop to just below 1,585. Good trades tend to work soon; dead money is a cost.

**Q16.** **Do not panic-market-sell at the open; have a pre-decided plan.** Your stop (24,620) will be gapped through, so a resting stop-loss becomes a market order at ~24,550 — a worse fill. Options to decide *before* the open: (1) accept you're stopped and exit on the open, taking the larger-than-planned loss as the cost of overnight risk; (2) if the gap-down halts at a known support with a reversal candle in the first 15-30 min, some traders wait for that confirmation before exiting to avoid selling the low. The professional lesson is that **overnight gap risk is real and un-stoppable** — position size for it, and never carry more overnight than you can afford to gap against.

## Section D

**Q17.** The ATM straddle price (180 points) is the market's implied expected move by expiry — roughly ±180 points, so an expected range of about **24,520 to 24,880**. A technician uses this to sanity-check targets: if your bullish target is 24,850, it sits inside the expected move (achievable); if you're targeting 25,100, the options market says that's a low-probability outsized move this week. Straddle pricing frames realistic intraweek expectations.

**Q18.** Heavy call OI at 51,000 and heavy put OI at 49,000 are **option-writer "walls"** — strikes where sellers have written large positions and have an incentive to defend. They often act as **resistance (call wall, 51,000) and support (put wall, 49,000)**, framing a likely range. A technician overlays these on chart S/R: confluence between a call wall and a chart resistance strengthens the level. They are not guarantees — walls break on strong trends/news — but they bias intraday range expectations.

**Q19.** VIX at 11 signals **complacency** — the market expects little movement, so options are cheap (low implied volatility). The risk is twofold: complacency often precedes volatility spikes (low VIX can be a contrarian caution near tops), and cheap options mean the market is under-pricing a possible shock. For a technician: low VIX favours premium *buyers* (cheap optionality) and warns that a grind-up may be vulnerable to a sudden expansion. Complacency is a condition, not a timing signal.

**Q20.** A far-OTM weekly call for a *slow two-week grind* is a poor expression because weekly options **decay rapidly (theta)** and a slow grind may not move price far enough, fast enough, to overcome that decay — you can be right on direction and still lose. Conceptually better: a longer-dated or closer-to-the-money option (less theta sensitivity, higher delta), or simply the underlying/futures if a directional view is clean. Match the *instrument's time decay* to your *expected timeline* — a slow thesis needs time on the trade.

**Q21.** **Max-pain / expiry pinning** — price gravitates toward the strike with the largest combined OI where the most options expire worthless, because writers hedge to defend it. Implication for intraday trend-trading that day: **trend-following is treacherous** near the pin — price tends to mean-revert to the pin level rather than trend, so breakouts fail and range-fading the pin works better. Recognising an expiry-pin regime = smaller size or stand aside for trend strategies.

## Section E

**Q22.** This is **overconfidence / house-money effect** — a good month lowers your guard and you start risking loosely because it "feels like the market's money." Guardrail: enforce your *normal* position-sizing and rules regardless of monthly P&L, and optionally *reduce* size late in a strong month to protect the gain (a "green-month lock-in" rule). The fastest way to give back a great month is to abandon the discipline that produced it.

**Q23.** Because it **rewards a rule violation** — you broke your stop discipline and the market paid you for it, which powerfully reinforces the bad habit through intermittent reinforcement (the most addictive kind). Next time you'll hold past the stop again, and eventually the trade *won't* come back and will produce a catastrophic loss that wipes out many disciplined wins. A "lucky" rule-break is more dangerous than a clean loss, because it trains you to repeat the behaviour that eventually ruins accounts.

**Q24.** **Stop trading for the day — honour the rule.** The feeling of certainty *after* a 3% loss is exactly the emotional, tilted state the rule exists to protect you from; your judgement is compromised precisely when you feel most sure. The daily-loss limit is a circuit breaker: it caps the damage of a bad day and prevents a −3% from becoming −10% via revenge trades. The whole value of a pre-set rule is that it binds you *when you least want to be bound*.

**Q25.** You're missing the ability to **diagnose *why* you win and lose and to catch emotional/process leaks** — entries and exits alone tell you *what* happened, not *why*. Add to each journal entry: the **setup/reason for the trade, the timeframe context, your emotional state, whether it was planned or impulsive, and a post-trade note on execution quality**. That transforms the journal from a P&L ledger into a feedback system that surfaces your actual edge and your actual leaks — the single highest-leverage habit in trading development.
