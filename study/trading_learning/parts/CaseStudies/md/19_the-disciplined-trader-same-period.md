# Case Study: The Disciplined Trader Over the Same Period

*Price levels are approximate reconstructions of the same choppy market weeks used in the previous chapter; treat the numbers as a realistic composite, not a tick-accurate record. Pull up the actual charts to verify — the transferable value is the process, not the exact levels.*

Same eight weeks. Same choppy, range-bound Nifty and Bank Nifty. Same ₹5,00,000 of capital. In the last chapter Rahul turned it into ₹12,000. Here, a second trader — call her Meera — runs the identical market through a different process and ends roughly flat-to-slightly-up. Nothing about her is smarter at *predicting* the market. She simply refuses to die. This is the whole point of the pair: **the market did not decide the outcome. The process did.**

## The setup

- **Trader:** Meera, ₹5,00,000, same broker, same phone, same information.
- **Instruments:** Nifty and Bank Nifty — but she mostly *sells premium or trades defined-risk spreads* on a few high-quality setups, and occasionally buys options only with a hard stop.
- **Rules she wrote down before Week 1:**
  - Risk exactly **1% (₹5,000) per trade**, hard-defined.
  - **Two losers in a day = stop for the day.** Screen off.
  - **Max 3-4 trades a week** — quality over quantity.
  - Every trade **journaled**: setup, entry, stop, target, R-multiple, and an honest note on whether she followed her plan.

## The trade — the same Week 3 expiry Rahul blew up on

Recall Week 3: Nifty near 24,000 on expiry Wednesday, the 24,200 CE trading at ₹85. Rahul put 12.7% of his capital on it and lost it all to theta.

Meera looked at the identical screen and did **nothing** with it as a buy. Her note that day:

> "OTM call, one day to expiry, index going sideways. This is a theta trap, not a setup. Skip."

Where she *did* engage that week, earlier, was a **defined-risk Bank Nifty put spread** when price failed at the top of a clear range:

- **Structure:** sell the near put spread / buy protection so max loss is capped.
- **Entry:** on rejection at range resistance, ~44,900 on Bank Nifty.
- **Stop-loss:** a hard level above the range high; max loss pre-computed at **₹5,000 (1%).**
- **Target:** the mid/bottom of the range — roughly **2R (₹10,000).**
- **Position size:** chosen *backwards from the stop* — she sized so that being wrong cost ₹5,000, full stop.
- **R-multiple risked:** exactly 1R. Known before entry.

Price faded to the range middle; she took profit near +1.8R. Not a home run — a clean, planned, boring win.

## What happened — the exit and the real P&L after costs

Because Meera traded **3-4 times a week, not 14 times a day,** her cost drag was a rounding error, not a leak. A week with four defined trades cost her perhaps ₹300-₹500 in brokerage, STT (0.1% on option sells, 2026), exchange and statutory charges combined — trivial against ₹5,00,000. Rahul, on a single revenge day, paid more in charges than Meera did in a month.

Her losing trades were *real* and she took them without drama: Week 4's stop-out (-₹5,000, exactly 1R) and Week 5's two early losers that triggered her "stop for the day" rule. That rule mattered most on the day it cost her the least — Week 5 was the exact day Rahul revenge-traded ₹65,000 away. Meera lost ₹8,000, closed the app, and went for a walk. **Her worst day was a scratch; his worst day was a wound.**

Net after eight weeks and all costs: up ~3.8%. In a market that went essentially nowhere, staying alive *was* the win.

## The lesson

**What the professional sees:** Meera and Rahul had identical information and identical markets. The variable was not insight — it was **risk architecture and self-control.** Fixed 1% risk meant no single trade could hurt her. Fewer, higher-quality setups meant she wasn't feeding the market costs and theta on marginal trades. The "stop-for-the-day" circuit breaker removed the single most destructive retail behaviour — the tilt spiral — by force, not willpower. The journal turned every trade into feedback instead of emotion.

**What the typical retail trader gets wrong about this:** They believe the disciplined trader must be *right more often.* She isn't. Meera had losing trades and losing weeks. The difference is that her losers were **capped and pre-decided,** and her winners were **allowed to be a little bigger than her losers.** Over a flat market, small edge + strict risk + low costs = survival and a slight gain. Over the same market, no edge + no risk control + high costs = ruin. Prediction was never the deciding factor.

## Transferable rules

- **Fix your risk per trade and size backwards from your stop.** 1% of capital, every time — the position adjusts, the risk never does.
- **Install a hard circuit breaker.** Two losers (or a set rupee loss) and you're done for the day. It saves you on exactly the days you're least able to save yourself.
- **Trade fewer, better setups.** Every marginal trade pays costs and theta while adding variance without edge. Absence of a setup is a position.
- **Let winners be bigger than losers.** You don't need a high hit-rate; you need average R-win > average R-loss and disciplined caps.
- **Journal every trade honestly, including whether you followed the plan.** The scorecard isn't P&L — it's process adherence. Do the process and the P&L follows over enough trades.
