# Case Study: The COVID Crash — February–March 2020

*Price levels here are approximate reconstructions of a real event; pull up the actual Nifty 50 and India VIX charts for Jan–March 2020 to verify — the transferable value is the mechanics and the lesson, not the exact ticks.*

Every trader alive in 2020 learned the same lesson at the same time: markets do not fall in an orderly line, and "buying the dip" is a strategy that gets people killed when the dip is actually the first 10% of a 40% waterfall. The COVID crash is the definitive Indian-market case study in **tail risk, position sizing, and the value of cash**.

## Case A — The waterfall (late Feb to 23 March 2020)

### The setup
Nifty printed an all-time high of ~12,430 in January 2020. The virus was "a China problem." Through February the index drifted, then late-February the selling began in earnest as the outbreak went global. What followed was not a correction — it was a repricing of the entire world's cash flows in five weeks.

### Price-action walkthrough

| Date (approx) | Nifty (approx) | What happened |
|---------------|----------------|----------------|
| ~20 Jan | ~12,430 | All-time high; complacency |
| ~28 Feb | ~11,200 | First serious break; "healthy correction" |
| ~9 Mar | ~10,450 | Oil price war adds a second shock |
| **13 Mar** | ~9,600 intraday | **Lower circuit hit; trading halted 45 min**, then a huge bounce back near ~9,955 |
| ~19 Mar | ~8,470 | Relentless lower highs, lower lows |
| **23 Mar** | ~7,610 close | **Second lower-circuit region**; capitulation low ~7,511 |

India VIX, normally 12–18, spiked toward **~85** — a level that means options were pricing daily moves of 4–6% as normal. Every rally lasted hours, not days. Circuit breakers (13 March, and pre-open halts) told you the machinery itself was struggling to price risk.

### The trade — the "buy the dip" retail massacre
The classic error played out in slow motion. A retail trader who "always buys dips" started accumulating at 11,200 ("20% off the high, bargain"). He added at 10,500, again at 9,600, again at 8,500 — averaging down with increasing size and, fatally, often on **leverage or margin**. Consider ₹5 lakh capital deployed in tranches:

- Buy ₹1.5L at ~11,200
- Buy ₹1.5L at ~9,600
- Buy ₹2L at ~8,500 (now fully invested, some on margin)

By the 7,511 low, the blended entry was ~9,400 and the portfolio was down **~20% on capital**, with margin positions facing calls that forced selling *near the bottom*. The averaging-down that felt like discipline was actually **increasing position size into a market with no visible floor** — the exact opposite of risk control.

### What happened
The trader who used margin got liquidated in the 7,500–8,000 zone, converting a paper drawdown into a locked-in ~30–40% loss and — this is the tragedy — being *out of the market* for the reversal. The cash-only, un-leveraged trader survived but sat at a painful drawdown, having spent all his powder by 8,500 with nothing left to buy the actual low.

### The lesson
"Buy the dip" has no meaning without a **framework for how much and how far down**. In a genuine tail event, price can fall 40% and each 10% down looks like the bottom. Averaging down with *increasing* size and *any* leverage is the single most reliable way retail turns a temporary crash into permanent ruin. The professional's rule: **never let a crash force you to sell.** If leverage can force liquidation, your position size is already wrong.

## Case B — The capitulation and reversal (24 March 2020 onward)

### The setup
On 23 March Nifty bottomed near ~7,511. The very next day, 24 March, the market ripped higher — and it never looked back for two years. What separated the low from the preceding "false bottoms"?

### Price-action walkthrough

| Date (approx) | Nifty (approx) | What happened |
|---------------|----------------|----------------|
| 23 Mar | ~7,511 low / 7,610 close | Capitulation: VIX ~85, maximum fear, forced selling |
| 24 Mar | ~8,318 | +~8% single day; ₹1.7L cr stimulus talk globally |
| 26 Mar | ~8,640 | Follow-through — the signature of a real bottom |
| ~7 Apr | ~8,790 | Higher highs and higher lows re-establish |
| Late 2020 | back above ~12,400 | Full recovery to pre-crash high |

The reversal signs, in hindsight but also readable in real time: **volatility peaked and started falling** (VIX rolling over from ~85 is more meaningful than any price level), the down-days stopped making new lows, and a violent up-day got *follow-through* the next session rather than being sold. Fear was maximal exactly when the risk-reward of buying was best.

### The trade — the disciplined buyer
The trader who kept **cash in reserve** and refused leverage entered *after* the capitulation confirmed, not during the fall. He bought a first tranche on 24 March near ~8,300 once VIX rolled over and the day held, with a stop under the ~7,500 low (risk ~800 points, ~10%), sized so that being wrong cost only a small fraction of capital. He added on the 26 March follow-through. Because he had dry powder — precisely because he *didn't* blow it all catching the knife at 9,600 — he could act.

### What happened
That entry rode one of the great bull runs in Indian market history. But the point is not the profit — it's that the disciplined trader's *worst case* was a ~10% stop, while the knife-catcher's worst case was liquidation and permanent loss. Same event, opposite outcomes, driven entirely by cash management and sizing.

### The lesson
Tops are events, bottoms are processes — but in a panic, the bottom often prints on **peak volatility with a follow-through day**, not on a specific price. You cannot catch the exact low and you shouldn't try. What you *can* do is keep enough cash and zero leverage so that (a) you survive the fall and (b) you have ammunition to buy *after* fear peaks. The retail trader was fully invested and margin-called at the low; the professional was patient and liquid at the low. That difference is the whole game.

## Transferable rules
- **Size for the tail, not the average day.** A 40% waterfall is rare but real; if a plausible crash can force-liquidate you, you are over-leveraged now.
- **Averaging down with increasing size into a falling market is not discipline — it's accelerating risk.** Have a max-loss and a max-allocation rule before you start.
- **Cash is a position.** Keeping dry powder is what lets you buy the actual bottom instead of being tapped out at the false one.
- **Watch volatility for the turn, not just price.** A VIX rolling over from an extreme plus a follow-through up-day is a better bottom signal than any round number.
- **Never let the market decide when you sell.** Zero forced-liquidation risk is the one rule that guarantees you survive to trade the recovery.
