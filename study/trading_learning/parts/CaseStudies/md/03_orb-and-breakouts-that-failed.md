# Breakouts That Failed: Fakeouts & Whipsaws (the Losses)

*Price levels here are approximate reconstructions of real events; pull up the actual chart to verify — the transferable value is the mechanics and the lesson, not the exact ticks.*

The last chapter showed the ORB running all day. This chapter shows the same setup lying to your face. The cruel truth of intraday trading is that the breakout candle looks *identical* on a trend day and a fakeout day — same close above the range, same volume spike. The difference only reveals itself in the next twenty minutes, after your money is already committed. On low-volatility, event-anticipating, or simply directionless days, the market's default behaviour is to **suck in breakout traders at the extremes and reverse** — a bull trap above the range high, a bear trap below the low. These are the days that pay the market makers and bleed the retail crowd. You cannot avoid them entirely; you can only size small, honour your stop, and refuse to revenge-trade. Here is how they hurt.

## Case 1 — Nifty, pre-RBI-policy chop and the double whipsaw (approx. mid-2025)

**The setup.** The session before an RBI monetary-policy decision. Big money sits on its hands ahead of a known binary event, so volatility contracts and the market drifts in a tight, purposeless range. This is the *worst* environment for ORB — there is no trend fuel, only noise — yet the range looks textbook-clean, which is exactly the trap.

**Price-action walkthrough.** (Approximate 5-minute levels.)

| Time | Nifty (approx) | What happened |
|------|---------------|---------------|
| 09:15–09:30 | 24,510–24,560 | Opening range: high 24,560, low 24,510 (tight, 50 pts) |
| 09:50 | 24,575 | 5-min close *above* range high — looks like a breakout |
| 10:05 | 24,545 | Immediately fails back inside the range — bull trap |
| 10:20 | 24,505 | Breaks the range *low* — looks like a breakdown |
| 10:40 | 24,540 | Fails back inside again — bear trap |
| rest of day | 24,510–24,580 | Chops sideways into the close, no trend either way |

Both breaks failed. Anyone trading each break mechanically got stopped twice, long *and* short, inside 90 minutes.

**The trade.** First trade: long on the 09:50 close above 24,560 at **24,575**, stop below the range at **24,500** (75-pt risk). Nifty lot = 25, risk = 75 × 25 = **₹1,875 = 1R.** Price failed back inside and the stop was hit at 24,500 — **−1R.** Discipline says *stop trading a range that just faked you.* Instead, the tempting "it's really breaking down now" short went on at 24,505 with a stop at 24,580 — and got trapped by the bear-trap reversal: **another −1R.**

**What happened.** Two stops, both correct to take (honouring a stop is never the mistake). Gross loss ≈ 75 × 25 × 2 = ₹3,750, plus costs on two futures round-trips (≈ ₹400) ≈ **−₹4,150, about −2.2R** on a day that went nowhere.

**The lesson.** The professional's edge here is *not trading.* An anticipatory pre-event range with contracting volatility is a no-trade filter — or at most one attempt, size halved, and done for the morning if it fails. The retail trader treats every clean-looking break as gospel, takes the long, gets trapped, flips short out of frustration, and gets trapped again — turning a −1R annoyance into a −2R-and-climbing spiral. The failed first break was information: *this range is not ready to trend.* The second trade ignored that information.

## Case 2 — Bank Nifty, dull mid-week fade and the over-trade spiral (approx. a Wednesday, 2025)

**The setup.** A quiet mid-week session, no domestic event, flat global cues, India VIX low. Bank Nifty opens roughly flat and builds a wide-but-hollow opening range — big candles, but no directional conviction underneath. High intrabar volatility with zero net progress is the signature of a **whipsaw day**, the most expensive kind for an active trader.

**Price-action walkthrough.** (Approximate 5-minute levels.)

| Time | Bank Nifty (approx) | What happened |
|------|--------------------|---------------|
| 09:15–09:30 | 51,200–51,450 | Wide opening range (250 pts), choppy |
| 09:55 | 51,480 | Break above high — filled, then stalls |
| 10:10 | 51,300 | Whipsaws back through the range — long stopped |
| 10:35 | 51,180 | Breaks low — short taken, then reverses |
| 11:00 | 51,420 | Back up through the range — short stopped |
| 13:00–15:15 | 51,250–51,470 | Endless chop, closes mid-range |

**The trade.** Long on the 09:55 break at **51,480**, stop **51,320** (160-pt risk, one lot = 15 → ₹2,400 = 1R). Stopped at 51,320: **−1R.** Then a short at 51,180, stopped at 51,420: **−1.5R** (wider stop, sloppy). Then, tilting, a third "revenge" long at 51,420 to "get it back": stopped again for **−1R.**

**What happened.** Three trades, three stops, on a day the index closed almost exactly where it opened. Gross ≈ (160 + 240 + 150) × 15 = ₹8,250, plus costs on three round-trips (≈ ₹600) ≈ **−₹8,850, roughly −3.7R.** None of the individual stops was wrong to take; the *number of trades* was the wound.

**The lesson.** The killer here was **over-trading a whipsaw day.** One failed break is a warning; a second failed break in the same direction-less tape is a klaxon. The professional would have been flat after the first stop, recognised the low-VIX, wide-hollow-range whipsaw signature, and shut the terminal. The retail trader instead let a −1R turn into −3.7R by revenge-trading — each loss shrinking judgment and widening the next stop. **On a chop day you do not lose money on the setup; you lose it on the tenth trade.** The discipline that saves you is a hard daily loss limit (e.g. stop after −2R or two consecutive stops) enforced *mechanically*, not by willpower in the moment.

## Reading the fakeout in advance

You will never eliminate fakeouts, but you can stack the odds. Warning signs that a break is likely to fail: a tight or hollow opening range with low India VIX; the session before a known binary event (RBI, Fed, Budget, expiry, major results); a break on unconvincing volume; and — the biggest tell — **the first pullback failing to hold**, the mirror image of the trend-day signal from the last chapter. When the break stalls and slides straight back inside the range within a candle or two, that is not a dip to buy; it is the trap closing.

## Transferable rules

- Assume any breakout can be a fakeout until the first pullback holds — a break that slides straight back inside the range is a trap, not a dip.
- Low India VIX, a tight or hollow opening range, or the session before a binary event are no-trade or half-size filters — do not run ORB into them.
- One failed break is information that the range is not ready to trend; a second attempt in the same tape is usually how −1R becomes −3R.
- Never flip from a stopped-out long straight into a "revenge" short — the reversal that trapped you is designed to trap the flip too.
- Set a hard daily loss limit (e.g. stop after two consecutive stops or −2R) and enforce it mechanically; on chop days the damage is done by the tenth trade, not the setup.
