### Q1. Ch: Preliminary & Definitions — Small Company Thresholds & Privileges (Marks: 5) [Application]

**Question:** ABC Manufacturers Private Limited furnishes the following figures as per its latest audited financial statements for the financial year ended 31st March, 2026. It is neither a holding nor a subsidiary company, nor a company registered under Section 8, nor a body corporate governed by any special Act.

| Particulars | Amount |
|---|---|
| Paid-up share capital | ₹3.90 crore |
| Turnover (as per profit & loss account) | ₹38 crore |

Examine, with reference to the Companies Act, 2013, whether ABC Manufacturers Private Limited can be treated as a "small company". Also state any TWO privileges available to a small company.

**Answer:** The expression "small company" is defined in Section 2(85) of the Companies Act, 2013. As per this section read with the Companies (Specification of Definitions Details) Rules, 2014 (as amended), a small company means a company, other than a public company, whose (i) paid-up share capital does not exceed ₹4 crore (the prescribed limit), and (ii) turnover as per the profit and loss account for the immediately preceding financial year does not exceed ₹40 crore (the prescribed limit).

The proviso to Section 2(85) excludes the following from being a small company, irrespective of the above limits: (a) a holding company or a subsidiary company; (b) a company registered under Section 8; and (c) a company or body corporate governed by any special Act.

**Application to facts:** In the present case, ABC Manufacturers Private Limited is a private company. Its paid-up share capital is ₹3.90 crore, which does not exceed the limit of ₹4 crore. Its turnover is ₹38 crore, which does not exceed the limit of ₹40 crore. Both the conditions of Section 2(85) are therefore satisfied. Further, the company falls under none of the exclusions in the proviso — it is neither a holding nor a subsidiary company, not a Section 8 company, and not governed by a special Act.

**Two privileges of a small company** — (1) A small company is required to hold only two board meetings in a calendar year, one in each half of the calendar year, with a gap of not less than ninety days between the two meetings (Section 173(5)). (2) The annual return of a small company may be signed by the company secretary alone, or where there is no company secretary, by a director of the company; it need not be signed by a company secretary in practice (Section 92(1)). (In addition, a small company is exempt from the requirement of preparing a cash flow statement as part of its financial statements, and its auditor is not required to report on internal financial controls.)

**Conclusion:** ABC Manufacturers Private Limited qualifies as a small company under Section 2(85) of the Companies Act, 2013.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards quoting the exact current limits — ₹4 crore paid-up capital and ₹40 crore turnover — and expressly checking the proviso exclusions. A common deduction is quoting the old limits (₹50 lakh / ₹2 crore or ₹2 crore / ₹20 crore); always cite the amended Rules.)*

### Q2. Ch: Preliminary & Definitions — One Person Company Threshold & Conversion (Marks: 6) [Application]

**Question:** Mr. Karan incorporated "Karan Solutions (OPC) Private Limited" as a One Person Company on 1st April, 2024. During the financial year ended 31st March, 2026, the company's paid-up share capital rose to ₹6 crore and its average annual turnover for the relevant period was ₹35 crore.

(i) State the conditions and consequences relating to conversion of an OPC into a private or public company under the Companies Act, 2013.
(ii) Advise whether, and by when, Karan Solutions (OPC) Private Limited must take any action.

**Answer:** A One Person Company (OPC) is defined in Section 2(62) of the Companies Act, 2013 as a company which has only one person as a member. The rules governing conversion are contained in the Companies (Incorporation) Rules, 2014.

**(i) Position after the 2021 amendment:** By the Companies (Incorporation) Second Amendment Rules, 2021 (effective 1st April, 2021), the erstwhile ceiling of ₹50 lakh paid-up capital and ₹2 crore average turnover for an OPC was **omitted**. Consequently, there is now **no threshold of paid-up capital or turnover** that compels an OPC to convert. An OPC may convert itself into a private or public company **voluntarily** at any time by increasing its minimum number of members and directors and by passing the required resolutions and filing Form INC-6, subject to compliance with the applicable provisions.

An OPC is nonetheless subject to certain inherent restrictions under Rule 3 — for instance, it cannot carry out Non-Banking Financial Investment activities including investment in securities of any body corporate, and only a natural person who is an Indian citizen (resident or otherwise, residency reckoned as ≥ 120 days) can incorporate an OPC.

**(ii) Application to facts:** Although the paid-up capital of Karan Solutions (OPC) Private Limited has risen to ₹6 crore and its average turnover to ₹35 crore, these figures no longer trigger any **mandatory** conversion, because the capital/turnover thresholds were removed with effect from 1st April, 2021. Therefore, the company is **not obliged** to convert into a private or public company merely on account of exceeding the old limits.

**Conclusion:** Karan Solutions (OPC) Private Limited need take no compulsory action; it may continue as an OPC, and any conversion into a private or public company would be purely at its own option.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner is testing awareness of the 2021 amendment. Full marks require stating that the ₹50 lakh / ₹2 crore mandatory-conversion trigger has been omitted. A student who says the company "must convert within six months" is applying the repealed rule and loses substantial marks.)*

### Q3. Ch: Preliminary & Definitions — Control [Sec 2(27)] & Significant Influence (Marks: 5) [Theory]

**Question:** Distinguish between the concepts of "control" and "significant influence" under the Companies Act, 2013, and explain their relevance in identifying, respectively, a subsidiary company and an associate company.

**Answer:** The two expressions are defined and used in the Companies Act, 2013 to determine the nature of the relationship between companies.

**Control** is defined in Section 2(27). It includes the right to appoint the majority of the directors, or to control the management or policy decisions exercisable by a person or persons acting individually or in concert, directly or indirectly, including by virtue of their shareholding or management rights or shareholders' agreements or voting agreements or in any other manner.

**Significant influence** is explained in the Explanation to Section 2(6) (the definition of "associate company"). It means control of **at least twenty per cent of the total voting power**, or control of or participation in business decisions under an agreement.

The distinction may be tabulated as follows:

| Basis | Control [Sec 2(27)] | Significant Influence [Explanation to Sec 2(6)] |
|---|---|---|
| Degree of power | Power to appoint majority of directors / control management or policy decisions | Participation in, not control of, business/financial policy decisions |
| Voting-power benchmark | More than one-half of total voting power (for a subsidiary under Sec 2(87)) | At least 20% of total voting power |
| Resultant relationship | The controlled company becomes a **subsidiary** [Sec 2(87)] | The company becomes an **associate company** [Sec 2(6)] |
| Nature of link | Dominant, decisive power | Material but not decisive influence |

**Relevance:** Section 2(87) defines a **subsidiary company** as one in which the holding company controls the composition of the Board of Directors or exercises or controls more than one-half of the total voting power — i.e., the test is one of **control**. Section 2(6) defines an **associate company** as one in which the other company has significant influence (but which is not a subsidiary) and includes a joint venture company — i.e., the test is one of **significant influence**.

**Conclusion:** Control leads to a holding-subsidiary relationship, whereas significant influence (at least 20% voting power) leads to an associate relationship; the two are matters of degree of dominance.

*(Full-marks tip: State the "at least 20%" benchmark for significant influence and the "more than one-half" benchmark for a subsidiary. Examiners deduct where students confuse the 20% associate test with control, or omit that a joint venture is included within "associate company".)*

### Q4. Ch: Preliminary & Definitions — Associate Company & Total Voting Power (Marks: 4) [Application]

**Question:** Alpha Ltd. holds shares in three companies as under. In each case state, with reasons, whether the company is an "associate company" of Alpha Ltd. under Section 2(6) of the Companies Act, 2013.

| Company | Voting power held by Alpha Ltd. | Other facts |
|---|---|---|
| P Ltd. | 22% | No board control |
| Q Ltd. | 15% | Alpha Ltd. participates in business decisions under a written agreement |
| R Ltd. | 55% | Alpha Ltd. controls the composition of the Board |

**Answer:** Section 2(6) defines an "associate company", in relation to another company, as a company in which that other company has **significant influence**, but which is not a subsidiary of the company having such influence, and includes a joint venture company. As per the Explanation, "significant influence" means control of at least **twenty per cent of the total voting power**, or control of or participation in business decisions under an agreement.

**P Ltd.:** Alpha Ltd. holds 22% of the voting power, which is not less than 20%. Alpha Ltd. therefore has significant influence, and as it is not a subsidiary, **P Ltd. is an associate company** of Alpha Ltd.

**Q Ltd.:** Although Alpha Ltd. holds only 15% voting power (below 20%), it participates in the business decisions of Q Ltd. under a written agreement. Significant influence can arise **either** from 20% voting power **or** from participation in business decisions under an agreement. Hence **Q Ltd. is also an associate company** of Alpha Ltd.

**R Ltd.:** Alpha Ltd. holds 55% of the voting power and controls the composition of the Board. This is **control**, making R Ltd. a **subsidiary** under Section 2(87). Since an associate company must specifically **not** be a subsidiary, **R Ltd. is a subsidiary, not an associate company**.

**Conclusion:** P Ltd. and Q Ltd. are associate companies of Alpha Ltd.; R Ltd. is a subsidiary of Alpha Ltd.

*(Full-marks tip: The trap is Q Ltd. — students who mechanically apply only the 20% test will wrongly say it is not an associate. Full marks require stating the alternative "participation under an agreement" limb.)*

### Q5. Ch: Incorporation of Company — Promoter Liability & Pre-incorporation Contracts (Marks: 6) [Application]

**Question:** Mr. Rehan, acting as a promoter, entered into a contract on 10th January, 2026 with M/s Fine Furnishings for the purchase of office furniture on behalf of "Rehan Interiors Private Limited", a company then proposed to be incorporated. The company was actually incorporated on 5th February, 2026. Fine Furnishings delivered the furniture but the company has refused to pay, contending that the contract was made before its existence.

(i) Explain the legal position of pre-incorporation contracts.
(ii) Advise Fine Furnishings and state the liability of Mr. Rehan and of the company.

**Answer:** A pre-incorporation (or preliminary) contract is a contract purported to be entered into by or on behalf of a company **before it has come into existence** as a legal person.

**(i) Legal position:** A company, before its incorporation, has no legal existence and therefore no contractual capacity. As a general rule, a pre-incorporation contract cannot bind the company because there is no principal in existence at the time the contract is made, and the company cannot, after incorporation, **ratify** such a contract (ratification requires the principal to have been in existence and competent to contract when the contract was made). This common-law position is subject to statutory relief under the Specific Relief Act, 1963.

Under **Sections 15(h) and 19(e) of the Specific Relief Act, 1963**, a pre-incorporation contract can be **enforced by and against the company** if two conditions are satisfied: (a) the contract was entered into for the **purposes of the company** and is **warranted by the terms of its incorporation** (i.e., within the objects for which it was formed); and (b) the company, after incorporation, **accepts the contract and communicates such acceptance** to the other party.

**Liability of the promoter:** Where the company does not or cannot adopt the contract, the **promoter who purported to contract on its behalf is personally liable** on the contract to the third party, and correspondingly can enforce it, since he is treated as the contracting party.

**(ii) Application to facts:** The purchase of office furniture is clearly for the purposes of "Rehan Interiors Private Limited" and warranted by its objects. If, after incorporation on 5th February, 2026, the company **accepts** the contract and communicates acceptance to Fine Furnishings, the contract becomes enforceable **against the company** under Section 15(h)/19(e) of the Specific Relief Act, and the company must pay. If the company declines to adopt the contract, Fine Furnishings can proceed against **Mr. Rehan personally**, since a promoter who contracts for a not-yet-existent company is personally liable.

**Conclusion:** The company can be made liable only if it adopts the contract after incorporation; otherwise Mr. Rehan, the promoter, is personally liable to Fine Furnishings.

*(Full-marks tip: Full marks require citing Sections 15(h) and 19(e) of the Specific Relief Act, 1963, and stating both conditions — "for the purposes of the company / warranted by incorporation" and "acceptance communicated". Merely saying the company cannot ratify, without the Specific Relief Act relief, is incomplete.)*

### Q6. Ch: Incorporation of Company — Doctrine of Ultra Vires with Case Law (Marks: 6) [Theory]

**Question:** Explain the doctrine of ultra vires as applied to companies, and its effects, with reference to decided case law.

**Answer:** The doctrine of ultra vires ("ultra" = beyond, "vires" = powers) means that a company can do only those acts which are **within the powers conferred on it by its objects clause** in the Memorandum of Association, and those reasonably incidental thereto. Any act done **beyond the objects clause** is ultra vires the company and is **void**. The doctrine flows from the fact that, under the Companies Act, 2013, the Memorandum defines the scope of a company's activities and protects both shareholders (who invest knowing the purpose) and creditors (who deal knowing the company's field of operation).

The leading authority is **Ashbury Railway Carriage & Iron Co. Ltd. v. Riche (1875)**. There, the company's objects were to make and sell, or lend on hire, railway carriages and wagons and to carry on the business of mechanical engineers and general contractors. The company entered into a contract to **finance the construction of a railway line** in Belgium. The House of Lords held the contract to be **ultra vires and void**, being outside the objects clause, and further held that it could not be validated even by the **subsequent assent of all the shareholders**, because an ultra vires act is void from the beginning.

**Effects of an ultra vires act:**

| Aspect | Effect |
|---|---|
| Validity | The act/contract is void ab initio and cannot be ratified even by the whole body of shareholders |
| Injunction | Any member can obtain an injunction to restrain the company from doing an ultra vires act |
| Company's liability | The company is neither bound by nor can enforce an ultra vires contract |
| Ultra vires acquired property | Property acquired under an ultra vires transaction is protected and belongs to the company |
| Directors' personal liability | Directors are personally liable to make good the funds of the company applied for an ultra vires purpose |

A distinction must be drawn: acts ultra vires the **company** are void and cannot be ratified, whereas acts merely ultra vires the **directors** (but within the company's powers) can be ratified by the shareholders, and acts ultra vires the **Articles** can be cured by altering the Articles.

**Conclusion:** An act beyond the objects clause of the Memorandum is ultra vires the company and void, as settled in Ashbury Railway Carriage & Iron Co. Ltd. v. Riche.

*(Full-marks tip: The case Ashbury v. Riche and the point that even unanimous shareholder assent cannot validate an ultra vires act are the twin scoring elements. Adding the distinction between ultra vires the company, the directors, and the Articles fetches the last mark.)*

### Q7. Ch: Incorporation of Company — Doctrine of Constructive Notice & Indoor Management (Marks: 5) [Application]

**Question:** Mr. Vivek advanced a loan to Zenith Ltd. against a promissory note signed by two directors, who under the company's Articles required a resolution of the Board to borrow. In fact no such Board resolution was passed. The company now refuses to repay, pleading absence of authority. Advise Mr. Vivek with reference to the relevant doctrines.

**Answer:** Two complementary doctrines operate here — the doctrine of constructive notice and the doctrine of indoor management.

**Doctrine of constructive notice:** The Memorandum and Articles of Association, on registration, become **public documents** open to inspection by anyone. Every person dealing with the company is therefore **deemed to have constructive notice** of their contents, whether or not he actually reads them. An outsider is presumed to know the powers and limitations contained therein.

**Doctrine of indoor management (Turquand Rule):** As a counter-balance, the doctrine of indoor management — established in **Royal British Bank v. Turquand (1856)** — provides that while outsiders are bound to know the **contents** of the public documents, they are **not bound to enquire into the regularity of the internal proceedings** of the company. A person dealing in good faith may **assume that all matters of internal management and procedure** (such as passing of the required Board resolution) have been **duly complied with**. In Turquand's case, the Articles permitted borrowing on bonds authorised by a resolution; the bank was held entitled to assume that the necessary resolution had been passed and the company was bound.

**Application to facts:** Under the company's Articles, the directors could borrow with the authority of a Board resolution — i.e., borrowing was **within the company's and the directors' powers subject to an internal procedure**. Whether that resolution was actually passed is a matter of **internal management**. Mr. Vivek, dealing in good faith and having no notice of the irregularity, is entitled to assume that the resolution was duly passed under the Turquand Rule. There is nothing to suggest bad faith, suspicion of irregularity, or forgery (which are exceptions to the rule).

**Conclusion:** Zenith Ltd. is bound to repay the loan; its plea of absence of a Board resolution fails because Mr. Vivek is protected by the doctrine of indoor management.

*(Full-marks tip: Cite Royal British Bank v. Turquand and note that borrowing was permitted subject only to an internal procedure. Mentioning the exceptions — knowledge of irregularity, suspicion, forgery — shows full command and earns the top band.)*

### Q8. Ch: Incorporation of Company — Effect of Registration & Separate Legal Entity (Marks: 5) [Theory]

**Question:** Explain the effect of registration of a company under Section 9 of the Companies Act, 2013 and the principle of separate legal entity, with reference to decided case law.

**Answer:** Section 9 of the Companies Act, 2013 provides that from the date of incorporation mentioned in the certificate of incorporation, the subscribers to the memorandum and all other persons who may from time to time become members of the company, shall be a **body corporate** by the name contained in the memorandum, capable forthwith of exercising all the functions of an incorporated company under the Act, and having **perpetual succession** and, with the exceptions there stated, power to acquire, hold and dispose of property, to contract, and to sue and be sued in its own name.

The most important consequence of incorporation is that the company becomes, in the eyes of law, a **person separate and distinct from its members**. This principle of **separate legal entity** was authoritatively laid down in **Salomon v. Salomon & Co. Ltd. (1897)**. Mr. Salomon incorporated a company and held the bulk of its shares, the remaining shares being held by his family members; he was also a secured creditor of the company by debentures. When the company was wound up, the unsecured creditors argued that the company was a mere sham/agent of Salomon. The House of Lords **rejected** the contention and held that the company, once duly incorporated, is a **distinct legal person** wholly different from its subscribers; Salomon, though virtually the sole owner, was a **separate person** and, as a secured creditor, was entitled to be paid in priority to the unsecured creditors.

The chief consequences flowing from incorporation include: (a) **separate legal personality** — the company is distinct from its members; (b) **limited liability** — members are liable only to the extent of the unpaid amount on their shares (in a company limited by shares); (c) **perpetual succession** — the company continues to exist notwithstanding the death, insolvency or exit of members ("members may come and go, but the company goes on"); (d) capacity to **own property** in its own name (Macaura v. Northern Assurance Co. Ltd.); and (e) capacity to **sue and be sued** in its own name.

**Conclusion:** On registration under Section 9, a company becomes a body corporate with a separate legal existence, perpetual succession and limited liability, as classically established in Salomon v. Salomon & Co. Ltd.

*(Full-marks tip: Salomon's case is indispensable. Examiners reward students who list the distinct consequences — separate personality, limited liability, perpetual succession, property-owning capacity — each tagged to the correct principle/case.)*

### Q9. Ch: Incorporation of Company — Commencement of Business [Sec 10A] (Marks: 5) [Application]

**Question:** "Nova Tech Private Limited" was incorporated on 1st April, 2026, having a share capital. It commenced business on 20th April, 2026 without filing any declaration with the Registrar, the subscribers not yet having paid the subscription money. Examine the legality of the company's action under the Companies Act, 2013 and state the consequences of default.

**Answer:** Section 10A of the Companies Act, 2013 governs the commencement of business by a company incorporated after the commencement of the Companies (Amendment) Ordinance, 2018 and **having a share capital**. Such a company shall **not commence any business or exercise any borrowing powers** unless —

(a) a **declaration** is filed by a director **within 180 days** of the date of incorporation, in the prescribed form (Form INC-20A) and verified in the prescribed manner, stating that **every subscriber to the memorandum has paid the value of the shares agreed to be taken** by him as on the date of the declaration; and

(b) the company has filed with the Registrar a **verification of its registered office** as provided in Section 12(2).

**Consequences of default:**

| Defaulter | Penalty |
|---|---|
| Company | Liable to a penalty of **₹50,000** |
| Every officer in default | Penalty of **₹1,000 for each day** of default, subject to a maximum of **₹1,00,000** |
| Registrar's power | Where no declaration is filed within 180 days and the Registrar has reasonable cause to believe the company is not carrying on any business, he may initiate action for **removal of the company's name** from the register of companies under Chapter XVIII |

**Application to facts:** Nova Tech Private Limited, being a company incorporated with share capital, was prohibited from commencing business or exercising borrowing powers until the Section 10A declaration was filed. By commencing business on 20th April, 2026 without filing the declaration in Form INC-20A — and while the subscribers had not even paid their subscription money — the company has **contravened Section 10A**. Its action is therefore **illegal/premature**.

**Conclusion:** The commencement of business by Nova Tech Private Limited is in contravention of Section 10A; the company is liable to a penalty of ₹50,000, every officer in default to ₹1,000 per day up to ₹1,00,000, and the company risks removal of its name from the register.

*(Full-marks tip: Quote the 180-day period, Form INC-20A, and the exact penalty figures (₹50,000 / ₹1,000 per day / max ₹1,00,000). The registered-office verification limb and the strike-off risk fetch the final marks.)*

### Q10. Ch: Incorporation of Company — Alteration of Registered Office from One State to Another (Marks: 6) [Theory]

**Question:** Explain the procedure and the approvals required for shifting the registered office of a company from the jurisdiction of one Registrar of Companies to another within the same State, and from one State to another State, under the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:** The registered office of a company is dealt with in **Section 12** and its alteration in **Section 13** of the Companies Act, 2013, read with the Companies (Incorporation) Rules, 2014.

**(A) Shift within the same State but from the jurisdiction of one Registrar to another Registrar:** Section 12(5), first proviso, requires that where the change of registered office is from the jurisdiction of one Registrar to another Registrar **within the same State**, such change shall be **confirmed by the Regional Director** on an application made by the company. The confirmation must be communicated within 30 days, and the company must file the confirmation with the Registrar within 60 days, who shall register it and certify the same within 30 days.

**(B) Shift from one State to another State:** This involves an **alteration of the Memorandum** (the situation/State clause) and is governed by Section 13. The steps are:

1. **Special resolution** of the members (Section 13(1)) approving the alteration of the memorandum to change the State of the registered office.
2. Such alteration shall have effect only when it is **approved by the Central Government** (power delegated to the **Regional Director**) on an application in the prescribed form (Section 13(4)).
3. Before confirming the alteration, the Central Government/Regional Director shall satisfy itself that the alteration has the **consent of the creditors, debenture-holders and other persons** concerned, or that sufficient provision has been made for their discharge (and that no interest is prejudiced). Advertisement of the application, and notice to the Registrar, the Chief Secretary of the State and affected persons, is required.
4. The **certified copy of the order** confirming the alteration, together with the altered memorandum, is to be **filed with the Registrar of each State** (the State it is leaving and the State to which it is going) within the prescribed time.
5. The **Registrar of the new State** shall register the same and **certify** the registration; that certificate is conclusive evidence that all requirements have been complied with (Section 13(7)).

**Conclusion:** A change within the same State (but across Registrars' jurisdictions) needs confirmation by the Regional Director, whereas a change from one State to another requires a special resolution plus confirmation by the Central Government (Regional Director) under Section 13, followed by registration with the Registrars concerned.

*(Full-marks tip: Distinguish clearly the four situations of office change; the State-to-State case must mention special resolution + Central Government/Regional Director approval + protection of creditors. Confusing the intra-State and inter-State procedures is the usual mark loss.)*

### Q11. Ch: Prospectus & Allotment of Securities — Civil Liability for Mis-statement [Sec 35] (Marks: 6) [Application]

**Question:** A prospectus issued by Sunrise Ltd. contained a statement, attributed to an independent expert, that the company's plant had a production capacity far in excess of the truth. Mr. Amit subscribed for shares relying on this statement and suffered loss when the truth emerged. Advise Mr. Amit as to the civil liability of the company and its directors under the Companies Act, 2013, and state the defences available to the directors.

**Answer:** Civil liability for mis-statements in a prospectus is governed by **Section 35** of the Companies Act, 2013.

**Who is liable:** Where a person has **subscribed for securities** of a company acting on the faith of the prospectus, and has sustained any **loss or damage** as a consequence of any **untrue or misleading statement** included therein (or the inclusion or omission of any matter calculated to mislead), the following persons shall be **liable to pay compensation** to every such person for the loss or damage sustained: (a) every **director** at the time of the issue; (b) every person who has **authorised himself to be named and is named** as a director; (c) every **promoter**; (d) every person who has **authorised the issue** of the prospectus; and (e) an **expert** referred to in Section 26(5).

**Application to facts:** Mr. Amit subscribed for shares on the faith of the prospectus and suffered loss on account of the untrue statement regarding production capacity. He is therefore entitled to compensation from the company, its directors, promoters and the persons who authorised the issue, under Section 35.

**Defences available (Section 35(2)):** A director or other person made liable can escape liability if he proves —

| Defence | Basis |
|---|---|
| Withdrawal of consent | That, having consented to become a director, he **withdrew his consent before the issue** of the prospectus, and it was issued without his authority or consent |
| Issue without knowledge | That the prospectus was issued **without his knowledge or consent**, and on becoming aware of the issue he **gave reasonable public notice** that it was issued without his knowledge or consent |
| Reasonable belief in truth / retraction | That, as regards every untrue statement not purporting to be that of an expert, he had **reasonable ground to believe, and did believe up to the time of allotment, that the statement was true**; and as regards an expert's statement, that it was a **correct and fair representation** of the expert's statement / the expert was competent and had given consent, which had not been withdrawn |

Additionally, no person shall be liable under Section 35 if he proves that the statement or omission was **immaterial** or that he had reasonable grounds to believe and did believe, up to the time of allotment, that the statement was true or the omission was proper.

**Conclusion:** The company, its directors and promoters are civilly liable under Section 35 to compensate Mr. Amit for his loss, unless a director establishes one of the statutory defences in Section 35(2).

*(Full-marks tip: Full marks require both the list of persons liable and the statutory defences under Section 35(2). Because the statement was an expert's, mention the expert-specific defence limb. Do not confuse Section 35 (civil) with Section 34 (criminal).)*

### Q12. Ch: Prospectus & Allotment of Securities — Criminal Liability & Fraud [Sec 34 & 447] (Marks: 6) [Theory]

**Question:** Explain the criminal liability for mis-statements in a prospectus under Section 34 of the Companies Act, 2013, and the punishment for fraud under Section 447. Distinguish criminal liability under Section 34 from civil liability under Section 35.

**Answer:** **Criminal liability under Section 34:** Where a prospectus, issued, circulated or distributed under Chapter III, **includes any statement which is untrue or misleading** in form or context in which it is included, or where any inclusion or omission of any matter is likely to mislead, **every person who authorises the issue** of such prospectus shall be liable under **Section 447**.

However, the proviso to Section 34 provides that **no person shall be liable** if he proves either (a) that the statement or omission was **immaterial**, or (b) that he had **reasonable grounds to believe, and did up to the time of issue of the prospectus believe, that the statement was true** or the inclusion or omission was necessary.

**Punishment for fraud under Section 447:** Section 447 provides that any person found guilty of fraud **involving an amount of at least ₹10 lakh or 1% of the turnover of the company, whichever is lower**, shall be punishable with **imprisonment for a term not less than 6 months but which may extend to 10 years**, and shall also be liable to a **fine not less than the amount involved and up to three times** that amount. Where the fraud in question involves **public interest**, the minimum term of imprisonment shall be **3 years**. Where the amount involved is **less than ₹10 lakh or 1% of turnover, whichever is lower, and does not involve public interest**, the punishment is imprisonment up to **5 years** or fine up to **₹50 lakh** or both.

**Distinction between Section 34 (criminal) and Section 35 (civil):**

| Basis | Section 34 (Criminal liability) | Section 35 (Civil liability) |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Criminal — punishment (imprisonment + fine) under Sec 447 | Civil — compensation for loss/damage |
| Who is liable | Every person who **authorised the issue** of the prospectus | Directors, persons named as directors, promoters, persons authorising issue, and experts |
| Beneficiary | The State prosecutes; punishment is penal | The **subscriber** who sustained loss is compensated |
| Requirement of loss | Not necessary that a subscriber suffered loss | The subscriber must have **subscribed and sustained loss** on faith of the prospectus |
| Defence | Immateriality / reasonable belief in truth | Withdrawal of consent, no knowledge, reasonable belief, expert's fair representation, immateriality |

### Q13. Ch: Prospectus & Allotment of Securities — Global Depository Receipts [Sec 41] (Marks: 5) [Theory]

**Question:** Write a short note on Global Depository Receipts (GDRs) under Section 41 of the Companies Act, 2013, including the conditions for their issue.

**Answer:** **Meaning:** A **Global Depository Receipt (GDR)** is defined in the Explanation to **Section 41** of the Companies Act, 2013 as **any instrument in the form of a depository receipt, by whatever name called, created by a foreign depository outside India and authorised by a company making an issue of such depository receipts**. In substance, a GDR is a negotiable instrument denominated in foreign currency, issued by a foreign depository abroad against the underlying equity shares of an Indian company, which enables the Indian company to raise funds from **international capital markets**.

**Statutory provision:** Section 41 provides that a company **may, after passing a special resolution in its general meeting, issue depository receipts in any foreign country** in such manner, and subject to such conditions, as may be prescribed. The relevant rules are the **Companies (Issue of Global Depository Receipts) Rules, 2014**, read with the applicable scheme/regulations notified by the Central Government/RBI/SEBI.

**Principal conditions and features:**

1. The issue of GDRs must be authorised by a **special resolution** passed at a general meeting of the company (Section 41).
2. The underlying shares are held by a **domestic custodian** in India, against which the **foreign depository** issues the depository receipts abroad.
3. GDRs are issued to and held by **non-resident investors**; a holder of GDRs is entitled to have them converted into the underlying equity shares.
4. The **holder of a GDR** may become a member of the company only upon **conversion** of the receipt into the underlying shares; till then the voting and other rights are exercised in the manner provided in the scheme.
5. The proceeds are received in **foreign currency**, giving the company access to foreign capital, a wider investor base and, often, a better valuation, while investors gain exposure to Indian equity without a direct Indian shareholding.

**Conclusion:** GDRs, governed by Section 41 and the Companies (Issue of GDR) Rules, 2014, are foreign-currency instruments issued abroad against Indian shares, and require the company to pass a special resolution before their issue.

**Answer:** **Private placement** is defined in the Explanation to **Section 42** of the Companies Act, 2013 as any offer or invitation to subscribe or issue of securities to a **select group of persons** by a company (other than by way of a public offer) through the issue of a **private placement offer-cum-application letter (Form PAS-4)** and which satisfies the conditions specified in Section 42, read with the Companies (Prospectus and Allotment of Securities) Rules, 2014.

If the offer exceeds this limit, it is deemed to be a **public offer** and the provisions relating to public issues apply.

5. No fresh offer/invitation shall be made unless the allotments with respect to any earlier offer have been **completed, withdrawn or abandoned**.

**Conclusion:** Orbit Ltd. may make a private placement to a maximum of 200 persons (per kind of security) in the financial year, must receive money only through banking channels into a separate account, and must allot within 60 days, failing which it must refund within 15 days with 12% interest on further default.

### Q16. Ch: Prospectus & Allotment of Securities — Shelf Prospectus & Red Herring Prospectus (Marks: 4) [Theory]

**Question:** Distinguish between a "Shelf Prospectus" and a "Red Herring Prospectus" under the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:** Both are special types of prospectus recognised under Chapter III of the Companies Act, 2013 to facilitate flexible fund-raising.

A **Shelf Prospectus** is defined in the Explanation to **Section 31** as a prospectus in respect of which the securities or class of securities included therein are **issued for subscription in one or more issues over a certain period without the issue of a further prospectus**. A class of companies, as prescribed by SEBI, may file a shelf prospectus with the Registrar at the stage of the first offer, valid for a period **not exceeding one year**. For subsequent offers within that validity, the company files only an **information memorandum (Form PAS-2)** updating the material facts, rather than a fresh prospectus.

A **Red Herring Prospectus** is defined in the Explanation to **Section 32** as a prospectus which **does not include complete particulars of the quantum or price of the securities** included therein. It is issued **prior to the issue of a prospectus**, is filed with the Registrar at least three days before the opening of the offer, and carries the same obligations as a prospectus; upon closure of the offer, the company files with the Registrar and SEBI a prospectus stating the total capital raised and the closing price of the securities.

| Basis | Shelf Prospectus (Sec 31) | Red Herring Prospectus (Sec 32) |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | To make **multiple issues** over time without a fresh prospectus | To make an issue where **price/quantum** is not yet finalised (book-building) |
| Missing particulars | None — it is complete but reused | Does **not** contain full particulars of **price or number** of securities |
| Follow-up filing | **Information memorandum (PAS-2)** for later offers | A final **prospectus** filed after closure with total capital and price |
| Validity | Valid up to **one year** from first offer | Relates to the **single** offer for which it is issued |

**Conclusion:** A shelf prospectus enables repeated issues over one year without re-filing a prospectus, whereas a red herring prospectus is an incomplete prospectus (as to price/quantum) used in book-built issues, completed by a final prospectus after closure.

*(Full-marks tip: Cite Section 31 (shelf, one-year validity, information memorandum PAS-2) and Section 32 (red herring, missing price/quantum). The comparison table is what earns the marks in a 4-mark distinguish question.)*

### Q17. Ch: Prospectus & Allotment of Securities — Allotment Irregularities & Return of Allotment (Marks: 5) [Application]

**Question:** Meridian Ltd. made a public issue and completed allotment of shares on 12th May, 2026. The company, however, did not file the return of allotment with the Registrar. Separately, in an earlier private issue, it had allotted shares to a certain group before receiving the minimum subscription. Examine the company's obligations regarding the **return of allotment under Section 39(4)** and state the consequences of default.

**Answer:** **Return of allotment — Section 39(4):** Section 39(4) of the Companies Act, 2013 provides that **whenever a company having a share capital makes any allotment of securities, it shall file with the Registrar a return of allotment** in the prescribed manner. As per the Companies (Prospectus and Allotment of Securities) Rules, 2014, the return of allotment is to be filed in **Form PAS-3 within 30 days** of the allotment, containing the particulars of the allottees, the number and nominal amount of shares allotted, the amount paid or payable, and, where shares are allotted as fully or partly paid up otherwise than in cash, the relevant contract/valuation particulars.

### Q18. Ch: Share Capital & Debentures — Reduction vs Diminution vs Buy-back (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** The Board of Sunrise Textiles Ltd. is confused between three routes that all shrink the figure of share capital appearing in its Balance Sheet — "reduction of share capital", "diminution of share capital" and "buy-back of securities". Distinguish these three concepts with reference to the relevant sections of the Companies Act, 2013, and state which of them does NOT require the confirmation of the Tribunal.

**Answer:**
The three concepts, though all reduce the capital figure, arise under different provisions of the Companies Act, 2013 and carry different legal consequences.

**Reduction of share capital** is governed by **Section 66**. It is a deliberate, court-supervised scaling down of the issued/paid-up capital — for example, by extinguishing liability on partly paid shares, cancelling capital lost or unrepresented by available assets, or paying off capital which is in excess of the wants of the company. It requires a **special resolution** and must be **confirmed by an order of the Tribunal (NCLT)** under Section 66(1).

**Diminution of share capital** is dealt with under **Section 61(1)(e)** as a form of alteration of the authorised capital by a limited company having a share capital, if so authorised by its articles. Here the company merely **cancels shares which have NOT been taken or agreed to be taken** by any person and diminishes the amount of its authorised capital by the amount of the shares so cancelled. Under **Section 61(2)**, such cancellation is expressly deemed **NOT to be a reduction of share capital**, and therefore does **not** require confirmation of the Tribunal — an **ordinary resolution** in general meeting suffices.

**Buy-back of securities** is governed by **Section 68**. It is the repurchase by a company of its own shares/specified securities out of free reserves, securities premium account or proceeds of a fresh issue, subject to the ceilings (10% by Board resolution; 25% of paid-up capital and free reserves by special resolution) and the debt-equity ratio of 2:1. Buy-back does **not** require Tribunal confirmation, but bought-back shares must be **physically extinguished and destroyed within 7 days**.

| Basis | Reduction (Sec 66) | Diminution (Sec 61) | Buy-back (Sec 68) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nature | Reduction of paid-up capital | Cancellation of un-issued/un-subscribed shares | Repurchase of own shares |
| Resolution | Special resolution | Ordinary resolution | Board (10%)/Special (25%) |
| Tribunal confirmation | **Required** | Not required | Not required |
| Effect on capital | Genuine reduction | Deemed NOT a reduction | Reduction + extinguishment |

**Conclusion:** Diminution under Section 61(1)(e) and buy-back under Section 68 do NOT require Tribunal confirmation; only a reduction under Section 66 does.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the express citation of Section 61(2) which "deems" diminution not to be a reduction — students who miss this lose the distinguishing mark. Presenting the distinction as a table earns the presentation mark.)*

### Q20. Ch: Acceptance of Deposits — Consequences of Failure to Repay & Role of Tribunal (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Metro Foods Ltd. has accepted deposits from its members under Section 73(2) but is unable to repay the deposits or the interest thereon on the due date owing to a liquidity crunch. Explain the remedies available to the depositors and the powers of the Tribunal (NCLT) in this situation, along with the penal consequences on the company and its officers under the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:**
Where a company fails to repay a deposit or part thereof or any interest thereon on the due date, the Act provides both a remedial mechanism before the Tribunal and penal consequences.

**Remedy before the Tribunal — Section 73(4):** Where a company fails to repay the deposit or part thereof or any interest thereon under Section 73, the **depositor concerned may apply to the Tribunal** for an order directing the company to pay the sum due or for any loss or damage incurred by him as a result of such non-payment and for such other orders as the Tribunal may deem fit.

**Wider power of the Tribunal — Section 73(4) read with Section 74:** The Tribunal is empowered to pass appropriate orders. Additionally, under **Section 74**, where deposits accepted before the commencement of the Act remain unpaid, and the company files a statement, the Tribunal may on application allow **further time** for repayment if it considers the company's financial position and other circumstances justify it.

**Penal consequences — Section 74(3):** If a company fails to repay the deposit or part thereof or any interest thereon within the time specified or such further time as allowed by the Tribunal, the **company shall be punishable with a fine of not less than ₹1 crore or twice the amount of deposit so accepted, whichever is lower, extending up to ₹10 crore**; and **every officer of the company who is in default** shall be punishable with **imprisonment which may extend to 7 years and with fine of not less than ₹25 lakh extending up to ₹2 crore**.

**Contravention — Section 76A:** For any contravention of Sections 73 to 76 or the rules, the company is punishable with the fine stated in Section 74(3), and defaulting officers are liable to imprisonment and fine as above.

**Conclusion:** The depositor may approach the Tribunal under Section 73(4) for a repayment order; the company and its officers face heavy monetary penalties under Sections 74(3) and 76A, and officers may incur unlimited personal liability and Section 447 fraud liability under Section 75.

### Q21. Ch: Share Capital & Debentures — Debenture Redemption Reserve & Creation of Charge (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the requirement of creating a **Debenture Redemption Reserve (DRR)** under Section 71 read with Rule 18 of the Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules, 2014, and state whether every issue of debentures must be secured by a charge on the assets of the company.

**Answer:**
The issue and redemption of debentures is governed by **Section 71** read with **Rule 18 of the Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules, 2014**.

**Debenture Redemption Reserve (DRR) — Section 71(4) and Rule 18(7):** Where debentures are issued by a company, the company shall create a **Debenture Redemption Reserve account out of the profits of the company available for payment of dividend**, and the amount credited to such account shall **not be utilised by the company except for the redemption of debentures**.

As per the amended Rule 18(7), the requirement of DRR is:
- **No DRR is required** for debentures issued by **All India Financial Institutions** regulated by RBI and Banking Companies (for both public and privately placed debentures), and for **listed companies** (NBFCs, HFCs and other listed companies) — for both public issues and private placements.

- For **other unlisted companies**, DRR is required equal to **at least 10% of the outstanding value of debentures**.

However, a company may also issue **unsecured debentures**; but a debenture carrying a charge is a "secured debenture" only if it complies with Rule 18.

### Q22. Ch: Share Capital & Debentures — Buy-back Ceiling & Debt-Equity Test (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** The following is the summarised Balance Sheet position of Pioneer Alloys Ltd. as on 31st March, 2026. The Board proposes to buy back equity shares by passing a special resolution. Examine whether the proposed buy-back of ₹90 lakh of equity shares is permissible under Section 68 of the Companies Act, 2013.

| Particulars | ₹ (in lakh) |
|---|---|
| Paid-up equity share capital (shares of ₹10 each) | 200 |
| Free reserves (General reserve ₹120 + Surplus in P&L ₹40) | 160 |
| Securities premium account | 40 |
| Secured loans | 480 |
| Unsecured loans | 120 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Maximum buy-back by value — 25% of (paid-up capital + free reserves)**
Section 68(2)(c) permits buy-back up to **25% of the aggregate of paid-up capital and free reserves** where approved by special resolution.
Aggregate = Paid-up equity capital ₹200 lakh + Free reserves ₹160 lakh + Securities premium ₹40 lakh = ₹400 lakh.
(Securities premium is treated as free reserves for this ceiling as it is available for buy-back under Section 68(1).)
Maximum by value = 25% × ₹400 lakh = **₹100 lakh.**

**WN-2: 25% of paid-up equity capital test (quantity ceiling for equity)**
The proviso to Section 68(2)(c) requires that buy-back of **equity shares in any financial year shall not exceed 25% of the total paid-up equity capital** in that financial year.
25% × ₹200 lakh = **₹50 lakh (by nominal/face value of equity capital).**

**WN-3: Debt-Equity ratio test — Section 68(2)(d)**
After buy-back, the ratio of aggregate secured and unsecured debts to (paid-up capital + free reserves) must not be more than **2:1**.

| Step | Computation | ₹ (lakh) |
|---|---|---|
| Total debt (secured 480 + unsecured 120) | | 600 |
| Owned funds pre buy-back (Cap 200 + Free res 160 + Sec prem 40) | | 400 |
| Buy-back amount (proposed) | | 90 |
| Owned funds after buy-back (400 − 90) | | 310 |
| Post buy-back ratio | 600 ÷ 310 | **1.94 : 1** |

The post buy-back debt-equity ratio of **1.94 : 1 is within the 2:1 limit** — this test is satisfied.

**Conclusion / Answer:**
Although the value ceiling (₹100 lakh, WN-1) and the debt-equity ratio (1.94:1, WN-3) are satisfied, the **buy-back of equity shares in a financial year cannot exceed 25% of the paid-up EQUITY capital, i.e., ₹50 lakh (WN-2)**. The proposed buy-back of **₹90 lakh exceeds this ₹50 lakh limit** and is therefore **NOT permissible**. The company may buy back equity shares only up to **₹50 lakh** in the financial year.

### Q24. Ch: Share Capital & Debentures — Procedure for Reduction of Capital under Section 66 (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Crestwood Ceramics Ltd. wishes to reduce its share capital as a large part of its capital is lost and not represented by available assets. As the company's legal advisor, outline the procedure and the safeguards for creditors under Section 66 of the Companies Act, 2013, and state the effect of the Tribunal's order.

**Answer:**
The reduction of share capital is governed by **Section 66** and must follow the statutory procedure.

**(1) Authority in articles and special resolution:** The company must be authorised by its **articles**, and must pass a **special resolution** for reducing the share capital in any of the modes in Section 66(1) — here, cancelling paid-up capital which is lost or unrepresented by available assets.

### Q29. Ch: Share Capital & Debentures — Registration of Charge & Condonation of Delay (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the time-limit for registration of a charge created by a company under Section 77 of the Companies Act, 2013, and the powers of the Registrar and the Central Government to allow a delayed registration of the charge.

**Answer:**
The creation and registration of charges is governed by **Sections 77 to 87** of the Companies Act, 2013.

**Duty to register — Section 77(1):** It shall be the duty of every company creating a charge within or outside India, on its property or assets or any of its undertakings, whether tangible or otherwise, to **register the particulars of the charge** with the **Registrar within 30 days of its creation**, in the prescribed form (CHG-1/CHG-9) on payment of fees.

**First proviso — 60 days with additional fee:** The Registrar may, on an application by the company, **allow registration within a further period of 60 days** (i.e., a total of up to 90 days from creation) after the initial 30 days, **on payment of such additional fees** as prescribed, if the Registrar is satisfied that the company had **sufficient cause** for not filing within 30 days.

**Second proviso — further 60 days with ad valorem fee:** If registration is not made even within that period, the Registrar may, on an application, **allow registration within a further period of 60 days** on payment of such **ad valorem fees** as prescribed. (These extended timelines under the provisos apply to charges created on or after the commencement of the Companies (Amendment) Act, 2019; different outer limits applied earlier.)

**Effect of non-registration — Section 77(3):** No charge created by a company shall be **taken into account by the liquidator or any other creditor** unless it is duly registered and a **certificate of registration** is issued by the Registrar. Section 77(4) provides that this does not prejudice any contract or obligation for repayment of the money secured.

**Rectification by Central Government — Section 87:** The Central Government, on being satisfied that the omission to register a charge within time or the omission/misstatement of particulars was **accidental or due to inadvertence** or some other sufficient cause not prejudicial to creditors/shareholders, or that it is **just and equitable** to grant relief, may on application direct that the **time for registration be extended** or the omission/misstatement be rectified.

**Conclusion:** A charge must be registered within 30 days of creation; the Registrar may extend by 60 days (with additional fee) and a further 60 days (with ad valorem fee), while the Central Government may under Section 87 condone delay/rectify particulars where the omission was accidental or it is just and equitable to do so.

*(Full-marks tip: Distinguish the Registrar's power to extend time (Sec 77 provisos) from the Central Government's power to rectify/condone (Sec 87) — many candidates conflate the two. Citing the "certificate of registration" consequence under Sec 77(3) earns the application mark.)*

### Q30. Ch: Share Capital & Debentures — Sweat Equity vs ESOP (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between **Sweat Equity Shares** (Section 54) and shares issued under an **Employees' Stock Option Scheme (ESOP)** (Section 62(1)(b)). Also state the ceiling on issue of sweat equity shares in a year.

**Answer:**
Both sweat equity shares and ESOP are mechanisms of issuing shares to employees/directors, but they differ substantially in nature and legal basis.

**Sweat equity shares — Section 54:** These are equity shares **issued by a company to its directors or employees at a discount or for consideration other than cash**, for providing their **know-how or making available rights in the nature of intellectual property rights or value additions**. They are issued by passing a **special resolution** specifying the number of shares, current market price, consideration and the class of directors/employees, and are subject to a **lock-in of 3 years**.

**ESOP — Section 62(1)(b):** This is an **option (right, not obligation) granted to employees to subscribe** to the shares of the company at a **pre-determined price** on a future date, subject to vesting. It is a further issue of capital requiring a **special resolution** and compliance with Rule 12.

| Basis | Sweat Equity (Sec 54) | ESOP (Sec 62(1)(b)) |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Shares issued for know-how/IPR/value addition | Option to buy shares in future |
| Consideration | Discount or non-cash | Cash at exercise (pre-determined price) |
| Vesting/exercise | Directly allotted | Requires vesting (min 1 year) then exercise |
| Lock-in | 3 years mandatory | As per scheme |
| Provision | Section 54 + Rule 8 | Section 62(1)(b) + Rule 12 |

**Ceiling on sweat equity — Rule 8:** A company shall not issue sweat equity shares for **more than 15% of the existing paid-up equity share capital in a year OR shares of the issue value of ₹5 crore, whichever is higher**; and the issuance in the company shall **not exceed 25% of the paid-up equity capital at any time**. (For a start-up company, up to 50% of paid-up capital is permitted within the first 10 years.)

**Conclusion:** Sweat equity is an outright allotment for know-how/IPR at a discount or non-cash consideration with a 3-year lock-in, whereas ESOP is merely an option to subscribe later; sweat equity in a year is capped at the higher of 15% of paid-up capital or ₹5 crore, subject to the 25% overall ceiling.

*(Full-marks tip: State the "higher of 15% or ₹5 crore" yearly ceiling AND the 25% overall cap — quoting only one loses a mark. The 3-year lock-in and the "IPR/value addition" purpose are the defining features examiners look for.)*

### Q31. Ch: Acceptance of Deposits — Amounts Not Treated as Deposits (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Examine, with reference to Rule 2(1)(c) of the Companies (Acceptance of Deposits) Rules, 2014, whether the following amounts received by Larkspur Industries Ltd. are to be treated as "deposits":
(i) ₹15 lakh received as advance from a customer against supply of goods, appropriated fully within 300 days.
(ii) ₹40 lakh received from the Managing Director out of his own funds as an unsecured loan, accompanied by his declaration that it is not from borrowed funds.
(iii) ₹25 lakh raised by issue of commercial paper.
(iv) ₹10 lakh received as a non-interest-bearing security deposit from an employee, equal to his three months' salary.

**Answer:**
Whether an amount is a "deposit" depends on the exclusions in **Rule 2(1)(c) of the Companies (Acceptance of Deposits) Rules, 2014**, which lists receipts that are **NOT** deposits.

**(i) Advance from customer against supply of goods (₹15 lakh):** Under Rule 2(1)(c)(xii), an advance received in connection with consideration for the **supply of goods or provision of services**, accounted for as such, is **not a deposit** provided it is **appropriated within 365 days** from acceptance. Here it is appropriated within **300 days** (< 365 days), so it is **NOT a deposit**.

**(ii) Unsecured loan from Managing Director (₹40 lakh):** Under Rule 2(1)(c)(viii), any amount received from a **director of the company** (or a relative of a director, in the case of a private company) is **not a deposit**, provided the director furnishes a **written declaration that the amount is not being given out of funds acquired by him by borrowing or accepting loans/deposits from others**. **(iv) Security deposit from an employee (₹10 lakh):** Under Rule 2(1)(c)(x), any **non-interest-bearing amount received from an employee** by way of security deposit is **not a deposit**, provided it does **not exceed his annual salary** under a contract of employment. The employee's three months' salary is well within his annual salary, so the ₹10 lakh is **NOT a deposit**.

**Conclusion:** None of the four amounts is a deposit — (i) is an appropriated trade advance (< 365 days), (ii) is a director's loan with the requisite declaration, (iii) is commercial paper, and (iv) is a non-interest-bearing employee security deposit within the annual-salary limit.

*(Full-marks tip: Each exclusion has a rider — 365 days for advances, the "not out of borrowed funds" declaration for directors, RBI compliance for commercial paper, and the "not exceeding annual salary" cap for employees. Stating the rider, not merely the exclusion, secures each mark.)*

On account of the resignation of its company secretary, no particulars of the charge were filed with the Registrar. The Board realises the omission only on 20th June 2026. Advise the company on whether registration is still possible, the outer time-limits available, and the additional/ad valorem fee implications, with reference to the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:** The duty to register a charge is governed by **Section 77(1) of the Companies Act, 2013 read with Rule 3 of the Companies (Registration of Charges) Rules, 2014**.

Under **Section 77(1)**, it is the duty of every company creating a charge (within or outside India, on its property or undertaking) to register the particulars of the charge signed by the company and the charge-holder with the Registrar in **Form CHG-1** (other than debentures) **within thirty days** of the date of creation of the charge.

For charges created **on or after 2nd November 2018** (i.e., after commencement of the Companies (Amendment) Ordinance, 2018 / Companies (Amendment) Act, 2019), the extended timelines are as follows:

| Situation | Time allowed for registration | Fee payable |
|---|---|---|
| Normal registration [Sec. 77(1)] | Within **30 days** of creation | Normal fee |
| **First proviso** to Sec. 77(1) | Registrar may, on application, allow registration within a **further 30 days** (i.e., up to **60 days** from creation) | Prescribed **additional fee** |
| **Second proviso** to Sec. 77(1) | If not registered within 60 days, Registrar may on application allow a **further 60 days** (i.e., up to **120 days** from creation) | Prescribed **ad valorem fee** |

**Application to facts:** The charge was created on **5th April 2026**. The 30-day period expired on **5th May 2026**. On **20th June 2026**, i.e., 76 days after creation, the initial 30 days and the first extended 30 days (up to 60 days, ending 4th June 2026) have both lapsed. However, the company is still within the **second proviso window** — it may apply to the Registrar and register the charge on payment of **ad valorem fees**, since the outer limit of 120 days (ending 3rd August 2026) has not expired.

The application under the proviso is made in **Form CHG-1** accompanied by a declaration in **Form CHG-10** signed by the company secretary or a director that such belated filing shall not adversely affect the rights of any other intervening creditors.

**Conclusion:** Registration is still valid and permissible. Sunbeam Textiles Ltd. must file Form CHG-1 with the Registrar on or before **3rd August 2026** (120 days from 5th April 2026) on payment of **ad valorem fees**, together with Form CHG-10.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the precise 30 / 60 / 120-day post-2019 structure with the "additional fee" vs "ad valorem fee" distinction. Students lose marks by quoting the obsolete "300 days" period, which applies only to charges created **before** the 2018 ordinance.)*

### Q36. Ch: Registration of Charges — Satisfaction of charge and intimation to Registrar (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Meridian Steels Ltd. fully repaid a secured cash-credit facility of ₹4 crore to Canara Bank on 10th March 2026, and the charge over its stock stood satisfied on that date. Owing to an internal oversight, the company forgot to intimate the Registrar. On 5th October 2026 the company approaches you. Meanwhile, the bank is unwilling to sign the satisfaction memorandum. Advise the company on (i) the time-limit for intimating satisfaction, (ii) whether belated intimation is possible, and (iii) the procedure the Registrar follows where the charge-holder does not respond, under the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:** Intimation of satisfaction of a charge is governed by **Sections 82 and 83 of the Companies Act, 2013 read with Rule 8 of the Companies (Registration of Charges) Rules, 2014**.

**(i) Time-limit — Section 82(1):** A company shall give **intimation to the Registrar of the payment or satisfaction in full** of any charge registered under Chapter VI, in **Form CHG-4**, **within thirty days** from the date of such payment or satisfaction.

**(ii) Belated intimation — proviso to Section 82(1):** The **proviso** empowers the Registrar, on an application by the company or the charge-holder, to allow the intimation of payment or satisfaction to be made **within a period of three hundred days** of such payment or satisfaction, on payment of such **additional fees** as may be prescribed.

*Application:* Satisfaction occurred on **10th March 2026**; the 30-day period expired on **9th April 2026**. As on **5th October 2026** (about 209 days later), the company is beyond 30 days but well **within the 300-day outer limit** (which expires around 4th January 2027). Meridian Steels Ltd. may therefore still intimate the Registrar in **Form CHG-4** on payment of **additional fees** under the proviso.

**(iii) Where the charge-holder does not respond — Section 82(2) and Section 83:**

Under **Section 82(2)**, on receipt of intimation, the Registrar shall issue a notice to the holder of the charge calling upon it to show cause within a time **not exceeding fourteen days** as to why payment/satisfaction should not be recorded. If **no cause is shown**, the Registrar shall order that a memorandum of satisfaction be entered in the register of charges and shall inform the company. If cause **is** shown, the Registrar records a note to that effect and informs the company.

Separately, **Section 83** empowers the Registrar to make an entry of satisfaction and release of charge **even without intimation from the company**, on evidence to his satisfaction that the debt has been paid or satisfied in whole or in part, or that part of the property has been released from the charge.

**Conclusion:** Meridian Steels Ltd. can validly intimate satisfaction in **Form CHG-4 within 300 days** (i.e., by ~4th January 2027) on payment of additional fees. The bank's refusal to cooperate is not fatal — the Registrar will issue a 14-day show-cause notice to the bank under Section 82(2), and if no cause is shown, will record the memorandum of satisfaction; alternatively the Registrar may act under Section 83 on satisfactory evidence of repayment.

*(Full-marks tip: State the 30-day rule AND the 300-day proviso for satisfaction (Sec. 82), and clinch marks by explaining the 14-day show-cause mechanism of Sec. 82(2) plus the independent power under Sec. 83 — that resolves the "charge-holder won't sign" twist.)*

### Q37. Ch: Registration of Charges — Consequence of non-registration on liquidation (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Orbit Ceramics Ltd. borrowed ₹2 crore from Mr. Ratanlal (a private lender) in January 2026 against a charge on its factory building. The charge was duly created by a registered mortgage deed, but neither the company nor the lender ever filed particulars with the Registrar. In November 2026 the company goes into liquidation under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016. Mr. Ratanlal claims priority as a **secured creditor** in the liquidation. Examine his standing, and state whether the underlying loan itself becomes irrecoverable, under the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:** The issue is governed by **Section 77(3) and Section 77(4) of the Companies Act, 2013**, which deal with the consequence of failure to register a charge.

**Section 77(3)** provides that, notwithstanding anything contained in any other law, **no charge created by a company shall be taken into account by the liquidator** appointed under the Companies Act, 2013 **or the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, or any other creditor**, unless it is duly registered under Section 77 and a **certificate of registration** of such charge is issued by the Registrar under Section 77(2). In other words, an unregistered charge is **void against the liquidator and other creditors** — the charge-holder is treated as an **unsecured creditor** and loses priority.

**Section 77(4)**, however, contains an important saving: nothing in sub-section (3) shall **prejudice any contract or obligation for the repayment of the money secured by a charge**. Thus, non-registration destroys the *security interest* (the right to enforce the charge in priority), but it does **not** extinguish the *debt* itself — the borrower remains personally liable to repay.

**Application to facts:** Although a valid mortgage deed exists between Orbit Ceramics Ltd. and Mr. Ratanlal, the charge was **never registered** and no certificate of registration was obtained. Consequently:
- By virtue of **Section 77(3)**, the liquidator under the IBC **cannot take the charge into account**. Mr. Ratanlal **cannot claim priority as a secured creditor**; he ranks only as an **unsecured creditor** in the liquidation.
- By virtue of **Section 77(4)**, the ₹2 crore debt does **not** become irrecoverable as such; the obligation to repay survives, but recovery is limited to what an unsecured creditor can realise in the liquidation waterfall.

**Conclusion:** Mr. Ratanlal's claim to be treated as a secured creditor **fails** — his unregistered charge is disregarded by the liquidator under Section 77(3), and he must prove his claim as an **unsecured creditor**. The loan is legally still owed to him under Section 77(4), but in practical terms he loses his priority security.

*(Full-marks tip: The mark split is one half for Sec. 77(3) — charge void against the liquidator, becomes unsecured — and the other half for Sec. 77(4) — the debt/repayment obligation survives. Answers that say "the loan is lost entirely" lose marks for ignoring the Sec. 77(4) saving.)*

### Q38. Ch: Registration of Charges — Registration by charge-holder and modification of charge (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Two independent situations arise before the Registrar:
(a) Vertex Motors Ltd. created a charge in favour of Union Bank on 1st May 2026 but has taken no steps to register it despite repeated reminders from the bank. Can the bank protect itself, and what are its remedies?
(b) Later, the terms of a subsisting registered charge held by the same bank are varied — the rate of interest is increased and further property is brought within the security. Is any fresh filing required?
Advise with reference to the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:**
**(a) Registration by the charge-holder — Section 78:** Ordinarily the duty to register is on the company under Section 77(1). However, **Section 78** provides that where a company **fails to register** the charge within the period of **thirty days** referred to in Section 77(1), **the person in whose favour the charge is created** (i.e., the charge-holder) may **apply to the Registrar for registration** of the charge in **Form CHG-1 / CHG-9**, along with the instrument creating the charge.

The Registrar may, on such application, **within a period of fourteen days** after giving notice to the company (unless the company itself registers the charge or shows sufficient cause why it should not be registered), **allow such registration** on payment of the prescribed fees. Where registration is effected on the application of the charge-holder, that person is **entitled to recover** from the company the **amount of fees or additional fees** paid to the Registrar for registration.

*Application:* Union Bank, being the charge-holder, need not remain helpless. On Vertex Motors Ltd.'s failure to register within 30 days of 1st May 2026, the **bank itself may apply** under **Section 78** to register the charge; the Registrar will give 14 days' notice to the company, and on registration the bank may recover the fees paid from the company.

**(b) Modification of charge — Section 79:** **Section 79** provides that the provisions of Section 77 relating to **registration of charges shall apply** to (i) a company **acquiring property** already subject to a charge, and (ii) **any modification** in the terms or conditions or the extent or operation of any charge already registered. A change in the **rate of interest** and the **inclusion of additional property/security** clearly amounts to a **modification** of the charge.

*Application:* The variation of interest and extension of the security constitutes a **modification** under Section 79. Vertex Motors Ltd. must file particulars of the modification in **Form CHG-1** with the Registrar **within thirty days** (with the same extended timelines and fee consequences as a fresh charge under the provisos to Section 77), and the Registrar will issue a fresh **certificate of modification** in Form CHG-3.

**Conclusion:** (a) Union Bank may itself register the charge under **Section 78** and recover the fees from the company; (b) the change in interest and security is a **modification under Section 79** requiring fresh filing in Form CHG-1 within 30 days.

*(Full-marks tip: Cite Sec. 78 for the charge-holder's self-help remedy (with the 14-day notice and fee-recovery right) and Sec. 79 for modification. A common deduction is treating the interest/security change as a *new* charge instead of a *modification*.)*

### Q39. Ch: Registration of Charges — Condonation of delay, rectification and punishment (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the power of the Central Government to order rectification of the Register of Charges under the Companies Act, 2013, the grounds on which it may be exercised, and the punishment prescribed for contravention of the provisions relating to registration of charges.

**Answer:** Rectification of the Register of Charges and the penal consequences of default are governed by **Sections 87 and 86 of the Companies Act, 2013**.

**Rectification / condonation — Section 87:** Section 87 empowers the **Central Government** (the power having been delegated to the Regional Director), on being satisfied of the specified conditions, to **direct that the time for giving intimation of, or for the registration of, satisfaction, be extended**, or that the **omission or misstatement** of any particular be **rectified**. The Central Government may act on the following being satisfied:

(a) that the **omission to file** particulars of any charge, or of any modification, or of the memorandum of satisfaction, **within the prescribed time**, was **accidental or due to inadvertence or some other sufficient cause**, or **is not of a nature to prejudice the position of creditors or shareholders** of the company; **or**

(b) that on **other grounds it is just and equitable** to grant relief; **or**

(c) that the **omission or misstatement** of any particular with respect to any such charge, modification or satisfaction was **accidental or due to inadvertence** or some other sufficient cause, or is **not of a nature to prejudice** the position of creditors or shareholders.

On being so satisfied, the Central Government may, on the application of the company or any interested person, on such terms and conditions as it deems just and expedient, **direct that the time for giving intimation or for registration of satisfaction be extended**, or that the omission or misstatement be rectified.

It must be noted that after the 2019 amendment, condonation under Section 87 is **available only for the registration of satisfaction and for rectification of omissions/misstatements** — the extension of time for the **creation** of a charge is now governed solely by the provisos to **Section 77(1)** (the 60/120-day windows) and is **no longer** available under Section 87.

**Punishment — Section 86:** Section 86 provides that if any company is in default in complying with any provision of Chapter VI (Registration of Charges):
- the **company** shall be liable to a penalty of **₹5,00,000**; and
- **every officer of the company who is in default** shall be liable to a penalty of **₹50,000**.

Further, **Section 86(2)** (inserted by the 2019 amendment) provides that if any person **wilfully furnishes any false or incorrect information, or knowingly suppresses any material information** required to be registered under Section 77, he shall be liable for action for **fraud under Section 447**.

**Conclusion:** The Central Government (Regional Director) may, under **Section 87**, condone delay in intimating satisfaction and rectify omissions/misstatements on grounds of accident, inadvertence, sufficient cause or where just and equitable and not prejudicial to creditors/shareholders; default in Chapter VI attracts a penalty of **₹5,00,000 on the company and ₹50,000 on every officer in default** under **Section 86**, with fraud liability under Section 447 for wilful false information.

*(Full-marks tip: Post-2019, examiners specifically test that Sec. 87 no longer condones delay in *creating/registering* the charge — only satisfaction and rectification. State the exact penalties (₹5,00,000 / ₹50,000) and the Sec. 86(2) fraud hook to secure full marks.)*

### Q40. Ch: Registration of Charges — Company's register of charges and its inspection (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Every company is required to maintain its own register of charges apart from the register maintained by the Registrar. State the statutory requirements regarding the company's register of charges, the period of its preservation, and the persons entitled to inspect it under the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:** The company's own register of charges and its inspection are governed by **Section 85 of the Companies Act, 2013 read with Rule 7 and Rule 10 of the Companies (Registration of Charges) Rules, 2014**.

**Maintenance — Section 85(1) / Rule 7:** Every company shall keep at its **registered office** a **register of charges in Form CHG-7**, entering therein particulars of **all charges** registered with the Registrar on any property, assets or undertaking of the company, and of any **modification** or **satisfaction** of such charges, including the name of the charge-holder, the amount of the charge and particulars of the property charged. The entries are authenticated by a director or the secretary or any other authorised person. A **copy of the instrument** creating the charge is also to be kept at the registered office along with the register.

**Preservation — Rule 10:** The **register of charges** shall be preserved **permanently**, and the **instrument creating a charge or its modification** shall be preserved for a period of **eight years** from the date of satisfaction of the charge by the company.

**Inspection — Section 85(2):** The register of charges and the instrument of charges kept by the company shall be **open for inspection during business hours**:
| Person | Right of inspection | Fee |
|---|---|---|
| Any **member** or **creditor** of the company | Entitled to inspect | **Without any fee** |
| Any **other person** | Entitled to inspect | On payment of **prescribed fee**, subject to reasonable restrictions in articles |

**Conclusion:** A company must keep a register of charges in **Form CHG-7** at its registered office along with copies of the instruments; the register is preserved **permanently** and instruments for **eight years** after satisfaction; and under **Section 85(2)** it is open to inspection by **members and creditors free of charge** and by **any other person on payment of fee**, during business hours.

*(Full-marks tip: Distinguish the *company's* register (Sec. 85, Form CHG-7) from the *Registrar's* register (Sec. 81). Nail the preservation periods — register permanently, instrument 8 years — and the free-vs-fee inspection split under Sec. 85(2).)*

### Q41. Ch: Management & Administration — Default in holding AGM and extension of time (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Galaxy Appliances Ltd. closed its financial year on 31st March 2025 and duly held its annual general meeting on 20th September 2025. For the financial year ended 31st March 2026, the company anticipates that, owing to a delay in finalising its consolidated accounts of a foreign subsidiary, it will not be able to hold its AGM within the normal time. The Managing Director wants to know (i) the last date by which the AGM for FY 2025-26 must ordinarily be held, (ii) whether an extension can be obtained and for how long, and (iii) the consequences of failing to hold the AGM. Advise with reference to the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:** The holding of an annual general meeting is governed by **Section 96 of the Companies Act, 2013**, and the consequences of default by **Sections 97 and 99**.

**(i) Last date for the AGM — Section 96(1):** Every company other than a One Person Company shall hold an AGM. In the case of **every subsequent AGM** (i.e., other than the first AGM), it must be held within **six months from the date of closing of the financial year**, and **not more than fifteen months** shall elapse between the date of one AGM and the next.

*Application:* The financial year ended **31st March 2026**. Six months from that date is **30th September 2026**. The previous AGM was held on **20th September 2025**; fifteen months from that date is **20th December 2026**. The AGM for FY 2025-26 must therefore be held by the **earlier of the two dates**, i.e., on or before **30th September 2026**.

**(ii) Extension of time — third proviso to Section 96(1):** The **Registrar** may, for **any special reason**, extend the time within which **any annual general meeting, other than the first annual general meeting**, shall be held by a period **not exceeding three months**. Since this is not the company's first AGM, and the delay in finalising the foreign subsidiary's accounts constitutes a "special reason", Galaxy Appliances Ltd. may apply to the Registrar (in **Form GNL-1**, before the due date) for an extension of up to **three months**, i.e., up to **31st December 2026**. No extension is available for a **first** AGM.

**(iii) Consequence of default — Sections 97 and 99:**
- **Section 97:** If default is made in holding the AGM under Section 96, **any member** may apply to the **Tribunal (NCLT)**, which may call, or direct the calling of, the AGM and give consequential directions.
- **Section 99:** If default is made in holding a meeting under Sections 96/97/98, the **company and every officer in default** shall be **punishable with fine up to ₹1,00,000**, and in case of continuing default, a **further fine up to ₹5,000 for every day** during which the default continues.

**Conclusion:** The AGM for FY 2025-26 must ordinarily be held by **30th September 2026**. Galaxy Appliances Ltd. may seek an extension of **up to three months (to 31st December 2026)** from the Registrar for special reason under the third proviso to Section 96(1). Failure to hold the AGM exposes the company to Tribunal action under Section 97 and to the penalties under Section 99.

*(Full-marks tip: Show BOTH constraints — "6 months from FY close" AND "15 months from last AGM" — and pick the earlier date. State clearly that the 3-month Registrar extension is NOT available for the first AGM. Correct forum is NCLT under Sec. 97, not the Registrar.)*

### Q42. Ch: Management & Administration — First AGM timing and business hours/place (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Nimbus Foods Ltd. was incorporated on 15th June 2025. It decided to prepare its first financial statements for a period longer than twelve months, closing its first financial year on 31st March 2026. The Board proposes to hold the company's **first AGM** on 15th January 2027, at 7:00 p.m. at a resort located 40 km outside the city in which the registered office is situated, on a Sunday which happens to be Republic Day (26th January is a national holiday; assume the chosen date is a national holiday). Examine the validity of the proposed AGM with respect to timing, hour and place under the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:** The requirements as to the timing, hour and place of an annual general meeting are governed by **Section 96 of the Companies Act, 2013**.

**(a) Time-limit for the FIRST AGM — first proviso to Section 96(1):** In the case of the **first annual general meeting**, the company shall hold it within a period of **nine months from the date of closing of the first financial year**. Where the first AGM is held within this period, it shall **not** be necessary for the company to hold any AGM in the year of its incorporation. **No extension** of time is permissible for the first AGM.

*Application:* The first financial year closed on **31st March 2026**. Nine months from that date is **31st December 2026**. The proposed date of **15th January 2027** is **beyond** the permissible nine-month period. The AGM is therefore **time-barred**, and since no extension is available for a first AGM, the timing is **invalid**.

**(b) Hour and day — Section 96(2):** Every AGM shall be called during **business hours, that is, between 9 a.m. and 6 p.m.**, on **any day that is not a National Holiday**.

*Application:* The proposed hour of **7:00 p.m.** is **outside** the permitted 9 a.m.–6 p.m. window and is therefore invalid. Further, the chosen day being a **National Holiday** is expressly prohibited. (National Holidays include Republic Day, Independence Day and Gandhi Jayanti, and such other days as the Central Government may declare.)

**(c) Place — Section 96(2):** The AGM shall be held either at the **registered office** of the company or at **some other place within the city, town or village in which the registered office is situate**. (An unlisted company may hold its AGM at any place in India if consent is given in writing or electronic mode by **all the members** in advance.)

*Application:* The proposed venue is a resort **40 km outside the city** in which the registered office is situate. This is **outside** the permitted geographical limit and is invalid unless **all members** have consented in advance (which is not stated).

**Conclusion:** The proposed first AGM is **invalid on all three counts** — (i) it is held beyond nine months from the first financial year close (no extension available), (ii) at 7:00 p.m. which is outside business hours and on a National Holiday, and (iii) at a place outside the city of the registered office without unanimous member consent. Nimbus Foods Ltd. must reschedule the meeting on or before **31st December 2026**, between 9 a.m. and 6 p.m., on a non-national-holiday, within the city of its registered office.

*(Full-marks tip: For the first AGM, the trigger is "9 months from first FY close" (not 6) and NO extension is allowed. Test each of the three parameters — date, hour/day, place — separately; the unlisted-company "all members' consent" exception on place earns the extra mark.)*

### Q43. Ch: Management & Administration — Length of notice and "clear days" computation (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** Crescent Polymers Ltd., a company having share capital, proposes to hold its annual general meeting on **Monday, 28th September 2026**. The company intends to serve notice of the meeting by **post**. The company secretary seeks your advice on the **last date** by which the notice must be dispatched so that the meeting is validly convened, and on the meaning of "clear days". Compute the position, treating service by post as effected 48 hours after posting.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Statutory requirement (Section 101(1)):** Under **Section 101(1) of the Companies Act, 2013**, a general meeting may be called by giving **not less than twenty-one clear days' notice** in writing or through electronic mode. "Clear days" means that **both the day of service (dispatch/deemed receipt) of the notice and the day of the meeting are excluded** while counting the 21 days.

**WN-2 — Adjustment for service by post (SS-2 / Rule 35 read with Section 20):** Where notice is sent by post, it is deemed to be **served after the expiry of forty-eight hours (2 days)** from the time of posting. Therefore, to give 21 clear days of notice, the dispatch must allow for:
- **21 clear days** (excluding day of deemed service and day of meeting), **plus**
- **2 days** for deemed service by post.

Total lead time required from the date of **posting** to the date of **meeting** = 21 (clear) + 2 (postal) + the two excluded days.

**WN-3 — Counting backwards from the meeting date (28th September 2026):**
| Step | Basis | Date |
|---|---|---|
| Day of meeting (excluded) | 101(1) | 28-Sep-2026 |
| 21 clear days counted backwards (exclude meeting day; the 21st clear day back) | 101(1) | Day of deemed service must be on or before **6-Sep-2026** |
| Day of deemed service (excluded) | 101(1) | 6-Sep-2026 |
| Deemed service = posting + 48 hours | Sec. 20 / SS-2 | Posting must be on or before **4-Sep-2026** |

Counting 21 clear days between deemed service and the meeting: excluding **6th September** (service) and **28th September** (meeting), the days from **7th September to 27th September** inclusive = **21 clear days**. Deemed service must therefore fall **on 6th September 2026 (or earlier)**. Since deemed service = posting + 2 days, posting must occur **on or before 4th September 2026**.

**Main Solution — Statement showing the last date for dispatch of notice:**
| Particulars | Date |
|---|---|
| Date of AGM | 28-Sep-2026 (Monday) |
| Add: exclude day of meeting → first clear day | 27-Sep-2026 |
| 21 clear days end on (day of deemed service, excluded) | 06-Sep-2026 |
| Less: 2 days deemed postal service | — |
| **Last date for posting/dispatch of notice** | **04-Sep-2026** |

**Cross-check (OR method — forward count):** Post on 4th Sep → deemed served 6th Sep (posting + 48 hrs) → exclude 6th Sep and 28th Sep → clear days from 7th Sep to 27th Sep = 21 days. ✔ Requirement of 21 clear days is satisfied.

**Answer:** The notice of the AGM must be **posted on or before 4th September 2026** so that, allowing 48 hours for deemed service (6th September) and excluding both the day of service and the day of the meeting, the members receive the full **21 clear days' notice** required under Section 101(1).

*(Full-marks tip: Full marks require BOTH exclusions (service day and meeting day) PLUS the +2 days for postal service. A very common deduction is counting only 21 calendar days or forgetting the 48-hour postal deeming rule.)*

### Q44. Ch: Management & Administration — Shorter notice and consent thresholds (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Pinnacle Traders Ltd., a company having a share capital, wishes to hold (a) its **annual general meeting** and (b) an **extraordinary general meeting** at short notice, i.e., without giving the full 21 clear days' notice. The company has **200 members** holding paid-up voting share capital of ₹50,00,000. Explain the consent required in each case, and state whether the required consent may be obtained **after** the meeting. Advise with reference to the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:** Shorter notice for general meetings is governed by the **proviso to Section 101(1) of the Companies Act, 2013**.

The general rule is that a general meeting requires **not less than twenty-one clear days' notice**. However, the proviso permits a meeting to be called at **shorter notice** if consent is given as follows:

| Type of meeting | Consent required for shorter notice |
|---|---|
| **Annual General Meeting** | Consent, in writing or by electronic mode, of **not less than 95% of the members entitled to vote** at the meeting |
| **Any other general meeting (e.g., EGM)** — company having share capital | Consent of a **majority in number** of members entitled to vote, who also hold **not less than 95% of such part of the paid-up share capital** as gives a right to vote at the meeting |
| Any other general meeting — company having **no share capital** | Consent of members holding **not less than 95% of the total voting power** exercisable at the meeting |

**Application to facts (company has share capital, 200 members, ₹50,00,000 paid-up voting capital):**

**(a) AGM:** Shorter notice is valid only if members holding **not less than 95% of the voting members** consent — i.e., consent representing at least **95% of members entitled to vote** must be obtained.

**(b) EGM (other general meeting):** Two **cumulative** conditions must be satisfied:
- a **majority in number** of the members entitled to vote (i.e., **more than 100 of the 200 members**), **and**
- those consenting members must together hold **not less than 95% of the paid-up voting capital**, i.e., not less than **₹47,50,000** of the ₹50,00,000 (95% × ₹50,00,000 = ₹47,50,000).

**Timing of consent:** The consent for shorter notice must be obtained **before or at the time of** the meeting is convened (it is a pre-condition to validly calling the meeting at short notice); it **cannot** be ratified after the meeting is over. Consent may be given in writing or through electronic mode.

**Conclusion:** For the **AGM**, consent of not less than **95% of the members entitled to vote** is required. For the **EGM**, consent of a **majority in number** of voting members holding together **at least 95% of the paid-up voting capital (₹47,50,000)** is required. In both cases the consent must be obtained **at or before the meeting**, not afterwards.

*(Full-marks tip: The AGM test is a single 95%-of-members test; the "other meeting" test is a TWO-limb cumulative test (majority in number AND 95% of voting capital). Show the ₹47,50,000 computation and state that consent cannot be given post-facto.)*

### Q45. Ch: Management & Administration — Proxies and their rights (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the concept of a "proxy" under the Companies Act, 2013. State the rights and restrictions applicable to a proxy, the time-limit for depositing the proxy instrument, and whether a proxy is entitled to speak and to vote at a general meeting.

**Answer:** The appointment of proxies is governed by **Section 105 of the Companies Act, 2013 read with Rule 19 of the Companies (Management and Administration) Rules, 2014**.

**Meaning:** Under **Section 105(1)**, any member of a company entitled to attend and vote at a meeting is entitled to **appoint another person as a proxy** to attend and vote at the meeting on his behalf. A proxy is thus an agent appointed to represent the member. A proxy **need not be a member** of the company.

**Rights and restrictions of a proxy:**

| Aspect | Position under Section 105 |
|---|---|
| **Right to attend** | A proxy is entitled to **attend** the meeting on behalf of the member |
| **Right to speak** | A proxy **shall not have the right to speak** at the meeting |
| **Voting on show of hands** | A proxy is **not entitled to vote** except on a poll (i.e., cannot vote on a show of hands) |
| **Voting on a poll** | A proxy **is entitled to vote on a poll** |
| **Membership requirement** | A proxy **need not be a member** of the company |

**Notice of the right to appoint a proxy — Section 105(2):** In **every notice** calling a meeting of a company having a share capital, there must appear with **reasonable prominence** a statement that a member entitled to attend and vote is entitled to appoint a proxy, and that **the proxy need not be a member**. Default attracts a penalty of **₹5,000** on every officer in default under Section 105(5).

**Companies without share capital / Section 8 companies:** Unless the articles otherwise provide, a member of a company **not having a share capital** shall **not** be entitled to appoint a proxy. In the case of a **Section 8 company**, a member cannot appoint a proxy unless such person is **also a member** of that company.

**Deposit of proxy instrument — Section 105(4):** The instrument appointing a proxy must be in writing, signed by the appointer, and **deposited with the company not less than forty-eight hours before the time of the meeting** in order to be valid. Any provision in the articles requiring a longer period than 48 hours shall have effect as if a period of 48 hours had been specified.

**Inspection of proxies — Section 105(8):** Every member entitled to vote is entitled, during the period **beginning twenty-four hours before the time fixed for the meeting and ending with the conclusion of the meeting**, to inspect the proxies lodged, at any time during business hours, provided **not less than three days' notice** in writing is given to the company.

**Conclusion:** A proxy is a person (need not be a member) appointed under **Section 105** to attend and vote for a member. A proxy **may attend and vote only on a poll**, but **cannot speak** and **cannot vote on a show of hands**; the proxy instrument must be deposited **at least 48 hours before** the meeting to be valid.

*(Full-marks tip: The three "cannot" points — no right to speak, no vote on show of hands, but CAN vote on poll — are the marks. Add the 48-hour deposit rule and the no-share-capital/Section 8 exception to reach full marks.)*

### Q46. Ch: Management & Administration — Restriction on number of members a proxy may represent (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** At the general meeting of Horizon Cements Ltd. (a company having share capital with total paid-up voting share capital of ₹10 crore), the following situations arise regarding proxies:
(i) Mr. Anand is appointed as proxy by **55 members** who together hold shares carrying voting rights of ₹40 lakh.
(ii) Mr. Bhaskar is appointed as proxy by a **single member** who holds 12% of the total paid-up voting share capital.
(iii) Mr. Bhaskar, in addition, wishes to act as proxy for **two other members** as well.
Examine the validity of each appointment under the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:** The numerical limits on proxies are governed by the **proviso to Section 105(1) of the Companies Act, 2013 read with Rule 19(2) of the Companies (Management and Administration) Rules, 2014**.

**The rule — Rule 19(2):** A person **can act as proxy on behalf of members not exceeding fifty (50)** and holding in the aggregate **not more than ten percent (10%) of the total share capital** of the company carrying voting rights. **Proviso:** However, a member holding **more than ten percent** of the total voting share capital may appoint a **single person** as proxy, but **such person shall not act as a proxy for any other person or shareholder**.

**Application to facts (total paid-up voting capital = ₹10 crore; 10% = ₹1 crore):**

**(i) Mr. Anand — proxy for 55 members holding ₹40 lakh:**
- The **number of members** he represents is **55**, which **exceeds the limit of 50 members**. Although the aggregate holding of ₹40 lakh is **within** the 10% limit (₹1 crore), the appointment **violates the 50-member cap**.
- **Invalid** to the extent it exceeds 50 members — Mr. Anand can validly act as proxy for **only 50** of those members (both limits, i.e., 50 members AND 10% of capital, must be satisfied simultaneously).

**(ii) Mr. Bhaskar — proxy for a single member holding 12%:**
- The member holds **12%**, which is **more than 10%** of the total voting share capital. Under the **proviso**, such a member may validly appoint a **single person** (Mr. Bhaskar) as proxy. This appointment is **valid**.

**(iii) Mr. Bhaskar acting for two other members as well:**
- Since Mr. Bhaskar is already the proxy of a member holding **more than 10%**, the proviso expressly bars him from acting as proxy for **any other person or shareholder**. Therefore, his acting as proxy for the **two additional members is invalid**.

**Conclusion:**
| Situation | Validity |
|---|---|
| (i) Anand — 55 members / ₹40 lakh | **Invalid beyond 50 members**; valid for only 50 members |
| (ii) Bhaskar — single member holding 12% | **Valid** (proviso to Rule 19(2)) |
| (iii) Bhaskar also for 2 other members | **Invalid** — a proxy for a >10% member cannot act for any other member |

*(Full-marks tip: Both limbs — 50 members AND 10% of voting capital — apply cumulatively; failing either invalidates the excess. The proviso creates a special rule for a single >10% member: valid, but exclusive. Show the ₹1 crore (10% of ₹10 crore) figure.)*

### Q47. Ch: Management & Administration — Statutory registers, place of keeping and preservation (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the provisions of the Companies Act, 2013 regarding the maintenance of the register of members and other statutory registers, the place where such registers and the annual return are to be kept, and the circumstances in which they may be kept at a place other than the registered office. Also state the requirements as to indexing and preservation.

**Answer:** The maintenance and place of keeping of statutory registers is governed by **Sections 88 and 94 of the Companies Act, 2013 read with the Companies (Management and Administration) Rules, 2014**.

**Registers to be maintained — Section 88(1):** Every company shall keep and maintain the following registers in the prescribed form:
(a) **Register of members** — separately for each class of equity and preference shares (Form MGT-1);
(b) **Register of debenture-holders**; and
(c) **Register of any other security holders**.

**Index of members — Section 88(2):** Every register maintained under Section 88(1) shall include an **index of the names** entered therein. However, where the number of members/holders is **fifty or less**, maintaining a separate index is **not** required if the register itself is maintained in a form that operates as an index.

**Foreign register — Section 88(4):** A company may, if so authorised by its articles, keep in any country outside India a **"foreign register"** of members, debenture-holders or other security holders resident in that country.

**Place of keeping registers and annual return — Section 94(1):** The registers required under **Section 88** and copies of the **annual return** filed under **Section 92** shall be kept at the **registered office** of the company. However, by the **first proviso to Section 94(1)**, such registers/returns may be kept at **any other place in India** in which **more than one-tenth of the total number of members** entered in the register of members **reside**, if **approved by a special resolution** passed at a general meeting.

*(Note: The requirement of filing an advance copy of the special resolution with the Registrar was omitted by the Companies (Amendment) Act, 2017.)*

**Preservation periods (Rules):**
| Register / record | Preservation period |
|---|---|
| **Register of members** (Section 88) | **Permanently** |
| **Register of debenture-holders / other security holders** | **Eight years** from the date of redemption |
| **Copies of annual return** filed under Section 92 | **Eight years** from the date of filing |
| **Foreign register** | Same as principal register (a duplicate is maintained at the registered office) |

**Conclusion:** A company must maintain the register of members, debenture-holders and other security holders under **Section 88**, complete with an **index** (unless members are 50 or fewer), and keep these registers and the annual return at its **registered office** under **Section 94** — or at another place in India where **more than 1/10th of members reside**, if approved by **special resolution**. The register of members is preserved **permanently**, while debenture registers and annual returns are preserved for **eight years**.

*(Full-marks tip: Cite Sec. 88 for the registers/index and Sec. 94 for the place-of-keeping + the "1/10th members reside + special resolution" exception. The permanent-vs-8-year preservation distinction is a frequent mark-scoring point.)*

### Q48. Ch: Management & Administration — Inspection of registers and returns, and closure of register (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Two shareholders of Everest Chemicals Ltd. make the following requests:
(i) Mr. Prakash, a member, seeks to **inspect the register of members and the annual return** and to obtain **copies** of certain entries. The company demands a fee and, for copies, wants 15 days to comply.
(ii) An outsider, Mr. Qadir (not a member), seeks to inspect the same registers.
Additionally, the company wishes to **close its register of members** for a period to finalise dividend entitlements. Advise on (a) the inspection rights and fees, (b) the time-limit for supplying copies, and (c) the permissible period and procedure for closing the register, under the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:** Inspection and closure of registers are governed by **Sections 94 and 91 of the Companies Act, 2013 read with the Companies (Management and Administration) Rules, 2014**.

**(a) Right of inspection — Section 94(2):** The registers and their indices (except when closed under Section 91), and the copies of all returns, shall be **open for inspection during business hours** by:
| Person | Right | Fee |
|---|---|---|
| Any **member, debenture-holder, other security holder or beneficial owner** | Entitled to inspect | **Without payment of any fee** |
| Any **other person** | Entitled to inspect | On payment of such **fees** as prescribed in the articles (not exceeding ₹50 per inspection) |

*Application:* **(i)** Mr. Prakash, being a **member**, is entitled to inspect the register of members and the annual return **free of charge**; the company **cannot demand a fee** for his inspection. **(ii)** Mr. Qadir, being an outsider, may inspect on **payment of the prescribed fee**.

**(b) Copies of the register/return — Section 94(3):** Any such member, debenture-holder, security holder or beneficial owner, or any other person, may **require a copy** of any such register or index or return **on payment of prescribed fees**. The company shall supply the copy **within seven days** of the deposit of the fee. Therefore, the company's demand for **15 days** to supply copies is **not permissible** — the copies must be furnished **within seven days** of payment of the fee.

**(c) Closure of register of members — Section 91:** A company **may close** the register of members, register of debenture-holders or register of other security holders **for any period(s) not exceeding forty-five days in the aggregate in a year**, but **not exceeding thirty days at any one time**, subject to giving **previous notice of at least seven days** (or such lesser period as the Central Government may specify), by advertisement in a vernacular newspaper and an English newspaper circulating in the district of the registered office, and on the website if any.

*Application:* Everest Chemicals Ltd. may close its register of members to finalise dividend entitlements, provided the closure does **not exceed 30 days at any one time and 45 days in aggregate in the year**, and it gives at least **7 days' prior public notice** by newspaper advertisement. Default attracts a penalty of **₹5,000 per day** on the company and every officer in default (subject to the statutory maximum).

**Conclusion:** (a) Mr. Prakash inspects **free of charge**; Mr. Qadir inspects **on payment of fee**. (b) Copies must be supplied **within seven days** of fee payment — the 15-day demand is invalid. (c) The register may be closed for a maximum of **30 days at a time and 45 days in aggregate per year**, after **7 days' prior newspaper notice** under Section 91.

*(Full-marks tip: Keep the three sub-issues cleanly separated — free inspection for members (Sec. 94(2)), 7-day copy timeline (Sec. 94(3)), and the 30-day/45-day closure limits with 7-day notice (Sec. 91). The 7-day copy rule and the closure caps are the high-value marks.)*

### Q49. Ch: Management & Administration — Annual return, its contents and certification (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** State the provisions of the Companies Act, 2013 relating to the preparation and filing of the **annual return**. Who is required to sign it, in which cases must it be certified by a Company Secretary in practice, and what is the time-limit for filing it with the Registrar?

**Answer:** The annual return is governed by **Section 92 of the Companies Act, 2013 read with Rule 11 and Rule 12 of the Companies (Management and Administration) Rules, 2014**.

**Preparation and contents — Section 92(1):** Every company shall prepare an **annual return in Form MGT-7** (Form MGT-7A for OPCs and small companies) containing the particulars **as they stood on the close of the financial year**, in respect of, inter alia:
(a) its **registered office**, principal business activities, and particulars of its holding, subsidiary and associate companies;
(b) its **shares, debentures and other securities** and shareholding pattern;
(c) *(omitted by the 2017 amendment — its indebtedness need not be stated separately)*;
(d) its **members and debenture-holders** along with changes therein since the close of the previous financial year;
(e) its **promoters, directors and key managerial personnel** along with changes therein;
(f) **meetings** of members or a class thereof, the Board and its committees, along with attendance details;
(g) **remuneration** of directors and key managerial personnel;
(h) **penalties or punishments** imposed on the company, its directors or officers, and details of compounding of offences and appeals;
(i) matters relating to **certification of compliances** and disclosures; and
(j) details regarding **shares held by or on behalf of the Foreign Institutional Investors**, and such other matters as prescribed.

**Signing — Section 92(1):** The annual return shall be **signed** by a **director and the company secretary**; where there is **no company secretary**, it shall be signed by a **company secretary in practice**. In relation to a **One Person Company, small company and such other company as prescribed**, the annual return shall be signed by the **company secretary, or where there is no company secretary, by the director** of the company.

**Certification by a PCS — Section 92(2):** The annual return filed by:
- a **listed company**, or
- a company having **paid-up share capital of ₹10 crore or more**, or **turnover of ₹50 crore or more**,

shall be **certified by a company secretary in practice** stating that the annual return discloses the facts correctly and adequately and that the company has complied with all the provisions of the Act. Such certificate is given in **Form MGT-8**.

**Filing with the Registrar — Section 92(4):** Every company shall file a copy of the annual return with the Registrar **within sixty days from the date on which the annual general meeting is held**. Where **no AGM is held** in any year, the return must be filed within **sixty days from the date on which the AGM should have been held** (together with the reasons for not holding the AGM).

**Penalty — Section 92(5) / 92(6):** Default by the company/officer attracts a penalty of **₹10,000 plus ₹100 per day** of continuing default (subject to a maximum of ₹2,00,000 for the company and ₹50,000 for an officer in default); a PCS who certifies the return contrary to the requirements is liable to a penalty of **₹2,00,000**.

**Conclusion:** The annual return in **Form MGT-7** must contain the prescribed particulars as at the close of the financial year, be **signed by a director and the company secretary** (or a PCS where there is none), be **certified in Form MGT-8 by a PCS** for listed companies and companies with paid-up capital of ₹10 crore+/turnover of ₹50 crore+, and be **filed with the Registrar within 60 days** of the AGM.

*(Full-marks tip: The high-value marks are the certification thresholds (listed / ₹10 cr paid-up capital / ₹50 cr turnover → MGT-8) and the 60-day filing limit, including the "no AGM held" variant. Signing = director + CS is a frequently-missed detail.)*

### Q50. Ch: Management & Administration — Quorum for general meetings and adjournment (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Zenith Infra Ltd., a **public company**, has **6,500 members**. At its annual general meeting convened at 11:00 a.m., only **20 members** are personally present even after waiting for half an hour. Examine (i) whether the quorum was present, (ii) the consequences of the quorum not being present, and (iii) the position at the adjourned meeting if again only a few members turn up. Would your answer differ if Zenith Infra Ltd. were a **private company**? Advise under the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:** The quorum for a general meeting is governed by **Section 103 of the Companies Act, 2013**.

**(i) Quorum requirement — Section 103(1):** Unless the articles provide for a larger number, the quorum for a **public company** depends on its number of members:
| Number of members of the public company | Quorum (members personally present) |
|---|---|
| Up to **1,000** members | **5** members |
| More than **1,000** but up to **5,000** members | **15** members |
| More than **5,000** members | **30** members |

For a **private company**, the quorum is **two members** personally present.

*Application:* Zenith Infra Ltd. is a public company with **6,500 members**, i.e., more than 5,000. The required quorum is therefore **30 members** personally present. Only **20 members** are present — hence the **quorum is NOT present**.

**(ii) Consequence where quorum is absent — Section 103(2):** If the quorum is **not present within half an hour** from the appointed time:
(a) the meeting, **if called on the requisition of members**, shall **stand cancelled**; and
(b) **in any other case** (e.g., a meeting called by the Board, as here), the meeting shall stand **adjourned to the same day in the next week at the same time and place**, or to such other date, time and place as the **Board may determine**.

*Application:* Since this AGM was called by the Board (not on a members' requisition), it shall stand **adjourned to the same day next week at the same time and place** (or as the Board determines).

**(iii) Position at the adjourned meeting — Section 103(3):** At the **adjourned meeting**, if the quorum is again **not present within half an hour**, the **members present shall be the quorum**. (Where the adjourned meeting is held at a date/time/place other than the same day next week, the company must give not less than **3 days' notice** to the members.)

*Application:* At the adjourned meeting, even if only a few members turn up, **those members present will constitute the quorum**, and the meeting may validly transact business.

**Position if Zenith Infra were a private company:** The quorum would be only **2 members personally present**. Since 20 members are present, the quorum **would be satisfied** and the meeting could proceed validly at 11:00 a.m. itself.

**Conclusion:** For the public company with 6,500 members, the required quorum is **30 members**; with only 20 present the quorum fails, so the Board-called AGM stands **adjourned to the same day next week**, at which the **members then present form the quorum**. Had Zenith Infra Ltd. been a **private company**, a quorum of **2 members** would suffice and the meeting would be valid.

*(Full-marks tip: Get the public-company slab right (5/15/30 based on ≤1,000 / 1,001–5,000 / >5,000). Distinguish requisitioned meetings (cancelled) from Board meetings (adjourned), and state the Sec. 103(3) rule that "members present are quorum" at the adjourned meeting.)*

### Q51. Ch: Management & Administration — Power of the Tribunal to call an annual general meeting (Marks: 4) [Application]
**Question:** Falcon Engineering Ltd. failed to hold its annual general meeting for the financial year 2025-26 within the time prescribed, and the Board continues to delay it despite repeated requests from shareholders. Mr. Sinha, a member, wishes to know whether he can compel the company to hold the AGM and what directions the Tribunal may pass, including on quorum. Advise with reference to the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:** The power to compel the holding of a delayed annual general meeting is governed by **Section 97 of the Companies Act, 2013**.

**Provision — Section 97(1):** If any **default is made in holding the annual general meeting** of a company under **Section 96**, the **Tribunal (National Company Law Tribunal)** may, notwithstanding anything contained in the Act or the articles, on the application of **any member** of the company, **call, or direct the calling of, an annual general meeting** of the company, and give such ancillary or consequential directions as the Tribunal thinks expedient.

**Directions regarding quorum — Section 97(1) proviso:** The proviso empowers the Tribunal to give directions modifying or supplementing, in relation to the calling, holding and conducting of the meeting, the operation of the provisions of the Act and the company's articles. In particular, the Tribunal may direct that **one member of the company present in person or by proxy shall be deemed to constitute a meeting** — thus overcoming any deliberate attempt to defeat the meeting by non-attendance and failure of quorum.

**Status of the meeting — Section 97(2):** A general meeting held in pursuance of an order under Section 97(1) shall, subject to any directions of the Tribunal, be **deemed to be an annual general meeting** of the company.

**Application to facts:** Falcon Engineering Ltd. has defaulted in holding its AGM for FY 2025-26 under Section 96, and the Board continues to delay it. **Mr. Sinha, being a member, may apply to the NCLT** under **Section 97**. The Tribunal may **direct the calling of the AGM** and pass consequential directions, including a direction that **even one member present in person or by proxy shall constitute a valid meeting**. The meeting so held will be treated as the company's AGM for the year.

**Conclusion:** Yes, Mr. Sinha can compel the holding of the AGM by applying to the **Tribunal (NCLT) under Section 97**. The Tribunal may **call or direct the calling of the AGM**, may direct that **one member present in person or by proxy shall be deemed to constitute the meeting**, and the resulting meeting will be **deemed to be the AGM** of the company.

*(Full-marks tip: The correct forum is the NCLT under Sec. 97 (not the Registrar and not Sec. 98, which deals with meetings *other* than the AGM). The high-value point is the "one member = valid meeting" direction and that the meeting is deemed to be the AGM.)*

### Q52. Ch: Declaration & Payment of Dividend — Set-off of Previous Losses & Depreciation before Dividend (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Vindhya Alloys Ltd. earned a net profit of ₹80 lakh for the financial year 2025-26 and the Board proposes to declare a dividend out of these current-year profits. The company's records disclose the following brought-forward items which have not yet been adjusted:

| Particulars | Amount (₹ in lakh) |
|---|---|
| Unabsorbed depreciation of earlier years (as per books) | 30 |
| Accumulated business loss of earlier years (as per books) | 22 |
| Proposed dividend for FY 2025-26 (before adjustment) | 65 |

The Managing Director argues that since the company has a healthy current-year profit of ₹80 lakh, the entire proposed dividend of ₹65 lakh can be paid without disturbing the past losses. Examine the validity of this contention under the Companies Act, 2013 and advise the maximum amount that can legitimately be declared as dividend.

**Answer:**
The declaration of dividend out of profits of the current year is governed by the second proviso to **Section 123(1) of the Companies Act, 2013**, which lays down that no dividend shall be declared or paid by a company from its reserves other than free reserves, and, more importantly for this case, by the **third proviso to Section 123(1)** read with the scheme of the section, a company must first set off carried-over losses and depreciation.

The settled statutory position is that where a company proposes to declare dividend out of the profits of the current financial year, it must, before such declaration, **set off against the current-year profit the amount of loss of previous years or the amount of depreciation, whichever is lower, that remains unabsorbed and unprovided for.** This ensures that dividend is paid only out of profits that genuinely remain after making good past erosion of capital.

The rule requires setting off the **lower** of (i) the accumulated business loss and (ii) the unabsorbed depreciation of previous years. Therefore the amount to be compulsorily set off is the lower of ₹22 lakh (accumulated loss) and ₹30 lakh (unabsorbed depreciation), i.e. **₹22 lakh**.

**Working — profit available for dividend:**

| Particulars | Amount (₹ in lakh) |
|---|---|
| Net profit for FY 2025-26 | 80 |
| Less: Lower of accumulated loss (22) and unabsorbed depreciation (30) | (22) |
| Profit available for declaration of dividend | 58 |

The contention of the Managing Director that the entire proposed dividend of ₹65 lakh can be paid ignoring past losses is **not valid**, because the mandatory set-off of the lower of past loss and unabsorbed depreciation is a condition precedent to declaration. Since only ₹58 lakh is available after the compulsory set-off, a dividend of ₹65 lakh cannot be declared out of current-year profits.

**Conclusion:** The MD's contention is legally untenable. After setting off ₹22 lakh (lower of accumulated loss ₹22 lakh and unabsorbed depreciation ₹30 lakh), the **maximum dividend that can be declared is ₹58 lakh**, not ₹65 lakh.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the precise "whichever is lower of accumulated loss and unabsorbed depreciation" rule; a common deduction is deducting both figures (₹52 lakh) or the higher figure — only the LOWER is set off.)*

### Q53. Ch: Declaration & Payment of Dividend — Transfer to Reserves Optional Post-2013 (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Under the erstwhile Companies Act, 1956 a company was compelled to transfer a prescribed percentage of its profits to reserves before declaring dividend beyond a certain rate. A newly qualified accountant of Suryodaya Textiles Ltd. insists that even under the Companies Act, 2013 the company must transfer at least 10% of the year's profit to reserves before declaring a 15% dividend. Examine the correctness of this statement and state the present legal position regarding transfer of profits to reserves.

**Answer:**
The statement is **not correct** under the present law. The position regarding transfer of profits to reserves has undergone a fundamental change under the **Companies Act, 2013**.

Under the erstwhile Section 205(2A) of the Companies Act, 1956 read with the Companies (Transfer of Profits to Reserves) Rules, 1975, it was **mandatory** for a company to transfer a specified percentage of its profits (ranging up to 10%) to reserves before declaring dividend above a threshold rate.

However, the **first proviso to Section 123(1) of the Companies Act, 2013** now provides that a company **may, before the declaration of any dividend in any financial year, transfer such percentage of its profits for that financial year as it may consider appropriate to the reserves of the company.** The use of the word **"may"** in place of the earlier compulsory scheme makes such transfer **entirely optional and discretionary** at the option of the company (i.e., its Board).

Consequently, there is **no statutory compulsion** under the 2013 Act to transfer any particular percentage of profits to reserves before declaring dividend, whatever the rate of dividend proposed. A company is free to transfer nil, 5%, 10% or any percentage it deems fit, purely as a matter of prudent financial management, and the quantum is left to the commercial judgement of the Board.

The distinction may be tabulated as follows:

| Basis | Companies Act, 1956 (old) | Companies Act, 2013 (present) |
|---|---|---|
| Nature of transfer to reserves | Mandatory beyond threshold dividend rate | Optional / discretionary |
| Governing provision | Sec. 205(2A) + 1975 Rules | First proviso to Sec. 123(1) |
| Key word | "shall transfer" | "may transfer" |
| Percentage | Prescribed slabs (up to 10%) | Any percentage the company considers appropriate |

**Conclusion:** The accountant's insistence is incorrect. Under Section 123(1) of the Companies Act, 2013, transfer of profits to reserves before declaration of dividend is **optional**, and Suryodaya Textiles Ltd. may declare a 15% dividend without transferring any fixed percentage to reserves.

*(Full-marks tip: State the exact shift from "shall" (1956) to "may" (first proviso to Sec. 123(1), 2013). Merely saying "it is now optional" without citing the proviso loses a mark.)*

### Q54. Ch: Declaration & Payment of Dividend — Unpaid Dividend Account & Transfer to IEPF (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Meghna Ceramics Ltd. declared a dividend of ₹12 lakh at its AGM held on 20th August 2025. As on 25th September 2025 an amount of ₹1,80,000 out of this dividend remained unpaid because certain shareholders did not encash their warrants. Advise the company on the statutory steps it must take with regard to this unpaid amount, the timelines involved, and the consequences of shares in respect of which dividend has remained unclaimed for seven consecutive years.

**Answer:**
The matter is governed by **Sections 124 and 127 of the Companies Act, 2013** dealing with unpaid/unclaimed dividend.

**Step 1 — Transfer to Unpaid Dividend Account [Sec. 124(1)]:** Where a dividend has been declared but has not been paid or claimed within **thirty days** from the date of declaration, the company shall, **within seven days** from the expiry of the said thirty days, transfer the total unpaid or unclaimed amount to a special account called the **"Unpaid Dividend Account"** opened in a scheduled bank. Here, dividend was declared on 20-08-2025; thirty days expire on 19-09-2025; the unpaid ₹1,80,000 must be transferred by **26-09-2025**.

**Step 2 — Statement on website [Sec. 124(2)]:** The company shall, within a period of **ninety days** of making the transfer, prepare a statement containing the names, last known addresses and unpaid dividend to be paid to each person, and place it on the **website of the company** (and any other website approved by the Central Government).

**Step 3 — Interest on default [Sec. 124(3)]:** If the company fails to transfer the amount within the prescribed period, it shall pay **interest at 12% per annum** on the amount not transferred, from the date of default, and the interest accruing shall enure to the benefit of the members in proportion to the amount remaining unpaid.

**Step 4 — Transfer to IEPF after seven years [Sec. 124(5)]:** Any money transferred to the Unpaid Dividend Account which remains **unpaid or unclaimed for a period of seven years** from the date of such transfer shall be transferred by the company to the **Investor Education and Protection Fund (IEPF)** established under Section 125, along with a statement of details.

**Step 5 — Transfer of underlying shares [Sec. 124(6)]:** All shares in respect of which dividend has **not been paid or claimed for seven consecutive years or more** shall be transferred by the company in the name of the IEPF, together with a statement of details. However, any claimant of shares so transferred is entitled to claim the transfer of shares back from the IEPF in accordance with the prescribed procedure.

**Conclusion:** Meghna Ceramics Ltd. must transfer ₹1,80,000 to a separately opened Unpaid Dividend Account by 26-09-2025, upload the shareholder statement within 90 days, and after seven years transfer any still-unclaimed amount together with the underlying shares to the IEPF. Failure to make the initial transfer attracts interest at 12% p.a.

*(Full-marks tip: Get both timelines exact — 30 days + 7 days for the transfer, and the 7-year rule for both money AND shares to IEPF. Forgetting Sec. 124(6) share transfer is the usual omission.)*

### Q55. Ch: Declaration & Payment of Dividend — Dividend out of Free Reserves in a Year of Inadequate Profit (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** Owing to a downturn, Nalanda Pharma Ltd. earned an inadequate profit of ₹6 lakh during FY 2025-26 but wishes to maintain its dividend record by declaring dividend out of accumulated profits transferred to free reserves in earlier years. The relevant data is:

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Paid-up share capital | 1,00,00,000 |
| Free reserves as on 01-04-2025 | 40,00,000 |
| Rate of dividend declared in the three immediately preceding years (average) | 9% |
| Net profit for FY 2025-26 | 6,00,000 |
| Accumulated losses (if any) | Nil |

Compute the maximum amount of dividend Nalanda Pharma Ltd. can declare out of accumulated profits (free reserves) in accordance with the Companies (Declaration and Payment of Dividend) Rules, 2014.

**Solution:**
Where a company has inadequate or no profits in a year, it may declare dividend out of accumulated profits transferred to free reserves subject to the conditions in **Rule 3 of the Companies (Declaration and Payment of Dividend) Rules, 2014** read with the third proviso to Section 123(1). Three ceilings apply and the dividend must satisfy all of them.

**WN-1 — Ceiling on rate of dividend [Rule 3(1)]:** The rate of dividend declared shall not exceed the **average of the rates at which dividend was declared in the three years immediately preceding** that year.
Average rate = 9%. Maximum rate permitted = **9%**.

**WN-2 — Ceiling on amount drawn from reserves [Rule 3(2)]:** The total amount to be drawn from accumulated profits shall not exceed **one-tenth (1/10) of the sum of paid-up share capital and free reserves** as appearing in the latest audited financial statements.
= 1/10 × (₹1,00,00,000 + ₹40,00,000) = 1/10 × ₹1,40,00,000 = **₹14,00,000** (maximum drawable from reserves).

**WN-3 — Mandatory set-off of losses first [Rule 3(3)]:** The amount so drawn shall **first be used to set off the losses** incurred in the financial year before any dividend in respect of equity shares is declared. Accumulated losses are Nil, so no set-off is required.

**WN-4 — Minimum balance to be left in reserves [Rule 3(4)]:** The balance of reserves after such withdrawal shall **not fall below fifteen per cent (15%) of its paid-up share capital** as per the latest audited financial statements.
Minimum reserve to be retained = 15% × ₹1,00,00,000 = ₹15,00,000.
Maximum amount that can be withdrawn from ₹40,00,000 reserves = ₹40,00,000 − ₹15,00,000 = **₹25,00,000.**

**Statement Showing Maximum Dividend Declarable out of Free Reserves:**

| Constraint | Provision | Limiting amount (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| (a) Rate ceiling: 9% of paid-up capital ₹1,00,00,000 | Rule 3(1) | 9,00,000 |
| (b) Amount ceiling: 1/10 of (capital + reserves) | Rule 3(2) | 14,00,000 |
| (c) Reserve-floor test: withdrawal leaving 15% of capital | Rule 3(4) | 25,00,000 |
| **Maximum dividend = lowest of the above** | — | **9,00,000** |

Since current-year profit is only ₹6,00,000, the balance ₹3,00,000 (₹9,00,000 − ₹6,00,000) is drawn from free reserves — well within the ₹14,00,000 and ₹25,00,000 limits, and the reserve balance after withdrawal (₹40,00,000 − ₹3,00,000 = ₹37,00,000) remains above ₹15,00,000.

**Answer:** The maximum dividend Nalanda Pharma Ltd. can declare is **₹9,00,000 (i.e., 9% of paid-up capital)**, being the lowest of the three statutory ceilings, of which ₹3,00,000 is drawn from free reserves.

*(Full-marks tip: All three ceilings must be computed and the LOWEST taken. The classic error is stopping at Rule 3(2) (₹14 lakh) and forgetting the 9% rate cap, which is actually the binding constraint here.)*

### Q56. Ch: Declaration & Payment of Dividend — Punishment for Failure to Distribute Dividend (Marks: 4) [Application]
**Question:** The Board of Konkan Marine Ltd. declared a final dividend at its AGM on 10th July 2025. Owing to an internal dispute, the dividend was neither paid nor the warrants posted to certain shareholders even by 20th August 2025. One aggrieved shareholder alleges an offence under Section 127. The company pleads that the delay was because a shareholder had given inconsistent instructions on the bank account for direct credit. Examine the liability of the company and its directors under Section 127 of the Companies Act, 2013, and whether the pleaded reason is a valid defence.

**Answer:**
Section 127 of the Companies Act, 2013 deals with **punishment for failure to distribute dividends**. It provides that where a dividend has been declared but has **not been paid or the warrant not posted within thirty days** from the date of declaration to the entitled shareholder, **every director of the company, if he is knowingly a party to the default**, shall be punishable with **imprisonment which may extend to two years and with fine which shall not be less than ₹1,000 for every day during which such default continues**, and the **company shall be liable to pay simple interest at the rate of eighteen per cent per annum** during the period for which such default continues.

Here, dividend was declared on 10-07-2025; the thirty-day period expired on 09-08-2025. As payment/posting had not been effected even by 20-08-2025, there is a default attracting Section 127.

However, Section 127 itself carves out **statutory exceptions** where no offence is committed, the relevant ones being:
(a) where the dividend **could not be paid by reason of the operation of any law**;
(b) where a shareholder has **given directions to the company regarding the payment of the dividend and those directions cannot be complied with**, and the same has been communicated to him;
(c) where there is a **dispute regarding the right to receive the dividend**;
(d) where the dividend has been **lawfully adjusted** by the company against any sum due to it from the shareholder; or
(e) where, **for any other reason, the failure to pay the dividend was not due to any default on the part of the company.**

In the present case, the delay arose because the shareholder himself gave **inconsistent/conflicting instructions** regarding the bank account for direct credit, which the company was unable to comply with. This squarely falls within exception (b) — directions given by the shareholder that cannot be complied with — provided the company has communicated this fact to the shareholder.

**Conclusion:** As a general rule the company would attract interest at 18% p.a. and the knowingly-defaulting directors would face imprisonment up to two years and daily fine under Section 127. But since the non-payment to this particular shareholder resulted from his own conflicting bank instructions that the company could not comply with, the company can validly invoke the exception under Section 127, and **no offence is committed in respect of that shareholder**, provided the company duly communicated the difficulty to him.

*(Full-marks tip: State the punishment (2 years / ₹1,000 per day / 18% interest) AND the exceptions. Naming the specific applicable exception (shareholder's directions cannot be complied with) is what earns the application mark.)*

### Q57. Ch: Declaration & Payment of Dividend — Interim Dividend in a Loss-Making Period (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** During the current financial year 2025-26, the Board of Aravalli Cements Ltd. proposes to declare an interim dividend in the month of December 2025. However, the company has incurred a loss during the period from 1st April 2025 up to the end of the immediately preceding quarter (i.e., 30th September 2025). The average rate of dividend declared during the three immediately preceding financial years was 11%. Advise the Board whether it can declare an interim dividend and, if so, at what maximum rate, citing the relevant provision.

**Answer:**
The declaration of interim dividend is governed by **Section 123(3) of the Companies Act, 2013**. It provides that the **Board of Directors** of a company may declare interim dividend **during any financial year or at any time during the period from closure of the financial year till holding of the AGM**, out of the **surplus in the profit and loss account or out of profits of the financial year for which such interim dividend is sought to be declared or out of profits generated in the financial year till the quarter preceding the date of declaration** of the interim dividend.

The section contains a specific safeguard for loss situations. The proviso to Section 123(3) states that **in case the company has incurred loss during the current financial year up to the end of the quarter immediately preceding the date of declaration of interim dividend, such interim dividend shall not be declared at a rate higher than the average dividend declared by the company during the immediately preceding three financial years.**

Applying this to Aravalli Cements Ltd.:
- The company has incurred a loss during FY 2025-26 up to the end of the quarter immediately preceding December 2025 (i.e., up to 30-09-2025). Hence the proviso is squarely attracted.
- Therefore, the interim dividend, if declared, **cannot exceed the average rate of dividend of the three immediately preceding financial years**, which is **11%**.

**Conclusion:** The Board of Aravalli Cements Ltd. **can declare an interim dividend** in December 2025 even though it has incurred a loss up to 30-09-2025, but by virtue of the proviso to Section 123(3), the rate of such interim dividend shall **not exceed 11% (the average of the preceding three years' dividend)**.

### Q58. Ch: Accounts of Companies — Signing & Authentication of Financial Statements (Sec. 134) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** The financial statements of Charminar Foods Ltd. for FY 2024-25 were approved by the Board on 5th July 2025. At the relevant time the company had a Managing Director (who was travelling abroad), three other directors, a Company Secretary and a Chief Financial Officer. To save time, the Chairperson (who was duly authorised by the Board) alone signed the financial statements along with the Company Secretary. One director contends that at least two directors were required to sign and hence the authentication is defective. Examine the requirements for signing of financial statements under Section 134 of the Companies Act, 2013 and advise whether the authentication is valid.

**Answer:**
The authentication of financial statements is governed by **Section 134(1) of the Companies Act, 2013**. It provides that the financial statements, including the consolidated financial statements, if any, shall be **approved by the Board of Directors** before they are signed on behalf of the Board and submitted to the auditor for his report.

As to who must sign, Section 134(1) requires the financial statements to be signed on behalf of the Board **at least by**:
1. the **Chairperson of the company where he is authorised by the Board**; **or** by **two directors out of which one shall be the managing director, if any**;
2. the **Chief Executive Officer, if he is a director** in the company;
3. the **Chief Financial Officer**, wherever appointed; and
4. the **Company Secretary of the company**, wherever appointed.

The key point is that the signing by the **Chairperson (where authorised by the Board) is an alternative to signing by two directors** — the requirement is "Chairperson… where he is authorised by the Board **or** two directors, one of whom shall be the managing director." Thus, where the Chairperson is duly authorised by the Board, his single signature (in that capacity) satisfies the directors' signing requirement, and the presence of two directors' signatures is **not additionally required**.

In the case of Charminar Foods Ltd., the Chairperson was **duly authorised by the Board** and has signed. Therefore, the objection that at least two directors must sign is **misconceived** — the two-director requirement is only an alternative that applies when the Chairperson is not so authorised.

However, for complete compliance the financial statements must **also** be signed by the **Chief Financial Officer** (who is appointed) and the **Company Secretary**. In the given facts only the Chairperson and the Company Secretary have signed; the signature of the **CFO** appears to be missing. To that extent the authentication is **incomplete** and must be rectified by obtaining the CFO's signature.

**Conclusion:** The director's specific objection (that two directors must sign) is **not valid**, because a Board-authorised Chairperson may sign alone in place of two directors. Nonetheless, the authentication is defective for a **different** reason — the Chief Financial Officer, being appointed, must also sign under Section 134(1). The financial statements should be re-signed to include the CFO.

*(Full-marks tip: The trap is the "Chairperson OR two directors" alternative — a Board-authorised Chairperson alone is enough for the directors' part. Full marks require also flagging the mandatory CFO + CS signatures.)*

### Q59. Ch: Accounts of Companies — Filing of Financial Statements (Sec. 137) & Penalty (Marks: 6) [Application]

The filing of financial statements with the Registrar is governed by **Section 137 of the Companies Act, 2013**.

### Q60. Ch: Accounts of Companies — National Financial Reporting Authority (NFRA) Powers (Marks: 6) [Theory]

Examine the functions and powers of the NFRA under Section 132 of the Companies Act, 2013, and comment on the correctness of this contention.

**Answer:**
The National Financial Reporting Authority (NFRA) is constituted under **Section 132 of the Companies Act, 2013** as an independent regulator to oversee the auditing profession and the compliance with accounting and auditing standards. The contention that it is merely advisory is **incorrect**, as the section confers substantive investigative and disciplinary powers.

**Functions of NFRA [Sec. 132(2)]:** The NFRA shall—
(a) **make recommendations** to the Central Government on the formulation and laying down of **accounting standards and auditing standards** for adoption by companies or their auditors;
(b) **monitor and enforce compliance** with accounting standards and auditing standards;
(c) **oversee the quality of service** of the professions associated with ensuring compliance with such standards and suggest measures for improvement in the quality of service; and
(d) perform such other functions relating to the above as may be prescribed.

**Power to investigate [Sec. 132(4)]:** Notwithstanding anything contained in any other law, the NFRA shall have the power to **investigate**, either suo motu or on a reference made by the Central Government, into matters of **professional or other misconduct committed by any member or firm of chartered accountants** registered under the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949. Significantly, **where the NFRA has initiated an investigation, no other institute or body shall initiate or continue any proceedings** in such matters of misconduct — thereby overriding the ICAI's disciplinary jurisdiction to the extent of NFRA's action.

**Powers akin to a civil court [Sec. 132(4)(b)]:** For the purpose of investigation, NFRA has the **same powers as are vested in a civil court under the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908**, in respect of discovery and production of documents, summoning and enforcing attendance of persons and examining them on oath, and inspection of books, registers and other documents.

**Power to impose penalty and debar [Sec. 132(4)(c)]:** Where professional or other misconduct is proved, NFRA has the power to make an order for—
(i) **imposing penalty** of (A) not less than ₹1,00,000, extending up to five times the fees received, in the case of **individuals**; and (B) not less than ₹5,00,000, extending up to ten times the fees received, in the case of **firms**; and
(ii) **debarring** the member or the firm from being appointed as an auditor or from any of the functions/activities of an auditor for a **minimum of six months to a maximum of ten years**.

**Appeal [Sec. 132(5)]:** Any person aggrieved by an order of NFRA may prefer an **appeal before the Appellate Tribunal** (NCLAT).

**Conclusion:** The contention is **not correct**. Far from being merely advisory, NFRA under Section 132 possesses the power to investigate misconduct by chartered accountants and firms, exercise civil-court powers, impose monetary penalties and debar auditors for up to ten years, and its investigation **excludes** parallel ICAI proceedings.

### Q62. Ch: Accounts of Companies — Re-opening & Voluntary Revision of Accounts (Sec. 130 & 131) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** The Board of Kaveri Infratech Ltd. discovers, after the financial statements for FY 2023-24 were adopted at the AGM and filed with the Registrar, that a material provision for a foreseeable loss on a long-term contract was inadvertently omitted, rendering the accounts not compliant with the applicable Accounting Standard. The Board wishes to correct this. Separately, the income-tax authority alleges that the accounts of an earlier year were prepared in a fraudulent manner and seeks their re-opening. Distinguish between re-opening of accounts under Section 130 and voluntary revision under Section 131, and advise the Board on the correct route.

**Answer:**
The Companies Act, 2013 provides two distinct mechanisms for altering accounts already adopted — **compulsory re-opening under Section 130** and **voluntary revision under Section 131** — and the two must not be confused.

**Re-opening of accounts on court's or Tribunal's order [Sec. 130]:** A company shall **not re-open its books of account and not recast its financial statements** unless an application is made by the Central Government, the Income-tax authorities, SEBI, any other statutory regulatory body or authority, or any person concerned, and an **order is made by a court of competent jurisdiction or the Tribunal (NCLT)** to the effect that—
(i) the relevant earlier accounts were prepared in a **fraudulent manner**; or
(ii) the affairs of the company were **mismanaged** during the relevant period, casting doubt on the reliability of the financial statements.
Thus re-opening under Section 130 is **not voluntary** — it requires an order of a court/Tribunal, and here the income-tax authority's remedy is to move the Tribunal/court alleging fraudulent preparation.

**Voluntary revision of financial statements or Board's Report [Sec. 131]:** If it appears to the **directors** of a company that the **financial statements or the Board's Report do not comply with Section 129 (financial statements) or Section 134 (Board's Report)**, they may prepare **revised financial statements or a revised report** in respect of any of the **three preceding financial years**, after **obtaining approval of the Tribunal** on an application made by the company, and a copy of the Tribunal's order shall be filed with the Registrar. Such revision can be made **only once in a financial year**, and the detailed reasons for revision must be disclosed.

**Distinction:**

| Basis | Re-opening — Sec. 130 | Voluntary revision — Sec. 131 |
|---|---|---|
| Who initiates | CG, Income-tax, SEBI, other regulators, any concerned person | The directors (company) itself |
| Trigger | Fraudulent preparation or mismanagement | Non-compliance with Sec. 129 or Sec. 134 |
| Authority required | Order of court or Tribunal (mandatory) | Approval/order of Tribunal |
| Nature | Compulsory, on external application | Voluntary, at company's option |
| Period covered | No fixed outer limit (subject to 3rd proviso — 8 years) | Any of the three preceding financial years |

**Conclusion:** For the inadvertent omission of a provision that makes the accounts non-compliant with the Accounting Standard, the Board of Kaveri Infratech Ltd. should proceed by way of **voluntary revision under Section 131**, after obtaining the Tribunal's approval (once in the year). The income-tax authority's allegation of fraudulent preparation is to be pursued under **Section 130** by obtaining an order of the court/Tribunal for re-opening.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner looks for the "who initiates" and "trigger" distinction. Confusing the two sections — e.g., saying the Board can suo motu re-open under Sec. 130 — is a fatal error.)*

### Q63. Ch: Audit & Auditors — Disqualifications of Auditor (Sec. 141) with Fact Pattern (Marks: 6) [Application]

|---|---|

### Q64. Ch: Audit & Auditors — Auditor's Duty to Report Fraud (Sec. 143(12)) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** While auditing Yamuna Power Ltd., the statutory auditor, in the course of the performance of his duties, comes across two separate frauds: Fraud X, committed by an employee against the company, involving an amount of ₹1.20 crore; and Fraud Y, committed by a purchase officer, involving ₹90 lakh. State the reporting obligations of the auditor in respect of frauds under Section 143(12) of the Companies Act, 2013 read with the relevant Rules, specifying the thresholds, the authority to whom the report is to be made, the time limits and the manner of reporting.

**Answer:**
The auditor's duty to report fraud is contained in **Section 143(12) of the Companies Act, 2013** read with **Rule 13 of the Companies (Audit and Auditors) Rules, 2014**. It provides that if an auditor of a company, in the course of the performance of his duties as auditor, **has reason to believe that an offence of fraud involving such amount or amounts as may be prescribed is being or has been committed** in the company by its officers or employees, the auditor shall report the matter as follows.

**Case 1 — Fraud involving amount of ₹1 crore or more [reporting to Central Government]:** In respect of a fraud involving an amount of **₹1 crore or more**, the auditor shall report the matter to the **Central Government** in the following manner:
(i) the auditor shall **forward his report to the Board or the Audit Committee**, as the case may be, immediately but **not later than two days** of his knowledge of the fraud, **seeking their reply or observations within forty-five days**;
(ii) on receipt of such reply/observations, the auditor shall **forward his report and the reply, together with his comments, to the Central Government** within **fifteen days** of receipt of such reply/observations;
(iii) where the auditor **does not receive any reply/observations within forty-five days**, he shall forward his report to the Central Government along with a note containing the details of his report that was earlier forwarded to the Board/Audit Committee for which he has not received any reply;
(iv) the report shall be sent to the **Secretary, Ministry of Corporate Affairs**, in a **sealed cover by Registered Post with Acknowledgement Due or by Speed Post followed by an e-mail**, and shall be in the prescribed **Form ADT-4**.

**Case 2 — Fraud involving amount of less than ₹1 crore [reporting to Board/Audit Committee]:** In case of a fraud involving an amount **less than ₹1 crore**, the auditor shall report the matter to the **Audit Committee** (constituted under Section 177) **or to the Board** in other cases, within **two days** of his knowledge of the fraud, specifying the nature of the fraud with description, the approximate amount involved and the parties involved. Such frauds are **not reported to the Central Government**; instead, the company is required to **disclose the details** in the **Board's Report** (nature, amount, parties, remedial action).

**Application to Yamuna Power Ltd.:**
- **Fraud X (₹1.20 crore):** As it is ₹1 crore or more, the auditor must follow the **Central Government reporting route** — report to the Board/Audit Committee within 2 days seeking reply within 45 days, and thereafter forward to the Central Government (Secretary, MCA) in **Form ADT-4** within 15 days.
- **Fraud Y (₹90 lakh):** As it is less than ₹1 crore, the auditor must report to the **Audit Committee/Board within 2 days**, and the company shall disclose the specified particulars in its **Board's Report**; no report to the Central Government is required.

**Conclusion:** Fraud X is reportable to the **Central Government in Form ADT-4** following the two-stage 45-day/15-day procedure, whereas Fraud Y (below the ₹1 crore threshold) is reportable only to the **Audit Committee/Board within two days** with disclosure in the Board's Report.

*(Full-marks tip: The ₹1 crore line dividing CG-reporting from Board-reporting, the 2-day/45-day/15-day timeline, and Form ADT-4 are all mark-bearing. Omitting the Board's Report disclosure for the sub-₹1-crore fraud is a frequent slip.)*

### Q65. Ch: Audit & Auditors — Rotation of Auditors (Sec. 139(2)) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Deccan Textiles Ltd. is an unlisted public company with a paid-up share capital of ₹15 crore and public borrowings from a nationalised bank of ₹60 crore. The Board wishes to know whether the firm can be re-appointed for a further term, and, if not, the cooling-off period and related requirements under Section 139(2) of the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:**
The rotation of auditors is governed by **Section 139(2) of the Companies Act, 2013** read with **Rule 5 of the Companies (Audit and Auditors) Rules, 2014**.

**Applicability [Rule 5]:** The rotation provisions apply to (a) all **listed companies**; and among unlisted companies, to (b) **unlisted public companies** having paid-up share capital of **₹10 crore or more**; (c) **private companies** having paid-up share capital of ₹50 crore or more; and (d) all companies having **public borrowings from financial institutions, banks or public deposits of ₹50 crore or more.**

Deccan Textiles Ltd. is an unlisted public company with paid-up capital of ₹15 crore (≥ ₹10 crore) and, additionally, borrowings of ₹60 crore from a bank (≥ ₹50 crore). It is therefore **squarely covered** by the rotation requirements on both counts.

(i) an **individual** as auditor for **more than one term of five consecutive years**; and
(ii) an **audit firm** as auditor for **more than two terms of five consecutive years** (i.e., a maximum of ten consecutive years).

Hence the firm may complete the tenth year (i.e., it may be re-appointed for the balance one year to complete its second term), but thereafter it **must be rotated out** and shall not be re-appointed for a **cooling-off period of five years**; nor can any firm sharing a common partner with it be appointed during that period.

**Conclusion:** As Deccan Textiles Ltd. is covered by Section 139(2), M/s Sahyadri & Co. may serve only up to a **maximum of ten consecutive years** (two terms of five years). Beyond that it must be rotated, with a **five-year cooling-off period**, and no common-partner firm may replace it during the cooling-off.

### Q68. Ch: Audit & Auditors — Powers/Rights and Duties of Auditor (Sec. 143(1)) (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the specific matters into which the auditor of a company must inquire under Section 143(1) of the Companies Act, 2013 while conducting the audit. Also state the right of the auditor to access records and to obtain information under the same section.

**Answer:**
The powers, rights and duties of the auditor are set out in **Section 143(1) of the Companies Act, 2013**.

**Right of access [Sec. 143(1), main provision]:** Every auditor of a company has a **right of access at all times to the books of account and vouchers of the company, whether kept at the registered office of the company or at any other place**, and is entitled to **require from the officers of the company such information and explanation** as he may consider necessary for the performance of his duties as auditor. In the case of a holding company, the auditor also has a **right of access to the records of all its subsidiary and associate companies** in so far as it relates to the consolidation of financial statements.

**Matters of mandatory inquiry [Sec. 143(1)(a) to (f)]:** The auditor shall inquire into the following matters, namely—
(a) whether **loans and advances made** by the company on the basis of security have been **properly secured** and whether the terms on which they have been made are **prejudicial to the interests** of the company or its members;
(b) whether **transactions of the company which are represented merely by book entries** are prejudicial to the interests of the company;
(c) where the company **is not an investment company or a banking company**, whether so much of the **assets of the company as consist of shares, debentures and other securities have been sold at a price less than that at which they were purchased** by the company;
(d) whether **loans and advances made** by the company have been **shown as deposits**;
(e) whether **personal expenses have been charged to revenue account**; and
(f) where it is stated in the books and documents of the company that any **shares have been allotted for cash**, whether **cash has actually been received** in respect of such allotment, and if no cash has actually been so received, whether the position as stated in the account books and the balance sheet is correct, regular and not misleading.

**Conclusion:** Under Section 143(1), the auditor of a company enjoys an unrestricted right of access to the books, vouchers and (for a holding company) subsidiary/associate records, and to information from officers, and is under a duty to specifically inquire into the six matters enumerated in clauses (a) to (f), which are designed to detect prejudicial transactions, misclassifications and window-dressing.

### Q69. Ch: LLP Act 2008 — Conversion of a Firm into LLP: Procedure & Effect (Marks: 6) [Application]

**Question:** M/s Ganpati Traders is a partnership firm registered under the Indian Partnership Act, 1932, carrying on business with four partners. The partners now wish to convert the firm into a Limited Liability Partnership under the Limited Liability Partnership Act, 2008 to enjoy the benefit of limited liability. One partner, Mr. Rohan, argues that on conversion the firm's existing assets and pending court cases will have to be freshly transferred and re-instituted in the name of the LLP, and that all the earlier liabilities of the firm will automatically be extinguished. Examine, with reference to the LLP Act, 2008, whether Mr. Rohan is correct as to (i) which firms are eligible to convert, (ii) the effect of registration of conversion on assets and proceedings, and (iii) the continuing liability of partners for pre-conversion obligations.

**Answer:**
The conversion of a partnership firm into a Limited Liability Partnership is governed by **Section 55 read with the Second Schedule** of the Limited Liability Partnership Act, 2008.

**(i) Eligibility to convert.** As per **Section 55 read with paragraph 1 of the Second Schedule**, a firm may convert into an LLP in accordance with the provisions of that Schedule. Paragraph 2 clarifies that a firm may apply to convert only if and after **all the partners of the firm, and no one else, become the partners of the LLP**. Thus the entire body of partners of M/s Ganpati Traders (and only they) must become the partners of the resulting LLP; no incoming person may be introduced at the stage of conversion.

**(ii) Effect of registration on assets and proceedings.** On conversion, the Registrar issues a **certificate of registration** under paragraph 5 of the Second Schedule. As per **paragraph 6 of the Second Schedule**, on and from the date of registration specified in the certificate:
- there shall be an LLP by the name specified in the certificate of registration registered under the Act;
- **all tangible (movable or immovable) as well as intangible property vested in the firm, all assets, interests, rights, privileges, liabilities, obligations relating to the firm, and the whole of the undertaking of the firm, shall be transferred to and shall vest in the LLP without further assurance, act or deed**; and
- the firm shall be deemed to be dissolved and, if earlier registered under the Indian Partnership Act, 1932, removed from the records maintained under that Act.

Therefore, Mr. Rohan is **incorrect** in saying that assets must be freshly transferred. Vesting is automatic and by operation of law, "without further assurance". Further, under **paragraph 7 of the Second Schedule**, any proceeding pending before any Court, Tribunal or authority by or against the firm on the date of registration **may be continued, completed and enforced by or against the LLP** — the cases need not be re-instituted.

**(iii) Continuing liability for pre-conversion obligations.** Mr. Rohan is also **incorrect** that earlier liabilities stand extinguished. Under **paragraph 8 of the Second Schedule**, every partner of the firm that has converted into an LLP **continues to be personally liable (jointly and severally with the LLP) for the liabilities and obligations of the firm which were incurred prior to the conversion or which arose from any contract entered into prior to the conversion**. If such a partner discharges any such liability, he is entitled (to the extent of the amount discharged) to be indemnified by the LLP.

Additionally, under **paragraph 9**, for a period of **twelve months** from the date of registration, the LLP must ensure that every official correspondence bears a statement that it was, as from the date of registration, converted from a firm into an LLP, together with the name and registration number of the LLP and the name and registration number, if applicable, of the firm from which it was converted; failure attracts a penalty.

**Conclusion:** All partners (and only them) must become partners of the LLP; on registration the assets and pending proceedings pass automatically to the LLP without fresh transfer or re-institution; and the partners remain personally liable, jointly and severally with the LLP, for the firm's pre-conversion liabilities. Mr. Rohan's contentions are therefore not correct.

Marks are lost for merely writing "assets transfer" without the statutory phrase and for missing the joint-and-several liability with right of indemnity.)*

### Q70. Ch: LLP Act 2008 — Winding Up & Partner's Liability for Wrongful Acts (Marks: 6) [Application]

**Question:** Sunrise Consultants LLP has three designated partners — A, B and C. In the course of the LLP's business, Partner B, while acting within his authority to negotiate a service contract, made a fraudulent misrepresentation to a client, Mr. Verma, who suffered a loss of ₹8,00,000. Later the LLP goes into winding up and its assets are insufficient to meet all claims. Mr. Verma claims the whole amount from the LLP and, additionally, personally from Partner A who had no knowledge of and did not participate in the fraud. Examine, under the Limited Liability Partnership Act, 2008: (i) the liability of the LLP for B's wrongful act; (ii) whether B is personally liable; and (iii) whether the innocent partner A can be made personally liable.

**Answer:**
The question is governed by **Sections 27, 28 and 30** of the Limited Liability Partnership Act, 2008, which deal with the extent of liability of the LLP and of its partners.

**(i) Liability of the LLP for B's wrongful act.** Under **Section 27(2)**, the LLP is liable if a partner of the LLP is liable to any person **as a result of a wrongful act or omission on his part in the course of the business of the LLP or with its authority**. Here, B made the fraudulent misrepresentation while acting within his authority in the course of the LLP's service business. Therefore, under Section 27(2), **the LLP is liable to Mr. Verma for the loss of ₹8,00,000.** Under **Section 27(4)**, such liability of the LLP is met **out of the property of the LLP**.

**(ii) Personal liability of Partner B (the wrongdoer).** Under **Section 28(1)**, a partner is **not personally liable, directly or indirectly, for an obligation** of the LLP solely by reason of being a partner. **However**, the shelter of limited liability is expressly withdrawn from a wrongdoer by the proviso to **Section 30(1)**: the liability of the LLP and of a partner arising out of a **wrongful act or fraud** is not limited in the case of that partner. Moreover, **Section 30(1)** provides that where an act is carried out by the LLP or any of its partners **with intent to defraud creditors** of the LLP or any other person, or for any fraudulent purpose, the liability of the LLP **and the partners who acted with intent to defraud** shall be **unlimited for all or any of the debts or other liabilities of the LLP**. Hence **Partner B is personally liable without limit**, in addition to the LLP.

**(iii) Liability of the innocent Partner A.** Under **Section 28(2)**, this does **not** affect the personal liability of a partner **for his own wrongful act or omission**, but a partner shall **not be personally liable for the wrongful act or omission of any other partner** of the LLP. Since A neither knew of nor participated in the fraud, the fraud is not "his own" wrongful act. Under **Section 30(2)**, where fraud is carried out by a partner, the LLP is liable to the same extent as that partner **unless it is established by the LLP that such act was without the knowledge or the authority of the LLP**. Partner A, being an innocent partner who did not carry out or authorise the fraud, is **not personally liable** for B's wrongful act. His exposure is limited to his contribution/interest in the LLP.

**Conclusion:** The LLP is liable to Mr. Verma out of its property (Section 27); the fraudulent Partner B is personally liable without limit (Sections 28(2) and 30); but the innocent Partner A, who neither knew of nor participated in the fraud, cannot be made personally liable for B's wrongful act (Section 28(2)).

*(Full-marks tip: Distinguish Section 27 (LLP's liability), Section 28 (no personal liability except own wrongful act) and Section 30 (unlimited liability in case of fraud). The examiner deducts marks if the student holds the innocent partner liable or forgets that fraud removes the limited-liability shield of the wrongdoer.)*

### Q71. Ch: General Clauses Act 1897 — Meaning of "Year" and "Month" (Marks: 4) [Application]

**Question:** A tenancy agreement executed under a Central enactment provides that the tenant shall pay rent "for a period of one year" and that a notice to quit shall be given "one month in advance". A dispute arises as to (i) whether "one year" means 365 days or a British calendar year, and (ii) whether "one month" from 30th January would expire on 28th February (in a non-leap year) or on 1st March. Explain, with reference to the General Clauses Act, 1897, the meaning of the expressions "year" and "month".

**Answer:**
The expressions "year" and "month" are defined in the **General Clauses Act, 1897**, which applies for the interpretation of Central enactments.

**Meaning of "year".** As per **Section 3(66) of the General Clauses Act, 1897**, "**year**" shall mean **a year reckoned according to the British calendar**. Thus "one year" does not mean a fixed block of 365 days counted arithmetically; it means a **calendar year** — i.e. reckoned by reference to the British (Gregorian) calendar. Accordingly, a period of one year commencing on a given date ends on the day immediately preceding the corresponding date in the following year.

**Meaning of "month".** As per **Section 3(35) of the General Clauses Act, 1897**, "**month**" shall mean **a month reckoned according to the British calendar**. Therefore a "month" is not a period of 30 or 31 days counted arithmetically; it is a **calendar month**. The settled rule (as recognised by the courts in interpreting this provision) is that a calendar month is calculated by counting from the given date in one month to the date **immediately preceding** the corresponding date in the next month, and where there is no corresponding date in the succeeding month (because it is shorter), the period expires on the **last day of that succeeding month**.

Applying this to the facts:
- "One year" means a British calendar year — a year reckoned by the calendar, not a mechanical count of 365 days.
- One month's notice given on **30th January** (in a non-leap year, February having 28 days) would expire at the end of the day on which the corresponding date falls; since there is no 30th February, the month is reckoned to expire on the **last day of February, i.e. 28th February**, and not on 1st March.

**Conclusion:** Under Sections 3(66) and 3(35) of the General Clauses Act, 1897, both "year" and "month" are reckoned according to the British calendar. "One year" is a calendar year and "one month" is a calendar month; a month's notice given on 30th January thus expires on 28th February in a non-leap year.

*(Full-marks tip: Quote the exact clause numbers — Section 3(66) for "year" and Section 3(35) for "month" — and state that both are "reckoned according to the British calendar". Marks are lost for saying a month means 30 days or a year means 365 days.)*

### Q72. Ch: General Clauses Act 1897 — Coming into Operation of Enactments (Marks: 5) [Application]

**Question:** A Central Act received the assent of the President on **15th March 2026** and was published in the Official Gazette on **17th March 2026**. The Act itself is silent as to the date of its commencement. A person contends that the Act came into force only from the beginning of the day on **17th March 2026** (the date of Gazette publication), while another contends that it operated from an earlier point. Explain, with reference to the General Clauses Act, 1897, the rule as to the day on which, and the precise point of time from which, a Central Act comes into operation when no specific commencement date is provided.

**Answer:**
The commencement of Central enactments is governed by **Section 5 read with Section 3(13)** of the General Clauses Act, 1897.

**Rule as to the day of commencement.** As per **Section 5(1) of the General Clauses Act, 1897**, where any Central Act is **not expressed to come into operation on a particular day**, then it shall come into operation on **the day on which it receives the assent** — in the case of a Central Act made after the commencement of the Constitution, **of the President**. Thus, the relevant day is the day of **assent** and **not** the day of Gazette publication. On the facts, the President's assent was given on **15th March 2026**; therefore, in the absence of an express commencement date, the Act comes into operation on **15th March 2026**.

**Precise point of time on that day.** As per **Section 5(3)**, unless the contrary is expressed, a Central Act shall be construed as coming into operation **immediately on the expiration of the day preceding its commencement**. This is reinforced by the definition in **Section 3(13)**, which provides that "commencement", used with reference to an Act, means **the day on which the Act comes into force**. Reading these together, the Act comes into force **immediately on the expiration of the day preceding — i.e. at the first moment (midnight/00:00 hours) of 15th March 2026 — and operates for the whole of that day**, and there is no notion of a fraction of a day being excluded.

**Conclusion:** Since the Act is silent as to commencement, under Section 5(1) it comes into operation on the date of the President's assent, i.e. **15th March 2026** (not the date of Gazette publication), and by virtue of Section 5(3) it is deemed to come into force **immediately on the expiration of the day preceding**, operating from the very first moment of 15th March 2026.

### Q73. Ch: General Clauses Act 1897 — Power to Appoint Includes Power to Suspend/Dismiss (Marks: 5) [Application]

**Question:** Under a Central Act, a statutory authority is empowered "to appoint" a Chief Administrative Officer. The Act contains no express provision authorising the authority to suspend, remove or dismiss the officer so appointed. The authority now wishes to suspend and subsequently dismiss the officer for misconduct, but the officer contends that in the absence of an express power the authority cannot do so. Examine the contention with reference to the General Clauses Act, 1897, and state the conditions subject to which such implied power may be exercised.

**Answer:**
The issue is governed by **Section 16 of the General Clauses Act, 1897**, which deals with the power to appoint including the power to suspend or dismiss.

**Statutory provision.** As per **Section 16 of the General Clauses Act, 1897**, where, by any Central Act or Regulation, a power to make any **appointment** is conferred, then, **unless a different intention appears**, the authority having for the time being power to make the appointment **shall also have power to suspend or dismiss any person appointed** (whether by itself or any other authority) in exercise of that power.

**Application to the facts.** The Central Act confers on the statutory authority the power "to appoint" the Chief Administrative Officer. 3. The exercise of the power (suspension/dismissal) must nevertheless conform to any **procedural safeguards** and principles of natural justice attaching to the office (e.g. an opportunity of being heard where the removal is by way of punishment for misconduct).

**Conclusion:** By virtue of Section 16 of the General Clauses Act, 1897, the power to appoint carries with it, unless a different intention appears, the power to suspend or dismiss. The statutory authority may therefore suspend and dismiss the Chief Administrative Officer even without an express provision, subject to any contrary intention in the Act and to the applicable procedural safeguards. The officer's contention fails.

### Q74. Ch: General Clauses Act 1897 — Meaning of Service by Post (Marks: 5) [Application]

**Question:** A Central enactment requires that a statutory notice "shall be served" on the assessee. The Department sent the notice by registered post to the assessee's correct address, properly addressed, pre-paid and posted, on 2nd April. The assessee claims he never actually received the notice and therefore no valid service was effected. Examine, with reference to the General Clauses Act, 1897, when service by post is deemed to be effected, and the point of time at which such service is deemed to be complete.

**Answer:**
The question is governed by **Section 27 of the General Clauses Act, 1897**, which deals with the meaning of service by post.

**Statutory provision.** As per **Section 27 of the General Clauses Act, 1897**, where any Central Act or Regulation authorises or requires any document to be served by post, whether the expression "**serve**" or "**give**" or "**send**" or any other expression is used, then, **unless a different intention appears**, the service shall be deemed to be effected by:
(a) **properly addressing** the document,
(b) **pre-paying** it, and
(c) **posting it by registered post**, a letter containing the document;
and, unless the contrary is proved, to have been effected **at the time at which the letter would be delivered in the ordinary course of post**.

**Application to the facts.** The Department has satisfied all three conditions — the notice was properly addressed to the assessee's correct address, was pre-paid, and was posted by registered post on 2nd April. Consequently, under Section 27, **service is deemed to have been effected**. The presumption of due service arises once the three conditions are met, and it is **not necessary to prove actual receipt** by the addressee. The mere assertion by the assessee that he did not receive the notice does not, by itself, rebut the statutory presumption — the burden lies on him to **prove the contrary** (e.g. that the letter was in fact not delivered in the ordinary course of post).

**Point of time of deemed service.** Service is deemed to have been effected at the time at which the letter **would be delivered in the ordinary course of post**, and not the moment of posting; but this is a rebuttable presumption ("unless the contrary is proved"). Thus, service is presumed complete when, in the ordinary course of post, the registered letter would have reached the assessee, unless he discharges the burden of proving otherwise.

**Conclusion:** Under Section 27 of the General Clauses Act, 1897, since the notice was properly addressed, pre-paid and sent by registered post, service is deemed to have been effected in the ordinary course of post, notwithstanding the assessee's claim of non-receipt; the burden is on the assessee to prove the contrary, failing which the service is valid.

### Q75. Ch: LLP Act 2008 — Conversion of Private Company into LLP: Eligibility & Effect (Marks: 6) [Application]

Advise with reference to the LLP Act, 2008.

**Answer:**
Conversion of a private company into an LLP is governed by **Section 56 read with the Third Schedule** of the Limited Liability Partnership Act, 2008.

(i) there is **no security interest in its assets subsisting or in force** at the time of application; and
(ii) the partners of the LLP to which it converts **comprise all the shareholders of the company and no one else**.

Existing proceedings continue by or against the LLP, and the partners' pre-conversion obligations continue as provided in the Schedule.

**Conclusion:** Techsprint Private Limited is **not presently eligible** to convert because a security interest (the bank charge) subsists on its assets (paragraph 2, Third Schedule). ### Q76. Ch: LLP Act 2008 — Winding Up: Modes and Circumstances (Marks: 5) [Theory]

| Step | Particulars | Date |
|------|-------------|------|
| 1 | Date of order (starting point) | 31st December 2025 |
| 2 | Period allowed | 3 calendar months |
| 3 | Corresponding date three months later | 31st March 2026 |
| 4 | Last date for filing (calendar-month reckoning) | 31st March 2026 |

### Q81. Ch: General Clauses Act 1897 — Effect of Repeal (Marks: 5) [Application]

### Q82. Ch: LLP Act 2008 — Conversion vs. Non-Applicability of Indian Partnership Act (Marks: 5) [Theory]

**Question:** "On conversion of a firm into an LLP, the resulting entity is not merely a re-named partnership firm but a distinct legal person, and the general law governing ordinary partnerships ceases to apply to it." The statement is **correct** and is supported by **Sections 3, 14 and 55 read with the Second Schedule**, and by **Section 4** of the Limited Liability Partnership Act, 2008.

LLP is a body corporate — separate legal person.** As per **Section 3(1) of the LLP Act, 2008**, an LLP is a **body corporate** formed and incorporated under the Act and is a **legal entity separate from that of its partners**. **Conclusion:** On conversion, the firm ceases to exist and a **separate body corporate with perpetual succession** comes into being; its undertaking vests automatically in it, the Indian Partnership Act, 1932 no longer applies (Section 4), and its internal governance shifts to the LLP agreement/First Schedule. The statement is therefore correct.

The creditor can proceed against him personally to that extent, and the situation independently justifies winding up by the Tribunal under Section 64(b).

### Q85. Ch: Interpretation of Statutes — Rule of Harmonious Construction (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the "Rule of Harmonious Construction". When is a Court required to resort to this rule, and what is the guiding principle laid down by the Courts where two provisions of the same statute appear to be in conflict?

**Answer:**
The Rule of Harmonious Construction is a settled principle of statutory interpretation which requires that a statute should be read as a whole and its provisions construed together, so that effect is given to every part of the enactment. When there are, in an enactment, two or more provisions which cannot be reconciled with each other, they should be so interpreted that, if possible, effect is given to **both**. This is what is meant by the principle of harmonious construction.

A statute must be read as a whole and one provision of the Act should be construed with reference to the other provisions in the same Act so as to make a consistent enactment of the whole statute. Such a construction has the merit of avoiding any inconsistency or repugnancy either within a section or between two different sections of the same statute.

The following guiding principles have been laid down by the Courts:
1. The Courts must avoid a head-on clash of seemingly contradictory provisions and must construe the contradictory provisions so as to harmonise them.
2. The provision of one section cannot be used to defeat the provision contained in another section unless the Court, despite all its efforts, is unable to find a way to reconcile their differences.
3. When it is impossible to completely reconcile the differences in contradictory provisions, the Courts must interpret them in such a way so that **effect is given to both the provisions as much as possible**.
4. Courts must also keep in mind that an interpretation which reduces one of the provisions to a "dead letter" or "useless lumber" is not a harmonious construction.
5. To harmonise is not to destroy any statutory provision or to render it fruitless.

**Conclusion:** The rule of harmonious construction is applied where there is an apparent conflict between two provisions of the same statute, and it obliges the Court to interpret them in a manner that gives effect to both, avoiding rendering either provision otiose.

### Q86. Ch: Interpretation of Statutes — Reading Down a Provision (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** What is meant by the doctrine of "Reading Down" a statutory provision? State the circumstances in which a Court adopts this technique and how it differs from striking down a provision.

**Answer:**
"Reading down" is a technique of interpretation adopted by Courts to preserve the constitutionality or validity of a statutory provision. The doctrine of reading down means that where the literal or wide language of a provision would render it unconstitutional or take it beyond the competence of the legislature, the Court, instead of declaring the provision void, gives it a **narrower and restricted meaning** so that it may be brought within the constitutional limits and be sustained as valid.

The rule proceeds on the presumption that the legislature does not intend to exceed its jurisdiction and that it intends to enact a valid law. Therefore, where two constructions are possible — one which would make the provision valid and the other which would make it invalid — the Court leans in favour of the construction that upholds validity by reading down the provision.

Circumstances in which reading down is adopted:
1. Where the plain and wide language of a provision, if literally applied, would make it ultra vires the Constitution or the parent statute.
2. Where a restricted interpretation is reasonably possible on the language used, so as to keep the provision within the legislative competence and constitutional limits.
3. Where the object is to make the enactment workable and to save it from the vice of vagueness, arbitrariness or excessive delegation.

**Distinction between "Reading Down" and "Striking Down":**

| Basis | Reading Down | Striking Down |
|---|---|---|
| Effect on provision | The provision survives with a narrowed meaning | The provision is declared void / invalid |
| Judicial approach | Interpretative — saves the law | Adjudicatory — nullifies the law |
| Presumption relied on | Legislature intends a valid law | Provision cannot be saved by any construction |
| Result | Provision is read in a restricted sense | Provision ceases to have effect |

**Conclusion:** Reading down is a saving device by which the Court narrows the scope of a provision to sustain its validity; it is resorted to only where the language reasonably permits such a restricted meaning, failing which the provision must be struck down.

*(Full-marks tip: State that reading down rests on the presumption of constitutionality; the common deduction is treating it as identical to striking down instead of showing it is a saving/validating technique.)*

### Q87. Ch: Interpretation of Statutes — Beneficial Construction of Welfare Statutes (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the rule of "Beneficial Construction" as applied to welfare and social legislation. Why do Courts adopt a liberal construction in respect of such statutes?

**Answer:**
Beneficial construction is a rule of interpretation under which a statute enacted for the benefit or protection of a particular class of persons — such as workmen, labourers, consumers or other weaker sections — is construed **liberally and in favour of that class** so as to advance the object and purpose for which the statute was enacted, and to suppress the mischief it seeks to remedy.

Welfare or social legislation (for example, labour and industrial laws, and statutes protecting the weaker sections of society) is enacted with a benevolent object of ameliorating the conditions of a particular class of persons. Since the very purpose of such a statute is to confer a benefit, the Courts adopt a construction which advances the beneficent purpose and gives the fullest relief to the persons for whose benefit the enactment is made, rather than a narrow or literal construction which would defeat that purpose.

The principles governing beneficial construction are:
1. Where the language of the statute is capable of **two constructions**, that construction which is beneficial to the class sought to be benefited and which advances the object of the Act should be preferred.
2. The Court should adopt the construction that suppresses the mischief and advances the remedy.
3. However, the beneficial construction should not be stretched to the extent of doing violence to the plain language of the statute; the Court cannot re-write the section under the guise of beneficial construction.
4. It is essentially a construction applied where the words are ambiguous and reasonably admit of two meanings.

**Conclusion:** Beneficial construction requires that a welfare statute be interpreted liberally in favour of the class it is meant to protect, but only within the limits of the language actually used; it cannot be employed to supply words the legislature did not use.

*(Full-marks tip: Emphasise the pre-condition that the words must be capable of two meanings and the limit that the Court cannot re-write the provision; the common deduction is omitting this limitation and treating beneficial construction as an unlimited licence to favour the beneficiary.)*

### Q89. Ch: Interpretation of Statutes — Harmonious Construction (Application) (Marks: 6) [Application]

The issue is whether the general power under Section A can be exercised in a manner that overrides the specific prohibition contained in Section B, and how the apparent conflict between the two provisions is to be resolved.

**Applicable rule — Harmonious Construction:** Where two provisions of the same statute appear to conflict, they must be read together and construed harmoniously so that effect is given to both, and neither is reduced to a dead letter. A general provision must ordinarily yield to a specific provision dealing with the same subject-matter (*generalia specialibus non derogant*), so that the special provision operates as an exception to, or a limitation on, the general provision.

**Application to the facts:**

3. The two provisions are reconciled by reading the general power in Section A as **subject to** the specific bar in Section B. Thus, the authority may grant licences on such conditions as it thinks fit, but it cannot grant a licence to a person disqualified under Section B.
4. On the facts, the person was convicted under the Act only two years earlier, i.e. within the five-year prohibited period. The grant therefore falls squarely within the bar in Section B.

**Conclusion:** Reading Sections A and B harmoniously, the general power in Section A is subject to the specific prohibition in Section B. The grant of the licence to a person convicted within the preceding five years is **invalid** and is liable to be set aside.

### Q92. Ch: Interpretation of Statutes — Harmonious Construction vs. Non-obstante Clause (Marks: 6) [Application]

**Answer:**

1. **Non-obstante clause:** A non-obstante clause ("notwithstanding anything contained in this Act") is a legislative device used to give the enacting part of the section an **overriding effect** over the provisions mentioned in the clause, in case of conflict. 3. Consequently, in respect of the case in question, the special summary procedure under Section 12 prevails over the elaborate procedure under Section 20.

**Conclusion:** The conflict is first to be resolved, if possible, by harmonious construction; but where the conflict is irreconcilable, **Section 12, by virtue of its non-obstante clause, overrides Section 20** and prevails. The special summary procedure under Section 12 will therefore apply.

All the five remittances listed (gift, private travel, overseas investment, maintenance of close relative, and donation) are permissible transactions under LRS and are **aggregated** against the single overall ceiling of USD 2,50,000.

**WN-2 — Aggregation of remittances made during FY 2025-26:**

| Sr. No. | Purpose | Amount (USD) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Gift to brother abroad | 60,000 |
| 2 | Private visit to Europe | 40,000 |
| 3 | Investment in foreign equity shares | 90,000 |
| 4 | Maintenance of son studying abroad | 50,000 |
| 5 | Donation to charitable organisation | 30,000 |
| | **Total remitted** | **2,70,000** |

**WN-3 — Comparison with LRS ceiling:**
- Total remitted under LRS = USD 2,70,000
- Permissible LRS ceiling = USD 2,50,000
- Excess over ceiling = 2,70,000 − 2,50,000 = **USD 20,000**

**Statement showing LRS position of Mr. Rakesh for FY 2025-26:**

| Particulars | USD |
|---|---|
| Aggregate remittances under LRS | 2,70,000 |
| Less: Permissible LRS limit (free) | 2,50,000 |
| **Amount in excess of LRS limit (requires RBI prior approval)** | **20,000** |

**Conclusion:** Mr. Rakesh has exceeded the LRS ceiling. Remittances up to USD 2,50,000 are freely permitted, and for the **excess of USD 20,000** he requires prior approval of the Reserve Bank of India.

*(Full-marks tip: The key is to recognise that ALL permissible current and capital account remittances are aggregated under the single USD 2,50,000 ceiling; a common deduction is treating each purpose as having a separate limit or leaving out the capital-account investment from the aggregation.)*

### Q98. Ch: FEMA, 1999 — LRS and Medical/Studies beyond Limit (Application) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** With reference to the Liberalised Remittance Scheme and Schedule III of the FEM (Current Account Transactions) Rules, 2000, advise in the following independent situations relating to resident individuals:
(i) Mr. X requires USD 3,50,000 for medical treatment abroad, supported by an estimate from the overseas hospital.
(ii) Ms. Y wishes to remit USD 2,80,000 for pursuing higher studies abroad, supported by an estimate from the foreign university.
(iii) Mr. Z, having already remitted USD 2,50,000 under LRS during the year for a private visit, now wishes to gift a further USD 20,000 to a friend abroad.

**Answer:**
Under **Schedule III** read with the **Liberalised Remittance Scheme**, a resident individual may draw foreign exchange up to **USD 2,50,000 per financial year** for the permissible transactions. However, for certain purposes — namely **medical treatment abroad, studies abroad, and emigration** — a resident individual may avail of exchange facility **exceeding** USD 2,50,000 **without RBI approval** if so required by a **cost estimate** from the hospital / institution / country concerned. Purposes other than these require RBI approval for any amount beyond the LRS limit.

| Case | Purpose | Position |
|---|---|---|
| (i) Medical treatment (USD 3,50,000, with hospital estimate) | Exceeds USD 2,50,000 but supported by estimate | Permitted **without RBI approval** to the extent of the estimate |
| (ii) Studies abroad (USD 2,80,000, with university estimate) | Exceeds USD 2,50,000 but supported by estimate | Permitted **without RBI approval** to the extent of the estimate |
| (iii) Further gift of USD 20,000 after exhausting LRS limit | Gift is not medical/studies/emigration | **RBI prior approval required** for the excess |

**Analysis:**
1. **Case (i):** For medical treatment abroad, an individual may draw foreign exchange up to USD 2,50,000 or even in excess of it, provided the requirement is supported by an estimate from a doctor/hospital abroad. Since Mr. X's requirement of USD 3,50,000 is supported by the hospital's estimate, it is permissible without RBI approval.
2. **Case (ii):** For studies abroad, an individual may draw up to USD 2,50,000 or the estimate from the institution, whichever is higher, without RBI approval. As Ms. Y's remittance of USD 2,80,000 is backed by the university's estimate, it is permissible without RBI approval.
3. **Case (iii):** Mr. Z has already exhausted the LRS limit of USD 2,50,000 for a private visit. A gift is not one of the purposes (medical/studies/emigration) for which the limit can be exceeded on the strength of an estimate. Hence the further USD 20,000 gift requires prior approval of the RBI.

**Conclusion:** In cases (i) and (ii) the excess over USD 2,50,000 is permissible without RBI approval as it is supported by the requisite estimate; in case (iii) the further remittance of USD 20,000 requires prior RBI approval, the LRS limit having been exhausted.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards distinguishing the three "estimate-based" purposes (medical, studies, emigration) which can exceed the limit without RBI approval, from all other purposes which cannot; the common deduction is treating the gift in (iii) the same as the medical/studies cases.)*

### Q99. Ch: FEMA, 1999 — Compounding of Contravention (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the provisions relating to "compounding of contravention" under Section 15 of the Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999. Who is the compounding authority, within what period must the compounding be completed, and what is the effect of compounding a contravention?

**Answer:**
**Meaning:** Compounding refers to the process of voluntarily admitting a contravention, pleading guilty and seeking redressal. It is a mechanism by which a person who has committed a contravention under FEMA may apply to have the contravention compounded (i.e. settled by payment of a sum) instead of facing prolonged adjudication proceedings.

**Statutory basis — Section 15 of FEMA, 1999:**
1. **Section 15(1):** Any contravention under **Section 13** may, on an application made by the person committing such contravention, be compounded within **180 days from the date of receipt of the application** by the Director of Enforcement or such other officers of the Directorate of Enforcement and officers of the Reserve Bank as may be authorised by the Central Government.
2. **Section 15(2):** Where a contravention has been compounded, **no proceeding or further proceeding**, as the case may be, shall be initiated or continued against the person committing such contravention in respect of the contravention so compounded.

**Compounding authority:** The contravention is compounded by the officers of the **Directorate of Enforcement** and the **Reserve Bank of India** as authorised by the Central Government, the appropriate authority being determined with reference to the sum involved in the contravention.

**Period for compounding:** The compounding must be completed within **180 days** from the date of receipt of the application by the compounding authority.

**Effect of compounding:**
1. On compounding, and on payment of the sum for which the contravention is compounded, the proceedings stand concluded.
2. Under Section 15(2), no proceeding or further proceeding shall be initiated or continued against the person in respect of the contravention so compounded — i.e. the person is not exposed to any further penalty or prosecution for that contravention.

**Important points:**
- Compounding is available in respect of a contravention under Section 13; however, contravention of the nature referred to in **Section 3(a)** (which is dealt with by the Directorate of Enforcement) and cases where the amount is not quantifiable are treated differently.
- Where a person has once been compounded, a similar contravention committed within a specified period is not ordinarily compounded again on the same lenient basis.

**Conclusion:** Under Section 15, a contravention under Section 13 may be compounded on application within 180 days by the authorised officers of the Directorate of Enforcement / RBI, and once compounded, no further proceedings can be initiated or continued in respect of that contravention.

*(Full-marks tip: Cite both Section 15(1) — the 180-day period and the authorities — and Section 15(2) — the bar on further proceedings; a common deduction is omitting the 180-day period or the Section 15(2) effect.)*

### Q100. Ch: FEMA, 1999 — Compounding: Application and Consequences (Marks: 8) [Application]
**Question:** Mr. Suresh, a resident, contravened certain provisions of FEMA, 1999 in the course of an overseas investment. On being advised, he wishes to have the contravention compounded rather than face adjudication. Advise Mr. Suresh on the following, with reference to Section 15 of FEMA, 1999 and the relevant rules:
(i) Whether he can voluntarily apply to compound the contravention, and to whom the application is to be made.
(ii) Within what time the compounding authority must dispose of his application.
(iii) The consequence if, after compounding, the sum for which the contravention is compounded is not paid within the prescribed period.
(iv) Whether, once the contravention is compounded, adjudication proceedings can still be initiated against him for the same contravention.

**Answer:**
The issues concern the availability, procedure, time-limit and consequences of compounding a contravention under **Section 15 of FEMA, 1999** read with the Compounding Proceedings Rules.

**(i) Voluntary application and authority to whom made:**
Under Section 15(1), any contravention under Section 13 may, **on an application made by the person committing such contravention**, be compounded. Compounding is thus a voluntary process initiated by the contravener himself. Mr. Suresh may therefore make an application in the prescribed form for compounding of the contravention. The application is to be made to the **compounding authority**, i.e. the authorised officers of the **Reserve Bank of India** or the **Directorate of Enforcement**, the appropriate authority being determined with reference to the amount/sum involved in the contravention.

**(ii) Time-limit for disposal:**
The compounding authority must dispose of the application and compound the contravention within **180 days from the date of receipt of the application** by it, as provided in Section 15(1).

**(iii) Consequence of non-payment of the compounded sum:**
On the contravention being compounded, the compounding authority issues an order specifying the sum payable. This sum must be paid within the period specified in the order (generally **15 days** from the date of the order). **If the sum is not paid within the specified period, it shall be deemed that the contravener has never made any application for compounding**, and the compounding shall not take effect; consequently the person becomes liable to face **adjudication proceedings** for the contravention as if no compounding had taken place. Further, the amount cannot ordinarily be paid in instalments.

**(iv) Whether adjudication can still be initiated once compounded:**
Under Section 15(2), where a contravention has been compounded (and the sum duly paid), **no proceeding or further proceeding shall be initiated or continued** against the person in respect of the contravention so compounded. Therefore, once the contravention is validly compounded and the compounded sum is paid, **no adjudication proceedings can be initiated or continued** against Mr. Suresh for the same contravention.

**Conclusion:**
1. Mr. Suresh can voluntarily apply for compounding to the authorised officers of the RBI / Directorate of Enforcement.
2. The application must be disposed of within 180 days of its receipt.
3. If the compounded sum is not paid within the specified period, it will be deemed that no compounding application was made and he will be liable to adjudication.
4. Once validly compounded and the sum paid, no adjudication proceedings can be initiated or continued against him for the same contravention (Section 15(2)).

*(Full-marks tip: Address all four limbs separately and cite Section 15(1) and 15(2); the most-missed points are the "deemed never applied" consequence of non-payment within 15 days and the Section 15(2) bar on further proceedings.)*
