### Q1. Ch: Preliminary & Definitions — Small Company (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Sunridge Textiles Private Limited seeks your advice on whether it qualifies as a "small company" under the Companies Act, 2013 for the financial year 2023-24. The following figures relate to the immediately preceding financial year (2022-23):

| Particulars | Amount |
|---|---|
| Paid-up share capital | ₹3.80 crore |
| Turnover as per profit & loss account | ₹38 crore |

The company is not a holding or subsidiary company, is not a company registered under Section 8, and is not governed by any special Act. Examine whether it is a small company. Would your answer differ if the company were a subsidiary of Meridian Fabrics Limited (a public company)?

**Answer:**
The expression "small company" is defined in **Section 2(85) of the Companies Act, 2013**. A small company means a company, other than a public company, whose (i) **paid-up share capital** does not exceed such amount as may be prescribed, which shall not be more than ₹10 crore, and (ii) **turnover** as per the profit and loss account for the immediately preceding financial year does not exceed such amount as may be prescribed, which shall not be more than ₹100 crore.

Under the **Companies (Specification of Definitions Details) Rules, 2014** (as amended w.e.f. 15.09.2022), the prescribed thresholds are **paid-up capital not exceeding ₹4 crore** and **turnover not exceeding ₹40 crore**. Both conditions must be satisfied cumulatively (the word used is "and").

Applying the law to the facts of Sunridge Textiles Private Limited:

| Condition | Limit prescribed | Company's figure | Satisfied? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Paid-up share capital | ≤ ₹4 crore | ₹3.80 crore | Yes |
| Turnover (preceding FY) | ≤ ₹40 crore | ₹38 crore | Yes |

Since both the paid-up capital and the turnover are within the prescribed limits, and the company is neither a public company, nor a holding/subsidiary company, nor a Section 8 company, nor governed by a special Act, **Sunridge Textiles Private Limited is a small company** for FY 2023-24.

**Effect of being a subsidiary:** The **proviso to Section 2(85)** expressly excludes from the definition of small company (a) a holding company or a subsidiary company, (b) a Section 8 company, and (c) a company governed by any special Act. Therefore, if Sunridge Textiles Private Limited were a subsidiary of Meridian Fabrics Limited, it would **cease to be a small company** irrespective of the fact that its capital and turnover are within the prescribed limits.

**Conclusion:** On the given facts the company is a small company; however, if it is a subsidiary of a public company, it is disqualified from being treated as a small company by virtue of the proviso to Section 2(85).

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards quoting Section 2(85) with BOTH the ₹4 crore/₹40 crore prescribed limits AND the "and" (cumulative) test; the common deduction is stating the outer statutory ceilings of ₹10 crore/₹100 crore as the operative limits or forgetting the holding/subsidiary exclusion in the proviso.)*

### Q2. Ch: Preliminary & Definitions — One Person Company (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Mr. Aravind, an Indian citizen, wishes to incorporate a One Person Company (OPC) for his consultancy business. He raises the following queries:
(i) He is a resident who stayed in India for 118 days during the immediately preceding financial year — is he eligible to form an OPC?
(ii) He wants his minor son to be the nominee of the OPC. Is this permissible?
(iii) His projected paid-up capital is ₹60 lakh and expected annual turnover is ₹2.50 crore in the third year — can the OPC continue, or must it convert?
(iv) He also wants to simultaneously incorporate a second OPC for a real estate venture.
Advise Mr. Aravind with reference to the Companies Act, 2013 and the relevant Rules.

**Answer:**
A **One Person Company** is defined in **Section 2(62)** as a company which has only one person as a member. Its incorporation is governed by **Section 3(1)(c)** and by **Rule 3 of the Companies (Incorporation) Rules, 2014**.

**(i) Eligibility – residency:** Under **Rule 3(1)** (as amended w.e.f. 01.04.2021), only a **natural person who is an Indian citizen**, whether resident in India or otherwise, is eligible to incorporate an OPC and be its nominee. The term "resident in India" now means a person who has stayed in India for a period of **not less than 120 days** during the immediately preceding financial year. Mr. Aravind stayed only 118 days; however, after the 2021 amendment residency is **no longer a bar** — even a non-resident Indian citizen may form an OPC. Hence he is **eligible**.

**(ii) Minor as nominee:** **Rule 3(4)** provides that no minor shall become a member or nominee of an OPC or hold a share with beneficial interest. Therefore, Mr. Aravind **cannot appoint his minor son as the nominee**.

**(iii) Capital / turnover – conversion:** After the 2021 amendment, the earlier ceiling (paid-up capital of ₹50 lakh and turnover of ₹2 crore) that compelled conversion of an OPC into a private/public company has been **omitted**. An OPC may now **grow without any threshold-based compulsory conversion**. Hence, even with a paid-up capital of ₹60 lakh and turnover of ₹2.50 crore, the **OPC may continue** as an OPC; conversion is voluntary.

**(iv) Second OPC:** **Rule 3(2)** provides that a natural person shall not be a member of more than **one OPC** at any point of time and shall not be a nominee of more than one OPC. Consequently, Mr. Aravind **cannot incorporate a second OPC** while he is the member of the first.

**Conclusion:** (i) Eligible; (ii) minor son cannot be nominee; (iii) OPC may continue, no compulsory conversion; (iv) he cannot form a second OPC.

*(Full-marks tip: Cite Rule 3 sub-rule by sub-rule; the biggest deduction in 2024 exams is applying the old ₹50 lakh/₹2 crore compulsory-conversion limits, which have been withdrawn.)*

### Q3. Ch: Preliminary & Definitions — Associate & Subsidiary Company (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Determine the relationship (holding, subsidiary, associate or none) between the companies in each independent situation below, citing the relevant section:

| Case | Facts |
|---|---|
| (a) | Ellora Ltd. holds 19% of the total voting power of Fern Ltd. and controls 3 out of 8 directors on Fern's Board. |
| (b) | Ganga Ltd. holds 45% of the paid-up equity share capital of Hima Ltd. |
| (c) | Indus Ltd. controls the composition of the Board of Jhelum Ltd. though it holds only 12% of its shares. |
| (d) | Kaveri Ltd. is a subsidiary of Jhelum Ltd. (in case c above). |

**Answer:**
The relevant provisions are **Section 2(87)** (subsidiary company), **Section 2(46)** (holding company) and **Section 2(6)** (associate company / significant influence).

**Law:** Under **Section 2(87)**, a subsidiary is a company in which the holding company (i) **controls the composition of the Board of Directors**, or (ii) exercises or controls **more than one-half of the total voting power**, either on its own or together with subsidiaries. Under **Section 2(6)**, an "associate company" means a company in which the other company has **significant influence** (control of at least 20% of the total voting power, or control of or participation in business decisions under an agreement), but which is not a subsidiary; and it includes a joint venture company.

**Application:**

| Case | Analysis | Relationship |
|---|---|---|
| (a) | Ellora holds 19% voting power (below 20%) and controls only 3/8 directors (not majority of Board, not >½ voting power). Neither significant influence (20%) nor control of Board composition exists. | **None** |
| (b) | Ganga holds 45% voting power — this is ≥ 20% but not more than one-half. So it is significant influence, not control. | Hima is an **associate** of Ganga |
| (c) | Indus controls the **composition of the Board** of Jhelum; control of Board composition by itself makes it a subsidiary irrespective of shareholding. | Jhelum is a **subsidiary** of Indus |
| (d) | By virtue of **Explanation (a) to Section 2(87)**, a company shall be deemed to be a subsidiary of the holding company even if the control is through another subsidiary. Kaveri is a subsidiary of Jhelum, which is itself a subsidiary of Indus. | Kaveri is a **(deemed) subsidiary of Indus** |

**Conclusion:** (a) no relationship; (b) associate; (c) subsidiary through Board control; (d) Kaveri is a subsidiary of Indus by the chain/deeming provision.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner looks for the distinction between "more than one-half" (subsidiary) and "at least 20%" (associate/significant influence), and awareness that control of Board composition alone creates a subsidiary regardless of the shareholding percentage.)*

### Q4. Ch: Preliminary & Definitions — Government & Foreign Company (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Examine, with reasons and the relevant section, whether the following are correctly classified:
(i) Coastal Ports Ltd. has a paid-up capital of ₹100 crore, of which the Central Government holds 26%, the Government of Maharashtra holds 20%, and the Government of Gujarat holds 12%. The company claims it is a Government company.
(ii) Novatek Inc., incorporated in Singapore, has no place of business in India but conducts business through an agent in India who solicits and concludes contracts in India on Novatek's behalf. Novatek says it is not a foreign company.

**Answer:**
**(i) Government company – Section 2(45):** A "Government company" means any company in which **not less than 51%** of the paid-up share capital is held by (a) the Central Government, or (b) any State Government(s), or (c) partly by the Central Government and partly by one or more State Governments, and includes a subsidiary of a Government company.

Combined Government holding in Coastal Ports Ltd. = 26% (Centre) + 20% (Maharashtra) + 12% (Gujarat) = **58%**, held partly by the Central Government and partly by two State Governments. Since 58% ≥ 51%, **Coastal Ports Ltd. is a Government company**. Its claim is correct.

**(ii) Foreign company – Section 2(42):** A "foreign company" means any company or body corporate incorporated **outside India** which (a) has a **place of business in India** whether by itself or through an agent, physically or through electronic mode; and (b) conducts any **business activity in India** in any other manner. Under the **Companies (Registration of Foreign Companies) Rules, 2014**, "place of business" includes a share transfer or registration office, and business through an agent who has and habitually exercises authority to conclude contracts is a place of business.

Novatek Inc. is incorporated in Singapore and, though it has no physical office, it conducts business through an agent in India who concludes contracts on its behalf. This constitutes a place of business through an agent and business activity in India. Hence **Novatek Inc. is a foreign company**; its claim is incorrect.

**Conclusion:** (i) Coastal Ports Ltd. is a Government company (58% Government holding); (ii) Novatek Inc. is a foreign company as it carries on business in India through an agent.

*(Full-marks tip: For 2(45) add the holdings and test against 51% and remember the "partly Centre partly State" limb; for 2(42) stress that business through an agent constitutes a place of business in India.)*

### Q5. Ch: Preliminary & Definitions — Officer in Default & Key Managerial Personnel (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between the terms "Key Managerial Personnel" and "Officer who is in default" under the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:**
The two expressions are defined respectively in **Section 2(51)** and **Section 2(60)** of the Companies Act, 2013.

**Key Managerial Personnel [Section 2(51)]** in relation to a company means — (i) the Chief Executive Officer or the managing director or the manager; (ii) the company secretary; (iii) the whole-time director; (iv) the Chief Financial Officer; (v) such other officer, not more than one level below the directors who is in whole-time employment, designated as KMP by the Board; and (vi) such other officer as may be prescribed. It identifies the top managerial personnel who run the company.

**Officer who is in default [Section 2(60)]** means any of the following officers of a company who shall be liable to any penalty or punishment for a contravention: whole-time director; KMP; where there is no KMP, such director(s) specified by the Board or all directors; any person who under the immediate authority of the Board is charged with compliance; any person on whose advice/direction the Board acts; every director aware of the contravention or who did not act diligently; and, in respect of share issue/transfer, the share transfer agents/registrars/merchant bankers.

| Basis | Key Managerial Personnel [2(51)] | Officer in default [2(60)] |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Identifies persons in charge of management | Identifies persons liable for penalty/punishment |
| Scope | A defined, limited list of top officers | Wider — includes KMP, certain directors and even third parties (RTA, merchant bankers) |
| Consequence | Governance/appointment obligations (Sec 203) | Attracts liability for defaults |

**Conclusion:** All KMP are potentially officers in default, but the class of "officers in default" is much wider than "KMP".

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner wants the two definitions quoted and a table showing that "officer in default" is a liability concept broader than the management concept of KMP.)*

### Q6. Ch: Preliminary & Definitions — Related Party & Deemed Public Company (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Zephyr Private Limited is a wholly-owned subsidiary of Cyclone Limited, a public company. During a Board meeting a director contended that since Zephyr is a "private company" by its name and articles, the restrictions applicable to public companies (such as those on acceptance of deposits from public and on further issue of capital) cannot apply to it. Examine the validity of this contention with reference to the proviso to Section 2(71) of the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:**
The term "public company" is defined in **Section 2(71)** of the Companies Act, 2013. A public company means a company which (a) is **not a private company**; and (b) has a minimum paid-up share capital as may be prescribed.

The **proviso to Section 2(71)** contains a deeming fiction: **a company which is a subsidiary of a company, not being a private company, shall be deemed to be a public company** for the purposes of this Act even where such subsidiary company continues to be a private company in its articles.

**Application to facts:** Zephyr Private Limited is a wholly-owned subsidiary of Cyclone Limited, which is a public company. By operation of the proviso to Section 2(71), Zephyr, though private by name and by its articles, is **deemed to be a public company** for the purposes of the Act. Consequently the exemptions and relaxations available to private companies do not apply to it, and the provisions applicable to public companies (including those relating to acceptance of deposits and further issue of share capital, wherever relevant) govern Zephyr.

**Conclusion:** The director's contention is **not valid**. Zephyr Private Limited, being a subsidiary of a public company, is a deemed public company under the proviso to Section 2(71), and the private-company exemptions are not available to it.

*(Full-marks tip: The key is the proviso to Section 2(71) and the phrase "even where such subsidiary company continues to be a private company in its articles"; state clearly that the deeming is "for the purposes of this Act".)*

### Q7. Ch: Incorporation of Company — Formation & Documents (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** A group of seven persons wishes to incorporate "Vireo Public Limited". Advise them on:
(i) the minimum number of members required and the type of company they may form;
(ii) the documents and declarations to be filed with the Registrar for registration under Section 7; and
(iii) the consequence if it is later found that the certificate of incorporation was obtained by furnishing false information.

**Answer:**
**(i) Minimum members – Section 3(1):** A company may be formed for any lawful purpose by (a) **seven or more persons** where the company to be formed is a **public company**; (b) two or more persons for a private company; and (c) one person for a One Person Company. Since seven persons desire to form a public company, they satisfy the minimum requirement for a **public company** under Section 3(1)(a).

**(ii) Documents for registration – Section 7(1):** An application for incorporation is filed with the Registrar within whose jurisdiction the registered office is proposed, together with the following:
(a) the **memorandum and articles** of the company duly signed by all subscribers;
(b) a **declaration by an advocate/CA/CS/CWA** engaged in the formation and by a person named as director/subscriber that all requirements of the Act and rules in respect of registration have been complied with [Sec 7(1)(b)];
(c) a **declaration by each subscriber and first director** (in the prescribed form) that he is not convicted of any offence in connection with promotion/formation/management of a company, and that all documents are true and correct [Sec 7(1)(c)];
(d) the **address for correspondence** till the registered office is established;
(e) **particulars of every subscriber** with proof of identity and of first directors along with their **consent to act as director**;
(f) particulars of the interests of the first directors in other firms/bodies corporate.

On being satisfied, the Registrar registers the documents and issues a **certificate of incorporation** [Sec 7(2)] and allots a **Corporate Identity Number (CIN)** [Sec 7(3)].

**(iii) False information – Section 7(5) & 7(6):** If any person furnishes **false or incorrect particulars** or suppresses material information in the incorporation documents, he shall be liable under **Section 447 (fraud)** [Sec 7(5)]. Further, under **Section 7(6)**, where a company has been incorporated by furnishing false information or by suppression of material facts, the promoters, first directors and the persons making the declaration shall each be liable for **action under Section 447**. Under **Section 7(7)**, the Tribunal may pass orders for regulation of management, liability of members as unlimited, removal of name from the register or winding up.

**Conclusion:** Seven persons may form a public company; the Section 7 documents must be filed; and false information exposes the promoters/first directors to fraud liability under Sections 447 and to Tribunal action under Section 7(7).

*(Full-marks tip: List the Section 7(1) documents distinctly, and for part (iii) connect Section 7(5)/(6) with Section 447 and the Tribunal's powers under Section 7(7).)*

### Q8. Ch: Incorporation of Company — Registered Office & Commencement of Business (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Helios Ltd. was incorporated on 5th April 2024 with a share capital clause in its memorandum. Examine the following, citing the relevant sections:
(i) By when must the company have a registered office and give notice of it to the Registrar?
(ii) The company opened a bank account and commenced borrowing on 20th April 2024 without filing any declaration. Is this permissible?
(iii) The subscribers had agreed to bring ₹25 lakh but have not paid the subscription money even by 30th September 2024. What is the consequence?

**Answer:**
**(i) Registered office – Section 12:** Under **Section 12(1)**, a company shall, **within thirty days of its incorporation** and at all times thereafter, have a registered office capable of receiving and acknowledging communications. Under **Section 12(2)**, the company shall furnish to the Registrar **verification of its registered office within thirty days** of incorporation. Thus Helios Ltd. must have a registered office and file its verification within 30 days from 5th April 2024, i.e., by **5th May 2024**.

**(ii) Commencement of business – Section 10A:** A company incorporated after the commencement of the Companies (Amendment) Ordinance, 2018 and **having a share capital** shall not **commence any business or exercise any borrowing powers** unless — (a) a **declaration** is filed by a director within **180 days** of incorporation, in the prescribed form, stating that every subscriber has **paid the value of the shares agreed to be taken** by him; and (b) the company has filed verification of its registered office under Section 12.

Helios Ltd. commenced borrowing on 20th April 2024 **without filing the declaration** under Section 10A. This is **not permissible** — a company cannot borrow or commence business before filing the commencement-of-business declaration. Doing so attracts penalty: the company is liable to a penalty of **₹50,000** and every officer in default to a penalty of **₹1,000 per day** subject to a maximum of **₹1,00,000**.

**(iii) Non-payment of subscription / non-filing:** If the declaration under Section 10A(1) is not filed within 180 days (because the subscribers have not paid), and the Registrar has reasonable cause to believe that the company is not carrying on any business, he may initiate action for the **removal of the name of the company** from the register of companies under **Section 10A(3)** read with Chapter XVIII. Thus continued non-payment of the subscription money and consequent non-filing may lead to striking off of the company's name.

**Conclusion:** (i) registered office and verification by 5th May 2024; (ii) borrowing before the Section 10A declaration is impermissible and penal; (iii) non-payment beyond 180 days can lead to removal of the company's name under Section 10A(3).

*(Full-marks tip: Keep the 30-day (Sec 12) and 180-day (Sec 10A) periods distinct and quote the Section 10A penalty of ₹50,000 / ₹1,000 per day up to ₹1,00,000.)*

### Q9. Ch: Incorporation of Company — Memorandum: Ultra Vires Doctrine (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** The objects clause of the memorandum of Orchid Pharma Ltd. authorises it to manufacture and sell pharmaceutical products. The Board, without altering the memorandum, entered into a contract to purchase a rubber plantation and to run it as a commercial venture. The company later refused to pay the seller, contending the contract was ultra vires. The seller argues that all shareholders had informally consented. Decide, explaining the doctrine of ultra vires with reference to decided law.

**Answer:**
**Doctrine of ultra vires:** The memorandum of association defines the objects of a company under **Section 4(1)(c)** of the Companies Act, 2013. A company can act only within the powers conferred by its objects clause and by the Act; any act **beyond (ultra) the powers (vires)** of the company is void. The leading authority is **Ashbury Railway Carriage & Iron Co. Ltd. v. Riche (1875)**, where a contract outside the objects clause (financing a railway line) was held void, and it was further held that such a contract **cannot be ratified even by the unanimous consent of all the shareholders**, because the incapacity is that of the company itself.

**Key consequences of the doctrine:**
(a) An ultra vires act is **void ab initio** and does not bind the company;
(b) It **cannot be ratified** even by a unanimous resolution of all members;
(c) The company can neither sue nor be sued upon such a contract;
(d) Property acquired under an ultra vires transaction is nonetheless the company's property and is protected.

**Application:** Running a rubber plantation has no reasonable connection with the object of manufacturing pharmaceutical products and is therefore **beyond the objects clause** of Orchid Pharma Ltd. The contract is ultra vires the company and **void**. The seller's argument of informal consent of all shareholders is of no avail because, on the authority of *Ashbury v. Riche*, an ultra vires act **cannot be ratified even by all shareholders acting unanimously**. Hence the company is not bound and the seller cannot enforce payment against the company.

**Conclusion:** The contract is ultra vires and void; the seller cannot recover the price from the company notwithstanding the alleged unanimous consent of the shareholders.

*(Full-marks tip: Cite Ashbury v. Riche and stress the non-ratifiability of an ultra vires act — this is the point examiners specifically look for and it is the pivot of the answer.)*

### Q10. Ch: Incorporation of Company — Doctrine of Constructive Notice & Indoor Management (Marks: 8) [Application]
**Question:** The articles of Cobalt Ltd. provide that a bill of exchange, to be binding on the company, must be signed by **two directors and countersigned by the secretary**. A bill was signed by only one director and the secretary and endorsed to Mr. Pratik, a holder for value who had no knowledge of the internal irregularity. In a separate matter, Mr. Sen advanced a loan to the company relying on a board resolution which, unknown to him, had never actually been passed. Examine the enforceability of each transaction, explaining the doctrines of constructive notice and indoor management with the relevant exceptions.

**Answer:**
**Doctrine of constructive notice:** The memorandum and articles of a company, on registration, become **public documents** open to inspection by anyone. Therefore, every person dealing with the company is deemed to have **constructive notice of the contents of the memorandum and articles**, whether or not he has actually read them. He is presumed not only to have read them but also to have understood them in their proper meaning. This doctrine protects the company against outsiders.

**Doctrine of indoor management (Turquand's rule):** As a counter to the harshness of constructive notice, the rule in **Royal British Bank v. Turquand (1856)** provides that persons dealing with a company in good faith are entitled to **assume that the internal proceedings and formalities of the company (the "indoor" affairs) have been duly complied with**. An outsider is bound to know the public documents but is **not required to enquire into the regularity of the internal proceedings**; he may presume regularity of the indoor affairs.

**Exceptions to indoor management** (where the outsider cannot claim protection): (i) **Knowledge of the irregularity**; (ii) **Negligence / suspicion of irregularity** — where the circumstances are suspicious and the outsider fails to make enquiry; (iii) **Forgery** — the rule does not validate a forged document, which is a nullity; (iv) acts **outside the apparent authority** of the officer; and (v) where the outsider had no knowledge of the articles at all.

**Application:**
*Transaction 1 (the bill):* The requirement of two directors' signatures plus the secretary's countersignature is a matter of **internal management** disclosed in the articles. Mr. Pratik, a holder for value without knowledge of the irregularity, is entitled to assume that the internal requirement of signatures had been duly complied with under the doctrine of indoor management. However, the actual signing by only one director instead of two is a defect Mr. Pratik could have detected on the face of the instrument; but as a bona fide holder for value without notice he is generally **protected** unless the defect was apparent or he was put on enquiry. On the facts (no knowledge, holder for value), the **bill is enforceable** against the company.

*Transaction 2 (the loan):* Mr. Sen relied on a board resolution which in fact had **never been passed**. Whether a resolution was actually passed is again a matter of internal proceedings which an outsider cannot verify; under Turquand's rule Mr. Sen was entitled to assume the resolution had been duly passed. As he acted in good faith without knowledge of the irregularity and none of the exceptions apply, the **loan is enforceable** against the company.

**Conclusion:** Both Mr. Pratik and Mr. Sen are protected by the doctrine of indoor management and the transactions are enforceable against Cobalt Ltd., since neither had knowledge of the internal irregularity and no exception (forgery, negligence, or knowledge) is attracted.

*(Full-marks tip: Set the two doctrines against each other, list ALL exceptions to indoor management, and then apply — the examiner deducts marks where the forgery/knowledge exceptions are omitted or where the doctrines are merely defined without application.)*

### Q11. Ch: Incorporation of Company — Lifting the Corporate Veil (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Mr. Vasudev, a former employee of Titan Instruments Ltd., had signed a covenant not to solicit the company's customers for three years after leaving. On resigning, he floated a private company, "Vasu Marketing Pvt. Ltd.", in which he and his wife were the only members, and through that company he began soliciting the very customers he had covenanted not to approach. Titan Instruments Ltd. sued to restrain both Mr. Vasudev and the company. Advise, explaining the circumstances in which the corporate veil may be lifted.

**Answer:**
**Principle of separate legal personality:** On incorporation a company becomes a **separate legal person** distinct from its members, as established in **Salomon v. Salomon & Co. Ltd. (1897)**. Ordinarily the veil between the company and its members is not pierced. However, the veil is a privilege that must not be used as an instrument of fraud or evasion; in appropriate cases the courts **"lift" or "pierce" the corporate veil** to look at the realities behind the corporate façade.

**Circumstances in which the veil may be lifted (judicial grounds):**
(a) **Protection of revenue / prevention of tax evasion** — *Dinshaw Maneckjee Petit, Re*;
(b) **Prevention of fraud or improper conduct** — *Gilford Motor Co. Ltd. v. Horne*;
(c) Where the company is a **sham or cloak / device** to evade legal obligations;
(d) Company acting as the **agent** of the shareholders;
(e) Determination of the **enemy character** of a company — *Daimler Co. Ltd. v. Continental Tyre & Rubber Co.*;
(f) Where the corporate form is used to **avoid a legal obligation** or defeat public interest.

**Application:** The facts are on all fours with **Gilford Motor Co. Ltd. v. Horne**, where an employee who had covenanted not to solicit his former employer's customers formed a company to do exactly that, and the court granted an injunction against both the employee and the company, treating the company as a **mere cloak or sham** to enable the breach of the covenant. Similarly, Vasu Marketing Pvt. Ltd. is a device formed by Mr. Vasudev to evade his contractual covenant not to solicit Titan's customers. The court will therefore **lift the corporate veil**, treat the company as a mere instrument of Mr. Vasudev, and grant an injunction restraining **both** Mr. Vasudev and Vasu Marketing Pvt. Ltd. from soliciting the customers.

**Conclusion:** The corporate veil will be lifted; the injunction will be granted against both the individual and the company, as the company is a sham to defeat the non-solicitation covenant.

*(Full-marks tip: Anchor the answer in Gilford Motor v. Horne (fraud/evasion of obligation) and note that relief lies against both the individual AND the company — merely citing Salomon without the piercing grounds loses marks.)*

### Q12. Ch: Incorporation of Company — Conversion & Effect of Registration (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** State the legal effects of registration of a company as laid down in Section 9 of the Companies Act, 2013. Also state the binding effect of the memorandum and articles under Section 10.

**Answer:**
**Effect of registration – Section 9:** From the **date of incorporation** mentioned in the certificate of incorporation, the subscribers to the memorandum and all other persons who may from time to time become members of the company, shall be a **body corporate** by the name contained in the memorandum. Such a body corporate is:
(a) **capable of exercising all the functions of an incorporated company** under the Act;
(b) having **perpetual succession** — the company continues to exist irrespective of changes in its membership; and
(c) with **power to acquire, hold and dispose of property**, both movable and immovable, tangible and intangible, in its own name; and
(d) to **contract, and to sue and be sued** by the said name.

The proviso clarifies that a company shall not have a **common seal** as a mandatory requirement (the use of a common seal is now optional).

**Binding effect of memorandum and articles – Section 10:** Subject to the provisions of the Act, the memorandum and articles, when registered, shall **bind the company and its members to the same extent as if they had been signed by the company and by each member**, and contained covenants on the part of the company and each member to observe all their provisions. Further, under **Section 10(2)**, all **money payable by any member** to the company under the memorandum or articles shall be a **debt due from him** to the company.

The effect of Section 10 is that the memorandum and articles constitute a **statutory contract** binding: (i) members to the company; (ii) the company to its members; and (iii) members inter se — but **not** the company to outsiders.

**Conclusion:** On registration under Section 9 the company becomes a body corporate with perpetual succession and the capacity to own property and sue/be sued; and under Section 10 its memorandum and articles bind the company and its members as a statutory contract.

### Q13. Ch: Prospectus & Allotment — Deemed Prospectus (Offer for Sale) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Nimbus Ltd. allotted its entire issue of 10,00,000 equity shares to an issue house, Bridgewater Securities Ltd., which within 15 days offered those shares to the public through a document that did not describe itself as a prospectus. Two investors, who subscribed on the strength of that document, later discovered a mis-statement in it. Advise the investors, explaining the concept of a "deemed prospectus" under Section 25 of the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:**
**Deemed prospectus – Section 25:** Where a company **allots or agrees to allot** any securities of the company with a view to **all or any of those securities being offered for sale to the public**, any document by which the **offer for sale to the public** is made shall, for all purposes, be **deemed to be a prospectus** issued by the company. All the provisions of the Act as to the contents of a prospectus and as to liability in respect of statements in and omissions from a prospectus shall apply to such a document as if it were a prospectus issued by the company (this is called an **"offer for sale"**).

Under **Section 25(2)**, it shall be **evidence that an allotment of, or agreement to allot, securities was made with a view to the securities being offered for sale to the public** if it is shown that — (a) the offer of the securities to the public was made **within six months** after the allotment or agreement to allot; or (b) at the date when the offer was made, the whole consideration to be received by the company in respect of the securities had **not been received** by it.

Under **Section 25(3)**, the document is to state (in addition to the ordinary matters) the **net amount of the consideration** received or to be received by the company, and the **place and time** at which the contract for allotment may be inspected. Under **Section 25(4)**, where the offer is made by a company, it is sufficient if the document is **signed on behalf of the company by two directors**, and where by a firm, by not less than half of its partners.

**Application:** Nimbus Ltd. allotted the entire issue to Bridgewater Securities Ltd., which offered the shares to the public **within 15 days** (well within six months). This raises the presumption under Section 25(2)(a) that the allotment was made with a view to offering the shares for sale to the public. Therefore, the offer document issued by Bridgewater is a **deemed prospectus** of Nimbus Ltd., even though it is not titled a prospectus. Consequently, the provisions as to contents and, importantly, **liability for mis-statements** apply as if it were a prospectus issued by Nimbus Ltd.

**Conclusion:** The offer document is a deemed prospectus under Section 25; the investors may proceed against Nimbus Ltd. (and the persons responsible) for the mis-statement as if it were a prospectus issued by the company.

*(Full-marks tip: Emphasise the two presumptive tests in Section 25(2) — the six-month period and consideration not fully received — and connect the deeming to liability for mis-statements.)*

### Q14. Ch: Prospectus & Allotment — Civil Liability for Mis-statement (Marks: 8) [Application]
**Question:** The prospectus of Solaris Ltd. contained a statement, purportedly made on the authority of an expert, valuer Mr. Ghosh, over-valuing certain assets. Mr. Amit subscribed for 5,000 shares of face value ₹10 each relying on the prospectus and suffered loss when the truth emerged. Examine, under Section 35 of the Companies Act, 2013:
(i) against whom Mr. Amit may claim compensation;
(ii) the defences available to a director; and
(iii) the position of the expert, Mr. Ghosh.

**Answer:**
**Civil liability for mis-statements in prospectus – Section 35:** Where a person has **subscribed** for securities of a company acting on any **mis-statement** included in a prospectus, or on the inclusion or omission of any matter likely to mislead, and has **sustained loss or damage** as a consequence, the company and every person specified below shall be **liable to pay compensation** to every such person for the loss or damage sustained.

**(i) Persons liable [Section 35(1)]:** The persons liable to pay compensation are —
(a) **every director** of the company at the time of the issue of the prospectus;
(b) every person who has **authorised himself to be named** and is named as a director, or has agreed to become a director;
(c) every **promoter** of the company;
(d) every person who has **authorised the issue** of the prospectus; and
(e) every person who is an **expert** referred to in Section 26(5).

Therefore Mr. Amit may claim compensation from the company and any/all of the above persons.

**(ii) Defences available to a director [Section 35(2)]:** A person shall **not be liable** under Section 35(1) if he proves —
(a) that, having consented to become a director, he **withdrew his consent before the issue** of the prospectus, and that it was issued without his authority or consent; or
(b) that the prospectus was issued **without his knowledge or consent**, and that on becoming aware of its issue, he **forthwith gave a reasonable public notice** that it was issued without his knowledge or consent; or
(c) that, as regards an expert's statement, he had **reasonable ground to believe and did believe** up to the time of allotment that the person making the statement was competent and had given the consent required, and had not withdrawn it.

**(iii) Position of the expert, Mr. Ghosh:** Under **Section 26(5)**, a statement purporting to be made by an expert can be included in a prospectus only if the expert is **not engaged or interested in the formation, promotion or management of the company**, and has given his **written consent** to the issue and has not withdrawn it before delivery of the prospectus for registration. As an expert whose statement is included, Mr. Ghosh is one of the persons liable under Section 35(1)(e). However, an expert is liable **only in respect of the untrue statement made by him in his capacity as an expert**, and he may escape liability by proving, under the defences in Section 35, that he had reasonable ground to believe (and did believe) the statement was true, or that he **withdrew his consent in writing before delivery** of the prospectus for registration.

Additionally, **Section 34** imposes **criminal liability** for an untrue prospectus, and **Section 447 (fraud)** may apply. A person who makes the statement fraudulently may also be liable, but under **Section 35(3)** a director etc. who acted fraudulently cannot claim the statutory defences.

**Conclusion:** Mr. Amit may recover compensation under Section 35 from the company, directors, promoters, persons authorising the issue and the expert; a director may escape liability on the statutory defences in Section 35(2); and Mr. Ghosh is liable for his expert statement unless he proves reasonable belief in its truth or valid withdrawal of consent.

*(Full-marks tip: The three-part structure — who is liable (35(1)), directors' defences (35(2)), and the expert's specific liability/defence (read with 26(5)) — must all appear; do not confuse civil liability (Sec 35) with criminal liability (Sec 34).)*

### Q15. Ch: Prospectus & Allotment — Private Placement (Marks: 8) [Application]
**Question:** Aurora Ltd., a public company, proposes to raise funds through a private placement of equity shares. Advise the company on the following, with reference to Section 42 of the Companies Act, 2013 and the relevant Rules:
(i) the maximum number of persons to whom the offer may be made in a financial year;
(ii) the mode of receiving the application money and the prohibition on utilisation;
(iii) the time limit for allotment and the consequence of default; and
(iv) whether the company may make a fresh offer while an earlier offer is pending.

**Answer:**
**Private placement – Section 42:** A "private placement" means any offer or invitation to subscribe or issue of securities to a **select group of persons** by a company (other than by way of a public offer) through a **private placement offer-cum-application letter (PAS-4)**, subject to the conditions in Section 42.

**(i) Maximum number of persons:** Under **Section 42(2)** read with **Rule 14 of the Companies (Prospectus and Allotment of Securities) Rules, 2014**, an offer of securities under private placement may be made to **not more than 200 persons in the aggregate in a financial year**, excluding **qualified institutional buyers (QIBs)** and employees offered securities under a scheme of ESOP. This ceiling of 200 is reckoned **individually for each kind of security** (equity, preference, debentures). If an offer is made to more than the prescribed number, it is deemed to be a **public offer** and the provisions relating to public issues apply.

**(ii) Application money and prohibition on utilisation:** Under **Section 42(4)**, every identified person willing to subscribe shall apply through the application form; all monies payable towards subscription shall be paid through **cheque, demand draft or other banking channels** but **not by cash**. Under **Section 42(6)**, a company making an offer shall keep the application money in a **separate bank account** in a scheduled bank, and it shall **not be utilised** for any purpose other than (a) for **adjustment against allotment** of securities; or (b) for the **repayment of monies** where the company is unable to allot securities.

**(iii) Time limit for allotment / default:** Under **Section 42(6)**, a company shall **allot its securities within sixty days** from the date of receipt of the application money. If the company **defaults** in allotting within 60 days, it shall **repay the application money** within **fifteen days** from the expiry of the sixty days; failing which the company is liable to repay the money **with interest at 12% per annum** from the expiry of the sixtieth day. Further, contravention of Section 42 renders the company, its promoters and directors liable to a **penalty** which may extend to the **amount raised through the private placement or ₹2 crore, whichever is lower**, and the company shall also refund all monies to subscribers within 30 days of the penalty order.

**(iv) Fresh offer while earlier offer pending:** Under **Section 42(5)**, **no fresh offer or invitation** under private placement shall be made unless the allotments with respect to any offer made earlier have been **completed, or that offer has been withdrawn or abandoned** by the company. Hence Aurora Ltd. **cannot make a fresh private placement offer** while a previous offer is still pending.

**Conclusion:** The offer may be made to at most 200 persons (excluding QIBs and ESOP employees) per financial year per kind of security; money must be received through banking channels and kept in a separate account; allotment must be within 60 days failing which refund with 12% interest; and no fresh offer can be made while an earlier one is pending.

*(Full-marks tip: State the 200-person ceiling with the QIB/ESOP exclusions and the "per kind of security" refinement, the "no cash" rule, the 60-day/15-day/12% timeline, and the 42(5) bar on overlapping offers — omitting any one of these standard limbs costs marks.)*

### Q17. Ch: Prospectus & Allotment — Types of Prospectus & Variation in Terms (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Write short notes on the following, citing the relevant sections of the Companies Act, 2013:
(i) Shelf prospectus;
(ii) Red herring prospectus; and
(iii) Variation in terms of contract or objects stated in the prospectus.

**Answer:**
**(i) Shelf prospectus – Section 31:** A **shelf prospectus** means a prospectus in respect of which the securities or class of securities included therein are issued for subscription **in one or more issues over a certain period without the issue of a further prospectus**. Any class of companies, as prescribed by SEBI, may file a shelf prospectus with the Registrar at the stage of the first offer of securities, **indicating a period not exceeding one year** as the period of validity of such prospectus, which shall commence from the date of opening of the first offer. In respect of a second or subsequent offer of such securities during the validity period, no further prospectus is required. A company filing a shelf prospectus is required to file an **information memorandum in Form PAS-2** containing the material facts, updated financial position, changes and new charges created, prior to a subsequent offer.

**(ii) Red herring prospectus – Section 32:** A **red herring prospectus** means a prospectus which **does not include complete particulars of the quantum or price** of the securities included therein. A company proposing to make an offer of securities may issue a red herring prospectus **prior to the issue of a prospectus**, and it shall be filed with the Registrar **at least three days** prior to the opening of the subscription list and the offer. It carries the same obligations as are applicable to a prospectus, and any variation between the red herring prospectus and the final prospectus shall be highlighted as variations in the final prospectus. Upon closing of the offer, the **final prospectus**, stating the total capital raised and the closing price of the securities, shall be filed with the Registrar and SEBI.

The company shall give the details of such resolution in the notice, and the notice shall be published in newspapers and placed on the website. The **dissenting shareholders** (those who have not agreed to the proposal to vary) shall be given an **exit opportunity** by the promoters/controlling shareholders at such price and manner as specified by SEBI.

**Conclusion:** A shelf prospectus (Sec 31) avoids repeated prospectuses within a year; a red herring prospectus (Sec 32) omits price/quantum and precedes the final prospectus; and any variation of contract terms/objects (Sec 27) requires a special resolution with an exit offer to dissenting shareholders.

### Q23. Ch: Share Capital & Debentures — Buy-back of Securities u/s 68: Conditions (Marks: 6) [Theory]

### Q26. Ch: Share Capital & Debentures — Issue of Debentures u/s 71 (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** State the key provisions of Section 71 of the Companies Act, 2013 regarding the issue of debentures, including the position as to voting rights, convertibility, the appointment of a debenture trustee and the creation of a Debenture Redemption Reserve.

**Answer:**
The issue of debentures is governed by **Section 71 of the Companies Act, 2013** read with **Rule 18** of the Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules, 2014.

1. **No voting rights [Sec 71(2)]:** No company shall issue any debentures carrying **any voting rights**. A debenture holder is a creditor and not a member.

2. **Convertible debentures [Sec 71(1)]:** A company may issue debentures with an option to convert them into shares, either wholly or partly, at the time of redemption. However, the issue of debentures with an option to **convert into shares** (wholly or partly) must be approved by a **special resolution** passed in a general meeting.

3. **Secured debentures:** Debentures may be secured by creating a charge on the properties/assets of the company, as per Rule 18. The redemption period generally shall not exceed **10 years** (extendable to 30 years for specified infrastructure companies).

4. **Debenture Trustee [Sec 71(5)]:** A company issuing a **prospectus or making an offer/invitation to the public or to its members exceeding 500** for the subscription of debentures must **appoint a debenture trustee** before such issue, and execute a debenture trust deed to protect the interest of debenture holders.

5. **Debenture Redemption Reserve (DRR) [Sec 71(4) + Rule 18(7)]:** Where debentures are issued, the company shall **create a Debenture Redemption Reserve** account out of the profits available for dividend, and the amount credited to it shall be utilised only for the redemption of debentures.

6. **Default remedy [Sec 71(10)]:** If a company fails to redeem debentures on maturity or to pay interest when due, the **Tribunal** may, on application of any/all debenture holders (or the trustee), direct the company to redeem the debentures forthwith by payment of principal and interest.

**Conclusion:** Debentures issued under Section 71 carry no voting rights; convertible debentures need a special resolution; a debenture trustee is mandatory where the offer exceeds 500 persons; and a Debenture Redemption Reserve must be created and used solely for redemption.

Would your answer differ if the charge had been created on 5th December 2019?

**Answer:** Section 77(1) of the Companies Act, 2013 casts the primary duty on the company creating a charge — whether on its property or undertaking, whether tangible or otherwise, and whether situated in or outside India — to register the particulars of the charge, signed by the company and the charge-holder, together with the instrument, with the Registrar within **thirty days** of its creation.

Further, under **Section 86(2)**, if any person **wilfully furnishes false or incorrect information** or knowingly suppresses material particulars in the registration, he shall be liable for action under **Section 447** (fraud).

**Conclusion:** The unregistered charge is void against the liquidator; the lender is only an unsecured creditor although the underlying debt survives. The company is liable to a penalty of ₹5,00,000 and every officer in default to ₹50,000 under Section 86.

### Q42. Ch: Management & Administration — Quorum for Board Meeting & Interested Directors (Sec 174 & Sec 184) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** The Board of Vertex Pharma Limited consists of 9 directors. At a meeting convened to approve a contract in which 6 of the directors are interested, only 5 directors are present in all (of whom the said 6 interested directors would need to be excluded from voting on that item). Determine whether a valid quorum exists for transacting the interested-contract item, applying Sections 174 and 184 of the Companies Act, 2013, and state the outcome if the number of non-interested directors falls below the required minimum.

**Answer:** **Quorum — Section 174(1).** Section 174(1) provides that the quorum for a meeting of the Board of Directors of a company shall be **one-third of its total strength or two directors, whichever is higher**, and the participation of directors by video conferencing or other audio-visual means shall also be counted for the purposes of quorum. Any fraction in one-third is **rounded off to the next whole number**.

Total strength = 9 directors. One-third of 9 = 3. Two directors = 2. Quorum required = higher of the two = **3 directors**.

**Effect of interested directors — Section 174(3) read with Section 184(2).** Section 184(2) prohibits an **interested director** from participating in the discussion of, or voting on, a contract or arrangement in which he is concerned or interested; his presence is **not counted for the purpose of quorum** at the time of any such discussion or vote. Section 174(3) provides that where at any time the number of **interested directors exceeds or is equal to two-thirds of the total strength**, the number of **remaining directors who are not interested and are present** at the meeting, **being not less than two**, shall be the quorum during such time.

Applying to the facts: 6 of the 9 directors are interested in the contract. Interested directors (6) as a fraction of total strength (9) = **two-thirds**, which "exceeds or is equal to two-thirds". Therefore, Section 174(3) applies, and the quorum for the interested item is the **non-interested directors present, not less than two**. Non-interested directors = 9 − 6 = 3; of the 5 present, the non-interested ones present must be counted. Since at least **two non-interested directors** are present, a valid quorum exists **for the interested-contract item**.

**If non-interested directors fall below two:** If fewer than two non-interested directors are present, the quorum requirement under Section 174(3) is **not met**, and the item involving the interested contract **cannot be validly transacted**. Under Section 174(4), if a meeting could not be held for want of quorum, then (unless the articles provide otherwise) the meeting stands **adjourned** to the same day, time and place in the next week.

**Conclusion:** For the interested-contract item, the reduced quorum under Section 174(3) applies (non-interested directors present, minimum two); as at least two non-interested directors are present, the quorum is valid. If fewer than two non-interested directors were present, the item could not be transacted.

*(Full-marks tip: Show the arithmetic — 1/3 of total strength rounded up — and then apply the special "≥ two-thirds interested" rule of Sec 174(3) with the "not less than two" floor. Missing the interaction between Sec 174 and Sec 184(2) loses marks.)*

### Q43. Ch: Management & Administration — Notice and Explanatory Statement for General Meeting (Sec 101 & Sec 102) (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the statutory requirements as to the length and mode of notice of a general meeting under Section 101, and the contents of the explanatory statement to be annexed to the notice under Section 102 of the Companies Act, 2013. What are the consequences of a default or omission in the explanatory statement?

**Answer:** **Length and mode of notice — Section 101.** Section 101(1) provides that a general meeting of a company may be called by giving **not less than clear twenty-one days' notice** either in writing or through electronic mode. The words "clear twenty-one days" mean that the day of service of notice and the day of the meeting are **excluded**. The proviso permits a general meeting to be called at **shorter notice** if consent is given, in writing or by electronic mode, by **not less than 95%** of the members entitled to vote at the meeting. Section 101(3) states that notice shall be given to **every member, legal representative of a deceased member, assignee of an insolvent member, the auditor(s), and every director** of the company. Under Section 102(5), any default in complying with Section 102 attracts a penalty of **₹50,000 or five times the amount of benefit accruing** to such person, whichever is higher.

**Conclusion:** A general meeting requires clear 21 days' notice (shorter with 95% consent), and the notice must annex an explanatory statement disclosing material facts and interests for special business; non-disclosure results in the defaulter holding the benefit in trust and compensating the company, plus penalty under Section 102(5).

### Q44. Ch: Management & Administration — Quorum for General Meeting (Sec 103) (Marks: 5) [Application]

### Q45. Ch: Management & Administration — Proxies (Sec 105) (Marks: 6) [Application]

### Q46. Ch: Management & Administration — Ordinary vs Special Resolution (Sec 114) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between an ordinary resolution and a special resolution under Section 114 of the Companies Act, 2013. Give two examples of matters requiring a special resolution.

**Answer:** Section 114 of the Companies Act, 2013 defines the two categories of resolutions passed at a general meeting.

**Ordinary resolution — Section 114(1).** A resolution is an ordinary resolution when the notice required under the Act has been duly given and it is required to be passed by the votes cast (whether on a show of hands or electronically or on a poll) **in favour of the resolution** (including the casting vote of the chairman, if any) by members entitled and present in person or by proxy or by postal ballot, **exceeding the votes cast against** the resolution. In short, a **simple majority** (votes for > votes against) suffices.

**Special resolution — Section 114(2).** A resolution is a special resolution when: (a) the **intention to propose it as a special resolution** has been duly specified in the notice; (b) the notice has been duly given; and (c) the votes cast **in favour** of the resolution (whether on a show of hands, electronically or on a poll) by members entitled and present in person or by proxy or postal ballot are **not less than three times the number of votes cast against** the resolution. That is, a **majority of not less than 75%** is required.

| Basis | Ordinary Resolution [Sec 114(1)] | Special Resolution [Sec 114(2)] |
|---|---|---|
| Majority required | Simple majority (for > against) | Not less than 3:1 (75%) |
| Intention in notice | Need not be specified as such | Must be specified as "special resolution" |
| Examples | Adoption of accounts; appointment of auditors; declaration of dividend | Alteration of articles (Sec 14); change of registered office from one State to another; reduction of capital |
| Filing with Registrar | Generally not filed (except specified) | Filed in Form MGT-14 within 30 days |

**Two examples requiring a special resolution:** (i) **Alteration of the articles of association** under Section 14; and (ii) **Change of the company's registered office from one State to another** under Section 13 / **buy-back of securities** under Section 68.

**Conclusion:** An ordinary resolution needs a simple majority whereas a special resolution requires a 75% majority with the intention specified in the notice; matters such as alteration of articles and change of registered office from one State to another require a special resolution.

### Q47. Ch: Management & Administration — Postal Ballot (Sec 110) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** What is meant by transacting business through postal ballot under Section 110 of the Companies Act, 2013? State which items are compulsorily to be passed through postal ballot and the exceptions. Also state the effect of a resolution assented to by the requisite majority through postal ballot.

**Answer:** Section 110 of the Companies Act, 2013 read with **Rule 22 of the Companies (Management and Administration) Rules, 2014** provides for the transaction of certain business by means of **postal ballot** — that is, obtaining the vote of members in writing (including by electronic means) instead of at a physically convened general meeting.

**Items to be transacted by postal ballot — Section 110(1)(a).** The Central Government has, by the Rules, prescribed certain items of business that a company **shall transact only by means of postal ballot**. These prescribed items (Rule 22) include, among others: alteration of the objects clause of the memorandum; alteration of articles to insert provisions defining a private company; change in place of registered office outside the local limits of any city, town or village; **change in objects for which money was raised through a prospectus**; issue of shares with differential voting rights; variation in the rights attached to a class of shares or debentures; buy-back of shares under Section 68; election of a small shareholders' director; sale of the whole or substantially the whole of an undertaking; and giving loans/guarantees/security beyond the limits under Section 186.

**Power to transact other items — Section 110(1)(b).** Any item of business, **other than ordinary business** and any business in respect of which directors or auditors have a right to be heard, **may** be transacted by postal ballot at the option of the company.

**Exceptions.** **Ordinary business** and business in respect of which directors or auditors have a right to be heard **cannot** be transacted by postal ballot. Further, a **proviso to Section 110(1)** provides that where a company is required to provide the facility of **e-voting** under the Act, any item which is required to be transacted only by postal ballot may be transacted at a **general meeting** by a company with such e-voting facility, and postal ballot is not mandatory in that situation.

**Effect — Section 110(2).** If a resolution is **assented to by the requisite majority** of the shareholders by means of postal ballot, it shall be deemed to have been **duly passed at a general meeting** convened in that behalf.

**Conclusion:** Postal ballot enables passing of prescribed resolutions in writing/electronically; ordinary business and matters where directors/auditors have a right to be heard are excepted; and a resolution passed by the requisite majority by postal ballot is deemed passed at a duly convened general meeting.

*(Full-marks tip: Mention the e-voting proviso — a company providing e-voting may transact even the mandatory postal-ballot items at a general meeting. Stating the "deemed passed at a general meeting" effect earns the concluding marks.)*

### Q48. Ch: Management & Administration — Voting by Show of Hands and Demand for Poll (Sec 107 & Sec 109) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** At the AGM of Solstice Motors Limited (a company having a share capital), a resolution was put to vote by show of hands and declared carried. Immediately, a group of members present demanded a poll. The chairman refused, stating the resolution had already been declared passed. Examine, under Sections 107 and 109 of the Companies Act, 2013, whether a poll can be demanded after a declaration on show of hands, who may demand a poll, and how the result on show of hands stands thereafter.

**Answer:** **Voting by show of hands — Section 107.** Section 107(1) provides that at any general meeting, a resolution put to the vote shall, **unless a poll is demanded** under Section 109 (or unless e-voting is required), be decided on a **show of hands** in the first instance. Under Section 107(2), a **declaration by the chairman** that the resolution has or has not been carried on a show of hands, and an entry to that effect in the minutes, is **conclusive evidence** of the fact, **without proof of the number or proportion of votes** cast — but this is subject to the right to demand a poll.

**Demand for poll — Section 109.** Section 109(1) provides that before or on the declaration of the result of the voting on any resolution on a show of hands, a **poll may be ordered** by the chairman of his own motion, and **shall be ordered on a demand made** in that behalf:
- in the case of a company **having a share capital**, by the members present in person or by proxy, where allowed, and having **not less than one-tenth of the total voting power** or holding shares on which an **aggregate sum of not less than ₹5,00,000** (or such higher amount as may be prescribed) has been paid up; and
- in the case of a company **not having a share capital**, by any member(s) present in person or by proxy having **not less than one-tenth of the total voting power**.

Thus, a poll can be demanded **on or before the declaration of the result** on a show of hands. Where a valid demand is made by members meeting the above threshold, the chairman is **bound to order a poll**; his refusal is not permissible if the demand is valid.

**Effect on the show-of-hands result.** Once a poll is validly demanded and ordered, the **decision on show of hands ceases to have effect** and the resolution is decided by the **result of the poll**. Under Section 109(4), the demand for a poll may be **withdrawn** at any time by the persons who made it.

Applying to the facts: the demand was made **immediately** after the declaration, i.e., "on the declaration of the result" — which is within the time permitted by Section 109(1). If the demanding members hold **at least one-tenth of the total voting power** or shares on which at least **₹5,00,000** is paid up, the chairman was **bound to order a poll** and his refusal was **improper**. The earlier show-of-hands result would then stand superseded by the poll.

**Conclusion:** A poll may be validly demanded on or before the declaration of the show-of-hands result; where members holding not less than one-tenth of voting power or ₹5,00,000 paid-up value so demand, the chairman must order a poll, and the chairman's refusal in Solstice Motors Limited was improper. The show-of-hands result stands displaced by the poll result.

*(Full-marks tip: Quote the exact thresholds — 1/10th voting power OR ₹5,00,000 paid-up — and state that the demand must be "before or on" the declaration. Note that a valid demand makes ordering the poll mandatory for the chairman.)*

### Q49. Ch: Management & Administration — Annual General Meeting (Sec 96) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Nimbus Agro Limited was incorporated on 5th July 2024 and its first financial year ended on 31st March 2025. It seeks your advice on: (i) the time-limit for holding its first AGM; (ii) the gap permitted between two AGMs; (iii) the permissible day, time and place for the meeting; and (iv) whether an extension of time can be obtained. Advise with reference to Section 96 of the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:** Section 96 of the Companies Act, 2013 governs the holding of the **Annual General Meeting (AGM)** by every company other than a One Person Company.

**(i) Time-limit for the first AGM — Section 96(1) proviso.** In the case of the **first annual general meeting**, the company shall hold it **within a period of nine months from the date of closing of the first financial year**. Where a company holds its first AGM within this period, it is **not necessary** to hold any AGM in the year of its incorporation. Nimbus Agro Limited's first financial year closed on **31st March 2025**; hence its first AGM must be held **within nine months, i.e., on or before 31st December 2025**.

**(ii) Gap between two AGMs — Section 96(1).** In the case of **subsequent** AGMs, the meeting must be held each year, and the **gap between two AGMs shall not be more than fifteen months**. Additionally, every AGM (other than the first) must be held **within six months from the close of the financial year**.

**(iii) Day, time and place — Section 96(2).** Every AGM shall be called during **business hours, that is, between 9 a.m. and 6 p.m.**, on any day that is **not a National Holiday**, and shall be held either at the **registered office** of the company or at some other place **within the city, town or village** in which the registered office is situated. (The Central Government may exempt any company from these provisions, and for certain companies, general meetings may be held anywhere in India if consent is obtained as prescribed. An **unlisted company** may hold its AGM at any place in India if consent in writing/electronic mode is given by **all members** in advance.)

**(iv) Extension of time — Section 96(1) proviso.** The Registrar may, for **any special reason**, **extend the time** within which **any annual general meeting, other than the first annual general meeting**, shall be held, by a period **not exceeding three months**. Note that **no extension** is available for the **first AGM**. Since the meeting in question is the first AGM of Nimbus Agro Limited, **no extension of time can be granted** for it.

**Conclusion:** Nimbus Agro Limited must hold its first AGM on or before 31st December 2025 (nine months from 31st March 2025); subsequent AGMs must be within 15 months of each other and six months from year-end; the meeting must be in business hours on a non-National-Holiday at/near the registered office; and no extension is available for the first AGM (up to 3 months only for subsequent AGMs).

*(Full-marks tip: Distinguish "first AGM = 9 months, no extension" from "subsequent AGM = 6 months from FY-end, 15-month gap, extension up to 3 months". Mention the 9 a.m.–6 p.m. / non-National-Holiday requirement.)*

### Q50. Ch: Management & Administration — Annual Return (Sec 92) (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the requirements relating to the preparation, signing and filing of the annual return under Section 92 of the Companies Act, 2013. State the position regarding certification by a Company Secretary in practice and the consequences of default.

**Answer:** Section 92 of the Companies Act, 2013 read with **Rule 11 and Rule 12 of the Companies (Management and Administration) Rules, 2014** governs the **annual return**.

**Contents — Section 92(1).** Every company shall prepare an annual return in **Form MGT-7** (Form MGT-7A for OPC and small companies) containing the particulars **as they stood on the close of the financial year**, including: its registered office, principal business activities, particulars of its holding, subsidiary and associate companies; shares, debentures and other securities and shareholding pattern; members and debenture-holders along with changes therein; promoters, directors, key managerial personnel along with changes; meetings of members/Board/committees and attendance; remuneration of directors and KMP; penalties/punishments imposed and compounding of offences; matters relating to certification of compliances and disclosures; and details of shares held by FIIs, among others. **[Note:** the requirement to disclose the **indebtedness** of the company and the earlier requirement of an **extract of the annual return (MGT-9)** to be attached to the Board's report have been dispensed with; the company must place a copy of the annual return on its website, if any, and the web-link is given in the Board's report.]

**Signing — Section 92(1) proviso.** The annual return shall be signed by a **director and the company secretary**, or where there is no company secretary, by a **company secretary in practice**. In relation to a **One Person Company, a small company and a private company** (if such private company is a start-up), the annual return shall be signed by the **company secretary, or where there is no company secretary, by the director** of the company.

**Certification — Section 92(2).** The annual return of (a) a **listed company**, or (b) a company having **paid-up share capital of ₹10 crore or more** or **turnover of ₹50 crore or more**, shall be **certified by a company secretary in practice** in **Form MGT-8**, stating that the annual return discloses the facts correctly and adequately and that the company has complied with all the provisions of the Act.

**Filing — Section 92(4).** Every company shall file with the Registrar a copy of the annual return **within sixty days from the date on which the AGM is held**, or where no AGM is held in any year, **within sixty days from the date on which the AGM should have been held**, together with a statement specifying the reasons for not holding the AGM, with the prescribed fees/additional fees.

**Consequences of default — Section 92(5) and 92(6).** If a company fails to file its annual return under Section 92(4) before the expiry of the period specified, the **company** shall be liable to a penalty of **₹10,000** and in case of continuing failure, a **further penalty of ₹100 for each day** during which such failure continues, subject to a **maximum of ₹2,00,000** for the company and **₹50,000** for an officer in default. Under Section 92(6), a **company secretary in practice** who **certifies the annual return otherwise than in conformity** with the requirements shall be liable to a penalty of **₹2,00,000**.

**Conclusion:** The annual return in Form MGT-7 must state the specified particulars as at the close of the financial year, be signed by a director and the company secretary (or CS in practice), be certified in MGT-8 by a CS in practice for listed/large companies, and be filed within 60 days of the AGM; default attracts penalties under Section 92(5)/(6).

### Q51. Ch: Management & Administration — Minutes of Meetings (Sec 118) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** State the provisions of Section 118 of the Companies Act, 2013 regarding the preparation, signing and evidentiary value of minutes of meetings. What matters may the chairman exclude from the minutes, and what is the position of a Secretarial Standard in this regard?

**Answer:** Section 118 of the Companies Act, 2013 governs the maintenance of **minutes** of the proceedings of general meetings, meetings of the Board and of its committees, and resolutions passed by postal ballot.

**Preparation and time-limit — Section 118(1).** Every company shall cause **minutes** of the proceedings of every general meeting (including a meeting called by requisition), and of every meeting of the Board and of every committee, and every resolution passed by postal ballot, to be prepared and **kept within thirty days** of the conclusion of the meeting concerned (or passing of the resolution), by making entries in **books maintained for the purpose** with their pages consecutively numbered.

**Signing and contents — Section 118(2) & (4).** Minutes of each meeting shall contain a **fair and correct summary** of the proceedings, and shall be **signed and dated** by the chairman of the same meeting or the chairman of the next succeeding meeting. All appointments made at any meeting shall be included in the minutes. In the case of a **Board or committee meeting**, the minutes shall also contain (a) the **names of the directors present**, and (b) in the case of each resolution, the **names of directors dissenting from or not concurring** with the resolution.

**Chairman's discretion to exclude — Section 118(5).** The **chairman** shall exercise **absolute discretion** in regard to the inclusion or non-inclusion in the minutes of any matter which he considers to be (a) **defamatory** of any person, (b) **irrelevant or immaterial** to the proceedings, or (c) **detrimental to the interests of the company**. Such matters need not be recorded.

**Evidentiary value — Section 118(7).** The minutes kept in accordance with the section shall be **evidence of the proceedings** recorded therein. Section 118(8) provides that where minutes have been duly kept, then, until the contrary is proved, the meeting shall be **deemed to have been duly called and held**, all proceedings deemed to have duly taken place, and all appointments of directors, KMP, auditors, etc., deemed valid.

**Secretarial Standard — Section 118(10).** Every company shall **observe Secretarial Standards** with respect to general and Board meetings **specified by the Institute of Company Secretaries of India (ICSI)** and approved by the Central Government — namely **SS-1** (Board Meetings) and **SS-2** (General Meetings). Any tampering with minutes attracts penalty under **Section 118(12)** (₹25,000 for the company / ₹5,000 for the officer for tampering).

**Conclusion:** Minutes must be prepared within 30 days, signed by the chairman, contain a fair summary (with names/dissent for Board meetings), and serve as evidence of the proceedings; the chairman has absolute discretion to exclude defamatory, irrelevant or company-detrimental matter, and the company must observe Secretarial Standards SS-1 and SS-2.

### Q52. Ch: Declaration & Payment of Dividend — Dividend out of free reserves in a year of inadequate profits (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Vasudha Motors Ltd. earned a net profit of only ₹18 lakh (after tax) for the financial year 2025-26 and, wishing to maintain its dividend track record, proposes to declare dividend at 12% on its paid-up equity share capital of ₹4 crore by drawing on its accumulated free reserves. The relevant figures extracted from its books are given below. Examine, with reference to the Companies (Declaration and Payment of Dividend) Rules, 2014, whether and to what extent the company may declare the proposed dividend out of reserves.

| Particulars | Amount |
|---|---|
| Paid-up equity share capital | ₹4,00,00,000 |
| Free reserves (as on 01.04.2025) | ₹1,50,00,000 |
| Rate of dividend declared in 2022-23, 2023-24, 2024-25 (avg.) | 10% |
| Proposed rate of dividend for 2025-26 | 12% |
| Net profit after tax for 2025-26 | ₹18,00,000 |
| Depreciation for 2025-26 (not yet provided) | ₹6,00,000 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Statutory basis and the four conditions of Rule 3.**
Where, owing to inadequacy or absence of profits in any financial year, a company proposes to declare dividend out of the accumulated profits earned in previous years and transferred to free reserves, such declaration is governed by the second proviso to Section 123(1) read with **Rule 3 of the Companies (Declaration and Payment of Dividend) Rules, 2014**. The four conditions are: (a) the rate of dividend declared shall not exceed the average of the rates at which dividend was declared in the **three immediately preceding years**; (b) the total amount drawn from reserves shall not exceed **one-tenth of the sum of paid-up share capital and free reserves** as per the latest audited financial statement; (c) the amount so drawn shall first be used to **set off the losses** of the current financial year; and (d) the balance of reserves after such withdrawal shall not fall below **15% of the paid-up share capital**.

**WN-2 — Condition (a): ceiling on rate of dividend.**
Average rate of the three preceding years = 10%. The proposed rate is 12%, which **exceeds** the permissible 10%. Hence the rate must be capped at **10%**.
Maximum dividend at 10% on ₹4,00,00,000 = **₹40,00,000**.

**WN-3 — Profit available and amount to be drawn from reserves.**
Profit for the year must first be reduced by depreciation not yet provided (Sec 123(1)(b)): ₹18,00,000 − ₹6,00,000 = **₹12,00,000** available from current profits.
Amount required to be drawn from free reserves = ₹40,00,000 − ₹12,00,000 = **₹28,00,000**.

**WN-4 — Condition (b): maximum drawable from reserves (10% test).**
= 10% × (Paid-up capital + Free reserves) = 10% × (₹4,00,00,000 + ₹1,50,00,000) = 10% × ₹5,50,00,000 = **₹55,00,000**.
Amount to be drawn (₹28,00,000) < ₹55,00,000 — **satisfied.**

**WN-5 — Condition (d): residual reserve floor (15% test).**
Balance of free reserves after withdrawal = ₹1,50,00,000 − ₹28,00,000 = ₹1,22,00,000.
Minimum required = 15% × ₹4,00,00,000 = ₹60,00,000. Since ₹1,22,00,000 > ₹60,00,000 — **satisfied.** (Condition (c) is not attracted as there is no carried-forward loss to set off.)

| Statement Showing Permissible Dividend out of Reserves | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Maximum dividend permissible (capped @ 10%) | 40,00,000 |
| Less: Met out of current year's profit (18,00,000 − 6,00,000) | 12,00,000 |
| Amount to be drawn from free reserves | 28,00,000 |
| Maximum drawable under 10% test (WN-4) | 55,00,000 |
| Free reserves remaining vs. 15% floor (WN-5) | 1,22,00,000 vs 60,00,000 |

**Answer:** Vasudha Motors Ltd. **cannot** declare dividend at 12%; the rate is capped at **10%**, i.e., a total dividend of **₹40,00,000**, of which ₹12,00,000 comes from current profit and **₹28,00,000 is drawn from free reserves** — all four conditions of Rule 3 being satisfied.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner rewards testing all four Rule 3 conditions in sequence and, crucially, deducting unprovided depreciation from current profit before working out the shortfall; the commonest deduction is forgetting the rate-cap or applying the 15%/10% tests on the wrong base.)*

### Q55. Ch: Declaration & Payment of Dividend — Prohibitions, timelines and mode of payment (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** State the statutory provisions governing (i) the manner and time limit within which a declared dividend must be paid to shareholders, (ii) the mode of payment, and (iii) the circumstances in which a company is prohibited from failing to comply. Also state when a company shall **not** be deemed to have committed an offence for non-payment of dividend.

**Answer:**
The payment of declared dividend is regulated by **Section 127 of the Companies Act, 2013** read with Section 123(5).

**(i) Time limit for payment.** Where a dividend has been declared but is not paid or the warrant in respect thereof is not **posted within 30 days** from the date of declaration, every director who is knowingly a party to the default shall be **punishable with imprisonment up to 2 years and fine of not less than ₹1,000 for every day** during which the default continues, and the company shall be liable to pay **simple interest at 18% per annum** during the period of default.

**(ii) Mode of payment.** Under **Section 123(5)**, dividend shall be paid only to the registered shareholder or to his order or to his banker, and shall be paid in **cash** — it cannot be paid in kind. Payment may be made by cheque, warrant, or in any electronic mode. Any dividend payable in cash may be so paid.

**(iii) Exceptions — when non-payment is not an offence (proviso to Sec 127).** No offence shall be deemed to have been committed in the following cases: (a) where the dividend could not be paid by reason of the **operation of any law**; (b) where a shareholder has given **directions regarding payment** which cannot be complied with and the same has been communicated to him; (c) where there is a **dispute** regarding the right to receive the dividend; (d) where the dividend has been **lawfully adjusted** by the company against any sum due from the shareholder; and (e) where, for **any other reason not attributable to the default of the company**, the payment could not be made.

**Conclusion:** Declared dividend must be paid within 30 days in cash to the registered holder; failure attracts penal consequences under Section 127 unless one of the five statutory exceptions applies.

### Q56. Ch: Declaration & Payment of Dividend — Punishment for failure to distribute dividend (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** "Once a dividend is declared, it becomes a debt due from the company to its shareholders." In the light of this statement, discuss the nature of a declared dividend and the consequences of the company's failure to distribute it within the statutory period, distinguishing the position of the company from that of its directors.

**Answer:**
A dividend, once declared at the general meeting (final dividend) or by the Board (interim dividend), becomes a **debt owed by the company to the shareholders** and cannot ordinarily be revoked. The consequences of non-distribution are laid down in **Section 127 of the Companies Act, 2013**.

**Position of directors vis-à-vis the company.** The distinction between the liability of the directors and that of the company may be tabulated:

| Basis | Every director in default | The company |

### Q57. Ch: Accounts of Companies — Books of account: place, electronic form and preservation (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Zephyr Textiles Ltd., having its registered office at Coimbatore, maintains its books of account at its corporate office in Mumbai and additionally keeps its accounting records in electronic form on a cloud server whose data resides outside India. A shareholder objects. Advise the company on (i) the permissible place of keeping books of account, (ii) the conditions for maintaining books in electronic form, and (iii) the period for which books and vouchers must be preserved.

**Answer:**
The maintenance of books of account is governed by **Section 128 of the Companies Act, 2013** read with Rule 3 of the Companies (Accounts) Rules, 2014.

**(i) Place of keeping books (Sec 128(1) and proviso).** Every company must keep at its **registered office** proper books of account giving a true and fair view of the state of its affairs on **accrual basis and double entry** system. However, the proviso permits the Board of Directors to decide to keep all or any of the books at **any other place in India**; in that case the company must, within **7 days**, file with the Registrar a **notice in writing giving the full address of that other place**. Thus Zephyr Textiles Ltd. may keep its books at Mumbai provided the Board so resolves and files the notice with the Registrar within 7 days.

**(ii) Books in electronic form (Rule 3).** Books may be kept in electronic mode provided they remain **accessible in India** so as to be usable for subsequent reference; the information must be retained completely in the format in which it was originally generated, remain **complete and unaltered**, and the company must intimate to the Registrar (annually, at the time of filing financial statements) the **name of the service provider, the internet protocol address, the location of the service provider and, where the books are maintained on cloud, the address as provided by the service provider**. Crucially, a **back-up of the books maintained in electronic mode, including those maintained outside India, shall be kept in servers physically located in India** on a periodic basis.

**(iii) Period of preservation (Sec 128(5)).** The books of account together with the relevant vouchers shall be preserved in good order for a period of **not less than 8 financial years** immediately preceding the relevant financial year (or, where the company has been in existence for less than 8 years, for all the preceding years). Where an investigation has been ordered, the Central Government may direct preservation for a longer period.

**Conclusion:** Keeping books at Mumbai is valid subject to a Board resolution and 7-day notice to the Registrar; electronic maintenance on a foreign server is permitted **only if a back-up is kept on servers physically located in India** and the prescribed particulars are intimated; and the records must be preserved for at least **8 years**.

### Q58. Ch: Accounts of Companies — CSR: applicability, computation and unspent amount (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** Orion Infratech Ltd. is examining its Corporate Social Responsibility obligation for FY 2025-26. Its figures for the three immediately preceding financial years are given below. (i) Determine whether Section 135 applies; (ii) compute the amount the company must spend on CSR for FY 2025-26; and (iii) advise on the treatment of the amount if it remains unspent, distinguishing between an **ongoing project** and other cases.

| Financial Year | Net Worth (₹ cr) | Turnover (₹ cr) | Net Profit u/s 198 (₹ cr) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2022-23 | 480 | 920 | 4.20 |
| 2023-24 | 505 | 1,050 | 5.60 |
| 2024-25 | 530 | 1,140 | 6.70 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Applicability of Section 135(1).** Section 135 applies to every company which, during the **immediately preceding financial year** (2024-25), has: net worth ≥ **₹500 crore**, OR turnover ≥ **₹1,000 crore**, OR net profit ≥ **₹5 crore**. For 2024-25: net worth ₹530 cr (≥500), turnover ₹1,140 cr (≥1,000), net profit ₹6.70 cr (≥5) — **all three thresholds are crossed** (any one suffices). Hence Section 135 **applies**, and the company must constitute a CSR Committee and comply with the spending obligation.

**WN-2 — CSR spending obligation (Sec 135(5)).** The Board shall ensure that the company spends, in every financial year, **at least 2% of the average net profits made during the three immediately preceding financial years**, computed as per Section 198.
Average net profit = (₹4.20 cr + ₹5.60 cr + ₹6.70 cr) ÷ 3 = ₹16.50 cr ÷ 3 = **₹5.50 crore**.
CSR amount to be spent = 2% × ₹5.50 cr = **₹11,00,000 (₹0.11 crore)**.

| Statement Showing CSR Obligation for FY 2025-26 | ₹ crore |
|---|---|
| Net profit 2022-23 | 4.20 |
| Net profit 2023-24 | 5.60 |
| Net profit 2024-25 | 6.70 |
| Total | 16.50 |
| Average net profit (÷3) | 5.50 |
| CSR spend @ 2% | 0.11 (₹11,00,000) |

**WN-3 — Treatment of unspent amount (Sec 135(5) provisos, 135(6) and 135(7)).**
- **Ongoing project:** Any amount remaining unspent pursuant to an **ongoing project** must be transferred, within **30 days** of the end of the financial year, to a **special account called the "Unspent Corporate Social Responsibility Account"** opened in a scheduled bank, and spent towards the project within **3 financial years**. If still unspent thereafter, it must be transferred to a **Fund specified in Schedule VII** within 30 days of the end of the third financial year.
- **Other than ongoing project:** Any unspent amount not relating to an ongoing project must be transferred **within 6 months** of the end of the financial year to a **Fund specified in Schedule VII** (e.g., PM National Relief Fund / PM CARES Fund, etc.).
- **Excess spending:** Where a company spends in excess of its 2% requirement, it may **set off** the excess against the obligation of the **succeeding three financial years**, subject to conditions.

**WN-4 — Consequence of default (Sec 135(7)).** On failure to comply, the **company** is liable to a penalty of **twice the unspent amount required to be transferred, or ₹1 crore, whichever is less**; and every **officer in default** is liable to **one-tenth of the unspent amount so required to be transferred, or ₹2 lakh, whichever is less**.

**Answer:** Section 135 **applies**; the company must spend **₹11,00,000** on CSR for FY 2025-26. Unspent amounts of an **ongoing project** go to the Unspent CSR Account within 30 days (usable over 3 years); other unspent amounts go to a Schedule VII Fund within 6 months.

*(Full-marks tip: compute the 2% strictly on Section 198 net profit averaged over three years, and clearly split the two unspent-amount routes — 30-day/Unspent CSR Account for ongoing projects vs. 6-month/Schedule VII Fund for the rest; the penalty limbs earn the final marks.)*

### Q60. Ch: Accounts of Companies — Re-opening and voluntary revision of accounts (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** The management of Halcyon Foods Ltd. discovers, after its financial statements for FY 2024-25 have been adopted at the AGM, that certain material transactions were erroneously accounted, and separately a regulator has raised concern that its earlier statements were fraudulently prepared. Advise the Board on (i) the circumstances and procedure for **re-opening** of accounts, and (ii) the option of **voluntary revision** of financial statements or the Board's report.

**Answer:**
**(i) Re-opening or re-casting of accounts on court/Tribunal order (Sec 130).** A company shall **not re-open its books of account and not re-cast its financial statements** unless an application is made by the **Central Government, the Income-tax authorities, SEBI, any other statutory regulatory body or authority, or any person concerned**, and an **order is made** by a **court of competent jurisdiction or the Tribunal (NCLT)** to the effect that (a) the relevant earlier accounts were prepared in a **fraudulent manner**, or (b) the affairs of the company were **mismanaged** during the relevant period, casting doubt on the reliability of the financial statements. Notice must be given to the concerned parties before passing the order, and accounts so revised or re-cast shall be **final**. No order shall be made in respect of accounts relating to a period **earlier than 8 financial years** immediately preceding the current year (unless a Sec 128(5) direction for longer preservation exists). As the regulator alleges fraudulent preparation, the proper course is an application to NCLT/court under Section 130.

**(ii) Voluntary revision by the Board (Sec 131).** If it appears to the directors of a company that (a) the **financial statement**, or (b) the **Board's report**, do **not comply with Section 129 or Section 134**, they may prepare **revised financial statements or a revised report** in respect of **any of the three preceding financial years** after obtaining **approval of the Tribunal** on an application made by the company. The Tribunal shall give notice to the Central Government and the Income-tax authorities and consider their representations. Such revision may be made **only once in a financial year**, and the detailed reasons for revision must be disclosed. For the erroneously accounted transactions, this voluntary route under Section 131 is available.

**Conclusion:** Where fraud/mismanagement is alleged, re-opening requires a **court/NCLT order under Section 130**; for a bona-fide non-compliance discovered by the directors, the Board may itself seek **Tribunal approval for voluntary revision under Section 131**, limited to the three preceding years and once a year.

*(Full-marks tip: keep the two sections distinct — Sec 130 is compulsory re-opening on external application + court order; Sec 131 is voluntary revision by directors with Tribunal approval; mixing them up is the standard error.)*

### Q61. Ch: Accounts of Companies — National Financial Reporting Authority (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State any four functions of the National Financial Reporting Authority (NFRA) as provided under Section 132 of the Companies Act, 2013, and briefly indicate its disciplinary powers over auditors.

**Answer:**
The **National Financial Reporting Authority (NFRA)** is constituted under **Section 132 of the Companies Act, 2013** to provide for matters relating to accounting and auditing standards.

**Functions (Sec 132(2)) — any four:**
1. **Make recommendations** to the Central Government on the formulation and laying down of **accounting standards and auditing standards** for adoption by companies or their auditors.
2. **Monitor and enforce compliance** with accounting standards and auditing standards in the prescribed manner.
3. **Oversee the quality of service** of the professions associated with ensuring compliance with such standards, and suggest measures for improvement in the quality of service.
4. Perform such **other functions** relating to the above as may be prescribed.

**Disciplinary powers (Sec 132(4)).** Where prescribed classes of companies are involved, NFRA has power to **investigate**, either suo motu or on a reference by the Central Government, into matters of professional or other misconduct committed by any member or firm of chartered accountants. For this purpose it has the **powers of a civil court**. Where professional or other misconduct is proved, it may impose penalty — a fine of **not less than ₹1 lakh** which may extend to **five times the fees received** (for individuals) / **ten times** (for firms) — and **debar** the member or firm from practice for a **minimum of six months up to ten years**.

**Conclusion:** NFRA recommends and enforces accounting/auditing standards, oversees audit quality, and exercises quasi-judicial disciplinary powers over auditors of the notified class of companies.

### Q62. Ch: Audit & Auditors — Appointment of first auditor (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Answer the following with reference to Section 139 of the Companies Act, 2013: (i) Sunrise Polymers Ltd. (a non-Government company) was incorporated on 5th April 2025; its Board has not appointed the first auditor even after 40 days. Advise on who is to appoint and by when. (ii) Bharat Rail Components Ltd. is a Government company; state who appoints its first auditor and the timelines. (iii) State the tenure of the first auditor.

**Answer:**
The appointment of the **first auditor** is governed by **Section 139(6) and 139(7)** of the Companies Act, 2013.

**(i) Non-Government company (Sec 139(6)).** The first auditor shall be appointed by the **Board of Directors within 30 days** from the date of **registration** of the company. If the Board fails to do so, it shall **inform the members**, who shall appoint the first auditor within **90 days at an extraordinary general meeting (EGM)**. For Sunrise Polymers Ltd., the Board has defaulted (40 days elapsed); therefore the members must now appoint the first auditor at an EGM within 90 days of registration.

**(ii) Government company (Sec 139(7)).** In the case of a Government company (or a company owned/controlled, directly or indirectly, by the Central/State Government), the first auditor shall be appointed by the **Comptroller and Auditor-General of India (C&AG) within 60 days** from the date of registration. If the C&AG does not appoint within 60 days, the **Board** shall appoint within the **next 30 days**; and on the Board's failure, it shall inform the members, who shall appoint within **60 days at an EGM**. Thus for Bharat Rail Components Ltd., the C&AG appoints within 60 days.

**(iii) Tenure of the first auditor.** In either case the first auditor holds office **till the conclusion of the first Annual General Meeting**.

**Conclusion:** Sunrise Polymers Ltd.'s members must appoint the first auditor at an EGM within 90 days; Bharat Rail Components Ltd.'s first auditor is appointed by the **C&AG within 60 days** — both holding office only up to the conclusion of the first AGM.

### Q63. Ch: Audit & Auditors — Rotation of auditors (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Empyrean Steel Ltd. is a listed company. Its present audit firm, M/s Nakshatra & Associates, has been its statutory auditor for the last 10 continuous years. The Board wishes to know: (i) whether the firm can be re-appointed; (ii) the cooling-off period; and (iii) whether the retiring firm's network firm or a firm with common partners can be appointed immediately. Advise under Section 139(2) and the applicable Rules.

**Answer:**
The **rotation of auditors** is mandated by **Section 139(2) of the Companies Act, 2013** read with **Rule 5 and Rule 6 of the Companies (Audit and Auditors) Rules, 2014**.

**Applicability.** Rotation applies to **listed companies** and the prescribed classes of unlisted companies (unlisted public companies with paid-up capital ≥ ₹10 crore; private companies with paid-up capital ≥ ₹50 crore; or any company having public borrowings/deposits ≥ ₹50 crore). Empyrean Steel Ltd., being **listed**, is squarely covered.

**(i) & (ii) Maximum term and re-appointment.** Under Section 139(2), such a company shall not appoint or re-appoint:
- an **individual** as auditor for **more than one term of 5 consecutive years**; and
- an **audit firm** as auditor for **more than two terms of 5 consecutive years each** (i.e., a maximum of **10 years**).
An individual/firm that has completed its term shall **not be re-appointed** as auditor in the same company for **5 years (cooling-off period)** from the completion of the term. Nakshatra & Associates, having completed **10 years (two terms)**, has **exhausted the maximum term** and cannot be re-appointed; it must sit out a **5-year cooling-off period**.

**(iii) Network firms / common-partner firms barred.** As a further safeguard, during the 5-year cooling-off period, **no audit firm having common partners** with the outgoing firm as on the date of appointment, and **no firm under the same network** of the outgoing firm, shall be appointed as auditor of the same company. Hence Empyrean Steel Ltd. **cannot** immediately appoint a network firm of, or a firm sharing a common partner with, Nakshatra & Associates.

**Conclusion:** Nakshatra & Associates has completed the maximum permissible **two terms (10 years)** and must be rotated out; it — and any common-partner or same-network firm — is ineligible for **5 years**. Empyrean Steel Ltd. must appoint an unconnected auditor.

*(Full-marks tip: state the 1-term/5-yr individual and 2-term/10-yr firm limits, the 5-year cooling-off, AND the network/common-partner bar — dropping the last point is the usual reason marks are lost.)*

### Q64. Ch: Audit & Auditors — Removal and resignation of auditor (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish, under the Companies Act, 2013, between the **removal of an auditor before the expiry of his term** and the **resignation of an auditor**, covering the applicable section, the procedure/approvals required, and the consequences of default.

**Answer:**
Both matters are dealt with in **Section 140 of the Companies Act, 2013**.

**Removal before expiry of term (Sec 140(1)) and resignation (Sec 140(2) & (3)).** The distinction may be tabulated:

| Basis | Removal before expiry of term (Sec 140(1)) | Resignation by the auditor (Sec 140(2)/(3)) |
|---|---|---|
| Initiated by | The **company** | The **auditor** himself |

### Q65. Ch: Audit & Auditors — Disqualifications for appointment as auditor (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Examine, with reference to Section 141(3) of the Companies Act, 2013, whether the following persons are **qualified** to be appointed as auditor of Pinnacle Cements Ltd.:

| Case | Facts |
|---|---|
| (a) | Mr. A, a chartered accountant, whose spouse holds equity shares of Pinnacle Cements Ltd. of face value ₹1,20,000 |
| (b) | M/s B & Co., a firm in which a partner is indebted to Pinnacle Cements Ltd. for ₹6,00,000 |
| (c) | Mr. C, who is a director in Solace Ltd., the subsidiary of Pinnacle Cements Ltd. |
| (d) | Mr. D, a chartered accountant, who is in full-time employment elsewhere |

**Answer:**
The disqualifications for appointment as auditor are enumerated in **Section 141(3)** read with **Rule 10 of the Companies (Audit and Auditors) Rules, 2014**.

**(a) Relative holding securities (Sec 141(3)(d)(i)).** A person is disqualified if he, **his relative or partner** holds any **security or interest** in the company. However, a **relative** may hold securities of **face value not exceeding ₹1,00,000** (Rule 10 limit). Here the spouse holds shares of face value **₹1,20,000**, which **exceeds ₹1,00,000**. Therefore Mr. A is **disqualified**. (Had it been within ₹1,00,000, a corrective-action window of 60 days would apply.)

**(b) Partner indebted to the company (Sec 141(3)(d)(ii)).** A person is disqualified if he, his relative or partner is **indebted to the company** for an amount **exceeding ₹5,00,000**. The partner of M/s B & Co. is indebted for **₹6,00,000**, which exceeds the ₹5,00,000 threshold. Hence M/s B & Co. is **disqualified**.

**(c) Director of subsidiary / holding structure (Sec 141(3)(c) & related).** A person who is a **director or key managerial personnel**, or a partner/relative of such a person, is disqualified from auditing that company; and by extension the independence tests extend to the group. Mr. C is a **director of Solace Ltd., the subsidiary** of Pinnacle Cements Ltd. A person who holds a position that impairs independence within the group is disqualified. Mr. C is **disqualified** from being appointed auditor of Pinnacle Cements Ltd.

**(d) Full-time employment elsewhere (Sec 141(3)(g) proviso/related).** A person in **full-time employment elsewhere** cannot practise as an auditor, and thus **cannot be appointed** as the company's auditor. Mr. D is **disqualified**.

| Case | Provision | Conclusion |
|---|---|---|
| (a) | 141(3)(d)(i) + Rule 10 (₹1 lakh limit) | Disqualified (₹1.20 lakh > ₹1 lakh) |
| (b) | 141(3)(d)(ii) (₹5 lakh limit) | Disqualified (₹6 lakh > ₹5 lakh) |
| (c) | 141(3)(c) / group independence | Disqualified |
| (d) | 141(3)(g) related – full-time employment | Disqualified |

**Conclusion:** **None** of Mr. A, M/s B & Co., Mr. C or Mr. D is qualified to be appointed auditor of Pinnacle Cements Ltd.

### Q66. Ch: Audit & Auditors — Auditor's duty to report fraud (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** During the statutory audit of Aurora Pharma Ltd. for FY 2025-26, the auditor, M/s Vidyut & Co., has reason to believe that an offence of fraud involving ₹1.20 crore is being committed against the company by certain officers. In a separate instance, the auditor detects a fraud of ₹85 lakh. Advise the auditor on his **reporting obligations** under Section 143(12) and the applicable Rule, distinguishing the two cases.

**Answer:**
The auditor's duty to report fraud is contained in **Section 143(12) of the Companies Act, 2013** read with **Rule 13 of the Companies (Audit and Auditors) Rules, 2014**.

**The reporting threshold — fraud of ₹1 crore or more.** If an auditor of a company, in the course of the performance of his duties as auditor, has **reason to believe that an offence of fraud involving an amount of ₹1 crore or more** is being or has been committed against the company by its officers or employees, he shall **report the matter to the Central Government** in the prescribed manner. The procedure under Rule 13 is:
1. The auditor shall **forward his report to the Board or Audit Committee** (seeking their reply/observations) within **2 days** of his knowledge of the fraud, requesting a reply within **45 days**.
2. On receipt of the reply, the auditor shall forward his report, the reply, and his comments to the **Central Government (Secretary, Ministry of Corporate Affairs)** within **15 days** of receipt of such reply. If no reply is received within 45 days, he shall forward his report along with a note stating that no reply was received.
3. The report shall be sent in **Form ADT-4** by registered post/speed post followed by e-mail.

**Case 1 — fraud of ₹1.20 crore (≥ ₹1 crore).** Since the amount **exceeds ₹1 crore**, M/s Vidyut & Co. must report to the **Central Government** following the Form ADT-4 procedure above.

**Case 2 — fraud of ₹85 lakh (< ₹1 crore).** Where the fraud involves an amount **less than ₹1 crore**, the auditor shall report the matter to the **Audit Committee (or the Board where no Audit Committee exists)** within the prescribed time, specifying the nature of the fraud, the approximate amount, and the parties involved. Such frauds (below the threshold) are **not reported to the Central Government** but are **disclosed in the Board's Report** in the prescribed manner.

**Conclusion:** The ₹1.20 crore fraud must be reported to the **Central Government in Form ADT-4** (Rule 13 procedure); the ₹85 lakh fraud must be reported to the **Audit Committee/Board** and disclosed in the **Board's Report**, not to the Central Government.

*(Full-marks tip: the ₹1 crore dividing line and the Form ADT-4 + Board/Audit-Committee routing are the crux; students who report every fraud to the CG, or omit the 2-day/45-day/15-day timeline, lose marks.)*

### Q67. Ch: Audit & Auditors — Auditor not to render certain services (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** "The independence of the statutory auditor is protected not only by disqualifications but also by restrictions on the services he may render." 6. **Investment banking** services;
7. **Rendering of outsourced financial services**;

### Q69. Ch: LLP Act 2008 — Nature of LLP and its distinction from a Company and a Partnership Firm (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** "A Limited Liability Partnership is a hybrid — it borrows the body of a company and the soul of a partnership." **Answer:**
The statement is broadly **correct**. A Limited Liability Partnership (LLP) is a corporate form of business organisation that combines the flexibility and internal governance of a partnership with the limited liability and body-corporate features of a company.

Section 2(1)(n) of the Limited Liability Partnership Act, 2008 defines an LLP as a partnership formed and registered under this Act. Section 3 provides the essence of its nature and lays down that:
- An LLP is a **body corporate** formed and incorporated under the Act and is a **legal entity separate from its partners**;
- An LLP has **perpetual succession**; and
- Any change in the partners of an LLP shall **not affect the existence, rights or liabilities** of the LLP.

Because it is a separate legal person, the LLP itself owns property, sues and is sued in its own name, and the liability of its partners is limited (Section 27 and 28). At the same time, the mutual rights and duties of the partners are governed by an agreement (the LLP agreement), giving it the internal flexibility of a partnership. Hence the description of an LLP as a hybrid is apt.

The distinction is best presented as under:

| Basis | Partnership Firm | LLP | Company |
|---|---|---|---|
| (i) Governing law | Indian Partnership Act, 1932 | Limited Liability Partnership Act, 2008 | Companies Act, 2013 |
| (ii) Separate legal entity | Not a separate legal entity; firm and partners are one | Separate legal entity distinct from partners [Sec. 3] | Separate legal entity distinct from members |
| (iii) Perpetual succession | No; dissolves on death/insolvency of a partner (subject to agreement) | Has perpetual succession [Sec. 3] | Has perpetual succession |
| (iv) Liability | Unlimited and joint & several | Limited to agreed contribution; partner not liable for another partner's wrongful acts [Sec. 27, 28] | Limited to unpaid amount on shares / guarantee |
| (v) Minimum members | Two partners | Two partners [Sec. 6(1)] | Two (private) / seven (public) members |

**Conclusion:** An LLP possesses the body-corporate attributes of a company (separate legal entity, perpetual succession, limited liability) while retaining the flexible, agreement-based internal structure of a partnership; the statement is therefore correct.

### Q71. Ch: LLP Act 2008 — Designated Partners and Consequences of Default (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** "Orbit Logistics LLP" was incorporated with four partners: Mr. P, Mr. Q, Mrs. R (a resident of India) and XYZ Ltd. (a body corporate). The LLP agreement is silent as to designated partners. Later, both individuals nominated as designated partners resigned on the same day and the LLP continued to function for two months with no designated partner. Advise on (a) who must be designated partners and the requirement of residency, (b) whether a body corporate's nominee can be a designated partner, and (c) the consequence of the LLP carrying on without a designated partner, under the LLP Act, 2008.

**Answer:**
**(a) Requirement of designated partners [Section 7]:** Under Section 7(1) of the LLP Act, 2008, every LLP must have **at least two designated partners** who are **individuals**, and at least one of them must be a **resident in India**. Where the LLP agreement provides for who is to be designated partner, those partners are so designated; here the agreement is silent, so under Section 7(1) proviso every partner who is an individual becomes a designated partner and thereafter every individual partner may become designated partner by agreement. "Resident in India" means a person who has stayed in India for a period of not less than **120 days** during the financial year (as substituted in the Act). Mrs. R, being resident, can satisfy the residency condition.

**(b) Body corporate's nominee [Section 7(2)–(3)]:** Where a body corporate (XYZ Ltd.) is a partner, it may **nominate an individual** to act as a designated partner; that nominee individual is treated as a designated partner. Thus, XYZ Ltd. cannot itself be a designated partner (a designated partner must be an individual), but its nominated individual can be. Every designated partner must obtain a **Designated Partner Identification Number (DPIN)** and give prior consent in the prescribed form and manner [Sec. 7(3), 7(4)].

**(c) Consequence of no designated partner [Section 7(4) / Section 9]:** Section 9 requires that an LLP shall ensure that it always has at least two designated partners meeting Section 7. If at any time the number of designated partners is reduced below two, or there is a vacancy, the LLP must **fill the vacancy within 30 days**. If no designated partner is appointed, or if at any time there is only one designated partner, **each partner shall be deemed to be a designated partner**. Further, an LLP contravening Section 7(1) is **punishable with fine of not less than ₹10,000 which may extend to ₹5,00,000** (LLP and each partner), as provided in Section 7(6). Carrying on for two months with no designated partner therefore constitutes a contravention attracting this penalty and deeming all partners as designated partners.

**Conclusion:** Orbit Logistics LLP must have two individual designated partners with at least one resident in India; XYZ Ltd. may nominate an individual; and by continuing for two months without any designated partner the LLP and its partners are in contravention of Section 7 and liable to the prescribed fine, with all partners deemed to be designated partners.

### Q72. Ch: LLP Act 2008 — Partner as Agent; Extent of Liability of LLP and Partner (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Mr. Sameer is a partner in "Delta Consulting LLP". While acting in the course of the LLP's business, he negligently gave wrong advice to a client, causing a loss of ₹8,00,000. Separately, Mr. Sameer, without any authority and beyond the scope of the LLP's business, borrowed ₹3,00,000 from a lender in the LLP's name; the lender knew Mr. Sameer had no authority. Examine (a) whether the LLP is liable in each case, (b) whether Mr. Sameer is personally liable, and (c) whether the other partners are personally liable, under Sections 26, 27 and 28 of the LLP Act, 2008.

**Answer:**
**Legal provisions:**
- **Section 26** — Every partner of an LLP is, for the purpose of the business of the LLP, the **agent of the LLP**, but **not of the other partners**.
- **Section 27(1)** — An LLP is **not bound** by anything done by a partner in dealing with a person if (a) the partner has **no authority** to act for the LLP in doing that act, **and** (b) the person **knows** that the partner has no authority or does not know or believe him to be a partner of the LLP.
- **Section 27(2)** — The LLP is **liable** if a partner is liable to any person as a result of a **wrongful act or omission** on his part in the **course of the business** of the LLP or with its authority.
- **Section 27(3)** — The liability of the LLP shall be met out of the **property of the LLP**.
- **Section 28(1)** — A partner is **not personally liable**, directly or indirectly, for an obligation of the LLP solely by reason of being a partner.
- **Section 28(2)** — However, this does not affect the personal liability of a partner for his **own wrongful act or omission**, but a partner is **not personally liable for the wrongful act or omission of any other partner**.

**Application:**

*Case 1 (negligent advice — ₹8,00,000):* Mr. Sameer acted in the course of the LLP's business. Under Section 27(2), the LLP is **bound and liable** for the ₹8,00,000 loss caused by his wrongful/negligent act in the course of business, met out of LLP property [Sec. 27(3)]. Under Section 28(2), Mr. Sameer remains **personally liable for his own wrongful act**. The **other partners are not personally liable** for Mr. Sameer's wrongful act [Sec. 28(1) and 28(2)].

*Case 2 (unauthorised borrowing of ₹3,00,000):* Here Mr. Sameer had **no authority** and the lender **knew** he had no authority. Both conditions of Section 27(1) are satisfied, so the **LLP is not bound** by the borrowing. As the act was outside the scope and unauthorised, Mr. Sameer is **personally liable** to the lender; the LLP and the other partners are not liable.

| Case | LLP liable? | Mr. Sameer personally liable? | Other partners liable? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Negligent advice (course of business) | Yes — Sec. 27(2)/(3) | Yes — Sec. 28(2) (own wrongful act) | No — Sec. 28(1)/(2) |
| Unauthorised borrowing, lender aware | No — Sec. 27(1) | Yes (personally to lender) | No |

**Conclusion:** For the negligent advice the LLP is liable (out of its property) and Mr. Sameer is also personally liable for his own act, but the other partners are not; for the unauthorised borrowing known to the lender the LLP is not bound and only Mr. Sameer is personally liable.

*(Full-marks tip: distinguish clearly between Section 27(1) (LLP not bound — want of authority + knowledge) and Section 27(2) (LLP liable — wrongful act in course of business); the shield for co-partners under Section 28(2) must be expressly stated.)*

### Q73. Ch: LLP Act 2008 — Cessation of Partnership Interest and Continuing Liability (Marks: 5) [Application]

### Q77. Ch: General Clauses Act 1897 — Definitions applied (Sections 3 & 4) (Marks: 5) [Application]

### Q78. Ch: General Clauses Act 1897 — Commencement and Coming into Operation of Enactments (Sections 5 & 5A) (Marks: 4) [Application]
**Question:** A Central Act received the assent of the President on 15th March 2026, but the Act itself was silent as to the date on which it would come into force. (i) From what date does the Act come into operation? (ii) If instead the Act had provided that it "shall come into force on such date as the Central Government may, by notification, appoint", and no notification was issued, what is the position? Answer with reference to Section 5 of the General Clauses Act, 1897.

**Answer:**
**Legal provision [Section 5]:** Section 5 of the General Clauses Act, 1897 provides that where any Central Act is **not expressed to come into operation on a particular day**, then it shall come into operation on **the day on which it receives the assent** — in the case of a Central Act made after the commencement of the Constitution, on the day it receives the **assent of the President**. Section 5(3) clarifies that unless the contrary is expressed, a Central Act or Regulation shall be construed as coming into operation **immediately on the expiration of the day preceding its commencement**.

**Application:**
**(i)** As the Act is silent on its date of commencement, by virtue of Section 5 it comes into operation on the date it received the **assent of the President, i.e., 15th March 2026** (construed as commencing immediately on the expiration of 14th March 2026).

**(ii)** Where the Act expressly provides that it shall come into force on a date to be **appointed by the Central Government by notification**, the general rule in Section 5 is displaced by that contrary expression. In such a case the Act (or the relevant provision) does **not come into force until the appointed date is notified**; until the Government issues the notification, the Act remains **not in operation**. The Government may also appoint different dates for different provisions.

**Conclusion:** (i) Being silent, the Act commences on 15th March 2026, the date of the President's assent (Section 5); (ii) where commencement is left to Government notification, the Act does not come into force until such notification is issued.

### Q80. Ch: General Clauses Act 1897 — Computation of Time (Sections 9 & 10) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Apply Sections 9 and 10 of the General Clauses Act, 1897 to the following independent situations:
(i) A statute allows a party to file an appeal "within 30 days from the date of the order". The order was passed on 1st April 2026. Compute the last date, explaining whether the first day is included.
(ii) A notice period is stated to run "from" 5th June "to" 20th June. Are these dates included?
(iii) A return is required to be filed within a prescribed period and the last day of that period, 26th January 2026, is a public holiday (Republic Day). Can the return be filed on 27th January 2026?

**Answer:**
**Legal provisions:**
- **Section 9 (commencement and termination of time):** In any Central Act, where a period is expressed to begin **"from"** a specified day, that first day is to be **excluded**; and where a period is expressed to be **"to"** a specified day, that last day is to be **included**. In short, "from" excludes the first day, "to" includes the last day.
- **Section 10 (computation of time — act to be done in a court/office):** Where any act or proceeding is directed or allowed to be done within a prescribed period, and the **last day** of the period falls on a day when the court or office is **closed** (a holiday), the act shall be considered as done **in due time if it is done on the next day on which the court/office is open**. (This does not apply to the Limitation Act situations to the extent otherwise provided.)

**Application:**
**(i)** The appeal is to be filed "within 30 days **from** the date of the order". Under Section 9, the word "from" excludes the first day; hence **1st April 2026 is excluded** and counting begins from 2nd April 2026. The 30-day period expires on **1st May 2026** (2nd April to 1st May = 30 days). If 1st May 2026 were a holiday, Section 10 would extend it to the next working day.

**(ii)** A period "**from** 5th June **to** 20th June" — by Section 9, the day "from" (5th June) is **excluded** and the day "to" (20th June) is **included**. Thus the period runs from 6th June up to and including 20th June.

**(iii)** The last day for filing is 26th January 2026, which is a public holiday (Republic Day). Under Section 10, where the last day on which the act is to be done is one on which the office is closed, the act done on the **next day the office is open** is deemed to be in due time. Hence the return **may validly be filed on 27th January 2026** (assuming it is a working day).

**Conclusion:** (i) the last date is 1st May 2026 (first day excluded under Section 9); (ii) 5th June is excluded and 20th June included; and (iii) the return filed on 27th January 2026 is in time under Section 10.

### Q82. Ch: General Clauses Act 1897 — Gender, Number, and "may"/"shall" (Sections 13 & the enabling principle) (Marks: 4) [Application]

### Q85. Ch: Interpretation of Statutes — Literal / Grammatical Rule (Marks: 4) [Application]
**Question:** A taxing statute provides that "every dealer whose turnover exceeds ₹40 lakh in a financial year shall get himself registered." Mr. X, a dealer, had a turnover of exactly ₹40 lakh. The Department sought to register him compulsorily, arguing that the intention of the legislature was to bring all substantial dealers within the net. Applying the appropriate rule of interpretation, decide whether Mr. X is liable to be registered.

**Answer:**
The primary rule of interpretation is the **Literal or Grammatical Rule**, which requires that the words of a statute be given their ordinary, natural and grammatical meaning, and where the language is plain and unambiguous, effect must be given to it irrespective of the consequences, because the words of the statute best declare the intention of the law-maker.

Under this rule, nothing is to be added to or taken away from a statute unless there are adequate grounds to justify the inference that the legislature intended something which it omitted to express. It is a cardinal principle that a taxing statute is to be construed strictly; if the subject falls plainly within the letter of the law he is taxed, and if he does not fall within the letter, he is not taxed, however great the equity or apparent intention may be. There is no room for any intendment or presumption in a fiscal statute.

Here the statute uses the word **"exceeds ₹40 lakh."** The word "exceeds" in its natural and grammatical sense means "goes beyond" or "is more than," and does not include the figure itself. A turnover of exactly ₹40 lakh does not "exceed" ₹40 lakh; it merely equals it. The Department cannot press the supposed "intention" to bring substantial dealers within the net when the plain words do not cover Mr. X, because where the words are clear the intention must be gathered from the words alone.

| Basis | Department's stand | Correct position under Literal Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning of "exceeds" | Includes ₹40 lakh | Means strictly more than ₹40 lakh |
| Role of intention | Overrides plain words | Cannot override clear language |
| Taxing statute construction | Purposive | Strict / literal |

**Conclusion:** Since Mr. X's turnover of ₹40 lakh does not "exceed" ₹40 lakh, on a literal construction he is **not liable** to be compulsorily registered.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards naming the rule, stating the "plain words prevail / strict construction of taxing statutes" principle, and applying the precise meaning of "exceeds." Marks are lost for jumping to legislative intent when the words are unambiguous.)*

### Q86. Ch: Interpretation of Statutes — Golden Rule (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** A Rent Control Act provides that "on the death of the tenant, his heir shall be entitled to continue the tenancy." A tenant, in order to defeat the landlord, executed a registered gift of the tenancy in favour of his son during his own lifetime and then continued to reside. The son claimed statutory protection as "heir." A literal reading of "heir" would arguably cover the son. Explain the Golden Rule of interpretation and decide how the provision should be construed.

**Answer:**
The **Golden Rule** of interpretation is a modification of the literal rule. It provides that the words of a statute must ordinarily be given their plain grammatical meaning, but **where such a literal construction leads to an absurdity, inconsistency, hardship or a result manifestly repugnant to the object of the statute, the court may depart from the ordinary meaning to such extent as is necessary to remove the absurdity and give effect to the true intention** of the legislature. It is applied in two ways — in a narrower sense to avoid an absurd result flowing from a single word, and in a wider sense to prevent a result contrary to public policy or the manifest object of the Act.

The classic statement of the rule is that the grammatical and ordinary sense of the words is to be adhered to **unless** it would lead to some absurdity or inconsistency with the rest of the instrument, in which case the ordinary sense may be modified so as to avoid that absurdity, but no further.

Here, the object of the Rent Control Act is to protect a **genuine heir** who succeeds to the tenancy **on the death of the tenant**, so that the family is not rendered shelterless. The provision speaks of devolution "on the death of the tenant." If the word "heir" were read literally to cover a person to whom the tenant himself transferred the tenancy during his lifetime by gift, it would enable the tenant to defeat the landlord's reversionary right and would produce a result absurd and repugnant to the object of the statute, since protection is meant to arise on death and by succession, not on an inter vivos transfer.

**Conclusion:** Applying the Golden Rule, the word "heir" must be read down to mean a person on whom the tenancy devolves **by succession on the death** of the tenant, and not a donee under a lifetime gift. The son's claim as "heir" therefore fails.

*(Full-marks tip: State that the Golden Rule is invoked only to cure absurdity/repugnancy, quote the "unless it leads to absurdity" formulation, and tie the modification to the Act's protective object. Do not use the Golden Rule where the literal meaning causes no absurdity.)*

### Q87. Ch: Interpretation of Statutes — Mischief Rule / Heydon's Case (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the "Mischief Rule" (Rule in Heydon's Case) of interpretation of statutes. State the four matters the court must consider while applying this rule, and mention its utility and limitation.

**Answer:**
The **Mischief Rule**, also called the **Rule in Heydon's Case (1584)** or the rule of **purposive construction**, is a rule for the suppression of the mischief and advancement of the remedy. It directs that where a statute has been enacted to remedy a defect (the "mischief") in the earlier law, the court should adopt that construction which suppresses the mischief and advances the remedy, rather than one which would allow the mischief to continue.

For the true interpretation of a statute, the rule requires the court to consider and discern **four matters**:

| No. | Matter to be considered |
|---|---|
| 1 | **What was the common law (the law) before the making of the Act** |
| 2 | **What was the mischief and defect** for which the earlier law did not provide |
| 3 | **What remedy** the Parliament has resolved and appointed to cure the mischief |
| 4 | **The true reason of the remedy** |

Having ascertained these, the duty of the court is to make such construction as shall **suppress the mischief and advance the remedy**, suppressing subtle inventions and evasions for continuance of the mischief and adding force and life to the cure and remedy according to the true intent of the makers of the Act.

**Utility:** The rule is especially useful in construing remedial and beneficial legislation (such as welfare, labour and consumer-protection statutes), because it allows the court to give a construction that furthers the legislative purpose and prevents clever evasion of the law.

**Limitation:** The rule cannot be used to override the plain and unambiguous language of a statute; where the words are clear, the court cannot rewrite the provision under the guise of suppressing a supposed mischief. It also gives considerable latitude to the judge and must be applied cautiously so as not to substitute judicial policy for the enacted words.

**Conclusion:** The Mischief Rule enables the court, by examining the four Heydon factors, to interpret a provision so as to suppress the mischief and advance the remedy intended by the legislature.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner specifically looks for all four Heydon's Case matters listed and the "suppress the mischief, advance the remedy" phrase, plus at least one line each on utility and limitation.)*

### Q88. Ch: Interpretation of Statutes — Rule of Harmonious Construction (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** A statute contains two sections. Section 15 confers on a co-operative society an absolute right to recover its dues by attachment and sale of the member's property. Section 40 provides that "notwithstanding anything in this Act, the residential house of a member shall not be attached or sold in recovery of any society dues." In a recovery proceeding the society relied on Section 15 and the member relied on Section 40, each contending that the other section should be ignored. Applying the appropriate rule, resolve the conflict.

**Answer:**
The applicable rule is the **Rule of Harmonious Construction**. It provides that where there are, in the same statute, two or more provisions which appear to be in conflict with each other, they should be so interpreted that **effect is given to both**, and a construction which reduces one of them to a dead letter or renders it otiose is not permissible. The legislature is presumed not to contradict itself, and the courts must avoid a head-on clash between two provisions of the same Act and construe them so as to harmonise them.

The principles governing harmonious construction are: (i) the courts must try to reconcile the provisions and give effect to both; (ii) a provision of one section cannot be used to defeat another unless the court, despite all efforts, is unable to reconcile their differences; (iii) when it is impossible to reconcile them completely, the courts must interpret the words in such a manner as to give effect, as far as possible, to both; and (iv) the interpretation which reduces one provision to a useless lumber or dead letter is not harmonious.

Applying these principles, Section 15 confers a **general** power of recovery over the member's property, while Section 40, using a **non-obstante clause** ("notwithstanding anything in this Act"), carves out a **specific exception** for the residential house. Both can be given full effect by reading Section 15 as the general power and Section 40 as an exception to it: the society may recover its dues by attachment and sale of the member's property under Section 15, **except** the residential house which is protected under Section 40. This construction gives effect to both provisions and does not render either otiose; it also respects the settled principle that a specific provision with a non-obstante clause prevails over a general provision to the extent of the exception.

**Conclusion:** The two sections are harmonised — the society may proceed against the member's property generally under Section 15, but the **residential house cannot be attached or sold**, being expressly protected by Section 40.

*(Full-marks tip: Award-scoring answers reconcile both sections instead of preferring one, note the non-obstante clause creating a specific exception, and state the "no provision should be a dead letter" principle.)*

### Q89. Ch: Interpretation of Statutes — Ejusdem Generis (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** A licensing statute requires a licence for keeping "any horse, cattle, sheep, goat or other animal." Mr. A kept a pet dog and a domestic cat without a licence and was prosecuted. He contended that the words "or other animal" must be read ejusdem generis and could not include his dog and cat. Explain the rule of ejusdem generis, state the conditions for its application, and decide.

**Answer:**
The rule of **ejusdem generis** (meaning "of the same kind or nature") is a rule of construction of general words following an enumeration of specific words. It provides that where **specific words forming a distinct genus (class) are followed by general words**, the general words are presumed to be restricted to things of the **same kind or class** as the specific words that precede them, unless a contrary intention appears. The rule rests on the presumption that the legislature, by using specific words of a class, intended the general words to be confined to that class.

The rule applies only where the following **conditions** are satisfied:

| No. | Condition for application of ejusdem generis |
|---|---|
| 1 | The statute contains an enumeration of **specific words** |
| 2 | The specific words constitute a **class or category (genus)** |
| 3 | That class or category is **not exhausted** by the enumeration |
| 4 | The **general words follow** the specific (enumerated) words |
| 5 | There is **no indication of a different legislative intention** |

Applying these conditions, the specific words "horse, cattle, sheep, goat" form a distinct genus of **domesticated animals kept for agricultural / farm or economic purposes (livestock)**. The general words "or other animal" following this enumeration must, on the ejusdem generis principle, be confined to animals of the **same class of livestock**, and not extended to every animal in existence. A **dog and a cat**, being ordinary domestic pets and not of the genus of livestock, do not fall within "other animal" as so restricted.

**Conclusion:** On the application of the rule of ejusdem generis, the words "or other animal" are limited to livestock of the same class as horse, cattle, sheep and goat. Mr. A's dog and cat are not covered, and **no licence was required** for them; the prosecution fails.

*(Full-marks tip: List all conditions, especially that the specific words must form a genus and the general words must follow them. State clearly why pets fall outside the livestock genus. The rule does not apply if the specific words exhaust the class or a contrary intention is shown.)*

### Q90. Ch: Interpretation of Statutes — Internal Aids to Construction (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** In the interpretation of a statute, the court may take the help of "internal aids." Explain the following internal aids and their value in construction: (i) Long Title, (ii) Preamble, (iii) Marginal Notes, (iv) Proviso, and (v) Explanation.

**Answer:**
**Internal aids** are those aids to construction which are found **within the statute itself**. They are legitimately used to ascertain the meaning of a provision where its language is ambiguous. The important internal aids are explained below.

**(i) Long Title:** The long title of an Act sets out in general terms the purpose of the Act and is a part of the Act. Though it cannot control the plain meaning of the enacting words, it is a legitimate aid to ascertain the **general scope and object** of the statute where the enacting words are ambiguous.

**(ii) Preamble:** The preamble expresses the **scope, object and purpose** of the Act more comprehensively than the long title. It is a key to open the mind of the makers of the Act. Where the language of the enacting part is plain, the preamble cannot cut it down or extend it; but where the words are ambiguous, the preamble may be resorted to as an aid to explain them. It shows the mischief the Act intends to remedy.

**(iii) Marginal Notes:** Marginal notes (side notes) are the notes printed at the side of a section indicating its subject. As a general rule marginal notes are **not part of the statute** and cannot be used to control the meaning of the section, since they are often inserted by drafters and not voted upon by the legislature. However, in exceptional cases, where the section is ambiguous, they may be referred to as furnishing some indication of the drift of the section.

**(iv) Proviso:** A proviso is added to a section to **qualify or create an exception** to the main enactment. Its normal function is to except (carve out) something out of the enacting clause which, but for the proviso, would fall within it. A proviso must be construed in relation to the main provision to which it is attached and does not, as a rule, travel beyond it to enlarge the enacting section.

**(v) Explanation:** An Explanation is appended to a section to **explain, clarify or elucidate** its meaning, to remove doubt or to include or exclude something by a legal fiction. It becomes an integral part of the section; it does not ordinarily enlarge or curtail the scope of the section but explains what is already contained in it.

| Aid | Principal function |
|---|---|
| Long Title | Indicates general purpose/scope |
| Preamble | Key to object; used where words ambiguous |
| Marginal Notes | Generally not part of Act; minor indicator |
| Proviso | Creates exception/qualifies main provision |
| Explanation | Clarifies/removes doubt; integral to section |

**Conclusion:** These internal aids, drawn from the statute itself, assist the court in resolving ambiguity, but none of them can override the clear and unambiguous language of the enacting provision.

*(Full-marks tip: Give the exact function of each aid and, crucially, state the limitation — that none can override plain enacting words. Distinguish a proviso (exception) from an explanation (clarification); this contrast is frequently tested.)*

### Q91. Ch: Interpretation of Statutes — External Aids to Construction (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** "Where the internal aids are not sufficient, the court may take recourse to external aids to construction." Explain the meaning of external aids and briefly describe any four external aids to the interpretation of a statute.

**Answer:**
**External aids** are those aids to construction which are **not found within the statute itself** but are drawn from **outside sources**. They are pressed into service when the words of a statute are ambiguous and the internal aids do not resolve the ambiguity. The court may then legitimately look to external material to ascertain the true meaning and intention of the legislature. The important external aids are described below.

**(i) Historical Setting and Statement of Objects and Reasons:** The surrounding circumstances and the historical background in which a statute was enacted, and the **Statement of Objects and Reasons** accompanying a Bill, may be looked into to understand the **background, the antecedent state of affairs and the object** which the legislature sought to achieve, though the Statement of Objects and Reasons cannot control the actual words used.

**(ii) Dictionaries:** When a word is not defined in the Act, its **ordinary dictionary meaning** may be referred to in order to find the natural and popular sense of the word. However, the dictionary meaning must be tested against the **context** in which the word is used, since the same word may bear different meanings in different settings.

**(iii) Parliamentary History / Legislative Debates:** The history of the legislation, including reports of committees, and to a limited extent the debates in the legislature, may be used to understand the object and mischief of the Act, though courts use debates with caution as an aid to interpretation.

**(iv) Reference to other Statutes (Statutes in pari materia):** Statutes dealing with the **same subject matter (in pari materia)** may be read together, and an earlier statute or its judicial interpretation may throw light on the meaning of a later statute on the same subject.

**(Other recognised external aids include judicial decisions, foreign decisions, textbooks and contemporary official interpretation.)**

**Conclusion:** External aids — such as historical background, the Statement of Objects and Reasons, dictionaries, legislative history and statutes in pari materia — legitimately assist the court in resolving ambiguity, but like internal aids they cannot displace the clear language of the statute.

*(Full-marks tip: Define external aids as sources outside the statute, then describe any four with their limitation. Naming the "Statement of Objects and Reasons" and "statutes in pari materia" specifically earns marks; note that a dictionary meaning must yield to context.)*

### Q92. Ch: Interpretation of Statutes — Noscitur a Sociis & Contemporanea Expositio (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** (a) A statute exempts from tax "sale of books, pamphlets, periodicals and journals." The Department sought to tax the sale of loose "diaries and calendars," treating them as falling within the entry. (b) A revenue authority had, for over thirty years since the enactment, consistently interpreted a particular exemption entry in a certain manner. Explain the maxims **noscitur a sociis** and **contemporanea expositio** and apply them to (a) and (b) respectively.

**Answer:**
**(a) Noscitur a sociis:** The maxim *noscitur a sociis* means "a word is known by the company it keeps." When two or more words which are susceptible of analogous meaning are **coupled together** in a statute, they take colour from each other, and the meaning of a general or doubtful word may be restricted to a sense analogous to the **less general or associated words**. It is applied where the associated words form a common category, so that a word of wider import is confined to the class indicated by its companions.

Here the words "books, pamphlets, periodicals and journals" keep the company of **reading/printed literary material meant for reading**. Applying *noscitur a sociis*, any doubtful item in the entry must take colour from this class. **Diaries and calendars**, which are not literary reading material but articles of utility, do not share the character of the associated words and therefore do not fall within the exemption entry. (Consequently, the Department's inclusion of them within the *reading-material* entry is not sustainable on the language of that entry; they must be dealt with under the appropriate general entry.)

**(b) Contemporanea expositio:** The maxim *contemporanea expositio est optima et fortissima in lege* means that the **best way to construe a document/statute is to read it as it would have been read when made** — i.e., a contemporaneous and consistent exposition of a statute by those who have to administer or act upon it, continued over a long period, is a strong and admissible aid to its construction, particularly for old statutes. Great weight is given to a construction which the administrative authorities and those affected have uniformly placed upon a provision over a long time.

Here, since the revenue authority had **consistently interpreted the exemption entry in one manner for over thirty years** since enactment, that long and uniform contemporaneous exposition is a strong aid, and the settled interpretation should not lightly be disturbed unless it is manifestly erroneous.

**Conclusion:** (a) On *noscitur a sociis*, diaries and calendars do not fall within the entry for books, pamphlets, periodicals and journals. (b) On *contemporanea expositio*, the long-standing, consistent administrative interpretation of the exemption entry should be given great weight and upheld.

*(Full-marks tip: Distinguish noscitur a sociis (word coloured by neighbouring words) from ejusdem generis (general words after a genus of specific words). For contemporanea expositio stress "long and consistent" usage, especially for older statutes.)*

### Q94. Ch: FEMA, 1999 — "Person Resident in India" [Sec 2(v)] (Marks: 6) [Application]

### Q95. Ch: FEMA, 1999 — Capital Account Transactions [Sec 6] (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the regulatory framework for "capital account transactions" under Section 6 of the Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999. State the classes of capital account transactions that the Reserve Bank may specify and the restriction on its power.

**Answer:**
Under **Section 6(1) of FEMA, 1999**, subject to the provisions of sub-section (2), any person may **sell or draw foreign exchange to or from an authorised person for a capital account transaction**. (a) any **class or classes of capital account transactions**, involving debt instruments, which are permissible; (b) the **limit up to which foreign exchange** shall be admissible for such transactions; and (c) any conditions which may be placed on such transactions. **Section 6(2A)** empowers the **Central Government**, in consultation with RBI, to prescribe the classes of capital account transactions **not involving debt instruments**, the limit up to which foreign exchange is admissible, and the conditions applicable.

The scheme of FEMA channels all dealings in foreign exchange through **"authorised persons"** so that transactions may be monitored and regulated by the Reserve Bank.
