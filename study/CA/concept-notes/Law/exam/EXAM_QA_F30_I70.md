## Part A — Foundation Level (Q1–Q30)

### Q1. Ch: The Indian Contract Act, 1872 — Essentials of a Valid Contract (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** "All contracts are agreements, but all agreements are not contracts." Explain this statement with reference to the essential elements of a valid contract under the Indian Contract Act, 1872.

**Answer:**
Under Section 2(h) of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, a **contract** is defined as "an agreement enforceable by law." An **agreement** is defined under Section 2(e) as "every promise and every set of promises, forming the consideration for each other." A promise, in turn [Section 2(b)], arises when a proposal is accepted. Therefore, the relationship may be expressed by the equation: **Contract = Agreement + Enforceability by Law.**

It follows that every contract is necessarily an agreement, because an agreement is the very foundation of a contract. However, every agreement is not a contract, because an agreement becomes a contract only when it satisfies the conditions of enforceability laid down in **Section 10**, which provides that "all agreements are contracts if they are made by the free consent of parties competent to contract, for a lawful consideration and with a lawful object, and are not hereby expressly declared to be void."

The essential elements of a valid contract are as follows:

| Element | Statutory Basis | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Offer and acceptance | Sec. 2(a), 2(b) | There must be a lawful offer and a lawful acceptance giving rise to an agreement. |
| Intention to create legal relationship | Judicial principle | Parties must intend legal consequences; social or domestic agreements are generally not contracts. |
| Lawful consideration | Sec. 2(d), Sec. 23 | Something in return, which must be real and lawful. |
| Capacity of parties | Sec. 11 | Parties must be of the age of majority, of sound mind and not disqualified by law. |
| Free consent | Sec. 13, 14 | Consent must not be caused by coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation or mistake. |
| Lawful object | Sec. 23 | The object must not be illegal, immoral or opposed to public policy. |
| Not expressly declared void | Sec. 24–30 | The agreement must not fall in the category of void agreements. |
| Certainty and possibility of performance | Sec. 29, Sec. 56 | Terms must be certain and the act must be capable of performance. |

Thus, an agreement that lacks any of these essentials remains a mere agreement and is not enforceable as a contract.

**Conclusion:** Since a contract is an agreement plus enforceability, all contracts are agreements, but only those agreements which satisfy Section 10 mature into contracts. Hence the statement is legally correct.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the Contract = Agreement + Enforceability equation with Sections 2(h) and 10 cited; do not merely list essentials — link them back to enforceability.)*

### Q2. Ch: The Indian Contract Act, 1872 — Offer/Proposal (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Define an "offer" (proposal) under the Indian Contract Act, 1872. State the essential legal rules relating to a valid offer.

**Answer:**
According to **Section 2(a)** of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, "when one person signifies to another his willingness to do or to abstain from doing anything, with a view to obtaining the assent of that other to such act or abstinence, he is said to make a proposal." The person making the proposal is the "promisor" or **offeror**, and the person to whom it is made is the **offeree** [Section 2(c)].

The essential legal rules of a valid offer are as follows:

1. **Offer must be capable of creating legal relations.** The offer must intend to give rise to legal consequences and not merely a social or moral obligation. A social invitation, even if accepted, does not create a contract.

2. **Offer must be certain, definite and unambiguous.** The terms of the offer must not be loose or vague. As held in *Taylor v. Portington*, an offer to take a house on lease "if put into thorough repair" is too vague to be enforced.

3. **Offer must be distinguished from an invitation to offer.** A price list, catalogue, or display of goods in a shop is merely an invitation to offer, inviting the public to make offers, and is not itself an offer.

4. **Offer may be specific or general.** A specific offer is made to a definite person and can be accepted only by that person; a general offer is made to the world at large and can be accepted by anyone fulfilling its terms (*Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co.*).

5. **Offer must be communicated.** An offer is complete only when it is communicated to the offeree [Section 4]. A person cannot accept an offer of which he has no knowledge (*Lalman Shukla v. Gauri Dutt*).

6. **Offer must not contain a term the non-compliance of which amounts to acceptance.** The offeror cannot say that silence of the offeree will be treated as acceptance.

7. **Offer may be conditional.** An offer can be made subject to conditions, but such conditions must be clearly communicated.

**Conclusion:** An offer that satisfies the above rules is a valid offer which, on acceptance, gives rise to a binding agreement.

*(Full-marks tip: Cite Section 2(a) verbatim and support the "invitation to offer" and "general offer" points with the leading cases; skipping communication (Lalman Shukla) is a common deduction.)*

### Q3. Ch: The Indian Contract Act, 1872 — Communication of Offer and Acceptance (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** Examine the following situations with reference to the rules regarding communication of offer, acceptance and revocation under Sections 4 and 5 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872:

| No. | Situation |
|---|---|
| (i) | A proposes, by a letter sent by post, to sell his house to B. The letter is posted on 1st April and reaches B on 3rd April. |
| (ii) | B accepts A's proposal by a letter sent by post on 4th April, which reaches A on 6th April. |
| (iii) | A revokes his proposal by telegram, dispatched on 3rd April and received by B on 4th April (before B posts the acceptance). |

Determine on what dates the communications are complete and whether a valid contract results.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Rule for communication of an offer [Section 4, para 1]:** The communication of a proposal is complete when it comes to the knowledge of the person to whom it is made.

**WN-2 — Rule for communication of acceptance [Section 4, para 2]:** The communication of an acceptance is complete —
(a) as against the proposer, when it is put in a course of transmission to him, so as to be out of the power of the acceptor; and
(b) as against the acceptor, when it comes to the knowledge of the proposer.

**WN-3 — Rule for revocation [Section 5]:** A proposal may be revoked at any time before the communication of its acceptance is complete as against the proposer, but not afterwards.

**Application:**

| Situation | Rule applied | Date complete / Effect |
|---|---|---|
| (i) Communication of offer | Sec. 4, para 1 | Complete on **3rd April**, when the letter reaches B. |
| (ii) Communication of acceptance | Sec. 4, para 2 | Complete against A (proposer) on **4th April** (posting); complete against B (acceptor) on **6th April** (receipt). |
| (iii) Revocation of proposal | Sec. 5 | A may revoke only before 4th April (the date acceptance is complete against A). The revocation reaches B on 4th April, i.e. before B posts the acceptance, so it is **effective**. |

**Answer:** Since A's revocation reached B on 4th April, before B put the acceptance into a course of transmission, the proposal stood validly revoked. Consequently, **no valid contract results** between A and B.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the two-limb test of Section 4 for acceptance and the exact cut-off in Section 5; students lose marks by treating acceptance as complete on receipt against both parties.)*

### Q4. Ch: The Indian Contract Act, 1872 — Consideration (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Define "consideration" under the Indian Contract Act, 1872. Explain the essential elements of a valid consideration.

**Answer:**
According to **Section 2(d)** of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, "when, at the desire of the promisor, the promisee or any other person has done or abstained from doing, or does or abstains from doing, or promises to do or to abstain from doing, something, such act or abstinence or promise is called a consideration for the promise." In simple terms, consideration is "something in return" (*quid pro quo*) for the promise.

The essential elements of a valid consideration are as follows:

1. **Consideration must move at the desire of the promisor.** An act done at the desire of a third party, or voluntarily, is not consideration (*Durga Prasad v. Baldeo*).

2. **Consideration may move from the promisee or any other person.** Under Indian law a stranger to consideration can sue, so long as he is a party to the contract (*Chinnaya v. Ramayya*).

3. **Consideration may be past, present or future.** The words "has done," "does," and "promises to do" in Section 2(d) recognise past, present (executed) and future (executory) consideration respectively.

4. **Consideration need not be adequate.** Section 25, Explanation 2, provides that an agreement is not void merely because the consideration is inadequate; adequacy is for the parties to decide. However, inadequacy may be evidence that the consent was not freely given.

5. **Consideration must be real and not illusory.** It must be of some value in the eyes of law; physically or legally impossible acts are not real consideration.

6. **Consideration must be lawful.** Under Section 23, consideration must not be forbidden by law, fraudulent, immoral or opposed to public policy.

7. **Consideration must be something over and above existing obligations.** A promise to do what one is already bound to do is no consideration.

**Conclusion:** A promise supported by consideration satisfying the above requirements is enforceable, for the general rule under Section 25 is that "an agreement made without consideration is void."

*(Full-marks tip: Quote Section 2(d) and pair "moves at the desire of the promisor" with Durga Prasad and "stranger to consideration" with Chinnaya v. Ramayya; adequacy vs reality is a frequently tested distinction.)*

### Q5. Ch: The Indian Contract Act, 1872 — "No Consideration, No Contract" and Exceptions (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** "An agreement made without consideration is void." State the exceptions to this rule as provided under Section 25 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872.

**Answer:**
The general rule under **Section 25** of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 is that "an agreement made without consideration is void." This is expressed in the maxim *"ex nudo pacto non oritur actio"* — a bare promise creates no cause of action. However, the section and the Act itself provide certain well-recognised exceptions where an agreement is valid even without consideration:

1. **Natural love and affection [Section 25(1)].** An agreement made without consideration is valid if it is (a) expressed in writing, (b) registered under the law for the time being in force, (c) made on account of natural love and affection, and (d) between parties standing in a near relation to each other. All four conditions must be satisfied.

2. **Compensation for past voluntary services [Section 25(2)].** A promise to compensate, wholly or in part, a person who has already voluntarily done something for the promisor, or something which the promisor was legally compellable to do, is valid even without fresh consideration.

3. **Promise to pay a time-barred debt [Section 25(3)].** A written and signed promise to pay a debt barred by the law of limitation is enforceable, provided the promise is made by the debtor or his duly authorised agent.

4. **Completed gift [Explanation 1 to Section 25].** The rule of "no consideration, no contract" does not affect the validity of any gift actually made between the donor and the donee.

5. **Agency [Section 185].** No consideration is necessary to create an agency.

6. **Contract of guarantee [Section 127].** Anything done, or any promise made, for the benefit of the principal debtor is sufficient consideration to the surety for giving the guarantee; consideration need not directly benefit the surety.

7. **Remission by the promisee [Section 63].** A promisee may dispense with or remit performance, wholly or in part, without consideration.

**Conclusion:** Although consideration is generally essential to the validity of a contract, the agreements falling within the above exceptions are enforceable despite the absence of consideration.

*(Full-marks tip: State the four cumulative conditions under Section 25(1) precisely — writing, registration, near relation and love/affection — and remember to add guarantee, agency and remission as statutory exceptions outside Section 25.)*

### Q6. Ch: The Indian Contract Act, 1872 — Capacity to Contract / Minor's Agreement (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** Discuss the legal position of an agreement entered into by a minor under the Indian Contract Act, 1872. Examine the following case:

| Facts |
|---|
| M, a minor aged 17 years, borrowed ₹50,000 from L by executing a promissory note and mortgaging his house. On attaining majority, L sued M for recovery of the loan and enforcement of the mortgage. |

State whether L can recover the amount.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Competence to contract [Section 11]:** Every person is competent to contract who is (a) of the age of majority according to the law to which he is subject, (b) of sound mind, and (c) not disqualified from contracting by any law. A minor (a person below 18 years under the Indian Majority Act, 1875) is therefore **not competent** to contract.

**WN-2 — Effect of a minor's agreement:** As held in the landmark case *Mohori Bibee v. Dharmodas Ghose* (1903), an agreement by a minor is **void ab initio** (void from the very beginning) and not merely voidable. A minor's agreement is a nullity in the eyes of law.

**WN-3 — Consequences flowing from the rule:**
(a) No estoppel against a minor — even if the minor misrepresents his age, he cannot be estopped from pleading minority.
(b) No liability in contract; the money advanced to a minor cannot be recovered because the contract itself is void.
(c) A minor's agreement cannot be ratified on attaining majority, as there was no valid contract to ratify.

**Application to facts:** M was 17 years old at the time of borrowing, i.e. a minor. The promissory note and the mortgage executed by him constitute an agreement which is void ab initio under Section 11 read with *Mohori Bibee v. Dharmodas Ghose*. Since the contract never came into existence in law, L cannot enforce the promissory note or the mortgage, and the agreement cannot be ratified by M after attaining majority.

**Answer:** L **cannot recover** ₹50,000 from M nor enforce the mortgage, as the minor's agreement is void ab initio.

*(Full-marks tip: Cite Section 11 and Mohori Bibee, and stress "void ab initio," "no estoppel" and "no ratification"; merely calling it voidable is a serious error.)*

### Q7. Ch: The Indian Contract Act, 1872 — Free Consent / Coercion vs Undue Influence (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Define "free consent" under the Indian Contract Act, 1872. Distinguish between "coercion" and "undue influence".

**Answer:**
Under **Section 13**, two or more persons are said to consent when they agree upon the same thing in the same sense (*consensus ad idem*). According to **Section 14**, consent is said to be **free** when it is not caused by (i) coercion (Sec. 15), (ii) undue influence (Sec. 16), (iii) fraud (Sec. 17), (iv) misrepresentation (Sec. 18), or (v) mistake (Sec. 20–22). Where consent is caused by any of the first four factors, the agreement is **voidable** at the option of the party whose consent was so caused [Section 19 / 19A].

**Coercion [Section 15]** means committing or threatening to commit any act forbidden by the Indian Penal Code, or the unlawful detaining or threatening to detain any property, to the prejudice of any person, with the intention of causing him to enter into an agreement.

**Undue influence [Section 16]** exists where the relations between the parties are such that one party is in a position to dominate the will of the other and uses that position to obtain an unfair advantage.

| Basis | Coercion (Sec. 15) | Undue Influence (Sec. 16) |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Involves physical force or threat | Involves moral or mental pressure |
| Relationship | No special relationship needed | Requires a relationship where one dominates the will of the other |
| Means | Act forbidden by IPC / detaining property | Domination of will to gain unfair advantage |
| Consent obtained by | Threat or physical compulsion | Influence arising from position/authority |
| Burden of proof | On the party alleging coercion | Shifts to the dominant party to prove absence of undue influence (Sec. 16(3)) |
| Consequence | Agreement is voidable (Sec. 19) | Agreement is voidable (Sec. 19A) |

**Conclusion:** Both coercion and undue influence vitiate free consent and render the agreement voidable at the option of the aggrieved party; the essential difference lies in coercion being physical and undue influence being moral in nature.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner expects the Section 14 list of five vitiating factors and a clean tabular distinction; the burden-of-proof row under Section 16(3) is the differentiator most students miss.)*

### Q8. Ch: The Indian Contract Act, 1872 — Fraud vs Misrepresentation (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain "fraud" and "misrepresentation" as defined under the Indian Contract Act, 1872, and distinguish between the two. State whether mere silence amounts to fraud.

**Answer:**
**Fraud [Section 17]** means and includes any of the following acts committed by a party to a contract, or with his connivance, or by his agent, with intent to deceive or to induce another party to enter into the contract: (a) the suggestion of a fact which is not true, by one who does not believe it to be true; (b) the active concealment of a fact by one having knowledge of it; (c) a promise made without any intention of performing it; (d) any other act fitted to deceive; and (e) any such act or omission as the law specially declares to be fraudulent.

**Misrepresentation [Section 18]** means a false statement made innocently, i.e. without any intention to deceive. It includes (a) a positive assertion of an untrue fact which the person believes to be true; (b) a breach of duty which brings an advantage to the person committing it by misleading another; and (c) causing a party to make a mistake as to the substance of the subject-matter of the agreement.

| Basis | Fraud (Sec. 17) | Misrepresentation (Sec. 18) |
|---|---|---|
| Intention | Made with intent to deceive | Made innocently, without intent to deceive |
| Belief | Maker knows the statement is false | Maker honestly believes it to be true |
| Remedies | Rescind + claim damages | Rescind; generally no damages |
| Nature | A civil wrong and may be criminal | Purely a bona fide error |

**Whether silence amounts to fraud:** As a general rule, **mere silence** as to facts likely to affect the willingness of a person to enter into a contract is **not fraud** (Explanation to Section 17), because there is no duty to disclose. However, silence amounts to fraud in two situations: (i) where the circumstances are such that it is the **duty of the person keeping silent to speak** — for example, contracts of *uberrimae fidei* (utmost good faith) such as insurance, or fiduciary relationships; and (ii) where **silence is, in itself, equivalent to speech**.

**Conclusion:** Both fraud and misrepresentation render the agreement voidable under Section 19 at the option of the deceived party; the essence of the distinction is the presence or absence of an intention to deceive.

*(Full-marks tip: Reproduce the five clauses of Section 17 and the two exceptions on silence with the phrase "duty to speak"; forgetting that misrepresentation gives no right to damages loses marks.)*

### Q9. Ch: The Indian Contract Act, 1872 — Void Agreements (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** State the agreements which have been "expressly declared to be void" under the Indian Contract Act, 1872.

**Answer:**
An agreement not enforceable by law is said to be **void** [Section 2(g)]. Besides agreements which are void for want of the essentials of Section 10, the Act **expressly declares** certain agreements to be void, namely:

1. **Agreement by an incompetent person [Section 11].** An agreement made by a minor or a person of unsound mind is void.

2. **Agreement made under a mutual mistake of fact [Section 20].** Where both parties to an agreement are under a mistake as to a matter of fact essential to the agreement, the agreement is void.

3. **Agreement with unlawful consideration or object [Section 23 & 24].** Where the consideration or object is unlawful, or where it is unlawful in part, the agreement is void.

4. **Agreement made without consideration [Section 25].** Subject to the exceptions, an agreement without consideration is void.

5. **Agreement in restraint of marriage [Section 26].** Every agreement in restraint of the marriage of any person, other than a minor, is void.

6. **Agreement in restraint of trade [Section 27].** Every agreement by which any person is restrained from exercising a lawful profession, trade or business is void to that extent, subject to the exception relating to sale of goodwill.

7. **Agreement in restraint of legal proceedings [Section 28].** An agreement that restricts a party absolutely from enforcing his rights through the ordinary courts, or that limits the time within which he may do so, is void.

8. **Agreement the meaning of which is uncertain [Section 29].** Agreements the meaning of which is not certain, or capable of being made certain, are void.

9. **Wagering agreement [Section 30].** Agreements by way of wager are void, and no suit can be brought to recover anything won on a wager.

10. **Agreement to do an impossible act [Section 56].** An agreement to do an act impossible in itself is void.

**Conclusion:** Such agreements are void ab initio, i.e. destitute of legal effect from the very beginning, and cannot be enforced by either party.

*(Full-marks tip: List each void agreement with its exact section number (26 to 30 especially); pairing restraint of marriage/trade/legal proceedings with the right sections is where marks are won.)*

### Q10. Ch: The Indian Contract Act, 1872 — Wagering Agreement (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** What is a "wagering agreement"? State its essential features and the legal effect of such an agreement under Section 30 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872.

**Answer:**
A **wagering agreement** is an agreement between two persons under which money or money's worth is payable by one person to another on the happening or non-happening of a future uncertain event, in which neither party has any interest other than the sum or stake he will win or lose. Although the Act does not define "wager," **Section 30** provides that "agreements by way of wager are void; and no suit shall be brought for recovering anything alleged to be won on any wager."

The essential features of a wagering agreement are:

1. **Uncertain future event.** The promise to pay must depend upon the determination of an uncertain event; ordinarily it is a future event, but it may relate to a past event if the parties are unaware of its result.

2. **Mutual chances of gain or loss.** Each party must stand to win or lose upon the outcome; it must be a two-sided chance.

3. **Neither party to have control over the event.** No party should have any control over the happening of the event.

4. **No other interest in the event.** Neither party should have any interest in the happening of the event except the stake — this distinguishes a wager from a contract of insurance, where the insured has an insurable interest.

**Legal effect:** A wagering agreement is **void** (though not illegal in most States, except Gujarat and Maharashtra where it is illegal). Being void, it cannot be enforced in any court. However, **collateral transactions** to a wager are not necessarily void, since a wagering agreement is void but not unlawful under Section 23.

**Conclusion:** A wagering agreement, characterised by mutual chances of gain or loss on an uncertain event in which the parties have no interest but the stake, is void under Section 30 and confers no right of action.

*(Full-marks tip: The "no interest except the stake" feature and the insurance contrast (insurable interest) are the marks-clinchers; also state that collateral transactions remain valid.)*

### Q11. Ch: The Indian Contract Act, 1872 — Contingent Contracts (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Define a "contingent contract" under the Indian Contract Act, 1872. State the rules regarding the enforcement of contingent contracts, and distinguish a contingent contract from a wagering agreement.

**Answer:**
According to **Section 31** of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, "a contingent contract is a contract to do or not to do something, if some event, collateral to such contract, does or does not happen." A contract of insurance and a contract of indemnity are common examples of contingent contracts.

The rules relating to the enforcement of contingent contracts are contained in Sections 32 to 36:

1. **Contract contingent on the happening of an event [Section 32].** Such a contract cannot be enforced unless and until the event has happened. If the event becomes impossible, the contract becomes void.

2. **Contract contingent on the non-happening of an event [Section 33].** Such a contract can be enforced only when the happening of that event becomes impossible.

3. **Event linked to the conduct of a living person [Section 34].** If the event is the way a person will act at an unspecified time, the event is considered impossible when such person does anything that renders it impossible for him to act so within any definite time.

4. **Contract contingent on happening of event within a fixed time [Section 35].** It becomes void if the event does not happen, or becomes impossible, within the fixed time.

5. **Agreement contingent on an impossible event [Section 36].** Such an agreement is void, whether or not the impossibility is known to the parties.

| Basis | Contingent Contract | Wagering Agreement |
|---|---|---|
| Validity | Valid (Sec. 31) | Void (Sec. 30) |
| Interest | Parties have real interest in the event | No interest except the stake |
| Nature | Collateral event | Reciprocal chances of gain/loss created by parties |
| Example | Insurance, indemnity | Betting on a cricket match |

**Conclusion:** Every wagering agreement is contingent in nature, but every contingent contract is not a wager; a contingent contract is valid and enforceable subject to Sections 32 to 36.

*(Full-marks tip: Cite Section 31 verbatim and the enforcement sections 32–36 individually; the tabular distinction from a wager (validity + interest) is expected.)*

### Q12. Ch: The Indian Contract Act, 1872 — Performance / Devolution of Joint Liability (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** Explain the rules relating to the performance of a contract by joint promisors under the Indian Contract Act, 1872. Examine the following case:

| Facts |
|---|
| A, B and C jointly borrow ₹90,000 from D under a single contract, each being a joint promisor. On the due date D demands the entire amount from A alone. A contends that he is liable only for ₹30,000, being his one-third share. |

Advise D as to his rights.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Liability of joint promisors [Section 43, para 1]:** When two or more persons make a joint promise, the promisee may, in the absence of an express agreement to the contrary, compel **any one or more** of such joint promisors to perform the whole of the promise. The liability of joint promisors is thus **joint and several**.

**WN-2 — Right of contribution among joint promisors [Section 43, para 2]:** Each of two or more joint promisors may compel every other joint promisor to contribute equally with himself to the performance of the promise, unless a contrary intention appears from the contract.

**WN-3 — Sharing of loss by default in contribution [Section 43, para 3]:** If any one of the joint promisors makes default in such contribution, the remaining joint promisors must bear the loss arising from such default in equal shares.

**Application to facts:** A, B and C are joint promisors to D for ₹90,000. Under Section 43, D may compel **any one** of them — here A — to pay the **whole** amount of ₹90,000, because the liability is joint and several. A's contention that he is liable only for his one-third share (₹30,000) is **not tenable** against D. However, after paying ₹90,000, A can, under the second paragraph of Section 43, recover **₹30,000 each** from B and C by way of contribution.

**Answer:** D can lawfully recover the **entire ₹90,000 from A** alone; A may then claim **₹30,000 each** from B and C as contribution.

*(Full-marks tip: Distinguish D's right against A (whole amount, Sec. 43 para 1) from A's right of contribution against B and C (para 2); students wrongly limit A's liability to his share.)*

### Q13. Ch: The Indian Contract Act, 1872 — Discharge of Contract by Impossibility (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the doctrine of "supervening impossibility" (frustration) under Section 56 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872. State the circumstances in which a contract is discharged by supervening impossibility and those in which it is not.

**Answer:**
**Section 56** of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 lays down the doctrine of impossibility of performance. The first paragraph provides that "an agreement to do an act impossible in itself is void" — this is **initial impossibility**. The second paragraph provides that "a contract to do an act which, after the contract is made, becomes impossible, or, by reason of some event which the promisor could not prevent, unlawful, becomes void when the act becomes impossible or unlawful" — this is **supervening impossibility** or the **doctrine of frustration**.

A contract is **discharged by supervening impossibility** in the following circumstances:

1. **Destruction of the subject-matter.** Where the specific subject-matter essential to performance is destroyed without the fault of either party (*Taylor v. Caldwell* — music hall destroyed by fire).

2. **Death or personal incapacity.** Where performance depends on the personal skill or existence of a party who dies or becomes incapacitated.

3. **Change of law.** Where a subsequent change in law renders performance unlawful.

4. **Non-existence or non-occurrence of a particular state of things.** Where a state of things which formed the basis of the contract ceases to exist (*Krell v. Henry* — coronation cancelled).

5. **Declaration of war.** Where the outbreak of war renders performance illegal (trading with the enemy).

A contract is **NOT discharged** by supervening impossibility (i.e. impossibility is no excuse) in the following cases:

1. **Difficulty of performance** — mere increase in cost or difficulty does not discharge the contract.
2. **Commercial impossibility** — a contract is not discharged merely because performance has become commercially unprofitable.
3. **Default of a third person** — failure of a third party on whose work the promisor relied.
4. **Strikes, lock-outs and civil disturbances** — unless otherwise agreed.
5. **Failure of one of several objects** — if the contract had several objects and only one fails.

**Conclusion:** Under Section 56, a contract is discharged when its performance becomes impossible or unlawful due to a supervening event beyond the control of the parties; but self-induced impossibility or mere difficulty does not discharge the parties from their obligations.

*(Full-marks tip: Structure the answer into "discharged" and "not discharged" heads with a case each (Taylor v. Caldwell, Krell v. Henry); the "commercial impossibility is no excuse" point is frequently tested.)*

### Q14. Ch: The Indian Contract Act, 1872 — Breach of Contract / Remedies (Marks: 8) [Theory]
**Question:** What is meant by "breach of contract"? Explain the various remedies available to an aggrieved party on the breach of a contract under the Indian Contract Act, 1872. Also state the rules regarding measurement of damages laid down in *Hadley v. Baxendale*.

**Answer:**
A **breach of contract** occurs when a party to a contract, without lawful excuse, fails or refuses to perform, or disables himself from performing, or does something inconsistent with the performance of his obligations. A breach may be **actual** (failure to perform at the time due) or **anticipatory** (repudiation of the contract before the time for performance arrives, under Section 39). On breach, the following remedies are available to the aggrieved party:

**1. Rescission of the contract [Section 39 / 75].** The aggrieved party may treat the contract as rescinded, be discharged from all his obligations under it, and claim compensation for any damage sustained.

**2. Suit for damages [Section 73].** This is the most common remedy. When a contract has been broken, the party who suffers is entitled to receive compensation for any loss or damage caused to him which naturally arose in the usual course of things from the breach, or which the parties knew, when they made the contract, to be likely to result from the breach. **No compensation is payable for any remote or indirect loss.** The kinds of damages are:
- *Ordinary (general) damages* — arising naturally in the usual course of things;
- *Special damages* — arising due to special circumstances known to both parties;
- *Exemplary or vindictive damages* — awarded in cases such as breach of promise to marry or wrongful dishonour of a cheque by a bank;
- *Nominal damages* — awarded where the breach is technical and no real loss occurs.

**3. Suit upon quantum meruit.** Where one party has performed part of the contract and the other has prevented complete performance, the party who has performed may claim reasonable remuneration for what he has done, "as much as is earned."

**4. Suit for specific performance.** Where damages are not an adequate remedy, the court may, under the Specific Relief Act, 1963, direct the party in breach to actually perform the contract.

**5. Suit for injunction.** Where a party is in breach of a negative term of the contract, the court may issue an injunction restraining him from doing what he promised not to do.

**Rule in *Hadley v. Baxendale*:** This leading English case, which is the foundation of Section 73, lays down that the damages recoverable are: (a) such as may fairly and reasonably be considered as **arising naturally**, i.e. in the usual course of things, from the breach (general damages); and (b) such as may reasonably be **supposed to have been in the contemplation of both parties**, at the time they made the contract, as the probable result of the breach (special damages, recoverable only if the special circumstances were communicated).

**Conclusion:** On breach of contract, the aggrieved party may pursue any of the remedies of rescission, damages, quantum meruit, specific performance or injunction; the measure of damages is governed by Section 73 and the twin rules in *Hadley v. Baxendale*.

*(Full-marks tip: For 8 marks, list all five remedies with sections and give the four kinds of damages, then state the two limbs of Hadley v. Baxendale distinctly; omitting the "remote loss not recoverable" rule of Section 73 is a common deduction.)*

### Q15. Ch: The Indian Contract Act, 1872 — Liquidated Damages / Penalty (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** Explain the rule regarding compensation for breach of contract where a sum is named in the contract, under Section 74 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872. Examine the following case:

| Facts |
|---|
| X agreed to construct a building for Y within 8 months and the contract provided that if X failed to complete on time, X would pay ₹2,00,000 as agreed damages. X completed the work 2 months late. Y actually suffered a loss of only ₹80,000 due to the delay, but claimed the full ₹2,00,000. |

Determine the amount Y is entitled to recover.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Statutory rule [Section 74]:** When a contract has been broken, and a sum is named in the contract as the amount to be paid in case of such breach, or the contract contains any other stipulation by way of penalty, the party complaining of the breach is entitled, **whether or not actual damage or loss is proved**, to receive from the party in breach **reasonable compensation not exceeding the amount so named** (or the penalty stipulated).

**WN-2 — Position under Indian law:** Unlike English law, Section 74 makes **no distinction between "liquidated damages" and "penalty."** In every case, the court awards only **reasonable compensation**, and the sum named merely fixes the **maximum ceiling** beyond which compensation cannot be awarded. The named sum is not automatically recoverable.

**WN-3 — Requirement of reasonableness:** The compensation must be reasonable in the circumstances. Where the actual loss can be proved, it forms a strong guide to what is reasonable; the aggrieved party cannot recover more than the loss reasonably attributable to the breach, subject to the ceiling.

**Application to facts:** The sum of ₹2,00,000 named in the contract is the **maximum** amount recoverable, not an amount payable as of right. Under Section 74, Y is entitled to **reasonable compensation not exceeding ₹2,00,000**. Since Y's actual proven loss is only ₹80,000, that amount represents reasonable compensation, and Y cannot recover the full ₹2,00,000.

**Answer:** Y is entitled to recover **reasonable compensation of ₹80,000** only, being the actual loss suffered, which is within the ceiling of ₹2,00,000 fixed by the contract.

*(Full-marks tip: Stress that Section 74 abolishes the penalty/liquidated-damages distinction and awards only "reasonable compensation not exceeding the named sum"; awarding the full ₹2,00,000 without regard to actual loss is the classic error.)*

### Q16. Ch: Special Contracts — Contract of Indemnity vs Contract of Guarantee (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between a "Contract of Indemnity" and a "Contract of Guarantee" under the Indian Contract Act, 1872. Support your answer with the relevant statutory definitions.

**Answer:**
A **contract of indemnity** is defined in **Section 124** of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 as a contract by which one party promises to save the other from loss caused to him by the conduct of the promisor himself, or by the conduct of any other person. The person who gives the indemnity is the **indemnifier** and the person for whose protection it is given is the **indemnity-holder** (indemnified). It is essentially a contract to make good a loss — for example, an insurance contract (other than life).

A **contract of guarantee** is defined in **Section 126** of the Act as a contract to perform the promise, or discharge the liability, of a third person in case of his default. It involves **three parties** — the **principal debtor** (whose default is guaranteed), the **creditor** (to whom the guarantee is given) and the **surety** (who gives the guarantee). A guarantee may be either oral or written.

The main points of distinction are set out below.

| Basis | Contract of Indemnity (Sec 124) | Contract of Guarantee (Sec 126) |
|---|---|---|
| Number of parties | Two — indemnifier and indemnity-holder | Three — creditor, principal debtor and surety |
| Number of contracts | One contract | Three contracts (creditor–debtor, creditor–surety, surety–debtor) |
| Nature of liability | Primary liability of the indemnifier | Surety's liability is secondary/collateral; primary liability is of the principal debtor |
| Purpose | To make good a loss | To give assurance/security to the creditor for the debtor's default |
| Existing debt | There need not be any existing debt | There is an existing debt or duty whose performance is guaranteed |
| Right to sue third party | Indemnifier cannot sue a third party in his own name | Surety, on paying, steps into the shoes of the creditor (Sec 140) and can sue the principal debtor |

**Conclusion:** In an indemnity there are two parties and the liability of the indemnifier is primary and independent, whereas in a guarantee there are three parties and the surety's liability is secondary, arising only on the default of the principal debtor.

### Q17. Ch: Special Contracts — Rights of an Indemnity-Holder (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the rights of an indemnity-holder when sued, under the Indian Contract Act, 1872.

**Answer:**
The rights of the indemnity-holder (the person indemnified) when he is sued are laid down in **Section 125** of the Indian Contract Act, 1872. The promisee in a contract of indemnity, acting within the scope of his authority, is entitled to recover from the promisor (the indemnifier) the following:

1. **Recovery of damages (Sec 125(1)):** He is entitled to recover from the indemnifier **all damages** which he may be compelled to pay in any suit in respect of any matter to which the promise to indemnify applies.

2. **Recovery of costs (Sec 125(2)):** He is entitled to recover **all costs** which he may be compelled to pay in bringing or defending such a suit, provided that in doing so he did not contravene the orders of the indemnifier and acted as it would have been prudent for him to act in the absence of any contract of indemnity, or the indemnifier had authorised him to bring or defend the suit.

3. **Recovery of sums paid on compromise (Sec 125(3)):** He is entitled to recover **all sums** which he may have paid under the terms of any **compromise** of such a suit, provided the compromise was not contrary to the orders of the indemnifier and was one which it would have been prudent for the promisee to make in the absence of a contract of indemnity, or the indemnifier authorised him to compromise the suit.

It may be noted that the indemnity-holder can recover these amounts only if he has acted **prudently and within the scope of his authority** and has not contravened the directions of the indemnifier.

**Conclusion:** Under Section 125, an indemnity-holder acting within his authority can recover from the indemnifier all damages, all costs and all sums paid on a bona fide, prudent compromise.

### Q18. Ch: Special Contracts — Bailment: Meaning and Essentials (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Define "Bailment" and explain its essential features under the Indian Contract Act, 1872.

**Answer:**
The term **bailment** is defined in **Section 148** of the Indian Contract Act, 1872. A bailment is the **delivery of goods by one person to another for some purpose, upon a contract that they shall, when the purpose is accomplished, be returned or otherwise disposed of according to the directions of the person delivering them**. The person delivering the goods is called the **bailor** and the person to whom they are delivered is called the **bailee**.

The essential features of a valid bailment are as follows:

1. **Contract:** Bailment is based upon a contract, express or implied. There must be an agreement between the bailor and the bailee (though under Section 71 a finder of goods is treated as a bailee even without an express contract).

2. **Delivery of goods:** There must be delivery of **movable goods** by the bailor to the bailee. The delivery may be **actual** (physical handing over) or **constructive/symbolic** (doing something that has the effect of putting the goods in the possession of the bailee, e.g. handing over the key of a warehouse) — **Section 149**.

3. **Purpose:** The goods are delivered for **some specific purpose**, such as safe custody, repair, carriage or use.

4. **Return or disposal:** It is agreed that after the purpose is accomplished the goods shall be **returned** to the bailor or otherwise **disposed of according to his directions**. The identical goods (though possibly in an altered form) must be returned; mere delivery of an equivalent will not amount to bailment.

5. **No change of ownership:** In a bailment only **possession** passes to the bailee; the **ownership** continues to remain with the bailor.

**Conclusion:** Bailment under Section 148 requires delivery of movable goods for a purpose under a contract, coupled with an obligation to return or dispose of the same goods, ownership remaining throughout with the bailor.

*(Full-marks tip: examiners look for the exact words of Sec 148 plus the "possession passes, ownership stays" point and the actual/constructive delivery distinction under Sec 149.)*

### Q19. Ch: Special Contracts — Pledge by Non-Owner (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** What is a "Pledge"? Explain the circumstances under which a person who is not the owner of goods can make a valid pledge under the Indian Contract Act, 1872.

**Answer:**
**Pledge** is defined in **Section 172** of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 as the **bailment of goods as security for payment of a debt or performance of a promise**. The bailor in this case is called the **pawnor** (pledgor) and the bailee is called the **pawnee** (pledgee). A pledge is therefore a special kind of bailment where the object of delivery is to furnish security.

The general rule is that only the owner of goods can create a valid pledge. However, the Act recognises certain exceptions where a **non-owner** may make a valid pledge:

1. **Pledge by a mercantile agent (Sec 178):** Where a mercantile agent is, with the consent of the owner, in possession of goods or the documents of title to goods, any pledge made by him while acting in the ordinary course of business is valid, provided the pawnee acts in **good faith** and has no notice of the agent's want of authority.

2. **Pledge by a person in possession under a voidable contract (Sec 178A):** When the pawnor has obtained possession of the goods under a **voidable contract** (e.g. by coercion, fraud, misrepresentation or undue influence) but the contract has **not been rescinded** at the time of the pledge, the pledge is valid, provided the pawnee acts in good faith and without notice of the pawnor's defect of title.

3. **Pledge by a person having a limited interest (Sec 179):** Where a person pledges goods in which he has only a **limited interest**, the pledge is valid to the **extent of that interest** (e.g. a person who has a lien or has himself paid something on the goods).

4. **Pledge by a co-owner in sole possession:** Where one of several joint owners is in sole possession of the goods with the consent of the other co-owners, he can make a valid pledge.

5. **Pledge by a seller or buyer in possession after sale:** Under the Sale of Goods Act, 1930, a seller left in possession after sale, or a buyer who has obtained possession before ownership passes, can make a valid pledge to a bona fide pledgee.

**Conclusion:** Although the owner alone can ordinarily pledge goods, Sections 178, 178A and 179 permit a mercantile agent, a person in possession under an unrescinded voidable contract and a person with a limited interest to create a valid pledge, provided the pawnee acts in good faith.

### Q20. Ch: Special Contracts — Modes of Creating Agency / Agency by Ratification (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain "Agency by Ratification" under the Indian Contract Act, 1872 and state the essential conditions for a valid ratification.

**Answer:**
**Ratification** is dealt with in **Sections 196 to 200** of the Indian Contract Act, 1872. **Section 196** provides that where acts are done by one person on behalf of another, but without his knowledge or authority, that other person may **elect to ratify or to disown** such acts. If he ratifies them, the same effects will follow as if they had been performed with his prior authority. Ratification thus relates back to and takes effect from the date of the original unauthorised act (the doctrine of "relation back").

Ratification may be **express or implied** (Section 197) — implied ratification arises from the conduct of the person on whose behalf the act was done, such as accepting the benefit of the transaction.

The essential conditions for a valid ratification are:

1. **Act done on behalf of another:** The act must have been done by a person purporting to act as an agent **on behalf of the principal**; ratification is not possible if the person acted on his own behalf.

2. **Competent principal in existence:** The principal must have been **in existence and competent to contract** both at the time the act was done and at the time of ratification. (Hence a company cannot ratify pre-incorporation contracts.)

3. **Full knowledge of facts (Sec 198):** No valid ratification can be made by a person whose knowledge of the facts of the case is **materially defective**. Ratification must be with **full knowledge** of the material facts.

4. **Ratification of the whole act (Sec 199):** A person ratifying an unauthorised act cannot ratify a **part** of the transaction and disown the rest; ratification must extend to the **whole transaction**.

5. **Lawful act:** The act ratified must be **lawful**; a void or illegal act, or one that is ultra vires, cannot be ratified.

6. **No injury to third party (Sec 200):** Ratification cannot be so made as to subject a third person to damages, or to terminate any right or interest of a third person.

**Conclusion:** Under Sections 196–200, an unauthorised act done on behalf of a competent principal may be ratified — expressly or impliedly — with full knowledge of the whole lawful transaction, whereupon the agency is deemed to have existed from the date of the original act.

*(Full-marks tip: state the "relation back" doctrine and list the whole-transaction rule (Sec 199) and full-knowledge rule (Sec 198); dropping the "principal must be in existence" condition is a common error.)*

### Q21. Ch: Sale of Goods Act, 1930 — Sale vs Agreement to Sell (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between a "Sale" and an "Agreement to Sell" under the Sale of Goods Act, 1930.

**Answer:**
Both a sale and an agreement to sell are species of a **contract of sale of goods**, which is defined in **Section 4(1)** of the Sale of Goods Act, 1930 as a contract whereby the seller transfers or agrees to transfer the property in goods to the buyer for a price. **Section 4(3)** clarifies that where the property in the goods is transferred **immediately**, the contract is called a **sale**; where the transfer of property is to take place at a **future time** or subject to some condition thereafter to be fulfilled, the contract is called an **agreement to sell**. Under **Section 4(4)**, an agreement to sell becomes a sale when the time elapses or the conditions are fulfilled.

The distinction is important and is summarised below.

| Basis | Sale | Agreement to Sell |
|---|---|---|
| Transfer of property | Property passes immediately to the buyer | Property passes at a future date or on a condition |
| Nature of contract | Executed contract | Executory contract |
| Type of right created | Creates a right *in rem* (against the whole world) | Creates a right *in personam* (against the seller only) |
| Risk of loss | Risk passes to the buyer with ownership (Sec 26) | Risk remains with the seller |
| Consequences of breach by buyer | Seller can sue for the **price** | Seller can sue only for **damages** for non-acceptance |
| Consequences of seller's insolvency | Buyer can claim the goods from the Official Assignee | Buyer can only claim rateable dividend for the price paid |
| Consequences of buyer's insolvency | Seller must deliver goods to the Official Assignee (subject to lien) | Seller may refuse to deliver unless paid |

**Conclusion:** In a sale the property in the goods passes at once and it is an executed contract creating a *jus in rem*, whereas in an agreement to sell the property passes in future, it is an executory contract creating only a *jus in personam*.

*(Full-marks tip: the risk-follows-ownership point (Sec 26) and the *jus in rem / jus in personam* distinction are the high-value marks; anchor the whole answer to Section 4.)*

### Q22. Ch: Sale of Goods Act, 1930 — Conditions and Warranties (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the terms "Condition" and "Warranty" under the Sale of Goods Act, 1930. When can a breach of condition be treated as a breach of warranty?

**Answer:**
Under a contract of sale, the parties may make **stipulations** with reference to the goods. **Section 12** of the Sale of Goods Act, 1930 classifies such stipulations into conditions and warranties depending on their importance to the main purpose of the contract.

**Condition (Sec 12(2)):** A condition is a stipulation **essential to the main purpose of the contract**, the breach of which gives the aggrieved party a right to **repudiate the contract** (treat it as void) and also to claim damages.

**Warranty (Sec 12(3)):** A warranty is a stipulation **collateral to the main purpose of the contract**, the breach of which gives rise to a claim for **damages only**, but **not** a right to reject the goods and repudiate the contract.

Whether a stipulation is a condition or a warranty **depends in each case on the construction of the contract** (Sec 12(4)); a stipulation may be a condition though called a warranty in the contract.

**When a breach of condition is to be treated as a breach of warranty (Section 13):** In the following circumstances a breach of a condition sinks to the level of a breach of warranty, so that the buyer loses the right to reject the goods and can claim only damages:

1. **Voluntary waiver by the buyer (Sec 13(1)):** Where the buyer voluntarily **waives** the condition, or **elects to treat** the breach of condition as a breach of warranty.

2. **Acceptance of goods (Sec 13(2)):** Where the contract is **not severable** and the buyer has **accepted the goods** or part thereof, the breach of condition can only be treated as a breach of warranty, and the buyer cannot reject the goods, unless there is a term of the contract (express or implied) to that effect.

3. **Impossibility of performance:** Where the fulfilment of the condition is **excused** by law by reason of impossibility or otherwise.

**Conclusion:** A condition (Sec 12(2)) is essential and its breach permits repudiation, whereas a warranty (Sec 12(3)) is collateral and its breach permits only damages; however, under Section 13 a breach of condition is treated as a breach of warranty where the buyer waives it or has accepted the goods under a non-severable contract.

*(Full-marks tip: attach Sec 12 to the definitions and Sec 13 to the "condition treated as warranty" situations — especially the acceptance rule under Sec 13(2), which carries the highest marks.)*

### Q23. Ch: Sale of Goods Act, 1930 — Rights of an Unpaid Seller (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Who is an "Unpaid Seller" under the Sale of Goods Act, 1930? Explain the rights of an unpaid seller against the goods.

**Answer:**
**Definition (Section 45):** The seller of goods is deemed to be an **"unpaid seller"** within the meaning of the Sale of Goods Act, 1930 (a) when the **whole of the price has not been paid or tendered**; or (b) when a **bill of exchange or other negotiable instrument** has been received as conditional payment and the condition on which it was received has not been fulfilled by reason of the dishonour of the instrument or otherwise. The term "seller" here includes any person who is in the position of a seller, such as an agent of the seller to whom the bill of lading has been endorsed.

The rights of an unpaid seller are of two kinds — rights **against the goods** and rights **against the buyer personally**. The rights **against the goods** (Section 46) are:

**A. Where the property in the goods HAS passed to the buyer:**

1. **Right of Lien (Sections 47–49):** The unpaid seller who is in **possession** of the goods is entitled to **retain possession** until payment of the price where (i) the goods have been sold without any stipulation as to credit, or (ii) the goods have been sold on credit but the term of credit has expired, or (iii) the buyer becomes insolvent. Lien is a right to retain possession, not a right of resale, and is lost once possession is given up.

2. **Right of Stoppage in Transit (Sections 50–52):** Where the buyer becomes **insolvent** and the goods are **in the course of transit**, the unpaid seller may **resume possession** of the goods and retain them until payment. This right can be exercised only after the seller has parted with possession but before the goods reach the buyer.

3. **Right of Resale (Section 54):** The unpaid seller can **re-sell** the goods (i) where the goods are of a **perishable** nature, or (ii) where he has exercised his right of lien or stoppage and gives **notice** to the buyer of his intention to re-sell and the buyer does not pay within a reasonable time, or (iii) where the seller has expressly **reserved** a right of re-sale.

**B. Where the property in the goods has NOT passed to the buyer:**

The unpaid seller has, in addition to his other remedies, a **right of withholding delivery** similar to and co-extensive with his rights of lien and stoppage in transit (Section 46(2)).

**Conclusion:** Under Section 45 a seller is "unpaid" when the price is not fully paid or the instrument received is dishonoured, and against the goods he enjoys the rights of lien (Secs 47–49), stoppage in transit (Secs 50–52) and re-sale (Sec 54), besides the right to withhold delivery where property has not passed.

*(Full-marks tip: define "unpaid seller" from Sec 45 first, then present the three rights against goods with their section numbers; the re-sale conditions under Sec 54 are frequently the deciding marks.)*

### Q24. Ch: Sale of Goods Act, 1930 — Doctrine of Caveat Emptor (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the doctrine of "Caveat Emptor" under the Sale of Goods Act, 1930 and state any two exceptions to it.

**Answer:**
The doctrine of **Caveat Emptor** — literally "**let the buyer beware**" — is embodied in **Section 16** of the Sale of Goods Act, 1930. The doctrine means that it is the **duty of the buyer to be careful** while purchasing goods of his requirement, and in the absence of any enquiry from the buyer, the seller is **not bound to disclose** every defect in the goods of which he may be cognisant. The opening words of Section 16 provide that, subject to the Act and any other law, **there is no implied warranty or condition as to the quality or fitness for any particular purpose** of goods supplied under a contract of sale. If the buyer makes a wrong choice, he cannot blame the seller for his own folly.

However, the doctrine is subject to important **exceptions**, in which the buyer is protected:

1. **Fitness for buyer's purpose (Sec 16(1)):** Where the buyer **makes known to the seller the particular purpose** for which the goods are required, so as to show that he **relies on the seller's skill or judgment**, and the goods are of a description which it is in the course of the seller's business to supply, there is an **implied condition** that the goods shall be reasonably fit for that purpose.

2. **Sale by description — merchantable quality (Sec 16(2)):** Where goods are bought **by description** from a seller who deals in goods of that description, there is an implied condition that the goods shall be of **merchantable quality**.

3. **Sale by sample (Sec 17):** In a sale by sample, the bulk must correspond with the sample.

4. **Usage of trade (Sec 16(3)):** An implied condition or warranty as to quality or fitness may be annexed by the **usage of trade**.

5. **Fraud or concealment:** Where the seller obtains consent by **fraud** or **conceals a latent defect**, the doctrine does not apply.

**Conclusion:** Caveat emptor under Section 16 casts the risk of choosing the goods on the buyer, but it does not apply where the buyer relies on the seller's skill for a made-known purpose (Sec 16(1)), where goods are sold by description (Sec 16(2)) or by sample (Sec 17), or where the seller is guilty of fraud.

*(Full-marks tip: state the meaning "let the buyer beware" and anchor it to Sec 16, then give at least two exceptions with sub-section numbers — Sec 16(1) fitness and Sec 16(2) merchantable quality are the safest picks.)*

### Q25. Ch: Indian Partnership Act, 1932 — Meaning and True Test of Partnership (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Define "Partnership" under the Indian Partnership Act, 1932. What is the "true test" of the existence of a partnership?

**Answer:**
**Definition (Section 4):** "Partnership" is defined in **Section 4** of the Indian Partnership Act, 1932 as **the relation between persons who have agreed to share the profits of a business carried on by all or any of them acting for all**. Persons who have entered into partnership with one another are individually called **"partners"** and collectively a **"firm"**, and the name under which their business is carried on is called the **"firm name"**.

From this definition, the essential elements of a partnership are: (i) an **agreement** between two or more persons; (ii) an agreement to **carry on a business**; (iii) an agreement to **share profits** of the business; and (iv) the business must be carried on **by all or any of them acting for all** — that is, the relationship of **mutual agency**.

**The true test — Mutual Agency (Section 6):** The mere sharing of profits is **not** a conclusive test of partnership. **Section 6** provides that in determining whether a group of persons is or is not a firm, regard shall be had to the **real relation between the parties as shown by all relevant facts taken together**. The **Explanation** to Section 6 makes it clear that the sharing of profits or of gross returns, or the receipt by a person of a share of profits (e.g. by a lender, a servant paid by commission, or the widow of a deceased partner), does **not of itself** make that person a partner.

Therefore, the **conclusive or true test** of partnership is the existence of **mutual agency** — i.e. whether each partner is at once a **principal and an agent** for the others, so that the business is carried on by all or any of them acting for all. If mutual agency exists, there is a partnership; if it is absent, there is none even though profits are shared.

**Conclusion:** Partnership under Section 4 is the relation between persons sharing the profits of a business carried on by all or any of them acting for all, and the true test of its existence, under Section 6, is not profit-sharing but the presence of **mutual agency**.

*(Full-marks tip: quote Sec 4 in full, list the four essentials, then clearly state that mutual agency (Sec 6) — not profit-sharing — is the conclusive test; missing "acting for all" loses the key mark.)*

### Q26. Ch: Indian Partnership Act, 1932 — Effects of Non-Registration of a Firm (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Registration of a partnership firm is optional and not compulsory. Explain the consequences (disabilities) of non-registration of a firm under the Indian Partnership Act, 1932.

**Answer:**
Under the Indian Partnership Act, 1932 the registration of a firm is **not compulsory**; it is optional. However, an **unregistered firm** and its partners suffer from certain **disabilities** which are laid down in **Section 69** of the Act. These disabilities operate as an indirect compulsion to get the firm registered. The consequences of non-registration are:

1. **No suit by a partner against the firm or co-partners (Sec 69(1)):** A partner of an unregistered firm **cannot file a suit** against the firm or any present or past partner to **enforce a right arising from the contract** or conferred by the Partnership Act, unless the firm is registered and the person suing is or has been shown as a partner in the Register of Firms.

2. **No suit by the firm against third parties (Sec 69(2)):** An unregistered firm **cannot file a suit against a third party** to enforce a right arising from a contract, unless the firm is registered and the persons suing are or have been shown as partners in the Register of Firms.

3. **No claim of set-off (Sec 69(3)):** An unregistered firm or its partner **cannot claim a set-off** exceeding ₹100, or pursue other proceedings to enforce a right arising from a contract, in a suit filed against the firm by a third party.

**Exceptions — rights NOT affected by non-registration (Sec 69(3) & (4)):** The disabilities do **not** affect:
- The right of a partner to sue for the **dissolution** of the firm, or for **accounts** of a dissolved firm, or to realise the property of a dissolved firm;
- The powers of an **Official Assignee/Receiver** to realise the property of an insolvent partner;
- The right of the firm or partners to sue where the claim does **not exceed ₹100** (subject to certain limits);
- The right of a **third party** to sue the firm or its partners (an unregistered firm can always be sued);
- Firms or partners in areas where Chapter VII does not apply, or a right or claim arising **otherwise than out of a contract** (e.g. for infringement of a statutory right).

### Q27. Ch: Indian Partnership Act, 1932 — Implied Authority of a Partner (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the "implied authority" of a partner under the Indian Partnership Act, 1932. State the acts which are outside the implied authority of a partner.

**Answer:**
**Statutory basis (Section 19):** Under **Section 19(1)** of the Indian Partnership Act, 1932, subject to the provisions of Section 22, the **act of a partner which is done to carry on, in the usual way, business of the kind carried on by the firm binds the firm**. The authority of a partner to bind the firm by such acts is called his **"implied authority"**. It flows from the principle of mutual agency, each partner being an agent of the firm for the purpose of its business (Section 18).

For an act to fall within a partner's implied authority: (i) the act must relate to the **normal business** of the firm; (ii) it must be done in the **usual way** of carrying on such business; and (iii) it must be done in the **firm name** or in a manner binding the firm (Section 22).

1. Submit a dispute relating to the business of the firm to **arbitration**;
2. Open a **banking account** on behalf of the firm in his **own name**;
3. **Compromise or relinquish** any claim or portion of a claim by the firm;
4. **Withdraw** a suit or proceeding filed on behalf of the firm;
5. **Admit any liability** in a suit or proceeding against the firm;
6. **Acquire immovable property** on behalf of the firm;
7. **Transfer immovable property** belonging to the firm;
8. Enter into **partnership** on behalf of the firm.

These acts can be done by a partner only if he is **expressly authorised** by the other partners or where a usage or custom of trade permits.

**Conclusion:** Under Section 19, a partner's implied authority binds the firm for acts done in the usual course of its business, but the eight acts enumerated in Section 19(2) — such as arbitration, compromise, acquisition or transfer of immovable property and entering into a new partnership — fall outside implied authority and require express authorisation.

### Q28. Ch: Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 — Meaning and Characteristics (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** What is a "Negotiable Instrument"? Explain its essential characteristics under the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881.

**Answer:**
**Meaning (Section 13):** The Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 does not define the term exhaustively. **Section 13(1)** provides that a **"negotiable instrument" means a promissory note, bill of exchange or cheque payable either to order or to bearer**. The word "negotiable" means **transferable by delivery** and "instrument" means a **written document creating a right in favour of some person**. Thus a negotiable instrument is a written document, the property in which is acquired by anyone who takes it *bona fide* and for value, notwithstanding any defect in the title of the transferor.

The essential **characteristics** of a negotiable instrument are:

1. **Freely transferable:** The property in a negotiable instrument passes freely — a bearer instrument by **mere delivery**, and an order instrument by **endorsement and delivery** — without any formality such as registration or a separate deed of transfer.

2. **Title free from defects (holder in due course):** A person who takes the instrument *bona fide*, for value and without notice of any defect, called a **holder in due course** (Sec 9), gets a **good and complete title** even if the transferor's title was defective. His title is better than that of the transferor.

3. **Right to sue in own name:** The holder of the instrument can **sue upon it in his own name**.

4. **Presumptions (Sections 118 and 119):** Certain **presumptions** are attached to negotiable instruments — for example, that they were made or drawn for **consideration**, that they were made/accepted/endorsed on the **date they bear**, and that the holder is a **holder in due course** — until the contrary is proved.

5. **Payable to order or bearer / certainty:** The instrument is **payable either to order or to bearer**, for a **certain sum of money**, and is payable on demand or at a fixed or determinable future time.

**Conclusion:** A negotiable instrument under Section 13 is a promissory note, bill of exchange or cheque payable to order or bearer; its distinctive features are free transferability, the acquisition of a defect-free title by a holder in due course, the right to sue in one's own name and the statutory presumptions under Sections 118–119.

*(Full-marks tip: begin with Sec 13, then give the free-transfer and holder-in-due-course points; mentioning the presumptions under Secs 118/119 lifts the answer to full marks.)*

### Q29. Ch: Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 — Promissory Note vs Bill of Exchange (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between a "Promissory Note" and a "Bill of Exchange" under the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881.

**Answer:**
A **promissory note** is defined in **Section 4** of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 as an **instrument in writing (not being a bank-note or a currency-note) containing an unconditional undertaking, signed by the maker, to pay a certain sum of money only to, or to the order of, a certain person, or to the bearer of the instrument**.

A **bill of exchange** is defined in **Section 5** of the Act as an **instrument in writing containing an unconditional order, signed by the maker, directing a certain person to pay a certain sum of money only to, or to the order of, a certain person or to the bearer of the instrument**.

The points of distinction between the two are as follows.

| Basis | Promissory Note (Sec 4) | Bill of Exchange (Sec 5) |
|---|---|---|
| Number of parties | Two — the maker and the payee | Three — the drawer, the drawee (acceptor) and the payee |
| Nature of undertaking | Contains an **unconditional promise** to pay | Contains an **unconditional order** to pay |
| Maker's position | The maker is the debtor and pays himself | The drawer is the creditor; the drawee is the debtor who pays |
| Payable to maker | The maker and payee cannot be the same person | The drawer and payee may be the same person |
| Acceptance | No acceptance is required | Requires **acceptance** by the drawee before he is liable |
| Liability | Liability of the maker is **primary and absolute** | Liability of the drawer is **secondary and conditional** (arises on the drawee's default) |
| Notice of dishonour | Not necessary to the maker | Notice of dishonour must be given to all prior parties |

**Conclusion:** A promissory note (Sec 4) is a two-party instrument containing an unconditional **promise** to pay with the maker primarily liable, whereas a bill of exchange (Sec 5) is a three-party instrument containing an unconditional **order** to pay, requiring the drawee's acceptance, with the drawer only secondarily liable.

*(Full-marks tip: quote Secs 4 and 5, then contrast "promise vs order" and "two vs three parties" and the primary/secondary liability point — these are the marks-carrying distinctions.)*

### Q30. Ch: Indian Regulatory Framework — Sources of Indian Law & the General Clauses Act, 1897 (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Briefly explain the principal sources of Indian law and state the object of the General Clauses Act, 1897 within the Indian regulatory framework.

**Answer:**
The Indian legal and regulatory framework draws upon several **sources of law**, which may be classified as principal (primary) and secondary sources.

**Principal (primary) sources of Indian law:**

1. **Customs or customary law:** Long-established usages and practices accepted by the community as having the force of law. A valid custom must be **ancient, reasonable, continuous and certain**, and not opposed to public policy or any statute. Much of early personal law developed from custom.

2. **Judicial precedents (case law):** The decisions of the superior courts. Under **Article 141 of the Constitution**, the law declared by the **Supreme Court is binding on all courts** within India. This is based on the doctrine of *stare decisis* — the *ratio decidendi* of a case is binding, while *obiter dicta* have only persuasive value.

3. **Statutes / legislation:** Law enacted by **Parliament and the State Legislatures**. Legislation is today the most important and direct source of law, being certain, prospective and comprehensive.

**Secondary sources:** These include **English common law, justice, equity and good conscience**, and the **opinions of jurists**, which are relied upon where the primary sources are silent.

**Object of the General Clauses Act, 1897:** The General Clauses Act, 1897 is a **consolidating statute** whose object is to **shorten the language of Central Acts, to provide uniform and settled definitions of common terms, and to state general rules of construction and interpretation**, so that these need not be repeated in every enactment. It thereby avoids superfluity and repetition of language, ensures consistency of expression across statutes, and guards against slips and mistakes in drafting. Its definitions apply to all Central Acts and Regulations unless there is anything repugnant in the subject or context.

**Conclusion:** The principal sources of Indian law are **custom, judicial precedent and legislation** (supplemented by equity and juristic opinion), and within this framework the **General Clauses Act, 1897** serves as an interpretation statute that supplies uniform definitions and general rules of construction to all Central legislation.

### Q36. Ch: Incorporation of Company — Doctrine of Ultra Vires (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the "Doctrine of Ultra Vires" with reference to the Memorandum of Association. State the consequences of an ultra vires act and the leading case establishing this doctrine.

**Answer:**
The **Doctrine of Ultra Vires** governs the capacity of a company to act and is rooted in the **object clause of the Memorandum of Association** required under **Section 4(1)(c)** of the Companies Act, 2013. "Ultra vires" means "beyond the powers." A company, being an artificial legal person, possesses only those powers that are conferred upon it by its memorandum, together with powers reasonably incidental to the attainment of its objects. Any act done or contract entered into by the company that falls **outside the scope of its objects clause is ultra vires the company** and is **void ab initio**.

The doctrine was firmly established in the leading case of **Ashbury Railway Carriage & Iron Co. Ltd. v. Riche (1875)**, where the House of Lords held that a contract to finance the construction of a railway line, being outside the company's objects (which were confined to manufacturing and selling railway carriages), was ultra vires and void, and could not be ratified even by the unanimous consent of all shareholders.

**Consequences of an ultra vires act:**
1. An ultra vires act is **void ab initio** and cannot be ratified even by the whole body of shareholders.
2. An **injunction** may be obtained by any member to restrain the company from proceeding with an ultra vires act.
3. **Directors are personally liable** to the company for any loss caused by application of the company's funds to ultra vires purposes, as they are in breach of their duty.
4. **Ultra vires contracts are not enforceable** by or against the company.
5. **Property acquired** through ultra vires expenditure is nevertheless the property of the company and can be protected.
6. **Ultra vires borrowing** does not create a debtor-creditor relationship; the lender, however, may pursue equitable remedies such as tracing or subrogation.

**Conclusion:** An act beyond the object clause is ultra vires the company and void, protecting shareholders and creditors from unauthorised diversion of the company's funds.

### Q37. Ch: Incorporation of Company — Doctrine of Indoor Management (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** The Articles of Association of XYZ Ltd provide that a bill of exchange must be signed by two directors and countersigned by the secretary. A bill was signed by two persons who were held out by the company as directors but whose appointment was later found to be defective. Mr. R, a holder of the bill for value, seeks to enforce it against the company. Advise whether the company can escape liability. Also state the exceptions to the doctrine relied upon.

**Solution:**
The issue is governed by the **Doctrine of Indoor Management** (the rule in **Turquand's case** — *Royal British Bank v. Turquand, 1856*) and its interplay with the **Doctrine of Constructive Notice**.

**WN-1 — The rule:** The Doctrine of Constructive Notice presumes that outsiders dealing with a company have read its public documents (Memorandum and Articles). The Doctrine of Indoor Management is the **counterbalancing rule** that protects outsiders: while they are deemed to know the **contents** of the public documents, they are **not required to inquire into the internal or indoor proceedings** of the company. They are entitled to assume that all matters of internal regulation and procedure have been duly complied with.

**WN-2 — Application to facts:** The requirement that a bill be signed by two directors and countersigned by the secretary is a matter of **internal management**. Mr. R, an outsider taking the bill for value, was entitled to assume that the directors were validly appointed and that the internal procedure had been complied with. The **defective appointment of directors** is exactly the kind of internal irregularity covered by the doctrine, as decided in *Mahony v. East Holyford Mining Co.* Therefore the company **cannot escape liability**, and Mr. R can enforce the bill.

**WN-3 — Exceptions to the doctrine (when the outsider is NOT protected):**
1. **Knowledge of irregularity** — where the outsider had actual or constructive knowledge of the irregularity (*Howard v. Patent Ivory Mfg. Co.*).
2. **Suspicion of irregularity / negligence** — where circumstances are suspicious and the outsider fails to make inquiry (*Anand Bihari Lal v. Dinshaw & Co.*).
3. **Forgery** — the doctrine does not validate a forged document, which is void ab initio (*Ruben v. Great Fingall Consolidated*).
4. **Acts outside apparent authority** — where the official had no ostensible authority.
5. **No knowledge of the Articles** — a person who has not actually read the Articles cannot claim their protection.

**Answer:** The company **cannot escape liability**; Mr. R can enforce the bill under the Doctrine of Indoor Management, as a defective appointment of directors is an internal irregularity, and none of the exceptions applies.

### Q40. Ch: Prospectus & Allotment — Meaning & Kinds of Prospectus (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Define "Prospectus" under the Companies Act, 2013. Explain the meaning of (i) Shelf Prospectus, (ii) Red Herring Prospectus, and (iii) Abridged Prospectus.

Any class of companies as prescribed by SEBI may file a shelf prospectus with the Registrar at the stage of the first offer, valid for a period **not exceeding one year**. For subsequent offers within that period, the company files only an **information memorandum** disclosing material changes; no fresh prospectus is required.

Upon closing of the offer, the final prospectus stating the total capital raised and the closing price is filed with the Registrar and SEBI.

**(iii) Abridged Prospectus [Section 2(1)]:** An abridged prospectus is a **memorandum containing such salient features of a prospectus as may be specified by SEBI**. Under **Section 33**, every application form for securities must be accompanied by an abridged prospectus, so that investors receive a concise summary of key disclosures, reducing cost while ensuring disclosure.

### Q42. Ch: Prospectus & Allotment — Private Placement (Sec 42) (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the concept of "Private Placement" under Section 42 of the Companies Act, 2013. State the key conditions and limits, and the consequences of contravention.

**Answer:**
**Private Placement** is defined under **Section 42** of the Companies Act, 2013 as **any offer or invitation to subscribe or issue of securities to a select group of persons** by a company (other than by way of a public offer) through the issue of a **private placement offer-cum-application letter (PAS-4)**, and which satisfies the conditions specified in Section 42.

**Key conditions and limits:**
1. **Identified persons / ceiling on number:** A private placement offer may be made to a **select group of persons identified by the Board**, whose number shall **not exceed 200 in the aggregate in a financial year** (excluding qualified institutional buyers and employees under an ESOP scheme). The ceiling of 200 is per kind of security and per financial year.
2. **Separate bank account:** All monies received on application must be kept in a **separate bank account** in a scheduled bank and shall not be utilised except (a) for **adjustment against allotment** of securities, or (b) for the **repayment of monies** where the company is unable to allot securities.
3. **No fresh offer:** A company shall **not make a fresh offer** or invitation under private placement unless the **allotments with respect to any earlier offer have been completed, withdrawn or abandoned**.
4. **Time limit for allotment:** The company shall **allot securities within 60 days** of receipt of application money; if it fails, it must **repay the money within 15 days** thereafter, failing which it is liable to repay with **interest at 12% p.a.** from the expiry of the 60th day.
5. **Mode of payment:** Application money must be paid through **cheque, demand draft or other banking channels but not by cash**.
6. **Filing of return of allotment:** A **return of allotment (PAS-3)** must be filed with the Registrar within **15 days** of allotment.
7. **No public advertisement:** The offer shall not carry any right of renunciation and no public advertisement or media marketing is permitted.

**Consequences of contravention [Section 42(10)]:** If a company makes an offer or accepts monies in contravention of Section 42, the **company, its promoters and directors are liable to a penalty** which may extend to the **amount raised through the private placement or ₹2 crore, whichever is lower**, and the company shall also **refund all monies to subscribers within 30 days** of the order imposing the penalty.

### Q48. Ch: Share Capital & Debentures — Reduction & Alteration of Capital (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between "alteration of share capital" under Section 61 and "reduction of share capital" under Section 66 of the Companies Act, 2013. State the modes of alteration and the approvals required for reduction.

**Answer:**
Both provisions deal with changes to a company's share capital, but they operate very differently — one reorganises capital without reducing liability, the other actually diminishes capital.

**Alteration of Share Capital [Section 61]:** A **limited company having a share capital** may, if authorised by its **articles**, alter its capital by an **ordinary resolution** in general meeting. Under Section 61(1), the company may:
(a) **increase** its authorised share capital;
(b) **consolidate and divide** all or any of its share capital into shares of larger amount (consolidation that changes voting percentage needs Tribunal approval);
(c) **convert fully paid shares into stock** and reconvert stock into shares;
(d) **sub-divide** shares into shares of smaller amount; and

(i) extinguishing or reducing the liability on any of its shares in respect of unpaid share capital;
(ii) cancelling any paid-up share capital which is **lost or unrepresented by available assets**; or
(iii) paying off any paid-up share capital which is **in excess of the wants** of the company.

Reduction requires (a) authorisation by the **articles**, (b) a **special resolution**, and (c) **confirmation by the Tribunal (NCLT)**, which considers the interests of **creditors** before confirming.

|---|---|---|
| Resolution | Ordinary resolution | Special resolution |
| Tribunal approval | Not required (generally) | Mandatory (NCLT) |
| Effect on capital | Reorganisation, no reduction | Actual reduction of capital |
| Creditor protection | Not involved | Tribunal safeguards creditors |

**Conclusion:** Section 61 permits reorganisation of capital by ordinary resolution without Tribunal approval, whereas Section 66 involves an actual reduction of capital requiring a special resolution and mandatory NCLT confirmation with creditor protection.

### Q54. Ch: Registration of Charges — Meaning of Charge & Duty to Register: Sec 77 (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Define "charge" under the Companies Act, 2013. Explain the duty of a company to register a charge under Section 77, the time limit for registration, and the consequence of non-registration.

**Answer:**
**Statutory basis:** The term "charge" is defined in **Section 2(16) of the Companies Act, 2013** as *an interest or lien created on the property or assets of a company or any of its undertakings, or both, as security, and includes a mortgage*. Thus a charge secures the repayment of money borrowed by creating a security interest over the company's assets.

**Duty to register — Section 77(1):** It is the duty of **every company creating a charge** — whether within or outside India, on its property or assets or any of its undertakings, whether tangible or otherwise, and whether situated in or outside India — to **register the particulars of the charge** signed by the company and the charge-holder together with the instruments creating the charge, with the **Registrar** in the prescribed form (**Form CHG-1** for charges other than debentures, **CHG-9** for debentures).

**Time limit for registration:** The charge must be registered **within 30 days** of its creation. Where it is not registered within 30 days, the Registrar may, on application, allow registration within a further period:
- For charges **created on or after** the commencement of the Companies (Amendment) Ordinance, 2019 — within a further period of **60 days** on payment of additional fees (i.e., total 30 + 60 days), and thereafter registration is not permissible under this route.
- For charges **created before** that commencement — within **300 days**, failing which within 6 months on ad valorem fees.

**Consequence of non-registration — Section 77(3):** Notwithstanding anything contained in any other law, **no charge created by a company shall be taken into account by the liquidator (appointed under the Companies Act or the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code) or any other creditor unless it is duly registered** and a certificate of registration is issued. In effect, an **unregistered charge becomes void against the liquidator and other creditors** — the security becomes unenforceable in insolvency, and the charge-holder ranks as an **unsecured creditor**. However, the contract to repay the money secured remains valid [Section 77(3) proviso].

**Conclusion:** A charge under Section 2(16) is a security interest (including a mortgage) on the company's assets; Section 77 casts a duty on the company to register every charge within 30 days (extendable by a further 60 days), and an unregistered charge, though the underlying debt survives, is void against the liquidator and other creditors.

### Q61. Ch: Management & Administration — Ordinary vs Special Resolution: Sec 114 (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between an "ordinary resolution" and a "special resolution" under Section 114 of the Companies Act, 2013. Give two examples of matters requiring a special resolution.

**Answer:**
**Statutory basis:** Resolutions are defined in **Section 114 of the Companies Act, 2013**. A resolution is the formal decision of the members of a company expressed in a general meeting; the Act classifies resolutions as ordinary or special depending on the majority required.

**Ordinary resolution [Section 114(1)]:** A resolution is an ordinary resolution when the notice required under the Act has been duly given and it is required to be passed by the votes cast (whether on a show of hands, electronically or on a poll) in favour of the resolution, including the casting vote of the chairman, being **more than the votes cast against** — i.e., a **simple majority (more than 50%)**.

**Special resolution [Section 114(2)]:** A resolution is a special resolution when (a) the **intention to propose it as a special resolution** has been duly specified in the notice; (b) the notice required under the Act has been duly given; and (c) the votes cast in favour of the resolution are **not less than three times** the number of votes cast against it (i.e., a majority of **at least 75%**).

**Distinction:**

| Basis | Ordinary Resolution [Sec 114(1)] | Special Resolution [Sec 114(2)] |
|---|---|---|

| Notice | Ordinary notice; intention need not be stated | Notice must **specifically state** the intention to propose it as a special resolution |
| Filing with Registrar | Generally not required (except specified cases) | Copy must be **filed in Form MGT-14** with the Registrar within 30 days |
| Typical matters | Appointment of auditors, adoption of accounts, declaration of dividend, ordinary appointment of directors | Alteration of MOA/AOA, reduction of share capital, change of registered office outside local limits, conversion of company |

**Examples requiring special resolution:** (i) **Alteration of the Articles of Association** under Section 14; (ii) **Change of the registered office** of the company from one State to another under Section 13; (iii) providing for **buy-back of securities** under Section 68.

**Conclusion:** An ordinary resolution requires a simple majority whereas a special resolution requires a three-fourths majority with the intention expressly stated in the notice and a mandatory filing in Form MGT-14; matters of fundamental importance such as alteration of the Articles require a special resolution.

A meeting of the Board must be called by giving **not less than 7 days' notice** in writing to every director at his registered address, whether in India or abroad, by hand delivery, post or electronic means. A meeting may be called at **shorter notice** to transact urgent business provided at least **one independent director** (if any) is present; if not present, the decisions are circulated to and ratified by an independent director.

**WN-3 — Quorum generally [Section 174(1)]:**
The quorum for a Board meeting is **one-third of the total strength or two directors, whichever is higher**, and directors participating through video conferencing are counted for quorum.
Total strength = 9 directors.
One-third of 9 = 3 directors. Higher of (3, 2) = **3 directors**. Hence the ordinary quorum = **3 directors**.

**WN-4 — Quorum where directors are interested [Section 174(3)]:**
Where at any time the number of **interested directors** exceeds or is equal to two-thirds of the total strength, the **remaining directors present**, being **not less than 2**, shall constitute the quorum. Here, only **2 directors are interested** (out of 9); interested directors do **not** exceed two-thirds. The 2 interested directors **cannot be counted** for quorum on that item, so the number of disinterested directors available must still meet the normal quorum of 3.
Disinterested directors = 9 − 2 = 7, of whom at least **3 must be present** to form the quorum for the interested contract.

| Statement Showing Board-Meeting Requirements | Position |
|---|---|
| Minimum Board meetings per year | 4 (gap ≤ 120 days) |
| First Board meeting | Within 30 days of incorporation |
| Notice period | Not less than 7 days |
| Ordinary quorum (1/3 of 9 or 2, higher) | 3 directors |
| Quorum for interested contract | 3 disinterested directors (interested directors not counted) |

**Answer:** (i) Minimum **4 Board meetings** a year with a gap not exceeding **120 days**; (ii) notice of **not less than 7 days**; (iii) ordinary quorum = **3 directors** (one-third of 9); (iv) for the interested contract the 2 interested directors are excluded, and **at least 3 disinterested directors** must be present to constitute the quorum.

*(Full-marks tip: Compute one-third of the total strength for quorum and exclude interested directors under Section 174(3); a common deduction is counting interested directors towards quorum or using "2 directors" as quorum when one-third is higher.)*

### Q63. Ch: Management & Administration — Annual Return: Sec 92 (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** State the provisions relating to the Annual Return under Section 92 of the Companies Act, 2013 — its contents, the requirement of signing and certification, the form and time-limit for filing with the Registrar, and the penalty for default.

**Answer:**
**Statutory basis:** The preparation and filing of the annual return is governed by **Section 92 of the Companies Act, 2013** read with the Companies (Management and Administration) Rules, 2014. The annual return is a comprehensive yearly snapshot of a company's constitution, capital, management and shareholding.

**Contents of the Annual Return [Section 92(1)]:** Every company shall prepare an annual return in **Form MGT-7** (MGT-7A for OPC and small companies), containing the particulars as they stood on the **close of the financial year**, regarding, *inter alia*:
(a) its registered office, principal business activities, particulars of holding, subsidiary and associate companies;
(b) its **shares, debentures and other securities** and shareholding pattern;
(c) its **members and debenture-holders** along with changes therein since the close of the previous financial year;
(d) its **promoters, directors and key managerial personnel** and changes therein;
(e) meetings of members/class of members, Board and its committees, along with attendance details;
(f) **remuneration** of directors and key managerial personnel;
(g) penalties or punishments imposed and details of compounding of offences;
(h) matters relating to **certification of compliances** and shareholding pattern.
*(The requirement to disclose the indebtedness of the company was omitted by amendment.)*

**Signing [Section 92(1) proviso]:** The annual return shall be **signed by a director and the Company Secretary**, or where there is no Company Secretary, by a **Company Secretary in practice**. In the case of an **OPC, small company and other prescribed companies**, it may be signed by the **Company Secretary alone, or where there is no CS, by the director**.

**Certification [Section 92(2)]:** The annual return of a **listed company** or a company having **paid-up capital of ₹10 crore or more or turnover of ₹50 crore or more** shall be **certified by a Company Secretary in practice in Form MGT-8**, stating that the return discloses the facts correctly and the company has complied with the provisions of the Act.

**Filing [Section 92(4)]:** A copy of the annual return shall be **filed with the Registrar within 60 days** from the date on which the AGM was held; where no AGM is held, within 60 days from the date on which it **should have been held**, with a statement of reasons.

**Penalty [Section 92(5)]:** If a company fails to file the annual return before the expiry of the period, the company and every officer in default are liable to a penalty of **₹10,000**, and in case of continuing failure a further penalty of **₹100 per day** subject to a maximum of **₹2,00,000 for the company** and **₹50,000 for an officer in default**.

**Conclusion:** Under Section 92, every company must prepare an annual return in Form MGT-7 containing prescribed particulars as at the close of the financial year, signed by a director and the CS, certified in Form MGT-8 for larger companies, and filed with the Registrar within 60 days of the AGM; default attracts a penalty of ₹10,000 plus ₹100 per day up to the prescribed maximums.

*(Full-marks tip: State Form MGT-7, the 60-day filing limit and MGT-8 certification threshold (₹10 crore capital / ₹50 crore turnover); deductions arise for omitting the signing requirement or the certification limits.)*

### Q64. Ch: Management & Administration — Voting: Show of Hands, Poll & Proxy: Sec 105, 107, 108, 109 (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the modes of voting at a general meeting of a company — voting by show of hands, demand for poll, and voting by proxy — with reference to the relevant sections of the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:**
**Statutory basis:** The methods of voting at general meetings are dealt with in **Sections 107 (show of hands), 108 (voting through electronic means), 109 (demand for poll)** and **Section 105 (proxies)** of the Companies Act, 2013.

**Voting by show of hands — Section 107:** At any general meeting, a resolution put to the vote shall, **unless a poll is demanded** under Section 109 or e-voting is required under Section 108, be **decided on a show of hands**. On a show of hands, **every member present in person has one vote**, irrespective of the number of shares held. A declaration by the chairman that the resolution is carried is **conclusive evidence** of the fact.

**Demand for poll — Section 109:** Before or on the declaration of the result of the voting on a show of hands, a **poll** may be ordered by the chairman on his own motion, and **shall be ordered** on a demand made by the requisite members:
- In a company **having a share capital**: by members present in person or by proxy holding at least **one-tenth of the total voting power** or holding shares on which an aggregate sum of **not less than ₹5,00,000** has been paid up.
- In **any other company**: by not less than **one-tenth of the total members** having the right to vote.
On a poll, voting is in **proportion to the shares held** (each share carries one vote). A poll on adjournment or procedural questions must be taken **forthwith**; on other questions, within **48 hours**.

**Voting by proxy — Section 105:** Any member entitled to attend and vote at a meeting is entitled to **appoint another person as a proxy** to attend and vote on his behalf. Key conditions:
(a) The proxy **need not be a member** of the company.
(b) A proxy **cannot vote on a show of hands** and **cannot speak** at the meeting; a proxy may vote **only on a poll**.
(c) The instrument of proxy (**Form MGT-11**) must be deposited with the company **not less than 48 hours before** the meeting.
(d) A person can act as proxy for **not more than 50 members** and for members holding in aggregate **not more than 10%** of the total share capital carrying voting rights; where a member holds more than 10%, that member may appoint a single proxy for the balance who shall not act as proxy for any other person.

**Conclusion:** Voting at a general meeting is ordinarily by show of hands (one vote per member, Section 107) unless a poll is validly demanded under Section 109 (voting by shares, one-tenth voting power or ₹5,00,000 threshold); a member may vote by proxy under Section 105, but a proxy may vote only on a poll and not on a show of hands.

*(Full-marks tip: Note the "one member one vote" on show of hands versus "one share one vote" on poll, the ₹5,00,000 / one-tenth poll threshold and that a proxy votes only on a poll; deductions arise from allowing a proxy to vote on a show of hands.)*

**Answer:**
**Statutory basis:** A company is required to maintain several statutory registers under different provisions of the Companies Act, 2013 to record its constitution, membership and dealings; the register of members and its inspection are governed principally by **Sections 88 and 94**.

### Q66. Ch: Management & Administration — Extraordinary General Meeting on Requisition: Sec 100 (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** The paid-up share capital of GHI Ltd. consists of 6,00,000 equity shares of ₹10 each fully paid. A group of members holding 65,000 equity shares submits a valid requisition to the Board on 1st August 2026 for calling an Extraordinary General Meeting (EGM) to remove a director. Answer with reasons: (i) whether the requisition is valid as to the shareholding threshold; (ii) the time within which the Board must proceed to call the meeting and the outer date by which the EGM must be held; (iii) the consequence if the Board fails to act; and (iv) the members' right to reimbursement.

**Solution:**
**Statutory basis:** The calling of an Extraordinary General Meeting on the requisition of members is governed by **Section 100 of the Companies Act, 2013**.

**WN-1 — Requisition threshold [Section 100(2)]:**
The Board shall call an EGM on the requisition of members holding, on the date of the requisition, **not less than one-tenth of the paid-up share capital** carrying the right of voting (or, for a company without share capital, one-tenth of total voting power).
Total paid-up capital = 6,00,000 shares × ₹10 = ₹60,00,000.
Required one-tenth = 1/10 × 6,00,000 shares = **60,000 shares** (i.e., ₹6,00,000).
Members hold **65,000 shares (₹6,50,000)**, which **exceeds** the 60,000-share threshold.
Hence the requisition is **valid** as to shareholding.

**WN-2 — Time for the Board to proceed and hold the EGM [Section 100(4)]:**
On receipt of a valid requisition, the Board must, **within 21 days** from the date of receipt, **proceed to call a meeting**, and the meeting must be **actually held within 45 days** from the date of receipt of the requisition.
Date of requisition = 1st August 2026.
Board must proceed to call the meeting by = 1st August + 21 days = **22nd August 2026**.
The EGM must actually be held by = 1st August + 45 days = **15th September 2026**.

**WN-3 — Failure of the Board to act [Section 100(4) & (6)]:**
If the Board **does not**, within 21 days of receipt of a valid requisition, proceed to call a meeting to be held within 45 days, the **requisitionists themselves may call and hold** the meeting. Such a meeting called by the requisitionists must be held **within 3 months** from the date of the requisition. The manner of calling shall, as far as possible, be the same as that in which meetings are called by the Board.

**WN-4 — Reimbursement of expenses [Section 100(6) proviso]:**
Any **reasonable expenses** incurred by the requisitionists in calling and holding such a meeting shall be **reimbursed to them by the company**, and the company shall in turn **recover** such sums from the fees or remuneration payable to the **directors who were in default** in calling the meeting.

| Statement Showing Position under Section 100 | Result |
|---|---|
| Paid-up capital | 6,00,000 shares (₹60,00,000) |
| One-tenth threshold | 60,000 shares (₹6,00,000) |
| Shares held by requisitionists | 65,000 shares (₹6,50,000) → **valid** |
| Board to proceed to call meeting by | 22 August 2026 (within 21 days) |
| EGM to be held by | 15 September 2026 (within 45 days) |
| If Board defaults — requisitionists' meeting within | 3 months of requisition (by 31 October 2026) |
| Expenses of requisitionists | Reimbursed by company, recovered from defaulting directors |

**Answer:** (i) The requisition is **valid**, since 65,000 shares exceed the one-tenth threshold of 60,000 shares. (ii) The Board must **proceed to call** the EGM within 21 days (by **22nd August 2026**) and **hold** it within 45 days (by **15th September 2026**). (iii) On the Board's failure, the **requisitionists may themselves call and hold** the EGM within **3 months** of the requisition. (iv) The **reasonable expenses** of the requisitionists shall be **reimbursed by the company**, which shall recover the amount from the **directors in default**.

*(Full-marks tip: Compute the one-tenth threshold on paid-up voting capital and state both the 21-day "proceed" and 45-day "hold" limits, plus the 3-month requisitionists' meeting window; deductions arise from confusing the 21-day and 45-day periods or omitting the reimbursement/recovery from defaulting directors.)*

### Q67. Ch: Declaration & Payment of Dividend — Declaration of Dividend out of Reserves (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** The Board of Directors of Sunrise Textiles Ltd. proposes to declare a dividend for the financial year 2025–26 at the rate of 12% on its paid-up equity share capital, even though the company has incurred a loss during the year. The following particulars are available:

| Particular | Amount |
|---|---|
| Paid-up equity share capital | ₹ 40,00,000 |
| Free reserves (accumulated) | ₹ 30,00,000 |
| Net loss for the year 2025–26 | ₹ 2,10,000 |
| Rate of dividend declared in the three immediately preceding years | 15%, 12%, 9% (average = 12%) |

Examine, with reference to the Companies (Declaration and Payment of Dividend) Rules, 2014, whether the company can declare the proposed dividend of 12% out of accumulated free reserves, and compute the maximum amount that may be drawn from reserves.

**Solution:**

Where a company proposes to declare dividend out of accumulated profits (free reserves) in a year of inadequate/absent profits, the declaration is governed by the **second proviso to Section 123(1)** read with **Rule 3 of the Companies (Declaration and Payment of Dividend) Rules, 2014**, which lays down four conditions.

**WN-1 — Condition 1: Ceiling on the rate of dividend**
The rate of dividend declared shall **not exceed the average of the rates** at which dividend was declared in the three immediately preceding years.
Average rate = (15% + 12% + 9%) ÷ 3 = **12%**.
Proposed rate = 12%, which does **not exceed** 12%. **Condition satisfied.**

**WN-2 — Condition 2: Ceiling on the amount drawn from reserves**
The total amount drawn from accumulated profits shall **not exceed one-tenth (1/10) of the sum of paid-up share capital and free reserves** as per the latest audited financial statement.
Maximum drawal = 1/10 × (Paid-up capital + Free reserves) = 1/10 × (₹40,00,000 + ₹30,00,000) = 1/10 × ₹70,00,000 = **₹7,00,000**.

**WN-3 — Amount actually required for the proposed dividend**
Dividend at 12% on ₹40,00,000 = ₹4,80,000.
Since the year is a loss year, the entire ₹4,80,000 (plus the loss) must be met from reserves. As ₹4,80,000 is **less than** the ceiling of ₹7,00,000, **Condition 2 is satisfied.**

**WN-4 — Condition 3: Set-off of current year's loss first**
The amount so drawn shall **first be used to set off the losses incurred in the financial year** in which the dividend is declared, before any dividend on equity shares is declared.
Loss to be set off = ₹2,10,000; balance available for dividend = ₹7,00,000 − ₹2,10,000 = ₹4,90,000, which is sufficient to pay ₹4,80,000. **Condition satisfied.**

**WN-5 — Condition 4: Minimum residual reserve**
The balance of reserves after such withdrawal shall **not fall below 15% of the paid-up share capital** as per the latest audited financial statement.
Minimum reserve to be maintained = 15% × ₹40,00,000 = ₹6,00,000.
Reserve after drawal = ₹30,00,000 − (₹4,80,000 dividend + ₹2,10,000 loss) = ₹30,00,000 − ₹6,90,000 = **₹23,10,000**, which is well above ₹6,00,000. **Condition satisfied.**

**Statement Showing Permissibility of Dividend**

| Test | Limit | Actual | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rate of dividend | ≤ 12% (avg. of 3 yrs) | 12% | OK |
| Amount from reserves | ≤ ₹7,00,000 | ₹6,90,000 | OK |
| Current-year loss set off first | Required | Done | OK |
| Residual reserve | ≥ ₹6,00,000 | ₹23,10,000 | OK |

**Answer:** All four conditions of Rule 3 are satisfied. Sunrise Textiles Ltd. **can validly declare the 12% dividend** out of accumulated free reserves. The maximum permissible drawal from reserves is **₹7,00,000**, against which ₹6,90,000 (₹4,80,000 dividend + ₹2,10,000 loss) is drawn.

*(Full-marks tip: Examiners reward showing all four Rule 3 tests separately with the arithmetic; the most common deduction is forgetting to set off the current-year loss first, or using the 1/10 and 15% limits on the wrong base.)*

### Q68. Ch: Declaration & Payment of Dividend — Conditions and Sources for Declaration of Dividend (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** "Dividend can be declared only out of profits and paid only in cash." Discuss the sources out of which a company may declare dividend and the conditions attached, with reference to Section 123 of the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:**
Under **Section 123(1) of the Companies Act, 2013**, no dividend shall be declared or paid by a company for any financial year except out of the following three sources:

**(a) Out of the current year's profits** — the profits of the company for that year arrived at after providing for depreciation in accordance with **Schedule II**.

**(b) Out of the profits of previous financial years** remaining undistributed (accumulated profits/free reserves), again after providing for depreciation.

**(c) Out of money provided by the Central or a State Government** for payment of dividend in pursuance of a guarantee given by that Government.

**Mandatory conditions and provisos:**
1. **Transfer to reserves is voluntary** — the first proviso to Section 123(1) provides that a company **may**, before declaration, transfer such percentage of its profits to reserves as it considers appropriate. Such transfer is no longer compulsory.
2. **Declaration out of reserves in a lean year** — the second proviso permits declaration out of accumulated profits in a year of inadequate/no profits only in accordance with **Rule 3** (rate not exceeding the three-year average, drawal not exceeding 1/10 of capital + free reserves, current-year loss to be set off first, and residual reserve not below 15% of paid-up capital).
3. **Set-off of past losses and depreciation** — the third proviso mandates that no dividend shall be declared unless carried-over previous losses and unabsorbed depreciation are **set off against the current year's profit.**
4. **Free reserves only** — under Section 123(1) read with the definition in Section 2(43), dividend can be declared only out of **free reserves**; unrealised gains, notional gains, or revaluation reserves cannot be used.
5. **Deposit in a separate bank account** — under **Section 123(4)**, the amount of dividend, including interim dividend, must be **deposited in a scheduled bank in a separate account within 5 days** of declaration.
6. **Payment in cash** — under **Section 123(5)**, dividend must be paid in cash (electronic transfer permitted) to the registered shareholder or his order; **capitalisation of profits (bonus shares)** is a permitted exception, but dividend itself cannot be paid in kind.
7. A company that has **defaulted in repayment of deposits** (Section 73/74) shall not declare dividend so long as the default continues.

**Conclusion:** Dividend may be declared only out of current profits, accumulated free reserves (subject to Rule 3), or Government money, after providing for depreciation and setting off past losses, and must be paid in cash within the statutory timelines.

*(Full-marks tip: Cite Section 123(1) and each proviso by number; state that transfer to reserves is now optional — quoting the old compulsory-transfer rule is a common error.)*

### Q69. Ch: Declaration & Payment of Dividend — Unpaid Dividend Account and Transfer to IEPF (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the statutory provisions relating to the "Unpaid Dividend Account" and the "Investor Education and Protection Fund (IEPF)" under Sections 124 and 125 of the Companies Act, 2013. State the consequences of default.

**Answer:**
**Statutory basis — Section 124 (Unpaid Dividend Account):**
Where a dividend has been declared but has **not been paid or claimed within 30 days** from the date of declaration, the company shall, **within 7 days** from the expiry of the said 30 days (i.e., within 37 days of declaration), **transfer the total unpaid/unclaimed amount to a special account** called the **"Unpaid Dividend Account"** opened in a scheduled bank [Section 124(1)].

- **Statement of unclaimed amounts** — the company shall, within 90 days of such transfer, prepare a statement containing the names, last known addresses and unpaid dividend of the claimants and place it on its **website** [Section 124(2)].
- **Interest for default** — if the company defaults in transferring the amount, it shall pay **interest @ 12% p.a.** on the amount not transferred, which enures to the benefit of the members in proportion to their unclaimed amounts [Section 124(3)].
- **Right to claim** — any person may claim the unpaid dividend from the Unpaid Dividend Account [Section 124(4)].

**Transfer to IEPF — Section 124(5) and Section 125:**
- Any money transferred to the Unpaid Dividend Account that **remains unpaid or unclaimed for a period of 7 years** from the date of such transfer shall be transferred by the company to the **Investor Education and Protection Fund (IEPF)** established under Section 125, along with a statement of details [Section 124(5)].
- **Transfer of shares** — all shares in respect of which dividend has **not been paid or claimed for 7 consecutive years** shall also be **transferred to the IEPF** in the name of the Fund [Section 124(6)]. The shareholder does not lose ownership and may claim back both the shares and the dividend from the Fund.
- **Section 125** establishes the IEPF and credits to it, inter alia, unpaid dividends, matured deposits/debentures, application money due for refund, and interest thereon. The Fund is utilised for the **refund of unclaimed amounts to rightful owners** and for **promotion of investor education and protection.**

**Consequences of default [Section 124(7)]:**
If a company fails to comply with any requirement of Section 124, the **company** is punishable with a fine of **₹1,00,000, which may extend to ₹10,00,000**, and every **officer in default** is punishable with a fine of **₹25,000 extending to ₹1,00,000**.

**Conclusion:** Unpaid dividend must be parked in a dedicated bank account within 37 days, and both the unclaimed dividend and the underlying shares migrate to the IEPF after 7 years, with the rightful owner retaining a perpetual right to claim.

*(Full-marks tip: State the exact timelines — 30 days, 7 days, 90 days, 7 years — and the 12% interest; forgetting the transfer of shares under Section 124(6) is the most common omission.)*

### Q70. Ch: Declaration & Payment of Dividend — Time Limit for Payment; Punishment for Default (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** ABC Ltd. declared a final dividend at its Annual General Meeting held on 20th August 2025. Due to a dispute among the directors, the dividend was not paid to the shareholders until 5th October 2025. Advise the company on the time limit for payment of dividend and the consequences of the delay under Section 127 of the Companies Act, 2013. Also state the exceptions where no offence is committed.

**Answer:**
**Statutory basis — Section 127:** Once a dividend has been declared, it becomes a **debt due** from the company to the shareholders. The dividend must be **paid or the warrant dispatched within 30 days** from the date of declaration.

**Application to facts:** ABC Ltd. declared the dividend on 20th August 2025. The 30-day period expired on **19th September 2025.** Payment was made only on 5th October 2025 — a delay of 16 days beyond the statutory limit. This constitutes a default under Section 127.

**Consequences of default under Section 127:**
- Every **director**, if he is knowingly a party to the default, is punishable with **imprisonment up to 2 years and a fine of not less than ₹1,000 for every day** during which the default continues; and
- The **company** is liable to pay **simple interest @ 18% p.a.** during the period for which the default continues.

**Exceptions (proviso to Section 127) — no offence is committed where:**
(a) the dividend could not be paid by reason of the **operation of any law**;
(b) a shareholder has given **directions regarding payment** which could not be complied with, and this was communicated to him;
(c) there is a **dispute regarding the right to receive the dividend**;
(d) the dividend has been **lawfully adjusted** by the company against any sum due from the shareholder; or
(e) the failure to pay was **not due to any default on the part of the company.**

**Conclusion:** The internal dispute among directors is **not** a valid exception under the proviso. Hence ABC Ltd. and the defaulting directors are liable under Section 127 — 18% p.a. interest on the company and imprisonment/fine on the directors knowingly party to the default.

### Q71. Ch: Accounts of Companies — Books of Account and Place of Keeping (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the provisions of Section 128 of the Companies Act, 2013 regarding the maintenance of "books of account," the place where they may be kept, and the period for which they must be preserved. Can a company keep its books at a place other than its registered office?

**Answer:**
**Statutory basis — Section 128(1):** Every company shall prepare and keep at its **registered office** books of account and other relevant books and papers and financial statements for every financial year which give a **true and fair view** of the state of affairs of the company, including its branches, and explain the transactions effected. The books must be kept on an **accrual basis and according to the double-entry system** of accounting.

**Keeping books at a place other than the registered office [proviso to Section 128(1)]:**
Yes. The Board of Directors may decide to keep all or any of the books of account at **any other place in India.** Where such a decision is taken, the company must, **within 7 days** of the Board's decision, file with the **Registrar a notice in writing** giving the full address of that other place.

**Books in electronic form [Section 128(1) read with Rule 3 of the Companies (Accounts) Rules, 2014]:** The company may keep the books in electronic mode, provided they remain accessible in India, are retained in their original format, are complete and unaltered, and the details of the service provider (if kept on a server outside India) are intimated to the Registrar.

**Branch offices [Section 128(2)]:** Where a company has a branch (in or outside India), proper books of account relating to the branch transactions must be kept there, and **summarised returns** must be periodically sent to the registered office or the other place where books are kept.

**Right of inspection [Section 128(3)]:** The books and papers shall be **open to inspection by any director** during business hours; for a subsidiary's books, inspection is by persons authorised by a Board resolution.

**Period of preservation [Section 128(5)]:** The books of account, together with vouchers, must be kept in good order for a period of **not less than 8 financial years** immediately preceding the relevant financial year (or from incorporation, if shorter). Where an investigation has been ordered, the Central Government may direct preservation for a longer period.

**Persons responsible [Section 128(6)]:** The MD, whole-time director in charge of finance, the CFO, or any other person charged with compliance, on default, is punishable with a fine of **₹50,000 extending to ₹5,00,000.**

**Conclusion:** Books must be kept at the registered office on an accrual, double-entry basis, but may be kept elsewhere in India after filing notice with the Registrar within 7 days, and must be preserved for at least 8 years.

### Q72. Ch: Accounts of Companies — Financial Statements and Board's Report (Marks: 5) [Theory]

### Q74. Ch: Accounts of Companies — CSR Committee, Net Profit and Treatment of Unspent Amount (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the composition of the CSR Committee, the meaning of "net profit" for CSR, and the treatment of any amount that remains unspent under Section 135 of the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:**
**Statutory basis — Section 135:** Corporate Social Responsibility is a mandatory obligation for companies crossing the thresholds in Section 135(1) (net worth ₹500 cr / turnover ₹1,000 cr / net profit ₹5 cr in the immediately preceding financial year).

**CSR Committee [Section 135(1)]:** Such a company must constitute a **CSR Committee of the Board** consisting of **three or more directors, of whom at least one shall be an independent director.** Where a company is not required to appoint an independent director, the Committee shall have two or more directors. **Exemption:** where the amount to be spent does not exceed **₹50 lakh**, the requirement of constituting a CSR Committee is **not applicable**, and its functions may be discharged by the Board itself.

**Functions [Section 135(3)]:** The Committee formulates and recommends the **CSR Policy** indicating activities from **Schedule VII**, recommends the amount of expenditure, and monitors the policy from time to time.

**Meaning of "net profit":** For CSR, "net profit" means the net profit computed in accordance with **Section 198**, which broadly means the profit as per the financial statements, but **excludes** (a) any profit arising from overseas branches, and (b) any dividend received from other companies in India which are themselves covered by and complying with Section 135. It is computed **before tax**.

**Treatment of unspent amount [Section 135(5) and 135(6)]:**
- If a company **fails to spend** the required 2% amount, the **Board shall specify the reasons** for not spending in its report.
- **Ongoing project:** the unspent amount must be transferred within **30 days** of the end of the financial year to a special account called the **"Unspent Corporate Social Responsibility Account"**, and spent within **3 financial years**; failing which it is transferred to a **Schedule VII fund within 30 days** of the end of the third year.
- **Other than ongoing project:** the unspent amount must be transferred within **6 months** of the end of the financial year to a **fund specified in Schedule VII** (e.g., PM National Relief Fund).

**Penalty [Section 135(7)]:** For default, the **company** is liable to a penalty of **twice the unspent amount, or ₹1 crore, whichever is less**, and every officer in default to **one-tenth of the unspent amount, or ₹2 lakh, whichever is less.**

**Conclusion:** The CSR Committee (with an independent director) formulates the policy, the spend is based on Section 198 net profit, and unspent amounts must be routed to the Unspent CSR Account (ongoing projects) or a Schedule VII fund, failing which stiff penalties apply.

*(Full-marks tip: State the ₹50 lakh CSR-committee exemption and the 30-day/6-month split for unspent amounts; citing Section 198 for "net profit" earns the definition mark.)*

### Q75. Ch: Accounts of Companies — Filing of Financial Statements and Internal Audit (Marks: 4) [Theory]

- **Every listed company;**

- Every **private company** with turnover ≥ ₹200 crore, or outstanding loans/borrowings ≥ ₹100 crore.

**Conclusion:** XYZ Ltd. must file its adopted financial statements in Form AOC-4 by **25th October 2025**; failure attracts monetary penalties under Section 137(3), and, if it falls within the Rule 13 thresholds, it must additionally appoint an internal auditor under Section 138.

### Q76. Ch: Audit & Auditors — Appointment of First and Subsequent Auditors (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the provisions of Section 139 of the Companies Act, 2013 relating to the appointment of the first auditor and the subsequent auditor of a company (other than a Government company). What is the tenure of an auditor so appointed?

**Answer:**
**Statutory basis — Section 139:** The appointment of auditors is governed by Section 139 read with the Companies (Audit and Auditors) Rules, 2014.

**Appointment of first auditor [Section 139(6)]:** The **first auditor** of a company (other than a Government company) shall be appointed by the **Board of Directors within 30 days** of the date of **registration** of the company. If the Board fails to do so, it shall inform the members, who shall appoint the first auditor within **90 days at an extraordinary general meeting.** The first auditor holds office **until the conclusion of the first Annual General Meeting.**

**Appointment of subsequent auditor [Section 139(1)]:** Every company shall, at its **first AGM**, appoint an individual or a firm as auditor, who shall hold office from the conclusion of that meeting **until the conclusion of its sixth AGM** (i.e., for a tenure of **five consecutive years**) and thereafter till the conclusion of every sixth meeting. Key requirements:
- The appointment is subject to **ratification is no longer required** (the ratification proviso has been omitted); the auditor once appointed for 5 years continues for the full term.
- Before the appointment, the company must obtain **written consent** and a **certificate** from the auditor that the appointment is within the limits and eligibility of the Act [Section 139(1) proviso].
- The company must file **Form ADT-1** with the Registrar within **15 days** of the meeting in which the auditor is appointed [Rule 4(2)].

**Government companies [Section 139(5) & (7)]:** For a Government company (or a company owned/controlled by Government), the auditor is appointed by the **Comptroller and Auditor-General of India (C&AG)** — the first auditor within 60 days of registration, and the subsequent auditor within 180 days of the commencement of the financial year.

**Filling of casual vacancy [Section 139(8)]:** A casual vacancy is filled by the Board within 30 days; if it arises due to **resignation**, the appointment must also be **approved by the company at a general meeting** within 3 months of the Board's recommendation.

**Conclusion:** The first auditor is appointed by the Board within 30 days of registration and holds office up to the first AGM; the subsequent auditor is appointed by the members at the first AGM for a five-year term up to the sixth AGM, with Form ADT-1 filed within 15 days.

### Q77. Ch: Audit & Auditors — Rotation of Auditors and Cooling-off Period (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** Zenith Industries Ltd. is a listed company. Its auditor, M/s P & Co., a firm of Chartered Accountants, has been the company's statutory auditor continuously for the last ten years (two terms of five years each) as on the conclusion of the AGM held in 2025. The Board wishes to reappoint the same firm for a further term. Examine the validity of the proposal under Section 139(2) of the Companies Act, 2013 and state the cooling-off requirement.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Applicability of mandatory rotation [Section 139(2) read with Rule 5]**
Mandatory rotation of auditors applies to:
(a) every **listed company**; and
(b) prescribed classes of unlisted public / private companies (unlisted public with paid-up capital ≥ ₹10 crore; private with paid-up capital ≥ ₹50 crore; or any of the above with public borrowings from banks/FIs/public deposits ≥ ₹50 crore).
Zenith Industries Ltd. is a **listed company**, so Section 139(2) applies.

**WN-2 — Maximum permissible tenure [Section 139(2)]**
- An **individual auditor** may hold office for **one term of five consecutive years.**
- An **audit firm** may hold office for a maximum of **two terms of five consecutive years** (i.e., 10 years).

M/s P & Co. is a **firm** and has already completed **two terms of 5 years = 10 years** as on the 2025 AGM. It has therefore exhausted the maximum permissible tenure.

**WN-3 — Cooling-off period [first proviso to Section 139(2)]**
An audit firm which has completed its maximum term (two terms of 5 years) **shall not be re-appointed as auditor in the same company for 5 years** from the completion of such term. This is the mandatory **"cooling-off period."**

- As on the date of appointment, **no other audit firm having a common partner(s)** with the outgoing firm (whose partnership continues) can be appointed for the same 5-year cooling-off period.
- The company had a **transition period of 3 years** from the commencement of the Act to comply; this has long expired.

**Statement Showing Position under Section 139(2)**

| Test | Position |
|---|---|
| Type of auditor | Audit firm |
| Maximum tenure allowed | 2 terms × 5 yrs = 10 years |
| Tenure already served | 10 years (2 terms) |
| Re-appointment for a further term | **Not permitted** |
| Cooling-off before re-eligibility | **5 years** |

**Answer:** The proposal to re-appoint M/s P & Co. for a further term is **invalid.** Having completed two terms of five years (10 years), the firm must undergo a **cooling-off period of 5 years** before it can again be appointed as the auditor of Zenith Industries Ltd. Further, no firm sharing a common partner with M/s P & Co. can be appointed during this 5-year period.

### Q78. Ch: Audit & Auditors — Removal and Resignation of Auditor (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** The management of Meridian Ltd. is dissatisfied with its statutory auditor and wishes to remove him before the expiry of his term. Advise the company on the procedure for removal of an auditor before expiry of term, and also state the obligations of an auditor who resigns, under Section 140 of the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:**
**Statutory basis — Section 140:** Section 140 protects auditor independence by imposing a stringent procedure for premature removal and by casting disclosure obligations on a resigning auditor.

**Removal before expiry of term [Section 140(1) read with Rule 7]:** An auditor appointed under Section 139 may be removed from office **before the expiry of his term** only by following this procedure:
1. A **Board resolution** must be passed for the removal.
2. An **application to the Central Government** (powers delegated to the Regional Director) must be made in **Form ADT-2 within 30 days** of the Board resolution.
3. On receipt of the Central Government's **approval**, a **special resolution** of the company must be passed at a general meeting **within 60 days** of receiving such approval.
4. Before the auditor is removed, he must be given a **reasonable opportunity of being heard.**

Thus removal requires both **Central Government approval and a special resolution** — an ordinary resolution or a mere Board decision is insufficient.

**Auditor's right of representation [Section 140(4)]:** Where a retiring auditor is **not being re-appointed** and a special notice is received for appointing someone else (or providing that the retiring auditor shall not be re-appointed), the retiring auditor has the right to make a **written representation** and require it to be circulated to members; if not circulated in time, he may require it to be read out at the meeting.

**Obligations of a resigning auditor [Section 140(2) & (3)]:** An auditor who resigns from a company shall file a **statement in Form ADT-3 within 30 days** of resignation with the **company and the Registrar** (and, in the case of a Government company, also with the C&AG), indicating the **reasons and other relevant facts** of the resignation. Failure to file attracts a penalty of **₹50,000 or the auditor's remuneration, whichever is less**, and ₹500 per day of continuing default (max ₹5,00,000).

**Tribunal's power [Section 140(5)]:** Where the NCLT is satisfied that the auditor has acted in a **fraudulent manner** or colluded in a fraud, it may direct the company to change the auditor; the auditor so removed is **not eligible for appointment for 5 years** and is liable under Section 447.

**Conclusion:** Meridian Ltd. can remove the auditor before term only by obtaining prior Central Government approval in Form ADT-2 followed by a special resolution, after giving the auditor a hearing; a resigning auditor must file Form ADT-3 within 30 days.

*(Full-marks tip: Emphasise the twin requirement of Central Government approval + special resolution and the ADT-2 (30 days) / ADT-3 (30 days) forms; treating removal as a simple special resolution is the standard mistake.)*

### Q81. Ch: LLP Act 2008 — Incorporation and Nature of Limited Liability Partnership (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the salient features of a Limited Liability Partnership (LLP) and the procedure for its incorporation under the Limited Liability Partnership Act, 2008.

**Answer:**
**Definition — Section 2(1)(n) of the LLP Act, 2008:** A **Limited Liability Partnership (LLP)** is a partnership formed and registered under the Act. It is a **body corporate** formed and incorporated under the Act and is a **legal entity separate from its partners** [Section 3(1)].

**Salient features:**
1. **Body corporate / separate legal entity [Section 3]:** An LLP has a legal existence distinct from its partners; it can own property, sue and be sued in its own name.
2. **Perpetual succession [Section 3(2)]:** Any change in the partners does not affect the existence, rights or liabilities of the LLP; it continues until wound up.
3. **Limited liability [Section 26 & 27]:** The liability of a partner is **limited to his agreed contribution**; partners are not personally liable for the obligations of the LLP, except in case of their own wrongful act or fraud. The LLP's liabilities are met out of its property.
4. **Partner as agent of the LLP, not of other partners [Section 26]:** A partner is the agent of the LLP but not of the other partners — hence one partner is generally not liable for the independent/unauthorised acts of another.
5. **Minimum two partners [Section 6]:** Every LLP must have at least **two partners**; if the number falls below two for more than six months, the sole partner becomes personally liable.
6. **Designated partners [Section 7]:** There must be at least **two designated partners**, of whom **at least one must be resident in India**; they are responsible for statutory compliances.
7. **LLP Agreement [Section 23]:** The mutual rights and duties of the partners are governed by the LLP Agreement; in its absence, the provisions of **Schedule I** apply.
8. **No maximum limit on partners** and no requirement of minimum capital.

**Procedure for incorporation [Sections 11–12]:**
1. **Reserve name** — apply to the Registrar for reservation of the LLP name (RUN-LLP), ensuring it is not undesirable or identical to an existing name.
2. **Incorporation document [Section 11]** — two or more persons associated for carrying on a **lawful business with a view to profit** subscribe their names to the incorporation document and file it with the **Registrar** in the prescribed form (FiLLiP), stating the name, registered office, and details of partners and designated partners.
3. **Statement of compliance [Section 11(1)(c)]** — a statement by an advocate/CA/CS/CMA engaged in the formation and by a subscriber that the requirements of the Act have been complied with.
4. **Certificate of Incorporation [Section 12]** — on registration, the Registrar issues a **Certificate of Incorporation**, which is **conclusive evidence** that the LLP is incorporated. Thereafter the partners execute and file the LLP Agreement within 30 days.

**Conclusion:** An LLP is a separate legal entity with perpetual succession and limited liability, combining the flexibility of a partnership with the corporate advantage of limited liability, and comes into existence on the issue of the Certificate of Incorporation under Section 12.

### Q87. Ch: General Clauses Act, 1897 — Effect of Repeal (Marks: 8) [Theory]
**Question:** "Repeal of a statute does not obliterate everything done under it." Explain the effect of repeal of a Central Act with reference to Sections 6 and 6A of the General Clauses Act, 1897. Also distinguish between "repeal" and "amendment", and between simple repeal and repeal-and-re-enactment.

**Answer:**
The effect of repeal is governed principally by **Section 6** of the General Clauses Act, 1897, which embodies the rule that a repeal does not, unless a contrary intention appears, destroy the rights and liabilities that had already accrued under the repealed enactment. The section thus preserves the past operation of a repealed law.

**Section 6 — Effect of Repeal:** Where any Central Act or Regulation repeals any enactment, then, unless a different intention appears, the repeal shall **not**:
(a) revive anything not in force or existing at the time the repeal takes effect;
(b) affect the previous operation of any enactment so repealed or anything duly done or suffered thereunder;
(c) affect any right, privilege, obligation or liability acquired, accrued or incurred under the repealed enactment;
(d) affect any penalty, forfeiture or punishment incurred in respect of any offence committed against the repealed enactment; and
(e) affect any investigation, legal proceeding or remedy in respect of such right, privilege, obligation, liability, penalty, forfeiture or punishment.

Any such investigation, legal proceeding or remedy may be instituted, continued or enforced, and any such penalty may be imposed, as if the repealing Act had not been passed.

**Section 6A — Repeal of Act making textual amendment:** Where any Central Act repeals any enactment by which the text of any Central Act was amended by the express omission, insertion or substitution of any matter, then, unless a different intention appears, the repeal shall not affect the continuance of any such amendment made by the repealed enactment and in operation at the time of such repeal.

**Distinctions:**

| Basis | Repeal | Amendment |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Abrogation/obliteration of a law wholly or partly | Alteration/modification of the existing law |
| Existence | Repealed law ceases to exist (subject to Sec 6) | Law continues to exist in the altered form |
| Section | Governed by Sec 6/6A | Merely changes text; Sec 6A protects effect |

| Basis | Simple Repeal | Repeal & Re-enactment (Sec 24) |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Old law abrogated; nothing substituted | Old law replaced by a fresh enactment |
| Continuity | Sec 6 preserves accrued rights | Sec 24 keeps subordinate legislation (rules, notifications, orders) in force under the new Act |

**Conclusion:** By virtue of Section 6, repeal is prospective and does not wipe out rights accrued, liabilities incurred or proceedings pending under the repealed Act, unless a contrary intention appears; Section 6A saves textual amendments and Section 24 continues subordinate legislation on re-enactment.

### Q91. Ch: Interpretation of Statutes — Primary Rules (Literal, Golden, Mischief) (Marks: 8) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the three primary rules of interpretation of statutes — the Literal Rule, the Golden Rule and the Mischief Rule (Rule in Heydon's case). When is each rule applied, and what are their respective limitations?

**Answer:**
The primary duty of a court is to ascertain and give effect to the intention of the Legislature as expressed in the words of the statute. For this purpose the courts have evolved three principal rules of construction.

**1. The Literal (Grammatical) Rule:** This is the foremost rule. It requires that the words of a statute be given their ordinary, natural, plain and grammatical meaning. If the language of the statute is clear and unambiguous and admits of only one meaning, the court must give effect to that meaning irrespective of the consequences, for the words themselves best declare the intention of the law-giver. The court is not concerned with what the Legislature might have intended but with what it has actually said. **Limitation:** the rule cannot be applied where the words are ambiguous, or where a literal construction leads to absurdity, repugnancy or inconsistency with the rest of the statute.

**2. The Golden Rule:** The golden rule is a modification of the literal rule. It permits the court to depart from the ordinary grammatical meaning of the words to the extent necessary to avoid an absurdity, inconsistency, hardship or repugnance that a literal reading would produce. The court adopts the meaning that avoids the absurd result while remaining as close as possible to the ordinary sense of the words. **Limitation:** it is to be used sparingly and only where the literal meaning produces a manifest absurdity — not merely because the result is inconvenient or the court dislikes it.

**3. The Mischief Rule (Rule in Heydon's Case):** Where the words are capable of more than one meaning, the court applies the mischief rule laid down in Heydon's case, which requires four things to be considered: (i) what was the **common law** (the law) before the making of the Act; (ii) what was the **mischief and defect** for which the common law did not provide; (iii) what **remedy** the Parliament resolved and appointed to cure the mischief; and (iv) the **true reason of the remedy**. The court must then adopt that construction which shall **suppress the mischief and advance the remedy**. **Limitation:** it applies chiefly where there is ambiguity and the object is to further the purpose of the Act; it cannot be used to give the statute a meaning its language will not bear.

| Rule | Approach | When applied |
|---|---|---|
| Literal | Plain, ordinary meaning | Words clear and unambiguous |
| Golden | Modify to avoid absurdity | Literal meaning causes absurdity/repugnancy |
| Mischief | Suppress mischief, advance remedy | Words ambiguous; purpose-oriented |

**Conclusion:** The literal rule is the primary rule and prevails where the language is plain; the golden rule modifies it to avoid absurdity; and where ambiguity remains, the mischief rule (Heydon's case) is applied to give effect to the true legislative purpose.

### Q92. Ch: Interpretation of Statutes — Internal Aids to Interpretation (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** What are "internal aids" to the interpretation of a statute? Explain any five internal aids that a court may use to ascertain the meaning of a statutory provision.

**Answer:**
Internal aids to interpretation are those aids which are found **within the statute itself** — that is, in the body and structure of the enactment — and which the court may legitimately use to ascertain the meaning of a doubtful provision. They are distinguished from external aids, which lie outside the statute.

The principal internal aids are as follows:

**1. Long Title:** The long title, which sets out the general purpose of the Act, is a part of the Act and may be looked at to ascertain its general scope and object, though it cannot control clear enacting words.

**2. Preamble:** The preamble expresses the scope, object and purpose of the Act and the reasons that led to its enactment. Where the enacting words are ambiguous, the preamble is a key to open the mind of the makers of the Act; but where the words are clear, the preamble cannot cut them down.

**3. Marginal Notes / Headings:** Marginal notes and headings prefixed to sections or groups of sections may be referred to for construing an ambiguous provision, as they indicate the drift of the section, though ordinarily they are not given great weight and cannot override the plain words.

**4. Definition / Interpretation Clause:** A definition clause (e.g., "In this Act, unless the context otherwise requires…") assigns a fixed meaning to terms used in the Act. An inclusive definition ("includes") extends the ordinary meaning, while an exhaustive definition ("means") restricts it.

**5. Provisos, Explanations and Illustrations:** A **proviso** carves out an exception from the main enactment; an **Explanation** is added to clarify or explain the meaning of the section and does not enlarge or curtail its scope; and **illustrations** show how the section operates in practice, though they cannot modify the language of the section.

**Conclusion:** Internal aids such as the long title, preamble, marginal notes and headings, the definition clause, and provisos/explanations/illustrations assist the court in construing ambiguous provisions, but none of them can override the clear and plain words of the enacting part.

*(Full-marks tip: Distinguish "means" (exhaustive) from "includes" (extensive) and note that a proviso is an exception while an Explanation only clarifies — examiners specifically test these nuances.)*

### Q93. Ch: Interpretation of Statutes — External Aids to Interpretation (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain what is meant by "external aids" to interpretation and discuss any four external aids that the courts may use while interpreting a statute.

**Answer:**
External aids to interpretation are those aids which lie **outside the text of the statute** but which the court may nevertheless resort to in order to ascertain the true meaning and intention of the Legislature, particularly where the words of the statute are ambiguous. They supplement the internal aids.

**1. Historical Setting / Object and Reason:** The historical background, the surrounding circumstances and the state of the law existing at the time the statute was enacted may be looked into to understand the object which the Legislature had in view. The "Statement of Objects and Reasons" accompanying a Bill may be referred to for the limited purpose of ascertaining the conditions prevailing at the time and the purpose behind the legislation, though it cannot control the interpretation of clear words.

**2. Parliamentary History:** Debates in the Legislature, the report of the select/joint committee, and the speech of the mover of the Bill may, within limits, be used to understand the background and purpose of the enactment, although courts traditionally treat legislative debates with caution.

**3. Dictionaries:** Where a word is not defined in the Act, the court may refer to standard dictionaries to find the ordinary meaning of the word; however, the dictionary meaning must yield to the meaning that the context of the statute requires.

**4. Reference to Other Statutes / Statutes in pari materia:** Statutes dealing with the same subject-matter (statutes *in pari materia*) may be read together and used as an aid, since the Legislature is presumed to use words consistently across such statutes. Foreign decisions and text-books of established authority may also be referred to for guidance.

**Conclusion:** External aids — such as the historical setting and Statement of Objects and Reasons, parliamentary history, dictionaries, and reference to statutes in pari materia — help resolve ambiguity in a statute, but they cannot displace the plain and unambiguous language of the enactment itself.

### Q94. Ch: Interpretation of Statutes — Golden Rule Application (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** A penal provision in a Central Act states: "Whoever, being under a legal obligation, forges a document shall be punished." **Solution:**

The Legislature is presumed not to have intended an absurd or unjust consequence.

**WN-3 — Application to facts:** Applying the Golden Rule, the succession clause must be read as being subject to the implied principle that a person cannot be permitted to profit from his own wrong (nemo ex suo delicto meliorem suam conditionem facere potest). The court will therefore read down the general words so as to exclude a murderer from inheriting the property of the person he has murdered, because the Legislature could not have intended such an absurd and repugnant outcome.

**Answer:** The murderer **cannot inherit** the property. Applying the Golden Rule of interpretation, the general words of the succession clause are modified to avoid the absurd and unjust result, and are construed so as to exclude a person who has murdered the property-holder from inheriting his estate.

*(Full-marks tip: Name the Golden Rule expressly, explain that it is a departure "to the minimum extent necessary" to avoid absurdity, and invoke the maxim that no one may profit from his own wrong — a mere conclusion without naming the rule loses marks.)*

### Q95. Ch: FEMA, 1999 — Objectives, Extent & Regulatory Scheme (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** State the objectives and salient features of the Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999 (FEMA). How is the regulatory scheme under FEMA structured, and how does it differ in approach from the erstwhile FERA, 1973?

**Answer:**
The **Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999 (FEMA)** was enacted to consolidate and amend the law relating to foreign exchange, with the objective of **facilitating external trade and payments** and **promoting the orderly development and maintenance of the foreign exchange market** in India. It replaced the earlier Foreign Exchange Regulation Act, 1973 (FERA), and reflects a shift from a regime of strict control and regulation to one of management, in line with the liberalisation of the Indian economy.

**Extent and application (Section 1):** FEMA extends to the whole of India and also applies to all branches, offices and agencies outside India which are owned or controlled by a person resident in India, and to any contravention thereunder committed outside India by any person to whom the Act applies.

**Salient features / regulatory scheme:**
1. **Current vs Capital account transactions (Sections 5 & 6):** FEMA classifies all foreign exchange transactions into current account transactions (freely permitted, subject to reasonable restrictions the Central Government may impose in public interest) and capital account transactions (permissible only to the extent specified/regulated by the RBI).
2. **Dealings through Authorised Persons (Section 3):** Dealings in foreign exchange must be routed through an "authorised person" (authorised dealer, money changer, off-shore banking unit, etc.) authorised by the RBI.
3. **Rule/Regulation-making powers:** The Central Government makes **Rules** and the RBI makes **Regulations** governing the manner of transactions.
4. **Civil, not criminal, character:** Contraventions under FEMA are treated as civil wrongs attracting monetary penalties (adjudication under Sections 13–15), unlike FERA where they were criminal offences.

**Distinction — FEMA vs FERA:**

| Basis | FERA, 1973 | FEMA, 1999 |
|---|---|---|
| Object | Regulation/conservation of forex | Management & facilitation of forex |
| Approach | Everything prohibited unless permitted | Everything permitted unless prohibited |
| Nature of offence | Criminal offence, imprisonment | Civil contravention, monetary penalty |
| Presumption | Presumption of guilt | Presumption of innocence |

**Conclusion:** FEMA, 1999 is a facilitative, civil legislation that manages foreign exchange by classifying transactions into current and capital account and routing them through authorised persons under RBI/Central Government regulation — a marked liberalisation over the restrictive, penal FERA regime.

*(Full-marks tip: The FEMA-vs-FERA table (management vs regulation; civil vs criminal; permitted-unless-prohibited) is the examiner's favourite — always include it and cite Sections 1, 3, 5 and 6.)*

### Q96. Ch: FEMA, 1999 — Current Account vs Capital Account Transactions (Marks: 8) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between a "current account transaction" and a "capital account transaction" under the Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999. Explain the regulatory treatment of each under Sections 5 and 6, and give suitable examples of both.

**Answer:**
The classification of a foreign exchange transaction as a **current account transaction** or a **capital account transaction** is fundamental to the scheme of FEMA, 1999, because the two categories are regulated very differently.

**Current Account Transaction — Section 2(j):** A "current account transaction" means a transaction other than a capital account transaction and, without prejudice to the generality of the foregoing, it **includes** —
(i) payments due in connection with foreign trade, other current business, services, and short-term banking and credit facilities in the ordinary course of business;
(ii) payments due as interest on loans and as net income from investments;
(iii) remittances for living expenses of parents, spouse and children residing abroad; and
(iv) expenses in connection with foreign travel, education and medical care of parents, spouse and children.

**Capital Account Transaction — Section 2(e):** A "capital account transaction" means a transaction which **alters the assets or liabilities**, including contingent liabilities, outside India of persons resident in India, or the assets or liabilities in India of persons resident outside India. In short, it is a transaction that affects the capital structure — the assets/liabilities position — of the parties.

**Regulatory treatment:**

**Section 5 — Current Account Transactions:** Any person may sell or draw foreign exchange to or from an authorised person if such sale or drawal is a current account transaction. However, the Central Government may, in public interest and in consultation with the RBI, impose **reasonable restrictions** on such transactions. Thus current account transactions are, as a rule, **freely permitted**, subject to certain prohibited/restricted items specified in the Foreign Exchange Management (Current Account Transactions) Rules, 2000 (some transactions prohibited outright, some requiring Central Government approval, and some requiring RBI approval).

**Section 6 — Capital Account Transactions:** Any person may sell or draw foreign exchange to or from an authorised person for a capital account transaction only to the extent that such transaction is **permissible** under the regulations made by the RBI. The RBI specifies, in consultation with the Central Government, the classes of permissible capital account transactions and the limits up to which foreign exchange may be drawn. Capital account transactions are therefore **regulated/permitted only if specified**.

| Basis | Current Account Transaction [Sec 2(j)] | Capital Account Transaction [Sec 2(e)] |
|---|---|---|
| Effect | Does not alter assets/liabilities outside India | Alters assets or liabilities (incl. contingent) outside/in India |
| Governing section | Section 5 | Section 6 |
| General rule | Freely permitted (unless restricted) | Permitted only if specified by RBI |
| Examples | Payment for imports, foreign travel, education, medical expenses, interest | Investment in shares/property abroad, borrowing/lending in forex, acquisition of immovable property outside India |

**Conclusion:** A current account transaction (Section 2(j), regulated by Section 5) is one that does not alter foreign assets/liabilities and is freely permitted subject to reasonable restrictions, whereas a capital account transaction (Section 2(e), regulated by Section 6) alters foreign assets/liabilities and is permissible only to the extent specified by the RBI.

*(Full-marks tip: The one-line test "current = does not alter assets/liabilities; capital = alters assets/liabilities" plus the Sec 5 'free' vs Sec 6 'permitted only if specified' contrast is what secures full marks; give at least two examples of each.)*

### Q98. Ch: FEMA, 1999 — Residential Status (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** "Residential status is the foundation of FEMA." Explain who is a "person resident in India" under Section 2(v) of FEMA, 1999, and determine the residential status in the following cases:

| No. | Facts |
|---|---|
| (a) | Mr. A, an Indian citizen, left India on 1 August 2025 for employment in Dubai. Determine his status for FY 2025-26. |
| (b) | Mr. B, a foreign national, came to India on 1 May 2025 to take up employment. Determine his status for FY 2025-26. |
| (c) | Ms. C, a company incorporated in India, has its head office in Mumbai. |

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Definition (Section 2(v)):** Under Section 2(v) of FEMA, a "person resident in India" means —
(i) a person residing in India for **more than 182 days during the preceding financial year**, but does **not** include —
  (A) a person who has gone out of India, or who stays outside India, for or on taking up employment outside India, or for carrying on a business/vocation outside India, or for any other purpose indicating his intention to stay outside India for an uncertain period; and
  (B) a person who has come to or stays in India otherwise than for or on taking up employment, business/vocation, or for a purpose indicating his intention to stay in India for an uncertain period;
(ii) any person or body corporate **registered or incorporated in India**;
(iii) an office, branch or agency in India owned or controlled by a person resident outside India;
(iv) an office, branch or agency outside India owned or controlled by a person resident in India.

**Note:** Unlike the Income-tax Act, FEMA's test is based on the **preceding financial year** stay of more than 182 days, but is overridden by the **purpose/intention** of leaving or coming to India.

**WN-2 — Application to each case:**

| No. | Analysis | Status |
|---|---|---|
| (a) | Mr. A left India for employment in Dubai. Irrespective of his days of stay in the preceding year, on leaving for employment outside India he falls in exception (A) and becomes a **person resident OUTSIDE India** from the date of leaving (1 Aug 2025) | Resident outside India (non-resident) |
| (b) | Mr. B came to India for taking up employment. On taking up employment in India he satisfies the intention test and becomes a **person resident in India** from the date of arrival (1 May 2025), despite not having stayed 182 days in the preceding year | Resident in India |
| (c) | A company incorporated in India is a person resident in India under Sec 2(v)(ii), regardless of stay | Resident in India |

**Answer:** (a) Mr. A is a **person resident outside India** (having left for employment abroad); (b) Mr. B is a **person resident in India** (having come for employment); (c) Ms. C's company, being incorporated in India, is a **person resident in India** under Section 2(v)(ii).

*(Full-marks tip: Stress that FEMA looks at the PRECEDING financial year's 182-day stay BUT the "purpose/employment" test overrides the day-count — this distinction from the Income-tax residence test is the examiner's key checkpoint.)*

### Q99. Ch: FEMA, 1999 — Key Definitions (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Define the following terms under the Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999: (i) Authorised Person [Section 2(c)]; (ii) Foreign Exchange [Section 2(n)]; (iii) Foreign Security [Section 2(o)]; and (iv) Person [Section 2(u)]. State briefly the significance of the concept of "Authorised Person".

**Answer:**
The following are the key definitions under FEMA, 1999:

**(i) Authorised Person — Section 2(c):** "Authorised person" means an **authorised dealer, money changer, off-shore banking unit** or **any other person for the time being authorised under Section 10(1)** to deal in foreign exchange or foreign securities. It is through authorised persons that dealings in foreign exchange are channelised.

**(ii) Foreign Exchange — Section 2(n):** "Foreign exchange" means foreign currency and includes — (a) deposits, credits and balances payable in any foreign currency; (b) drafts, travellers' cheques, letters of credit or bills of exchange, expressed or drawn in Indian currency but payable in any foreign currency; and (c) drafts, travellers' cheques, letters of credit or bills of exchange drawn by banks, institutions or persons outside India, but payable in Indian currency.

**(iii) Foreign Security — Section 2(o):** "Foreign security" means any security, in the form of shares, stocks, bonds, debentures or any other instrument denominated or expressed in **foreign currency**, and includes securities expressed in foreign currency, but where redemption or any form of return such as interest or dividend is payable in Indian currency.

**(iv) Person — Section 2(u):** "Person" includes — (i) an individual; (ii) a Hindu Undivided Family; (iii) a company; (iv) a firm; (v) an association of persons or a body of individuals, whether incorporated or not; (vi) every artificial juridical person not falling within the above; and (vii) any agency, office or branch owned or controlled by such person.

**Significance of "Authorised Person":** Section 3 of FEMA prohibits any person from dealing in or transferring foreign exchange except through an authorised person. The concept therefore acts as the **gateway/channel** for all foreign exchange transactions, enabling the RBI to regulate and monitor forex dealings, and imposing on authorised persons the duty to comply with RBI directions under Section 10.

**Conclusion:** The definitions of authorised person, foreign exchange, foreign security and person under Sections 2(c), 2(n), 2(o) and 2(u) delimit the scope of FEMA, and the "authorised person" is central to the Act as the sole permitted channel for foreign exchange dealings.

### Q100. Ch: FEMA, 1999 — Contravention, Compounding & Penalties (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the provisions of FEMA, 1999 relating to (i) penalties for contravention [Section 13]; (ii) power to compound contraventions [Section 15]; and (iii) enforcement of orders of the Adjudicating Authority [Section 14]. State briefly the appellate mechanism under the Act.

**Answer:**
FEMA, 1999 treats contraventions as **civil wrongs** attracting monetary penalties, adjudicated by an Adjudicating Authority, with a mechanism for compounding and an appellate structure.

**(i) Penalty for contravention — Section 13:** If any person contravenes any provision of FEMA, or any rule, regulation, notification, direction or order issued under it, or contravenes any condition subject to which an authorisation was issued by the RBI, he shall, upon adjudication, be liable to a penalty **up to thrice the sum involved** in such contravention, where the amount is quantifiable, or **up to ₹2 lakh** where the amount is not quantifiable. Where the contravention is a **continuing one**, a further penalty which may extend to **₹5,000 for every day** after the first day during which the contravention continues may be imposed.

**(ii) Compounding of contravention — Section 15:** Any contravention under Section 13 may be **compounded** on an application by the person committing the contravention, within **180 days** from the date of receipt of the application, by the Director of Enforcement or such other officers of the Directorate of Enforcement and officers of the RBI as may be authorised. Once a contravention is compounded, **no further proceeding** shall be initiated or continued against that person in respect of the same contravention.

**(iii) Enforcement of the order — Section 14:** If any person fails to make full payment of the penalty imposed within **90 days** from the date on which the notice for payment is served, he shall be liable to civil imprisonment. The Adjudicating Authority may, after giving the defaulter an opportunity of being heard, issue a **certificate to the Enforcement/recovery authorities** and the person may be detained in civil prison (subject to the maximum periods and safeguards specified in the section).

**Appellate mechanism:**
- An appeal against the order of the Adjudicating Authority lies to the **Appellate Tribunal** (Section 19), to be filed generally within **45 days** from the date of receipt of the order.
- A further appeal on a **question of law** lies to the **High Court** (Section 35), to be filed within **60 days** (extendable by a further 60 days).

**Conclusion:** Under FEMA, contraventions attract a civil penalty up to thrice the amount involved (Sec 13), may be compounded within 180 days (Sec 15), and unpaid penalties are enforced through Section 14, with appeals lying to the Appellate Tribunal (Sec 19) and thereafter to the High Court (Sec 35).

*(Full-marks tip: Quote the exact quantum — "up to thrice the sum / ₹2 lakh / ₹5,000 per day" and the time-limits (180 days compounding, 90 days enforcement, 45/60 days appeal) — vague answers without figures are heavily penalised.)*
