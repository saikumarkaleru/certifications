### Q1. Ch: Preliminary & Definitions — Listed vs Unlisted Company (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the meaning of a "listed company" under the Companies Act, 2013. In light of the Companies (Specification of Definitions Details) Rules, 2014 (as amended), state which classes of public companies are NOT to be considered as listed companies even though their securities are listed on a recognised stock exchange.

**Answer:**
As per Section 2(52) of the Companies Act, 2013, a "listed company" means a company which has **any of its securities listed on any recognised stock exchange**. The Companies (Amendment) Act, 2020 inserted a proviso empowering the Central Government, in consultation with SEBI, to prescribe such class or classes of companies which shall not be considered as listed companies.

By virtue of **Rule 2A of the Companies (Specification of Definitions Details) Rules, 2014**, the following classes of companies shall **not be considered as listed companies**:
1. Public companies which have **not listed their equity shares** on a recognised stock exchange but have listed only (a) their **non-convertible debt securities** issued on a private placement basis under the SEBI (Issue and Listing of Non-Convertible Securities) Regulations; or (b) their **non-convertible redeemable preference shares** issued on a private placement basis; or (c) **both** categories at (a) and (b).
2. **Private companies** which have listed their non-convertible debt securities on a private placement basis on a recognised stock exchange.
3. Public companies which have **not listed their equity shares** on a recognised stock exchange but whose equity shares are listed on a stock exchange **in a jurisdiction as specified under Section 23(3)** (i.e. permitted foreign jurisdictions).

The rationale is that a company which has accessed public equity is subjected to the fuller listing obligations; a company that has merely placed debt or preference securities on a private-placement basis has not made a public equity offer and is therefore excluded from the rigours applicable to listed companies.

**Conclusion:** A company is "listed" under Section 2(52) if any of its securities are listed, but the public companies (and one class of private companies) enumerated in Rule 2A are carved out and are **not** treated as listed companies despite such listing.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards citing Section 2(52) AND Rule 2A together with the exact three carve-out classes; a common deduction is stating only the bare definition and omitting the private-placement debt/preference carve-outs.)*

### Q2. Ch: Preliminary & Definitions — Government Company (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** The paid-up share capital of MNP Ltd. is held as under. Examine whether MNP Ltd. is a "Government company" under Section 2(45) of the Companies Act, 2013. Would your answer change if, instead, the shares were held jointly by two State Governments (28% + 24%)?

| Shareholder | % of paid-up share capital |
|---|---|
| Central Government | 26% |
| A public sector bank (a Government company) | 20% |
| Life Insurance Corporation of India | 9% |
| Private public shareholders | 45% |

**Answer:**
**Statutory basis — Section 2(45):** A "Government company" means any company in which **not less than 51% of the paid-up share capital** is held by (a) the Central Government, or (b) any State Government or Governments, or (c) partly by the Central Government and partly by one or more State Governments, and **includes a company which is a subsidiary company of such a Government company**.

**Analysis of the given holding:**
- Central Government holding = 26%.
- The public sector bank is itself a Government company; however, for the purpose of the 51% test the shareholding of a **Government company is not equated with the holding of the Government itself** — Section 2(45) counts only shares held by the Government(s). The bank's 20% therefore does not count towards the 51% direct-holding test.
- LIC (9%) is a statutory corporation, not "the Government," so it does not count either.

Aggregate Government (Central) holding directly = **26%**, which is far below 51%. Hence MNP Ltd. is **NOT a Government company** on the direct-holding test. (It could only qualify as a Government company if it were a subsidiary of the PSU bank as per the inclusive limb, which is not established on these facts as the bank holds only 20%.)

**Modified situation — two State Governments holding 28% + 24%:** The combined holding of the two State Governments = 28% + 24% = **52%**, which is not less than 51%, held partly by State Governments jointly — squarely covered by limb (c) of Section 2(45).

**Conclusion:** On the original facts MNP Ltd. is **not** a Government company (Government holding only 26%). In the modified situation, with two State Governments jointly holding **52%**, MNP Ltd. **is** a Government company.

*(Full-marks tip: Examiners award marks for aggregating only Government holdings and expressly rejecting the PSU-bank/LIC shareholding from the 51% count; losing the mark usually comes from wrongly treating a Government company's holding as Government holding.)*

### Q3. Ch: Preliminary & Definitions — Foreign Company (Marks: 4) [Application]
**Question:** WorldNet Inc., a company incorporated in Singapore, has no place of business in India but maintains an online interactive website through servers located in India through which it accepts orders and payments from Indian customers, and it has appointed an Indian agent to conduct business on its behalf. Examine whether WorldNet Inc. is a "foreign company" and whether it must comply with the registration requirements of the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:**
**Statutory basis — Section 2(42):** A "foreign company" means any company or body corporate incorporated outside India which (a) has a place of business in India whether by itself or through an agent, physically or **through electronic mode**; and (b) conducts any business activity in India in any other manner.

**Rule 2(1)(c) of the Companies (Registration of Foreign Companies) Rules, 2014** clarifies that "electronic mode" means carrying out **electronically-based, whether main server is installed in India or not**, transactions — including business-to-business and business-to-consumer transactions, data interchange and other digital supply transactions, offering to accept deposits or subscriptions, and all related data communication services, conducted by e-mail, mobile devices, social media, cloud computing, etc.

**Application:** WorldNet Inc. is incorporated outside India (Singapore). Although it has no physical office, it (i) operates an interactive website accepting orders and payments from Indian customers — this is "electronic mode," and (ii) has appointed an Indian agent conducting business on its behalf. Both limbs of Section 2(42) are satisfied. The absence of a physical office is irrelevant because a place of business through **electronic mode** and **through an agent** both qualify.

Consequently, WorldNet Inc. is required to comply with **Section 380** (delivery of documents to the Registrar within 30 days of establishing a place of business) and Section 381 (accounts) of the Act.

**Conclusion:** WorldNet Inc. is a **foreign company** under Section 2(42) read with the electronic-mode rule and must fulfil the registration and filing obligations under Sections 380–381.

*(Full-marks tip: Cite the "electronic mode" definition and note that the server's location and the absence of a physical office do not exempt the company; a common deduction is concluding "not a foreign company" merely because there is no physical premises.)*

### Q4. Ch: Preliminary & Definitions — Holding & Subsidiary Company (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Examine, with reasons, the holding-subsidiary relationships in the following independent situations under Section 2(46), Section 2(87) and the relevant rules:
| Situation | Facts |
|---|---|
| (i) | H Ltd. holds 40% of the total voting power of S Ltd. and controls the composition of S Ltd.'s Board of Directors. |
| (ii) | H Ltd. holds 55% of the total voting power of A Ltd.; A Ltd. in turn holds 60% of the voting power of B Ltd. Is B Ltd. a subsidiary of H Ltd.? |
| (iii) | H Ltd. wishes to have 6 layers of subsidiaries. |

**Answer:**
**Statutory basis:** Under **Section 2(87)**, a "subsidiary company" in relation to another company (the holding company) means a company in which the holding company (i) **controls the composition of the Board of Directors**; or (ii) exercises or controls **more than one-half of the total voting power** either at its own or together with one or more of its subsidiary companies. Section 2(46) defines a "holding company" correspondingly, and the Explanation clarifies that a company shall be deemed to be a subsidiary of the holding company even if the control is of a subsidiary of the holding company.

**Situation (i):** Although H Ltd. holds only 40% of the voting power (which is not "more than one-half"), it **controls the composition of the Board** of S Ltd. Control of Board composition is an **independent, alternative** test under Section 2(87)(i). Hence **S Ltd. is a subsidiary of H Ltd.** even though the voting power is below 50%.

**Situation (ii):** H Ltd. holds 55% (more than one-half) of A Ltd. → A Ltd. is a subsidiary of H Ltd. A Ltd. holds 60% of B Ltd. → B Ltd. is a subsidiary of A Ltd. By the **Explanation** to Section 2(87), a company is a subsidiary of the holding company even if controlled through a subsidiary. Therefore **B Ltd. is also a subsidiary of H Ltd.** (a step-down subsidiary).

**Situation (iii):** Proviso to Section 2(87) read with **Rule 2 of the Companies (Restriction on number of Layers) Rules, 2017** restricts a company from having **more than two layers** of subsidiaries (subject to specified exceptions and exclusion of one wholly-owned subsidiary layer). Six layers are **not permitted**.

**Conclusion:** (i) S Ltd. is a subsidiary (Board-control test); (ii) B Ltd. is a subsidiary of H Ltd. through A Ltd.; (iii) H Ltd. cannot maintain 6 layers — the limit is 2 layers.

*(Full-marks tip: Marks are earned by treating Board-control and voting-power as independent alternative tests and by citing the layers restriction rule; do not require both tests to be satisfied together.)*

### Q5. Ch: Preliminary & Definitions — Small Company & Body Corporate (Marks: 4) [Application]
**Question:** ABC Pvt. Ltd. has a paid-up share capital of ₹3.5 crore and a turnover of ₹38 crore in the immediately preceding financial year. It is not a holding or subsidiary company and is not a Section 8 company. Examine whether ABC Pvt. Ltd. is a "small company" under Section 2(85). Also state whether a co-operative society and a company incorporated outside India are "bodies corporate."

**Answer:**
**Statutory basis — Section 2(85):** A "small company" means a company, other than a public company, (i) whose **paid-up share capital does not exceed ₹4 crore** (or such higher amount not exceeding ₹10 crore as prescribed) — the prescribed limit under Rule 2(1)(t) of the Companies (Specification of Definitions Details) Rules is **₹4 crore**; and (ii) whose **turnover does not exceed ₹40 crore** (prescribed limit) as per the profit and loss account for the immediately preceding financial year. A holding company, subsidiary company, Section 8 company, or a company governed by a special Act are excluded.

**Application:** ABC Pvt. Ltd. is a private company; its paid-up capital ₹3.5 crore ≤ ₹4 crore, and its turnover ₹38 crore ≤ ₹40 crore. It is neither a holding/subsidiary nor a Section 8 company. **Both** conditions are satisfied. Hence ABC Pvt. Ltd. **is a small company**.

**Body corporate — Section 2(11):** "Body corporate" includes a company incorporated **outside India**, but **excludes** (a) a co-operative society registered under any law relating to co-operative societies, and (b) any other body corporate notified by the Central Government which is not a company. Therefore, a company incorporated outside India **is** a body corporate, whereas a **co-operative society is NOT** a body corporate for the purposes of the Act.

**Conclusion:** ABC Pvt. Ltd. is a **small company**; a foreign-incorporated company is a body corporate but a co-operative society is not.

*(Full-marks tip: Both the capital AND turnover limits must be satisfied — state the current ₹4 crore/₹40 crore thresholds; a deduction results from quoting outdated ₹2 crore/₹20 crore limits.)*

### Q6. Ch: Incorporation of Company — Effect of Registration (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** "On registration, a company becomes a body corporate distinct from its members." Explain the legal effects of the registration and incorporation of a company under Section 9 of the Companies Act, 2013, and the conclusiveness of the Certificate of Incorporation under Section 7(2).

**Answer:**
**Statutory basis — Section 9:** From the date of incorporation mentioned in the Certificate of Incorporation, the subscribers to the memorandum and all other persons who become members of the company shall be a **body corporate** by the name contained in the memorandum, **capable forthwith of exercising all the functions of an incorporated company** under the Act, and having **perpetual succession** with power to acquire, hold and dispose of property (both movable and immovable, tangible and intangible), to contract, and to sue and be sued in its own name.

The legal effects of registration are:
1. **Separate legal entity:** The company becomes a person in law distinct from its members and directors (the principle in *Salomon v. Salomon & Co. Ltd.*). Its property is its own, not that of the members.
2. **Perpetual succession:** The company continues to exist irrespective of death, insolvency or exit of members; members may come and go but the company continues until wound up.
3. **Capacity to contract, sue and be sued** in its own name.
4. **Limited liability** where the company is limited by shares or guarantee — members' liability is limited to the unpaid amount on shares or the guarantee.
5. **Common seal (optional)** — the company may have a common seal to authenticate documents.

**Conclusiveness — Section 7(2):** On and from the date of registration, the Registrar issues a **Certificate of Incorporation** stating that the company is incorporated and, in the case of a limited company, that it is limited. The certificate is conclusive evidence that the company is duly registered; the company is allotted a **Corporate Identity Number (CIN)** which is a distinct identity.

**Conclusion:** Registration under Section 9, evidenced by the Certificate of Incorporation under Section 7(2), brings into existence a distinct body corporate with perpetual succession, capacity to own property and contract, and (where applicable) limited liability.

*(Full-marks tip: Anchor the answer to *Salomon's* case and enumerate all five consequences; merely stating "separate legal entity" without perpetual succession, limited liability and the Section 7(2) certificate loses marks.)*

### Q7. Ch: Incorporation of Company — Alteration of Object Clause (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** TechGrow Ltd., which raised money from the public through a prospectus, has an unutilised amount of ₹5 crore out of the ₹20 crore raised. The company now wishes to alter the object clause of its memorandum to divert this money to a new line of business. Explain the procedure and the additional safeguards prescribed under Section 13(8) and the relevant rules for a company that has raised money from the public and has an unutilised amount.

**Answer:**
**Statutory basis — Section 13(1) & 13(9):** A company may, by a **special resolution**, alter the provisions of its memorandum, including the objects clause. The alteration of the memorandum takes effect only after it is **registered by the Registrar**, who certifies the registration within the prescribed time.

**Additional safeguards for money raised from public — Section 13(8) read with Rule 32 of the Companies (Incorporation) Rules, 2014:** Where a company has **raised money from the public through a prospectus and still has any unutilised amount** out of the money so raised, it **shall not change its objects** for which it raised the money unless:
1. A **special resolution** is passed by the company; **and**
2. The details prescribed under Rule 32 are **published in the newspapers** (one in English and one in vernacular language of the place of the registered office) and are placed on the company's website (if any), indicating the justification for the change; **and**
3. The **dissenting shareholders** (those who did not agree to the change) are given an **exit opportunity** by the promoters and controlling shareholders in accordance with the **SEBI regulations** (SEBI (ICDR) Regulations).

Additionally, the special resolution must be passed through **postal ballot** where applicable, and the notice must contain the prescribed particulars (total money received/utilised, the unutilised amount, the proposed objects, justification, estimated financial impact, place from which the money is proposed to be raised, and the risk factors).

**Application to TechGrow Ltd.:** Since TechGrow Ltd. raised ₹20 crore through a prospectus and ₹5 crore remains **unutilised**, Section 13(8) is attracted. It must pass a special resolution, make the prescribed newspaper/website publication with justification, and provide an exit offer to dissenting shareholders per SEBI regulations, and then file the resolution with the Registrar for registration; the alteration is effective only on the Registrar's registration.

**Conclusion:** TechGrow Ltd. can alter its object clause only by following the enhanced procedure of Section 13(8) with special resolution, prescribed publication, and an exit offer to dissenting shareholders, followed by registration by the Registrar.

*(Full-marks tip: The distinguishing marks lie in identifying the unutilised-money trigger and citing the newspaper publication + dissenting-shareholder exit route; simply saying "pass a special resolution" earns only partial credit.)*

### Q8. Ch: Incorporation of Company — Change of Registered Office from One State to Another (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Sunrise Ltd., whose registered office is in Maharashtra, wants to shift its registered office to Gujarat to reduce operational costs. A group of creditors and some employees object, alleging prejudice to their interests. Explain the procedure under Sections 12, 13(4), 13(7) and the powers of the Central Government (Regional Director) for shifting the registered office from one State to another.

**Answer:**
**Statutory basis:** The situation of the registered office falls in the **situation clause** of the memorandum. Under **Section 13(4)**, the alteration of the memorandum relating to the place of the registered office **from one State to another** shall not take effect unless it is **approved by the Central Government** on an application in the prescribed form. Under **Section 13(7)**, a certified copy of the Central Government's order approving the alteration must be filed with the Registrar of **each of the States** (the State from which and the State to which the office is shifted), and each Registrar shall register the same; the Registrar of the State to which the office is shifted issues a **fresh certificate of incorporation** indicating the alteration.

**Procedure:**
1. Pass a **special resolution** authorising the shift (Section 13(1)).
2. File an application to the **Central Government** (power delegated to the **Regional Director**) in the prescribed form (INC-23), together with the special resolution and prescribed documents.
3. **Rule 30 of the Companies (Incorporation) Rules, 2014** requires that the application be **advertised** in newspapers (English and vernacular) and served individually on the **Registrar, the SEBI (for listed companies)**, the **chief secretary of the State**, and every **creditor and debenture-holder** of the company, so that any person whose interest is likely to be affected may object.
4. The Regional Director must ensure that the shifting is **not prejudicial to the interests of creditors** — where objections are received, the confirmation may be made subject to terms (e.g. securing the creditors' dues), and consent of, or discharge/security to, the objecting creditors may be required.
5. The Regional Director passes the order within the prescribed time; the order is filed with both Registrars under Section 13(7), and a fresh Certificate of Incorporation is issued.

**Application to Sunrise Ltd.:** Sunrise Ltd. must pass a special resolution, apply to the Regional Director with prescribed advertisement and notice to the objecting creditors and employees, and satisfy the Regional Director that their interests are protected (e.g. by securing the creditors' claims). Objection by creditors does not by itself bar the shift, but the RD may impose conditions to safeguard them.

**Conclusion:** Sunrise Ltd. can shift its registered office from Maharashtra to Gujarat by special resolution and Central Government (Regional Director) approval under Section 13(4)/13(7), subject to protection of the objecting creditors' and other stakeholders' interests.

*(Full-marks tip: Highlight the Regional Director's role, the mandatory notice to creditors/RoC/State, and registration in both States with a fresh COI; omitting the "interest of creditors" safeguard is the usual deduction.)*

### Q9. Ch: Incorporation of Company — Conversion of Private into Public Company (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Comfort Pvt. Ltd. has 190 members and wishes to convert itself into a public company to widen its capital base. Explain the meaning and effect of such conversion and the procedure under Section 14 of the Companies Act, 2013 for altering the articles to convert a private company into a public company.

**Answer:**
**Statutory basis — Section 14(1):** A company may, by a **special resolution**, alter its articles including alterations having the effect of **conversion of a private company into a public company**. On such alteration, the company ceases to be a private company: the three restrictions defining a private company under Section 2(68) (restriction on transfer of shares, limit on the number of members, and prohibition on inviting the public to subscribe) shall **no longer apply**, and the articles must be altered to delete these restrictive provisions.

**Effect of conversion:**
- The word "**Private**" must be removed from the name; the company must henceforth use the name ending with "**Limited**" (Section 4(1)(a)).
- A public company must have a **minimum of 7 members** (Section 3) and a **minimum of 3 directors** (Section 149) — Comfort Pvt. Ltd. with 190 members already satisfies the members requirement; it must ensure it has at least 3 directors.
- It becomes subject to provisions applicable to public companies (e.g. relating to acceptance of deposits, board composition, etc.).

**Procedure (Section 14 read with Rule 33 of the Companies (Incorporation) Rules, 2014):**
1. Convene a Board meeting and issue notice for a general meeting.
2. Pass a **special resolution** altering the articles for conversion.
3. File the altered articles and the special resolution with the Registrar in the prescribed form (**INC-27**) within **15 days** of the resolution.
4. **Section 14(2):** Every alteration of the articles and a copy of the order (where approval is required) must be filed with the Registrar, who shall **register the same**.
5. **Section 14(3):** Any alteration of the articles registered under Section 14(2) shall, subject to the Act, be **valid as if originally in the articles**.

**Application:** Comfort Pvt. Ltd. must pass a special resolution altering its articles to delete the private-company restrictions, change its name to end with "Limited," ensure at least 3 directors, and file INC-27 with the Registrar within 15 days; on registration the conversion is effective.

**Conclusion:** By passing a special resolution and filing the altered articles under Section 14, Comfort Pvt. Ltd. can validly convert into a public company, whereupon the private-company restrictions cease to apply.

*(Full-marks tip: State both the removal of the three Section 2(68) restrictions AND the name change to "Limited" with the INC-27 filing; leaving out the name change or the filing timeline costs marks.)*

### Q10. Ch: Incorporation of Company — Commencement of Business & Registered Office (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** NewVenture Ltd. was incorporated on 1st April 2026 with a share capital. As on 30th September 2026, the subscribers have not paid the value of the shares agreed to be taken by them, and the company has not filed the declaration of commencement of business. Examine the consequences under Section 10A and the requirements regarding maintenance of a registered office under Section 12.

**Answer:**
**Statutory basis — Section 10A:** A company incorporated after the commencement of the Companies (Amendment) Act, 2019 and **having a share capital** shall **not commence any business or exercise any borrowing powers** unless:
(a) a **declaration** is filed by a director within **180 days** of the date of incorporation, in the prescribed form (INC-20A), verified in the prescribed manner, stating that **every subscriber to the memorandum has paid the value of the shares agreed to be taken** by him on the date of making the declaration; and
(b) the company has filed with the Registrar a **verification of its registered office** as provided in Section 12(2).

**Consequences of default (Section 10A(2) & (3)):**
- The **company** is liable to a penalty of **₹50,000**, and every **officer in default** is liable to a penalty of **₹1,000 per day** of default subject to a maximum of **₹1 lakh**.
- Where no declaration is filed within 180 days and the Registrar has reasonable cause to believe that the company is not carrying on business, the Registrar may **initiate action for removal of the company's name** from the register of companies (strike-off).

**Application to NewVenture Ltd.:** Incorporated on 1 April 2026, the 180-day period expires around 28 September 2026. As on 30 September 2026 the subscribers have not paid for their shares and no INC-20A declaration has been filed. Therefore NewVenture Ltd. **cannot commence business or exercise borrowing powers**, and it (and its officers in default) are liable to the penalties above, with exposure to strike-off action.

**Registered office — Section 12:** A company must have a registered office **within 30 days of incorporation** capable of receiving communications; it must furnish verification of the registered office to the Registrar (INC-22) within 30 days, paint/affix its name and address, get its name engraved on its seal (if any), and print its name, CIN, address, telephone, e-mail on business letters, bills, etc.

**Conclusion:** NewVenture Ltd. is in default of Section 10A, cannot commence business/borrow, and is exposed to penalties and possible strike-off; it must also comply with the registered-office requirements of Section 12.

*(Full-marks tip: State the 180-day limit, the exact penalty figures (₹50,000 / ₹1,000 per day up to ₹1 lakh) and the strike-off consequence; vague "penalty may be levied" answers lose marks.)*

### Q11. Ch: Incorporation of Company — Doctrine of Ultra Vires / Object Clause (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** The object clause of Zenith Ltd. authorises it to manufacture and sell textiles. The directors, without altering the memorandum, entered into a contract to finance and operate a chain of restaurants, which resulted in a loss. A shareholder challenges the transaction. Examine the validity of the transaction and the liabilities involved, applying the doctrine of ultra vires.

**Answer:**
**Statutory basis:** The **object clause** of the memorandum under Section 4(1)(c) defines the objects for which the company is proposed to be incorporated and matters considered necessary in furtherance thereof. A company can act **only within the powers conferred** by its memorandum; any act beyond those objects is **ultra vires** the company and is **void ab initio**.

**Doctrine of ultra vires:** The leading authority is *Ashbury Railway Carriage & Iron Co. Ltd. v. Riche*, where a contract outside the objects clause was held void and **incapable of ratification even by the unanimous consent of all shareholders**. The consequences of an ultra vires act are:
1. The transaction is **void** and does not bind the company; neither the company nor the other party can enforce it.
2. It **cannot be ratified** by the shareholders even by a special resolution — ratification would be validating something the company had no capacity to do.
3. Any shareholder may obtain an **injunction** to restrain the company from committing an ultra vires act.
4. The **directors are personally liable** to the company for any loss caused by applying the company's funds to an ultra vires purpose, as they exceed their authority and commit a breach of trust.

**Application to Zenith Ltd.:** Financing and operating restaurants is wholly outside the textile-manufacturing objects and no alteration of the memorandum was made. The restaurant contract is therefore **ultra vires and void**, cannot be enforced by or against the company, and cannot be ratified by the shareholders. The **directors are personally liable** to make good the loss suffered by Zenith Ltd. The challenging shareholder was entitled to seek an injunction to restrain the act.

**Conclusion:** The transaction is ultra vires and void; the directors are personally liable to compensate Zenith Ltd. for the loss, and the shareholder's challenge is valid.

*(Full-marks tip: Cite *Ashbury v. Riche* and stress the "cannot be ratified even by unanimous consent" point plus directors' personal liability; treating an ultra vires act as merely voidable/ratifiable is the classic error.)*

### Q12. Ch: Prospectus & Allotment — Deemed Prospectus / Offer for Sale (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Bluechip Ltd. allotted its entire fresh issue of 10,00,000 shares to an issue house, Capital Securities Pvt. Ltd., which within a fortnight offered these shares to the public through a document. No prospectus was issued by Bluechip Ltd. Examine, under Section 25 of the Companies Act, 2013, whether the document issued by Capital Securities is a prospectus and the consequences, given that shares were offered within a short time and the whole consideration had not been received by Bluechip Ltd.

**Answer:**
**Statutory basis — Section 25(1):** Where a company **allots or agrees to allot any securities** with a view to those securities being **offered for sale to the public**, any document by which the **offer for sale to the public** is made shall, for all purposes, be **deemed to be a prospectus** issued by the company; and all enactments and rules of law as to the contents of a prospectus and as to liability in respect of mis-statements in and omissions from a prospectus, or otherwise relating to prospectuses, shall apply as if the securities had been directly offered to the public by the company.

**Presumption under Section 25(2):** It shall be **evidence that an allotment of, or an agreement to allot, securities was made with a view to the securities being offered for sale to the public** if it is shown:
(a) that an **offer of the securities (or of any of them) for sale to the public was made within six months** after the allotment or agreement to allot; **or**
(b) that at the date when the offer was made, the **whole consideration** to be received by the company in respect of the securities had **not been received** by it.

**Additional requirements — Section 25(3) & (4):** The deemed prospectus must state (i) the **net amount of consideration** received or to be received by the company in respect of the securities to which the offer relates, and (ii) the **place and time** at which the contract under which the securities were allotted may be inspected. Where the offeror is a company or firm, it is sufficient if the document is signed on behalf of the company by **two directors** or by not less than one-half of the partners of the firm, respectively.

**Application to Bluechip Ltd.:** Bluechip Ltd. allotted the whole issue to Capital Securities, which offered them to the public within a fortnight — well within six months — and the whole consideration had **not** been received by Bluechip Ltd. **Both** presumptive conditions of Section 25(2) are satisfied. Hence the offer document of Capital Securities is a **deemed prospectus** issued by Bluechip Ltd., and Bluechip Ltd. (together with the issue house) is subject to the prospectus content requirements and liability for mis-statements as if it had directly offered the shares to the public.

**Conclusion:** The document is a **deemed prospectus (offer for sale)** under Section 25; Bluechip Ltd. cannot escape prospectus liability merely by routing the issue through an issue house.

*(Full-marks tip: State both presumptive conditions of Section 25(2) (six-month offer AND consideration not fully received) and the extra disclosures under Section 25(3); mentioning only "deemed prospectus" without the two conditions costs marks.)*

### Q13. Ch: Prospectus & Allotment — Variation of Terms of Contract / Objects (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Pioneer Ltd. raised ₹40 crore through a prospectus stating that the funds would be used for setting up a manufacturing plant. Six months later, the Board wishes to vary the terms of the contract referred to in the prospectus and to use part of the funds for a different purpose. Examine the requirements under Section 27 of the Companies Act, 2013 for variation in terms of contract or objects in the prospectus.

**Answer:**

Further, the details of the notice in respect of such resolution to shareholders shall also be **published in the newspapers** (one in English and one in vernacular language of the place where the registered office is situated) indicating clearly the **justification for such variation**.

**Exit offer to dissenting shareholders — Section 27(2):** The **dissenting shareholders** — being those shareholders who have not agreed to the proposal to vary the terms of contracts or objects referred to in the prospectus — shall be given an **exit offer** by the **promoters or controlling shareholders** at such exit price, and in such manner and conditions, as may be **specified by SEBI** by making regulations in this behalf.

**Application to Pioneer Ltd.:** Pioneer Ltd. proposes to vary the object/utilisation stated in its prospectus. It must therefore:
1. Pass a **special resolution** in general meeting approving the variation.
2. **Publish** the notice of the resolution in an English and a vernacular newspaper with the justification for the variation.
3. Provide an **exit offer** to dissenting shareholders through its promoters/controlling shareholders at the SEBI-specified price.

Without complying with Section 27, Pioneer Ltd. cannot lawfully divert the funds to a purpose different from that stated in the prospectus.

**Conclusion:** Variation of the terms/objects in the prospectus is permissible only by special resolution with the prescribed newspaper publication and an exit offer to dissenting shareholders under Section 27.

### Q14. Ch: Prospectus & Allotment — Underwriting Commission (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Growth Ltd. made a public issue of equity shares of face value ₹10 each at par. The issue was underwritten. The company proposes to pay underwriting commission. Compute the maximum underwriting commission payable and examine the conditions under Section 40(6) and Rule 13 of the Companies (Prospectus and Allotment of Securities) Rules, 2014, given the following data. Also state the position for a debenture issue.

| Particulars | Amount |
|---|---|
| Number of equity shares offered | 50,00,000 shares |
| Issue price per share | ₹10 (at par) |
| Rate of underwriting commission authorised by the articles | 6% (on shares) |

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Statutory ceiling on underwriting commission (Rule 13):**
Under **Section 40(6)** read with **Rule 13 of the Companies (Prospectus and Allotment of Securities) Rules, 2014**, a company may pay underwriting commission subject to the following conditions:
- The payment of commission is **authorised by the articles**.
- The commission paid **shall not exceed**:
  - in the case of **shares** — **5%** of the price at which the shares are issued, **or the rate authorised by the articles, whichever is less**; and
  - in the case of **debentures** — **2.5%** of the price at which the debentures are issued, **or the rate authorised by the articles, whichever is less**.
- The rate/amount of commission must be **disclosed in the prospectus**.
- The commission is paid only in respect of shares/debentures which are **actually subscribed or underwritten**, and a copy of the underwriting contract is delivered to the Registrar.

**WN-2 — Applicable rate for Growth Ltd.:**
Articles authorise 6%, but the statutory ceiling for **shares is 5%**. The permissible rate is the **lower** of the two = **5%**.

**WN-3 — Total issue value:**
Total issue value = 50,00,000 shares × ₹10 = **₹5,00,00,000 (₹5 crore).**

**Computation of Maximum Underwriting Commission:**

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Total value of shares issued (50,00,000 × ₹10) | 5,00,00,000 |
| Maximum permissible rate (lower of 5% statutory or 6% articles) | 5% |
| **Maximum underwriting commission payable = ₹5,00,00,000 × 5%** | **25,00,000** |

Formula → substitute → result: Commission = Issue value × permissible rate = ₹5,00,00,000 × 5% = **₹25,00,000**.

**Position for a debenture issue:** Had this been a debenture issue, the ceiling would be **2.5%** (or the articles' rate, whichever is lower). On the same value it would be ₹5,00,00,000 × 2.5% = **₹12,50,000**.

**Answer:** The maximum underwriting commission payable by Growth Ltd. on the share issue is **₹25,00,000** (at 5%, being lower than the 6% authorised by the articles). For an equivalent debenture issue it would be **₹12,50,000** (at 2.5%).

### Q15. Ch: Prospectus & Allotment — Minimum Subscription & Application Money (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Horizon Ltd. issued a prospectus offering 20,00,000 equity shares of ₹10 each to the public, stating a minimum subscription requirement. By the close of the issue, applications were received for only 17,00,000 shares. Examine the consequences under Section 39 of the Companies Act, 2013 regarding minimum subscription, the minimum application money, and the refund obligation.

**Answer:**
**Statutory basis — Section 39(1):** No allotment of any securities of a company offered to the public for subscription shall be made unless (a) the amount stated in the prospectus as the **minimum subscription** has been subscribed; and (b) the **sums payable on application** for such amount have been paid to and received by the company **by cheque or other instrument**.

**Minimum application money — Section 39(2):** The amount payable on application on every security shall **not be less than 5%** of the nominal amount of the security **or such other percentage/amount as may be specified by SEBI**. (For SEBI-regulated public issues, the SEBI ICDR minimum subscription norm of **90% of the issue** applies.)

**Refund on failure — Section 39(3) & the rules:** If the stated minimum subscription has **not been received within 30 days** of the date of issue of the prospectus (or such period as specified by SEBI), then as per **Rule 11 of the Companies (Prospectus and Allotment of Securities) Rules, 2014**, the amount received **shall be returned** within such time and manner as prescribed. Under the SEBI framework, application money must be refunded within the prescribed period, failing which the company and its officers are liable to repay with interest.

**Consequences of default — Section 39(5):** In case of default in complying with the provisions of Section 39(3), the **company and every officer in default** shall be liable to a **penalty**, for each default, of **₹1,000 for each day** during which the default continues **or ₹1,00,000, whichever is less**.

**Application to Horizon Ltd.:** The issue was for 20,00,000 shares but applications were received for only 17,00,000 shares. Whether allotment can be made depends on whether the stated **minimum subscription (and the SEBI 90% threshold, i.e. 18,00,000 shares) has been reached**. Since only 17,00,000 shares (85%) were subscribed, the minimum subscription is **not met**; therefore Horizon Ltd. **cannot allot** the shares and must **refund** the application money received within the prescribed time, failing which the penalty under Section 39(5) applies.

**Conclusion:** As the minimum subscription was not reached, Horizon Ltd. cannot proceed with allotment and must refund the application money; default attracts the Section 39(5) penalty.

### Q16. Ch: Prospectus & Allotment — Misstatement in Prospectus / Criminal & Civil Liability (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** The prospectus of Vantage Ltd. contained a statement, attributed to an "expert," that the company had secured a long-term supply contract, which was untrue. Mr. Rakesh subscribed for shares relying on this statement and suffered a loss. Examine the civil and criminal liability for the misstatement under Sections 34, 35 and 36 of the Companies Act, 2013, and the defences available to a director.

**Answer:**
**Statutory basis:**

**Criminal liability — Section 34:** Where a prospectus **includes any statement which is untrue or misleading** in form or context, or where any inclusion or omission is likely to mislead, **every person who authorises the issue** of such prospectus shall be liable under **Section 447** (fraud). Punishment under Section 447 includes imprisonment (6 months to 10 years) and fine (up to three times the amount involved).

**Civil liability — Section 35:** Where a person has subscribed for securities of a company acting on any misleading statement, or the inclusion or omission of any matter in the prospectus, and has sustained loss or damage as a consequence, the **company and every director, promoter, person who authorised the issue, and expert** shall be **liable to pay compensation** to every person who has sustained such loss or damage.

**Fraudulently inducing investment — Section 36:** Any person who, **knowingly or recklessly makes any misleading, false or deceptive statement, promise or forecast** to induce another to enter into an agreement for acquiring securities, shall be liable for action under Section 447.

**Defences available to a director — Section 35(2):** A director shall **not be liable** if he proves either:
(a) that **having consented to become a director, he withdrew his consent before the issue** of the prospectus, and that it was issued without his authority or consent; or
(b) that the prospectus was issued **without his knowledge or consent**, and on becoming aware of its issue he **forthwith gave a reasonable public notice** that it was issued without his knowledge or consent; or
(c) that, as regards an **expert's statement**, he had **reasonable ground to believe** that the expert was competent, that the expert had given the consent required and had not withdrawn it before delivery of the prospectus for registration.

**Application to Vantage Ltd.:** The prospectus contained an untrue expert-attributed statement about a supply contract, on which Mr. Rakesh relied and suffered loss. Vantage Ltd., its directors, promoters and the persons who authorised the issue are liable to **compensate Mr. Rakesh** under Section 35, and those who authorised the untrue statement face **criminal liability** under Section 34/447. A director may escape civil liability only by establishing one of the Section 35(2) defences.

**Conclusion:** The misstatement attracts both **civil liability (Section 35)** to compensate Mr. Rakesh and **criminal liability (Sections 34 & 36 read with Section 447)**; a director can avoid liability only by proving a statutory defence.

### Q17. Ch: Prospectus & Allotment — Private Placement & Shelf/Red-Herring Prospectus (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between a "Shelf Prospectus" and a "Red-Herring Prospectus." Also state the key conditions governing "private placement" of securities under Section 42 of the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:**
**Statutory basis:**

**Shelf Prospectus — Section 31:** A "shelf prospectus" means a prospectus in respect of which the securities or class of securities included therein are **issued for subscription in one or more issues over a certain period** without the issue of a further prospectus. Any class of companies as prescribed by SEBI may file a shelf prospectus with the Registrar at the stage of the first offer; the validity period is **not more than one year** commencing from the date of opening of the first offer. For subsequent offers within that period, the company files only an **information memorandum** (Form PAS-2) updating material changes; the shelf prospectus together with the information memorandum is deemed to be a prospectus.

**Red-Herring Prospectus — Section 32:** A "red-herring prospectus" means a prospectus which **does not include complete particulars of the quantum or price of the securities** included therein. A company may issue it **prior to the issue of a prospectus**; it must be filed with the Registrar at least **three days before** the opening of the subscription list and offer. Upon closing of the offer, the **prospectus stating the total capital raised and the closing price and other required details** not included in the red-herring prospectus must be filed with the Registrar and SEBI.

**Distinction:**

| Basis | Shelf Prospectus (S.31) | Red-Herring Prospectus (S.32) |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Issued for multiple offers over a period without a fresh prospectus | Prospectus not containing complete particulars of quantum/price |
| Validity/Timing | Valid for up to 1 year from first offer | Filed at least 3 days before opening of offer |
| Supporting document | Information memorandum (PAS-2) for later offers | Final prospectus filed after closure with price/capital details |
| Purpose | Avoid repeated prospectus filing for successive issues | Facilitate book-building / price discovery |

**Private placement — Section 42:** Key conditions:
1. Private placement means an offer or invitation to subscribe securities to a **select group of persons identified by the Board** (other than by way of public offer) through a **private placement offer-cum-application letter (Form PAS-4)**.
2. The offer can be made to **not more than 200 persons in the aggregate** in a financial year (excluding QIBs and employees under ESOP), for each kind of security.
3. The application money shall be received only by **cheque, demand draft or other banking channels**, not in cash, and kept in a **separate bank account**; it cannot be utilised until allotment and filing of the return of allotment.
4. **Allotment** must be completed within **60 days** of receipt of the application money; if not allotted, the money must be **refunded within 15 days**, failing which it is repayable with interest at 12% p.a.
5. A **return of allotment (Form PAS-3)** must be filed with the Registrar within **15 days** of allotment.
6. No fresh offer of private placement can be made unless the earlier offer has been completed or withdrawn/abandoned.

**Conclusion:** A shelf prospectus enables multiple issues over a year without a fresh prospectus, whereas a red-herring prospectus omits the final price/quantum pending price discovery; private placement under Section 42 is a restricted, non-public offer to a maximum of 200 identified persons subject to the PAS-4/PAS-3 and separate-bank-account safeguards.

*(Full-marks tip: Put the shelf-vs-red-herring distinction in a table and give the Section 42 numbers precisely — 200 persons, 60-day allotment, 15-day refund/PAS-3; vague statements without these limits lose marks.)*

**Question:** During the financial year 2025-26, Meghdoot Alloys Ltd., an unlisted public company, had a balance of ₹80,00,000 in its Securities Premium Account. | Sl. | Proposed application of Securities Premium | Amount (₹) |

| (iii) | Preliminary expenses ₹8,00,000 | Valid | Sec 52(2)(b) expressly permits writing off preliminary expenses. |
| (iv) | Premium on redemption of preference shares ₹12,00,000 | Valid | Sec 52(2)(d) expressly permits providing for premium payable on redemption of redeemable preference shares. |
| (v) | Buy-back under Sec 68 ₹15,00,000 | Valid | Sec 52(2)(e) expressly permits use for purchase of the company's own shares under Sec 68. |

### Q19. Ch: Share Capital & Debentures — Prohibition on issue at a discount (Sec 53) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Kaveri Textiles Ltd. is in financial difficulty. To attract investment, the Board passes a resolution to issue 2,00,000 equity shares of face value ₹10 each at ₹7 per share (i.e. at a discount of ₹3). A director argues that since the company simultaneously proposes to issue sweat equity shares to its technical staff at a discount, the discounted cash issue too should be permissible. Examine the validity of both proposals with reference to Sections 53 and 54 of the Companies Act, 2013, and state the penal consequence of contravention.

**Answer:** Section 53(1) of the Companies Act, 2013 lays down that, except as provided in Section 54, a company shall not issue shares at a discount. Section 53(2) declares that any share issued by a company at a discounted price shall be void. The only relaxation, introduced by the Companies (Amendment) Act, 2017 in Section 53(2A), is that a company may issue shares at a discount to its creditors when its debt is converted into shares in pursuance of any statutory resolution plan or debt restructuring scheme in accordance with any guidelines/directions/regulations of the RBI or the Banking Regulation Act, 1949.

The proposed issue of 2,00,000 equity shares at ₹7 (face value ₹10) is a plain issue of shares at a discount for cash. It does not arise out of any statutory resolution plan or RBI-approved debt restructuring, and therefore falls squarely within the prohibition of Section 53(1). Such shares, if issued, would be void under Section 53(2).

The director's reliance on the sweat equity argument is misconceived. Section 54 permits a company to issue sweat equity shares (which are shares issued to directors or employees at a discount or for consideration other than cash for providing know-how or value additions) subject to the conditions in Section 54 and the applicable rules. However, Section 54 is a specific and self-contained exception confined to sweat equity shares; it cannot be extended to justify a general discounted cash issue to outside investors. The two proposals must be judged independently.

**Penalty (Sec 53(3)):** Where a company contravenes the provisions of Section 53, the company and every officer who is in default shall be liable to a penalty which may extend to an amount equal to the amount raised through the issue of shares at a discount, or ₹5,00,000, whichever is less. The company shall also be liable to refund all monies received with interest at the rate of 12% per annum from the date of issue.

**Conclusion:** The discounted cash issue of 2,00,000 shares at ₹7 is prohibited under Section 53 and would be void; the sweat equity issue is separately permissible only if the conditions of Section 54 are met and does not validate the cash issue.

### Q20. Ch: Share Capital & Debentures — Conditions for issue of Bonus Shares (Sec 63) (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the conditions that a company must satisfy before it can capitalise its profits or reserves for the purpose of issuing fully paid-up bonus shares under Section 63 of the Companies Act, 2013. Also state the sources out of which, and the sources out of which it cannot, issue such bonus shares.

**Answer:** Section 63(1) of the Companies Act, 2013 empowers a company to issue fully paid-up bonus shares to its members, in any manner whatsoever, out of three specified sources only: (a) its free reserves; (b) the securities premium account; and (c) the capital redemption reserve account. The proviso to Section 63(1) expressly prohibits the issue of bonus shares by capitalising reserves created by the revaluation of assets. Thus, a revaluation reserve can never be used for bonus shares.

Section 63(2) provides that no company shall capitalise its profits or reserves for the purpose of issuing fully paid-up bonus shares unless the following conditions are satisfied:

| Condition | Requirement under Sec 63(2) |
|-----------|------------------------------|
| Authorisation | It is authorised by the Articles of Association of the company. |
| Recommendation & approval | It has, on the recommendation of the Board, been authorised in the general meeting of the company. |
| No default on debt | The company has not defaulted in payment of interest or principal in respect of fixed deposits or debt securities issued by it. |
| No default to employees | The company has not defaulted in respect of the payment of statutory dues of the employees, such as contribution to provident fund, gratuity and bonus. |
| Partly paid shares made fully paid | The partly paid-up shares, if any outstanding on the date of allotment, are made fully paid-up. |
| Compliance with prescribed conditions | It complies with such conditions as may be prescribed (Rule 14 of the Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules, 2014, which requires that a bonus issue once announced by the Board shall not be subsequently withdrawn). |

Further, Section 63(3) provides that the bonus shares shall not be issued in lieu of dividend.

**Sources permitted:** free reserves, securities premium account, capital redemption reserve.
**Sources not permitted:** reserves created by revaluation of assets (revaluation reserve), and bonus shares cannot be issued in lieu of dividend.

**Conclusion:** A company may issue fully paid bonus shares only out of free reserves, securities premium or capital redemption reserve, after satisfying the six conditions in Section 63(2); a revaluation reserve and an issue in lieu of dividend are strictly barred.

### Q23. Ch: Share Capital & Debentures — Transmission of Shares vs Transfer (Sec 56) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between "transfer" and "transmission" of shares. State whether an instrument of transfer is required in the case of transmission, and how the company should deal with a request for transmission of shares by the legal representative of a deceased member.

**Answer:** "Transfer" of shares is a voluntary act of the parties — a deliberate transaction whereby a member (transferor) conveys his shares to another person (transferee) by executing an instrument of transfer. "Transmission" of shares, on the other hand, is the operation of law — it takes place on the death, insolvency or lunacy of a member, whereby the shares devolve automatically upon the legal representative, official assignee or receiver, without any voluntary act of transfer.

The distinction may be tabulated as follows:

| Basis | Transfer | Transmission |
|-------|----------|--------------|
| How it arises | By a voluntary act of the parties (contract) | By operation of law (death, insolvency, lunacy) |
| Instrument of transfer | Required — a duly executed and stamped Form SH-4 | Not required — devolution is by operation of law |
| Consideration | Generally for consideration | No consideration passes |
| Stamp duty | Payable on the instrument | Not payable |
| Initiated by | Transferor and transferee jointly | Legal representative / heir / official assignee |
| Existing liabilities | Transferee may lose benefit of prior liabilities | Legal representative takes the shares subject to all liabilities of the deceased |

Section 56(2) of the Companies Act, 2013 expressly recognises transmission by providing that nothing in Section 56(1) shall prejudice the power of the company to register, on receipt of an intimation of transmission of any right to securities by operation of law from any person, such right on behalf of a member. Therefore, no instrument of transfer (Form SH-4) is required for transmission.

On receipt of a request for transmission from the legal representative of a deceased member, the company should require production of proof of title — such as a succession certificate, probate, letters of administration or, in appropriate cases (where the value is within the prescribed limit and subject to indemnity), other legally acceptable evidence — and, on being satisfied, register the transmission and issue the share certificate to the legal representative within the timelines prescribed under Section 56(4).

**Conclusion:** Transfer is a voluntary transaction requiring a stamped instrument, whereas transmission is a devolution by operation of law requiring no instrument; the company registers transmission on production of proper proof of title by the legal representative.

*(Full-marks tip: the tabular distinction is the scoring core; adding that the legal representative takes the shares "subject to all liabilities" and that no stamp duty/SH-4 is needed secures full marks.)*

### Q24. Ch: Share Capital & Debentures — Refusal of Registration & Appeal (Sec 58) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** The Board of Ganga Cements Ltd., a public company, refuses to register the transfer of 1,000 shares lodged by Mr. Bose (transferee) but fails to send any notice of refusal for four months. Separately, in Yamuna Traders Pvt. Ltd., a private company, the Board refuses to register a transfer relying on a restrictive clause in its Articles. Advise both transferees of their remedies under Section 58 of the Companies Act, 2013, including the time limits for appeal.

**Answer:** Section 58 of the Companies Act, 2013 governs refusal of registration of transfer and the right of appeal.

**Time limit for the company to act:** Under Section 58(4), in the case of a public company, if the company without sufficient cause refuses to register the transfer of securities within a period of thirty days from the date on which the instrument of transfer is delivered, the transferee may appeal to the Tribunal. Thus a public company must send notice of refusal within 30 days.

**Appeal against a public company (Sec 58(3) and (4)):** Where a public company refuses to register a transfer, or fails to act within 30 days, the transferee may appeal to the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT). As per Section 58(4), the appeal must be made within a period of sixty (60) days from the date of receipt of the notice of refusal, or, where no notice has been sent by the company, within ninety (90) days from the date on which the instrument of transfer was delivered to the company.

Applying this to **Ganga Cements Ltd.** (public company): as no notice of refusal was sent within 30 days, Mr. Bose may appeal to the NCLT within 90 days from the date on which he delivered the instrument of transfer.

**Refusal by a private company (Sec 58(1) and (2)):** In the case of a private company, Section 58(1) recognises the company's power to refuse registration under its Articles, but requires it to send notice of refusal within thirty days of the delivery of the instrument. Under Section 58(2), the securities of a private company are subject to any restriction in the Articles, but such restriction is valid; the transferee/transferor aggrieved may appeal to the Tribunal. The appeal must be filed within thirty (30) days of receipt of the notice of refusal, or within sixty (60) days from the delivery of the instrument where no notice was sent.

Applying this to **Yamuna Traders Pvt. Ltd.**: the private company is entitled to refuse the transfer relying on the restrictive clause in its Articles (as private companies restrict transferability by definition), provided it sends notice of refusal within 30 days; the aggrieved party may nevertheless appeal to the NCLT within the above time limits.

**Conclusion:** Mr. Bose (public company) may appeal to the NCLT within 90 days of delivery of the transfer deed since no refusal notice was issued. In Yamuna Traders, the private company may validly refuse under its Articles, but must issue notice within 30 days, and the aggrieved party may appeal to the NCLT within 30 days of that notice (or 60 days from delivery if no notice).

*(Full-marks tip: keep the two appeal timelines separate — 60/90 days for public companies vs 30/60 days for private companies — and note that a private company's Articles restriction is legally enforceable; blurring the limits is the usual deduction.)*

### Q25. Ch: Share Capital & Debentures — Rectification of Register of Members (Sec 59) (Marks: 6) [Application]

### Q26. Ch: Share Capital & Debentures — Secured vs Unsecured Debentures & Charge (Sec 71) (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between secured debentures and unsecured debentures. State the conditions subject to which secured debentures may be issued under Rule 18 of the Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules, 2014, and explain the requirement of creating a charge and appointing a debenture trustee under Section 71 of the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:** A debenture is an instrument acknowledging a debt of the company, generally under its seal, and usually (but not necessarily) secured by a charge on the company's assets — Section 2(30) of the Companies Act, 2013. Debentures may be secured or unsecured.

The distinction is as follows:

| Basis | Secured debentures | Unsecured debentures |
|-------|--------------------|----------------------|
| Security | Secured by a charge (fixed and/or floating) on the assets of the company | Not secured by any charge on assets; also called "naked" debentures |
| Ranking on winding up | Rank as secured creditors, paid ahead of unsecured creditors | Rank as ordinary unsecured creditors |
| Charge registration | Charge must be registered with the Registrar under Sec 77 | No charge, hence no registration under Sec 77 |
| Debenture trust deed | Required; a debenture trustee must be appointed | Governed by deposit rules if in the nature of deposits |
| Risk to investor | Lower risk | Higher risk |

**Conditions for issue of secured debentures — Rule 18 of the Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules, 2014:**
(a) The date of redemption of secured debentures shall not exceed ten years from the date of issue (certain classes of companies — infrastructure companies, infrastructure finance companies, etc. — may issue for a period exceeding ten years but not exceeding thirty years).
(b) The issue must be secured by the creation of a charge on the properties or assets of the company or its subsidiaries/holding/associate companies, having a value sufficient for the due repayment of the amount of debentures and interest thereon.
(c) The company must appoint a debenture trustee before the issue of the prospectus or letter of offer, and execute a debenture trust deed to protect the interest of the debenture holders.
(d) The security for the debentures must be created in favour of the debenture trustee.

**Requirement under Section 71:** Section 71(3) permits a company to issue secured debentures subject to such terms and conditions as may be prescribed. Section 71(4) requires that where debentures are issued, the company shall create a Debenture Redemption Reserve out of its profits available for dividend, and the amount credited to such reserve shall be utilised only for the redemption of debentures. Section 71(5) mandates that no company shall issue a prospectus or make an offer/invitation to the public or to more than 500 persons for subscription of its debentures without appointing a debenture trustee, and the duties of the debenture trustee are prescribed in Section 71(6).

**Conclusion:** Secured debentures carry a registered charge and are backed by a debenture trust deed and trustee, whereas unsecured debentures are "naked"; secured debentures must comply with Rule 18 (redemption within ten years, sufficient charge, trustee appointed before offer) and Section 71.

*(Full-marks tip: the ten-year redemption cap (and 30-year exception for infrastructure companies) and the mandatory appointment of a debenture trustee where the offer is to more than 500 persons are the high-value points examiners look for.)*

### Q27. Ch: Share Capital & Debentures — Debenture Redemption Reserve & DRR Investment (Sec 71) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Godavari Power Ltd., an unlisted public company, proposes to issue ₹50,00,000 secured, non-convertible debentures redeemable at the end of the fifth year. The finance manager is unsure about (i) the amount of Debenture Redemption Reserve (DRR) to be created and (ii) the requirement to invest/deposit a sum for debentures maturing during the year. Advise with reference to Section 71 read with Rule 18(7) of the Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules, 2014 (as amended).

**Answer:** Section 71(4) of the Companies Act, 2013 provides that where debentures are issued by a company, the company shall create a Debenture Redemption Reserve (DRR) account out of the profits of the company available for payment of dividend, and the amount credited to such account shall not be utilised by the company except for the redemption of debentures. The quantum of DRR and the investment/deposit requirement are governed by Rule 18(7) of the Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules, 2014, as amended by the Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Amendment Rules, 2019.

**(i) DRR requirement.** As per the amended Rule 18(7):
- Listed companies (whether NBFC/HFC or otherwise) and unlisted NBFCs/HFCs registered with RBI/NHB are **not** required to create a DRR for their debentures.
- However, **unlisted companies other than NBFCs and HFCs** are required to create a DRR equivalent to **ten per cent (10%)** of the value of the outstanding debentures.

Godavari Power Ltd. is an unlisted public company (not an NBFC/HFC). Therefore it must create a DRR of 10% of the outstanding debentures = 10% × ₹50,00,000 = **₹5,00,000**.

**(ii) Deposit/investment requirement for maturing debentures (DRR Investment).** Rule 18(7)(c)/(d) further requires that every company required to create/maintain a DRR shall, on or before the 30th day of April of each year, invest or deposit a sum which shall not be less than **fifteen per cent (15%)** of the amount of its debentures maturing during the year ending on the 31st day of March of the next year, in one or more prescribed methods (deposits with a scheduled bank free from charge, specified government/other securities, etc.). This amount must not be used for any purpose other than redemption of debentures maturing during that year, and the balance so invested must not at any time fall below 15% of the amount of debentures maturing during that 31st March.

Since Godavari's debentures mature only at the end of the fifth year, the 15% deposit/investment obligation will arise on or before 30th April of the financial year in which the 31st March falls that immediately precedes the year of maturity. In the year of maturity, the amount to be kept invested = 15% × ₹50,00,000 = **₹7,50,000**.

**Conclusion:** Godavari Power Ltd., being an unlisted non-NBFC/HFC public company, must create a DRR of ₹5,00,000 (10% of ₹50,00,000), and must, on or before 30th April of the year preceding maturity, invest/deposit ₹7,50,000 (15% of the debentures maturing in the ensuing year) in the prescribed instruments.

*(Full-marks tip: after the 2019 amendment DRR is 10% (not the old 25%) for unlisted non-NBFC companies and NIL for listed companies/NBFCs/HFCs; the separate 15% "on or before 30th April" deposit for maturing debentures is a distinct requirement — stating both, with the exemptions, earns full marks.)*

### Q28. Ch: Share Capital & Debentures — Further Issue of Capital / Rights Issue (Sec 62) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** The Board of Brahmaputra Steel Ltd. proposes to make a rights issue of equity shares to its existing members. Advise the Board on the following, with reference to Section 62(1)(a) of the Companies Act, 2013:
(i) The minimum and maximum period for which the rights offer must remain open.
(ii) Whether a member can renounce the rights shares in favour of a third person.
(iii) The consequence if a member does not accept the offer within the offer period.

**Answer:** Section 62(1) of the Companies Act, 2013 provides that where at any time a company having a share capital proposes to increase its subscribed capital by the issue of further shares, such shares shall first be offered to the existing equity shareholders in proportion to the paid-up share capital held by them (a "rights issue"), subject to the conditions in Section 62(1)(a).

**(i) Period of the offer — Sec 62(1)(a)(i).** The offer shall be made by a notice specifying the number of shares offered and limiting a time not being less than fifteen days and not exceeding thirty days from the date of the offer, within which the offer, if not accepted, shall be deemed to have been declined. (For a private company, the period may be less than 15 days if 90% of the members give their consent in writing or electronic mode.) Thus the offer must remain open for a minimum of 15 days and a maximum of 30 days.

**(ii) Right of renunciation — Sec 62(1)(a)(ii).** Unless the Articles of the company otherwise provide, the offer shall be deemed to include a right exercisable by the person concerned to renounce the shares offered to him, or any of them, in favour of any other person. Therefore, a member may renounce his rights shares wholly or partly in favour of a third person, and the notice of offer must mention this right of renunciation.

**(iii) Consequence of non-acceptance — Sec 62(1)(a)(iii).** After the expiry of the offer period, or on receipt of earlier intimation from the member that he declines to accept the shares offered, the Board of Directors may dispose of the shares in such manner which is not disadvantageous to the shareholders and the company. In other words, the unaccepted rights shares lapse and the Board may allot them to any other person on terms not prejudicial to the shareholders and the company.

**Conclusion:** The rights offer must stay open between 15 and 30 days; a member may renounce the shares in favour of a third person unless the Articles provide otherwise; and shares not accepted within the offer period may be disposed of by the Board in a manner not disadvantageous to the shareholders and the company.

### Q29. Ch: Acceptance of Deposits — Meaning & Exclusions from "Deposit" (Sec 2(31) & Rules) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Examine, with reference to Section 2(31) of the Companies Act, 2013 read with Rule 2(1)(c) of the Companies (Acceptance of Deposits) Rules, 2014, whether each of the following amounts received by Chenab Engineering Ltd. would be treated as a "deposit":

| Sl. | Amount received | Amount (₹) |
|-----|-----------------|-----------:|
| (i) | Advance received against supply of goods, appropriated within 300 days | 10,00,000 |
| (ii) | Amount received from the Central Government | 25,00,000 |
| (iii) | Amount received as a loan from a director out of his own funds (declaration furnished) | 8,00,000 |
| (iv) | Amount received as share application money pending allotment for 75 days | 15,00,000 |
| (v) | Non-interest-bearing security deposit from an employee, ₹1,50,000 (employee's annual salary ₹4,00,000) | 1,50,000 |

**Answer:** Section 2(31) of the Companies Act, 2013 defines "deposit" to include any receipt of money by way of deposit or loan or in any other form by a company, but excludes such categories of amount as may be prescribed in consultation with the RBI. Rule 2(1)(c) of the Companies (Acceptance of Deposits) Rules, 2014 lists the amounts that are **not** to be treated as deposits. Applying these to the facts:

| Sl. | Amount | Deposit? | Reasoning |
|-----|--------|----------|-----------|
| (i) | Advance for supply of goods appropriated within 300 days ₹10,00,000 | Not a deposit | Rule 2(1)(c)(xii): an advance received in the course of or for the purpose of business against supply of goods/services is not a deposit if appropriated within 365 days; here it is appropriated within 300 days, so it is excluded. |
| (ii) | Amount from Central Government ₹25,00,000 | Not a deposit | Rule 2(1)(c)(i): any amount received from the Central/State Government, or guaranteed by them, or from a local/statutory authority, is excluded. |
| (iii) | Loan from a director from own funds with declaration ₹8,00,000 | Not a deposit | Rule 2(1)(c)(viii): any amount received from a director (or, for a private company, a relative of a director) who furnishes a declaration that the amount is not given out of borrowed funds is excluded. |
| (iv) | Share application money pending allotment 75 days ₹15,00,000 | Not a deposit | Rule 2(1)(c)(vii): share application money pending allotment is excluded, provided allotment is made within 60 days; if not allotted within 60 days and not refunded within 15 days thereafter, it becomes a deposit. | (v) | Non-interest-bearing security deposit from employee ₹1,50,000 | Not a deposit | Rule 2(1)(c)(x): a non-interest-bearing security deposit received from an employee is excluded provided it does not exceed his annual salary under a contract of employment. Here ₹1,50,000 ≤ annual salary ₹4,00,000, so it is excluded. |

**Conclusion:** Items (i), (ii), (iii) and (v) are not deposits. Item (iv), the share application money, though prima facie excluded, will be **treated as a deposit** because it has remained unallotted beyond 60 days and unrefunded beyond the further 15 days permitted under Rule 2(1)(c)(vii).

### Q32. Ch: Acceptance of Deposits — Penal Provision for Deposit Default (Sec 76A) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Narmada Papers Ltd. accepted deposits of ₹4,00,00,000 from the public in contravention of Sections 73 and 76 of the Companies Act, 2013, and thereafter failed to repay the deposits on maturity. State the penal consequences under Section 76A of the Companies Act, 2013 for (i) the company and (ii) every officer of the company who is in default, and explain the additional statement made in Section 76A regarding fraud.

**Answer:** Section 76A of the Companies Act, 2013 provides for the punishment where a company accepts or invites, or allows or causes any other person to accept or invite on its behalf, any deposit in contravention of the manner or the conditions prescribed under Section 73 or Section 76, or the rules made thereunder, or where a company fails to repay the deposit or part thereof or any interest due thereon within the time specified or such further time as may be allowed by the Tribunal under Section 73(4).

**(i) Punishment of the company.** The company shall, in addition to the payment of the amount of deposit or part thereof and the interest due, be liable to a penalty which shall not be less than one crore rupees or twice the amount of deposit accepted by the company, whichever is lower, but which may extend to ten crore rupees.

Applying this to Narmada Papers Ltd.: deposits accepted = ₹4,00,00,000; twice the amount of deposits = ₹8,00,00,000. The minimum penalty is the lower of ₹1,00,00,000 and ₹8,00,00,000, i.e. **₹1,00,00,000**, and the penalty may extend up to **₹10,00,00,000**. This is in addition to the company repaying the ₹4,00,00,000 deposits with interest.

**(ii) Punishment of every officer in default.** Every officer of the company who is in default shall be punishable with imprisonment which may extend to seven years and with fine which shall not be less than twenty-five lakh rupees but which may extend to two crore rupees.

**(iii) Statement regarding fraud.** The proviso to Section 76A provides that if it is proved that the officer of the company who is in default has contravened the provisions of Section 73 or 76 knowingly or wilfully with the intention to deceive the company or its shareholders or depositors or creditors or tax authorities, he shall be liable for action under Section 447 (punishment for fraud). This is significant because it removes the earlier discretion and links a deliberate deposit default to the stringent fraud provisions.

**Conclusion:** Narmada Papers Ltd. is liable to repay ₹4,00,00,000 with interest plus a penalty of not less than ₹1,00,00,000 (the lower of ₹1 crore and twice the deposit) extending up to ₹10,00,00,000; every officer in default faces imprisonment up to seven years and a fine of ₹25 lakh to ₹2 crore, and, where the default is wilful and with intent to deceive, is additionally liable for fraud under Section 447.

*(Full-marks tip: the company's minimum penalty is "₹1 crore OR twice the deposit, whichever is LOWER" — students frequently write "higher"; and the officer's imprisonment is up to seven years with a fine range of ₹25 lakh to ₹2 crore, plus the Section 447 fraud link.)*

### Q33. Ch: Acceptance of Deposits — Class Action for Deposit Default (Sec 245) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** A large body of depositors of Beas Infra Ltd. is aggrieved by the company's conduct of its deposit affairs, which they believe is prejudicial to their interests and is likely to result in default. Advise the depositors on their remedy by way of a class action under Section 245 of the Companies Act, 2013 — who may apply, the reliefs that may be sought, and the minimum number required to file the application.

Under Section 245(1), a specified number of members or depositors, or any class of them, if they are of the opinion that the management or conduct of the affairs of the company are being conducted in a manner prejudicial to the interests of the company, its members or depositors, may file an application before the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) on behalf of the members or depositors for seeking specified orders. On the given facts there is no such fundamental defect.

**Conclusion:** The certificate of registration issued in Form CHG-2 is conclusive evidence under Section 77(2) that the registration requirements have been complied with. The subsequent creditor's contention that the charge is void on account of the errors in particulars is **not tenable**; the charge of Metro Bank Ltd. remains valid and enforceable against him.

|---|---|

### Q42. Ch: Management & Administration — Resolutions Requiring Special Notice (Marks: 5) [Theory]

### Q44. Ch: Management & Administration — Voting through Electronic Means (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** State the provisions of the Companies Act, 2013 relating to voting by members through electronic means. Which companies are compulsorily required to provide this facility, and what is the manner and period for which the electronic voting is to be kept open?

**Answer:**

**Statutory mandate (Section 108):** The Central Government may prescribe the class or classes of companies and the manner in which a member may exercise his **right to vote by electronic means**. ### Q51. Ch: Management & Administration — Minutes of Proceedings of General Meetings (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the statutory requirements relating to preparation and maintenance of minutes of the proceedings of a general meeting under the Companies Act, 2013. State the time limit for entering the minutes, the matters that may be excluded, and the evidentiary value of minutes.

**Answer:**
The requirements regarding **minutes of proceedings of general meetings, Board meetings and other meetings** are contained in **Section 118 of the Companies Act, 2013** read with **Rule 25 of the Companies (Management and Administration) Rules, 2014** and **Secretarial Standard-2 (SS-2)**.

**Preparation and entry (Section 118(1)):** Every company shall cause **minutes of the proceedings of every general meeting** (including meetings called on requisition), and of every meeting of its Board and committees, to be prepared and **entered in books kept for that purpose** within **thirty days of the conclusion of the meeting**, with the pages of such books being **consecutively numbered**.

**Authentication (Section 118(2)):** The minutes of each meeting shall be **signed and each page consecutively numbered and initialled** by the **Chairman of the same meeting** or the **Chairman of the next succeeding meeting**.

**Contents (Section 118(3), (4)):** Each page of every such book shall be consecutively numbered and initialled, and the minutes shall contain a **fair and correct summary of the proceedings**. All **appointments made at the meeting** shall be included in the minutes. In the case of a **Board or committee meeting**, the minutes shall also contain the **names of the directors present** and, in respect of each resolution, the **names of directors dissenting from or not concurring** with the resolution.

**Matters that may be excluded (Section 118(5)):** The Chairman shall **exercise absolute discretion** in regard to the inclusion or non-inclusion in the minutes of any matter which, in his opinion:
- is or could reasonably be regarded as **defamatory** of any person;
- is **irrelevant or immaterial** to the proceedings; or
- is **detrimental to the interests of the company**.

**Prohibition on alteration (Section 118(6)):** The minutes kept shall be **evidence of the proceedings** recorded therein, and once entered they cannot be tampered with.

**Evidentiary value (Section 118(7)):** Where minutes have been kept in accordance with Section 118(1), then, until the contrary is proved, the meeting shall be **deemed to have been duly called and held, and all proceedings thereat to have duly taken place**, and the resolutions passed shall be deemed valid. The minutes are thus **prima facie evidence** of the proceedings.

**Default (Section 118(11)/(12)):** Failure to comply attracts a **penalty on the company and the officer in default**, and **tampering with minutes** is punishable with imprisonment and/or fine.

**Conclusion:** Under **Section 118**, minutes of a general meeting must be entered in the minute book **within 30 days** of the meeting, be authenticated by the Chairman, contain a fair summary and all appointments, exclude (at the Chairman's discretion) defamatory, irrelevant or company-detrimental matter, and, once duly kept, serve as **evidence** of the proceedings, raising a presumption that the meeting was duly held and the resolutions validly passed.

### Q54. Ch: Declaration & Payment of Dividend — Interim Dividend where Company has Incurred Loss (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** The Board of Directors of Surya Cements Ltd. proposes to declare an interim dividend on 20th December, 2025 for the financial year 2025-26. However, the company has incurred a net loss during the period from 1st April, 2025 up to 30th September, 2025 (the quarter immediately preceding the date of the proposed declaration). The rates of dividend declared by the company in the three immediately preceding financial years were 9%, 8% and 10% respectively. The Board wishes to declare interim dividend at 11%. Advise the Board on the permissibility and the maximum rate of the interim dividend under the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:** The declaration of interim dividend is governed by **Section 123(3)** of the Companies Act, 2013. It provides that the Board of Directors of a company may declare interim dividend during any financial year, or at any time during the period from the closure of the financial year till holding of the annual general meeting, out of the surplus in the profit and loss account and out of profits of the financial year for which such interim dividend is sought to be declared.

The **proviso to Section 123(3)** contains a specific safeguard for a loss-making period. It states that in case the company has **incurred loss during the current financial year up to the end of the quarter immediately preceding the date of declaration of interim dividend**, such interim dividend shall **not be declared at a rate higher than the average dividends declared by the company during the immediately preceding three financial years.**

Applying this to Surya Cements Ltd., the company has incurred a loss during 1st April, 2025 to 30th September, 2025 — the quarter immediately preceding the proposed declaration on 20th December, 2025. The proviso is therefore attracted, and the interim dividend cannot exceed the average of the dividends of the last three financial years.

Average rate = (9% + 8% + 10%) ÷ 3 = 27% ÷ 3 = **9%.**

Therefore, the proposed rate of 11% is not permissible. The Board may declare interim dividend at a rate **not exceeding 9%.**

**Conclusion:** Owing to the loss incurred up to the quarter ended 30th September, 2025, the interim dividend for Surya Cements Ltd. is capped at the three-year average of **9%**; the proposed 11% is impermissible.

*(Full-marks tip: Identify that the trigger is a loss "up to the end of the quarter immediately preceding" the declaration — not a loss for the whole year. ### Q55. Ch: Declaration & Payment of Dividend — Unpaid Dividend Account and Transfer to IEPF (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Nutan Alloys Ltd. declared a dividend at its AGM held on 5th August, 2025. Explain the obligations of the company with reference to (i) transfer of the unpaid amount to a special account, (ii) transfer to the Investor Education and Protection Fund (IEPF), and (iii) the fate of Mr. Kabir's shares, under the Companies Act, 2013.

**Conclusion:** Nutan Alloys Ltd. must transfer ₹6,80,000 to the Unpaid Dividend Account within seven days after 4th September, 2025; amounts remaining unclaimed for seven years must go to the IEPF with interest; and Mr. Kabir's shares, unclaimed for seven consecutive years, must be transferred to the IEPF subject to his right of reclaim.

### Q56. Ch: Declaration & Payment of Dividend — Failure to Distribute Dividend within Thirty Days (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the consequences and penal provisions under the Companies Act, 2013 where a company, having declared a dividend, fails to pay it or fails to post the dividend warrant within thirty days from the date of declaration. Also state the circumstances in which such non-payment shall NOT be treated as an offence.

**Answer:** The consequences of failure to pay a declared dividend within the stipulated period are set out in **Section 127** of the Companies Act, 2013.

Section 127 provides that where a dividend has been declared by a company but has **not been paid or the warrant in respect thereof has not been posted within thirty days** from the date of declaration to any shareholder entitled to the payment of the dividend, then:
- **every director of the company**, if he is knowingly a party to the default, shall be punishable with **imprisonment which may extend to two years and with fine which shall not be less than one thousand rupees for every day during which such default continues**; and
- the **company shall be liable to pay simple interest at the rate of eighteen per cent per annum** during the period for which such default continues.

**Circumstances where no offence is deemed to have occurred:** The proviso to Section 127 lists situations in which non-payment shall not be treated as a default, namely —
(a) where the dividend could not be paid **by reason of the operation of any law**;
(b) where a shareholder has given **directions to the company regarding the payment** of the dividend and those directions cannot be complied with, and the same has been communicated to him;
(c) where there is a **dispute regarding the right to receive the dividend**;
(d) where the dividend has been **lawfully adjusted** by the company against any sum due to it from the shareholder; and
(e) where, for **any other reason, the failure to pay the dividend or to post the warrant was not due to any default on the part of the company.**

**Conclusion:** Failure to pay a declared dividend within thirty days attracts imprisonment up to two years and a minimum daily fine on knowingly defaulting directors, plus interest at 18% p.a. payable by the company, except in the five saving situations enumerated in the proviso to Section 127.

*(Full-marks tip: Reproduce the exact penalty — "two years", "₹1,000 per day", and "18% per annum" — and all five exceptions in the proviso. Losing marks usually comes from omitting the proviso exceptions or mis-stating the interest rate.)*

### Q57. Ch: Accounts of Companies — Consolidated Financial Statements (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Zenith Holdings Ltd., an unlisted company, has one wholly-owned subsidiary, one associate company and one joint venture as on 31st March, 2026. The management is of the view that since the company is unlisted, it is required to prepare only its own (standalone) financial statements and need not prepare consolidated financial statements. Further, the management contends that even if consolidation were required, an associate and a joint venture cannot be included. Examine the correctness of the management's views under the Companies Act, 2013, and state the additional statement required to be attached.

Section 129(3) provides that where a company has **one or more subsidiaries, or associate companies**, it shall, in addition to its own (standalone) financial statements, prepare a **consolidated financial statement (CFS) of the company and of all the subsidiaries and associate companies** in the same form and manner as that of its own, which shall also be laid before the annual general meeting of the company along with the standalone financial statement. The **Explanation to Section 129(3)** expressly clarifies that the word "subsidiary" **includes an associate company and a joint venture** for the purposes of consolidation.

The requirement to prepare CFS is **not confined to listed companies** — it applies to every company having a subsidiary, associate or joint venture, subject only to the limited exemptions in Rule 6 (which are not applicable here as Zenith Holdings Ltd. is itself a holding company preparing accounts). Therefore, the management's first view — that being unlisted it need not consolidate — is incorrect. **Conclusion:** Zenith Holdings Ltd. is legally bound to prepare consolidated financial statements incorporating its subsidiary, associate and joint venture, and to attach the statement in Form AOC-1; both contentions of the management are erroneous.

### Q58. Ch: Accounts of Companies — Board's Report and Internal Financial Controls (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** With reference to Section 134 of the Companies Act, 2013, (i) state who is required to sign the financial statements of a company, and (ii) explain the requirement relating to **internal financial controls** in the Directors' Responsibility Statement forming part of the Board's Report, including the meaning of the expression "internal financial controls".

**Answer:** The signing of financial statements and the contents of the Directors' Responsibility Statement are governed by **Section 134** of the Companies Act, 2013.

**(i) Signing of financial statements [Section 134(1)]:** The financial statements, including the consolidated financial statements, if any, shall be **approved by the Board of Directors** before they are signed on behalf of the Board. They shall be signed by at least —
- the **chairperson of the company** where he is authorised by the Board, **or by two directors** out of which one shall be the **managing director**, and
- the **Chief Executive Officer**, if he is a director in the company,
- the **Chief Financial Officer** and the **company secretary** of the company, wherever they are appointed.

In the case of a One Person Company, the financial statement shall be signed by **only one director** for submission to the auditor.

**(ii) Internal financial controls in the Directors' Responsibility Statement [Section 134(3)(c) & 134(5)]:** The Board's Report must include a **Directors' Responsibility Statement** under Section 134(5). ### Q59. Ch: Accounts of Companies — Voluntary Revision of Financial Statements (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** After the financial statements and Board's Report of Crestwave Industries Ltd. for the year ended 31st March, 2024 were adopted at the AGM and filed with the Registrar, the directors discovered that certain material items had been erroneously disclosed and that the Board's Report did not comply with the Act. Advise the directors on the procedure and limits for such revision under the Companies Act, 2013, and distinguish it from re-opening of accounts on a court/Tribunal order.

**Answer:** The voluntary revision of financial statements or the Board's Report is governed by **Section 131** of the Companies Act, 2013, whereas re-opening of accounts on the direction of a court or the Tribunal is governed by **Section 130.**

**Voluntary revision [Section 131(1)]:** If it appears to the directors of a company that (a) the **financial statement of the company**, or (b) the **report of the Board**, does **not comply with the provisions of Section 129 (financial statements) or Section 134 (Board's Report)**, they may prepare **revised financial statements or a revised report** in respect of **any of the three preceding financial years** after obtaining the **approval of the Tribunal** on an application made by the company. The Tribunal shall give notice to the Central Government and the Income-tax authorities and consider their representations before passing the order. A copy of the Tribunal's order shall be filed with the Registrar.

Revision is confined to correcting the non-compliance, and the reasons for the revision must be disclosed in the Board's Report of the relevant financial year in which the revision is made. Such revised statements/report **cannot be made more than once in a financial year** (proviso to Section 131(1)).

| Basis | Section 130 (Re-opening) | Section 131 (Voluntary revision) |
|---|---|---|
| Who initiates | Court or Tribunal, on application by Central Government, Income-tax authority, SEBI, or any other statutory regulatory body/person concerned | The **directors** of the company themselves |
| Ground | Accounts prepared in a fraudulent manner, or affairs mismanaged casting doubt on reliability | Non-compliance with Section 129 or Section 134 |
| Nature | Compulsory, ordered against the company | Voluntary, at the option of the directors |
| Approval | Court/Tribunal order | Prior approval of the Tribunal |
| Time limit | Not beyond eight financial years (subject to a longer period under Section 128) | Any of the three preceding financial years |

**Conclusion:** The directors of Crestwave Industries Ltd. may voluntarily revise the 2023-24 financial statements and Board's Report under Section 131, but only with the prior approval of the Tribunal, only for a year falling within the three preceding financial years, and not more than once in a financial year. This is distinct from the compulsory re-opening under Section 130 initiated by regulators.

*(Full-marks tip: The two anchors are "prior approval of the Tribunal" and the "three preceding financial years / once a year" limits. The distinction table sharply separates Section 130 (compulsory, regulator-driven, 8 years) from Section 131 (voluntary, director-driven, 3 years).)*

### Q60. Ch: Accounts of Companies — Books of Account: Place of Keeping and Period of Preservation (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the provisions of the Companies Act, 2013 regarding (i) the place at which the books of account of a company are to be kept, (ii) the procedure where a company decides to keep them at a place other than the registered office, and (iii) the period for which the books of account are to be preserved.

**Answer:** These matters are governed by **Section 128** of the Companies Act, 2013 read with the Companies (Accounts) Rules, 2014.

**(i) Place of keeping books of account [Section 128(1)]:** Every company shall prepare and keep **at its registered office** books of account and other relevant books and papers and financial statements for every financial year, which give a **true and fair view** of the state of the affairs of the company, including that of its branch office(s), and explain the transactions effected both at the registered office and its branches. Such books shall be kept **on accrual basis and according to the double entry system of accounting.**

**(ii) Keeping books at a place other than the registered office [proviso to Section 128(1)]:** The company may keep all or any of the books of account at **any other place in India** as the **Board of Directors may decide.** Where the Board takes such a decision, the company shall, **within seven days** of the Board's decision, **file with the Registrar a notice in writing giving the full address of that other place** (in Form AOC-5).

Where a company has a branch office in or outside India, it shall be deemed to have complied with these provisions if proper books of account relating to the branch transactions are kept at that branch office and **proper summarised returns are periodically sent** to the registered office or the other place where the books are kept.

**(iii) Period of preservation [Section 128(5)]:** The books of account, together with the vouchers relevant to any entry, shall be kept in good order **for a minimum period of eight financial years** immediately preceding a financial year, or where the company had been in existence for a period less than eight years, in respect of all such preceding years. Where an investigation has been ordered in respect of the company under Chapter XIV, the Central Government may direct that the books be kept for a **longer period.**

**Conclusion:** Books of account must ordinarily be kept at the registered office on accrual and double-entry basis; they may be kept elsewhere in India by a Board decision followed by filing Form AOC-5 with the Registrar within seven days; and they must be preserved for a minimum of eight financial years (or longer if the Central Government so directs).

*(Full-marks tip: State "seven days", "Form AOC-5" and "eight financial years" precisely, and add the accrual + double-entry requirement. The longer-preservation direction on investigation is the point that distinguishes an average answer from a full-marks one.)*

### Q64. Ch: Audit & Auditors — Removal of Auditor before Expiry of Term (Marks: 6) [Application]

Advise the Board on the correct legal procedure for removing an auditor before the expiry of the term under the Companies Act, 2013, including the role of the Central Government and the auditor's right to be heard.

**Answer:** The removal of an auditor before the expiry of his term is governed by **Section 140(1)** of the Companies Act, 2013 read with **Rule 7 of the Companies (Audit and Auditors) Rules, 2014.**

### Q65. Ch: Audit & Auditors — Reporting of Fraud by the Auditor (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** During the course of the statutory audit of Marvel Engineering Ltd. for the year ended 31st March, 2026, the auditor, CA Nandini, has reason to believe that an offence of fraud involving an amount of ₹1.8 crore is being committed against the company by two of its senior officers. In a separate instance, she identifies a fraud of ₹40 lakh committed by a junior employee. Advise CA Nandini on her reporting obligations in each case under the Companies Act, 2013 and the relevant Rules.

**Answer:** The auditor's duty to report fraud is contained in **Section 143(12)** of the Companies Act, 2013 read with **Rule 13 of the Companies (Audit and Auditors) Rules, 2014.** The reporting obligation depends on the **amount of the fraud**, the threshold being **₹1 crore.**

**Case 1 — Fraud of ₹1.8 crore (amount ≥ ₹1 crore) [Section 143(12) & Rule 13(1)]:** Where an auditor of a company, in the course of the performance of his duties, has **reason to believe that an offence of fraud involving an amount of ₹1 crore or more is being or has been committed** against the company by its officers or employees, the auditor shall **report the matter to the Central Government.** The procedure prescribed by Rule 13 is:
1. The auditor shall **forward his report to the Board or the Audit Committee**, as the case may be, **immediately but not later than two days** of his knowledge of the fraud, **seeking their reply or observations within forty-five days.**
2. On receipt of the reply, the auditor shall forward his report, the reply/observations of the Board or Audit Committee, and his own comments to the **Central Government within fifteen days** of receipt of such reply.
3. If no reply is received within forty-five days, the auditor shall forward his report to the Central Government along with a note stating that no reply was received.
4. The report to the Central Government shall be sent in **Form ADT-4** to the Secretary, Ministry of Corporate Affairs, by registered post with acknowledgement due or by speed post followed by an email.

Since the fraud of ₹1.8 crore exceeds ₹1 crore, CA Nandini must follow this Central Government reporting route.

**Case 2 — Fraud of ₹40 lakh (amount below ₹1 crore) [Section 143(12) proviso & Rule 13(3)]:** In case of a fraud involving an amount **less than ₹1 crore**, the auditor shall report the matter to the **Audit Committee constituted under Section 177 or to the Board** (in other cases) **within two days** of his knowledge of the fraud, specifying the nature of the fraud, the approximate amount involved and the parties involved. Such frauds (below ₹1 crore) are **not** reported to the Central Government; instead, the details are required to be **disclosed in the Board's Report** under Section 134(3)(ca), namely the nature of the fraud, the approximate amount, the parties involved (if remedial action not taken) and the remedial action taken.

**Conclusion:** For the ₹1.8 crore fraud, CA Nandini must report to the Central Government in Form ADT-4 following the Rule 13 procedure (Board/Audit Committee within 2 days, 45-day reply, CG within 15 days). For the ₹40 lakh fraud, she must report to the Audit Committee/Board within two days, and the fraud must be disclosed in the Board's Report; no report to the Central Government is required.

### Q66. Ch: Audit & Auditors — Disqualifications for Appointment as Auditor (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Examine, with reference to the disqualifications specified under Section 141(3) of the Companies Act, 2013, whether the following persons are eligible to be appointed as the statutory auditor of Nirvana Foods Ltd.:

(ii) Mr. Bhaskar, who owes ₹6,00,000 to Nirvana Foods Ltd. by way of an unsecured loan;
(iii) M/s Kiran & Co., a firm one of whose partners is a whole-time employee of another company;
(iv) Mr. Deepak, who is already the auditor of twenty other companies.

**(i) Spouse holding shares of ₹90,000 [Section 141(3)(d)(i)]:** A person is disqualified if he, or his relative or partner, is **holding any security of or interest in** the company. However, the proviso permits a **relative** to hold security or interest in the company of **face value not exceeding ₹1,00,000.** Since Mr. Anand's spouse (a relative) holds shares of face value ₹90,000, which is within the ₹1,00,000 limit, this does **not** disqualify Mr. Anand. He is **eligible.**

**(ii) Indebtedness of ₹6,00,000 [Section 141(3)(d)(ii)]:** A person is disqualified if he, or his relative or partner, is **indebted to the company** for an amount **exceeding ₹5,00,000.** Mr. Bhaskar owes ₹6,00,000, which exceeds the limit of ₹5,00,000. He is therefore **disqualified** and **not eligible** for appointment.

**(iii) Partner in full-time employment elsewhere [Section 141(3)(a)/(f) and 141(3)(b)]:** A firm is disqualified if any of its partners is disqualified. Under Section 141(3)(b), a person who is an **officer or employee of the company** is disqualified — but here the partner is a whole-time employee of **another** company, not of Nirvana Foods Ltd. The relevant bar is Section 141(3) read with Section 141(3)(a) concerning a person in **full-time employment elsewhere**; a person cannot act as auditor if he is in full-time employment elsewhere. Since one partner is in whole-time employment of another company, that individual cannot personally act as auditor; however, the **firm** M/s Kiran & Co. may be appointed provided the audit is conducted by a partner who is **not** so disqualified. Thus the firm is **eligible**, but the employed partner cannot sign/act.

**(iv) Auditor of twenty companies [Section 141(3)(g)]:** A person is disqualified if he is in **full-time employment elsewhere** or is a person or partner holding appointment as auditor of **more than twenty companies** at the date of appointment. Mr. Deepak is currently the auditor of **twenty** companies, which is **within** the ceiling of twenty. Appointment in Nirvana Foods Ltd. would be his twenty-first, exceeding the limit; therefore Mr. Deepak is **disqualified** from accepting this appointment.

**Conclusion:** (i) Mr. Anand — **eligible** (relative's holding within ₹1,00,000); (ii) Mr. Bhaskar — **disqualified** (indebtedness exceeds ₹5,00,000); (iii) M/s Kiran & Co. — **eligible** as a firm, but the employed partner cannot act; (iv) Mr. Deepak — **disqualified** (twenty-first appointment breaches the twenty-company ceiling).

### Q68. Ch: Audit & Auditors — Remuneration of Auditor and Branch Audit (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** (a) Explain how the remuneration of the auditor of a company is fixed under Section 142 of the Companies Act, 2013, and state what the remuneration is deemed to include. **Answer:** **(a) Remuneration of the auditor [Section 142]:** Section 142(1) provides that the **remuneration of the auditor** of a company shall be **fixed in its general meeting or in such manner as may be determined therein**, provided that the Board may fix the **remuneration of the first auditor** appointed by it. Thus, while the first auditor's remuneration may be fixed by the Board, in all other cases it is a matter for the members in general meeting (who may delegate the manner of fixation).

**(b) Audit of branch office accounts [Section 143(8)]:** Section 143(8) provides that where a company has a **branch office**, the accounts of that office shall be **audited either by the auditor appointed for the company (the company's auditor) under the Act, or by any other person qualified for appointment as an auditor of the company under Section 139 and appointed as such (the branch auditor).** Where the branch office is situated in a country **outside India**, the accounts of the branch office shall be audited **either by the company's auditor or by an accountant or by any other person duly qualified to act as an auditor of the accounts of the branch office in accordance with the laws of that country.** The **branch auditor shall prepare a report on the accounts of the branch office examined by him and send it to the auditor of the company**, who shall deal with it in his report in such manner as he considers necessary. ### Q69. Ch: The Limited Liability Partnership Act, 2008 — Extent of Liability of Partner & LLP (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the statutory scheme governing the "extent of liability" of a Limited Liability Partnership and of its partners under Sections 27 and 28 of the LLP Act, 2008. In particular, state (i) when an LLP is bound by the acts of a partner, (ii) when an LLP is NOT bound, and (iii) whether a partner is personally liable, and if so for what.

**Answer:**
The liability structure of an LLP proceeds on the fundamental principle in **Section 3** that an LLP is a body corporate and a legal person separate from its partners, so its obligations are its own and are met out of its property.

**LLP bound by the acts of a partner — Section 27(2):** The LLP is bound by anything done by a partner in relation to the LLP if the partner does that thing **for the purpose of the business of the LLP**. Thus a partner is, for this purpose, the **agent of the LLP** (Section 26), but he is **not an agent of the other partners** — this is the key departure from ordinary partnership.

**LLP NOT bound — Section 27(1):** The LLP is **not bound** by anything done by a partner in dealing with a person if (a) the partner in fact has **no authority** to act for the LLP in doing that particular act, and (b) the person **knows** that the partner has no authority, **or** does not know or believe him to be a partner of the LLP. In such a case the ostensible-authority protection to the outsider falls away.

**Liability of the LLP for wrongful acts — Section 27(3):** The LLP is liable if a partner, in the course of the business of the LLP or with its authority, commits a **wrongful act or omission** causing loss or injury to any person or incurs any **penalty**.

**Obligations met out of LLP property — Section 27(4):** Any such liability of the LLP is solely the obligation of the LLP; it is met out of the property of the LLP.

**Extent of a partner's own liability — Section 28:**

| Situation | Rule |
|---|---|
| Partner's liability for LLP obligations (Sec. 28(1)) | A partner is **not personally liable**, directly or indirectly, for an obligation of the LLP solely by reason of being a partner |
| Partner's own wrongful act (Sec. 28(2)) | A partner **remains personally liable** for his **own wrongful act or omission**, but is not personally liable for the wrongful act or omission of **any other partner** |
| Fraud (Sec. 30) | Where the LLP or its partner acts with intent to defraud creditors or for any fraudulent purpose, liability of the LLP and the **partners who acted with such intent** is **unlimited** |

**Conclusion:** An LLP is bound by, and bears out of its own property, acts done by a partner for the LLP's business; a partner is the agent of the LLP alone; and a partner is personally liable only for his own wrongful act (or where fraud attracts Section 30), never merely by reason of being a partner.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner rewards the precise pairing of Sec. 27 (LLP's liability) with Sec. 28 (partner's liability) and the point that a partner is agent of the LLP but NOT of the other partners. Losing the Sec. 27(1) "knows he has no authority / does not believe him a partner" limb is a common deduction.)*

### Q70. Ch: The Limited Liability Partnership Act, 2008 — Partner as Agent (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Rohan is one of five partners of "Zenith Consultants LLP". Without any authority under the LLP agreement, he approaches Mr. Kapoor and, describing himself as a partner, borrows ₹8,00,000 purportedly for the LLP but actually for his personal share trading. Advise Mr. Kapoor and the LLP in the following independent situations, citing the LLP Act, 2008:
(i) Mr. Kapoor knew that Rohan had no authority to borrow for the LLP.
(ii) Mr. Kapoor did not know of the restriction and honestly believed Rohan was a partner acting for the firm.
(iii) Whether the **other four partners** are personally liable to Mr. Kapoor.

**Answer:**
The governing provisions are **Sections 26, 27 and 28** of the LLP Act, 2008. Under **Section 26** every partner is the agent of the LLP (but not of the other partners) for the purpose of the LLP's business.

**(i) Kapoor knew Rohan had no authority — Section 27(1)(a):** The LLP is **not bound** by the act of a partner where the partner in fact has no authority to act for the LLP in that matter **and** the person dealing with him **knows** that he has no authority. Since Kapoor knew of the want of authority, the LLP is not bound; Kapoor cannot enforce the loan against Zenith Consultants LLP. He may proceed only against Rohan personally.

**(ii) Kapoor did not know and believed Rohan a partner acting for the firm:** Here neither disqualifying condition in Section 27(1) is met — Kapoor neither knew of the want of authority nor disbelieved that Rohan was a partner. Ordinarily the LLP would be bound. **However**, Section 27(2) binds the LLP only where the partner does the act **"for the purpose of the business of the LLP."** The borrowing was in truth for Rohan's personal share trading, not the LLP's business. Where an outsider deals in good faith and the act is apparently in the ordinary course, the LLP will generally be bound to the innocent third party, and its remedy lies against Rohan; but if it is shown the transaction was not for the LLP's business and this was apparent, the LLP escapes. On the facts as framed (Kapoor honest, act apparently for the firm) the LLP is bound and must repay, recovering from Rohan.

**(iii) Liability of the other four partners — Section 28:** By **Section 28(1)** a partner is **not personally liable**, directly or indirectly, for an obligation of the LLP solely by reason of being a partner, and by **Section 28(2)** a partner is not liable for the wrongful act or omission of any other partner. Therefore the other four partners have **no personal liability** to Kapoor; the obligation, if any, is that of the LLP met out of LLP property (Section 27(4)).

**Conclusion:** (i) LLP not bound — Kapoor knew; (ii) LLP bound where Kapoor acted in good faith and the act appeared to be for the firm, with recourse against Rohan; (iii) the other four partners are not personally liable.

*(Full-marks tip: distinguish clearly the two disqualifying limbs of Sec. 27(1) and tie the answer to "for the purpose of the business of the LLP." Do not import the ordinary Partnership Act rule of mutual agency between partners — under LLP a partner is agent of the LLP only.)*

### Q72. Ch: The Limited Liability Partnership Act, 2008 — Withdrawal of Contribution & Creditor Reliance (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** "Aarav & Meera Textiles LLP" has two partners. The LLP agreement records that Aarav will contribute ₹20,00,000 and Meera ₹15,00,000. On the strength of the LLP agreement disclosing these contributions, "Sunrise Bank" advanced a term loan of ₹25,00,000 to the LLP. Later, by a private compromise, the partners agreed to reduce Aarav's contribution obligation to ₹5,00,000 and Aarav withdrew ₹15,00,000 already brought in. The Bank had no notice of this compromise. The LLP subsequently defaults. Examine, under the LLP Act, 2008, whether the Bank can enforce Aarav's original ₹20,00,000 contribution obligation, and whether withdrawal of contribution is permissible.

**Answer:**
The issue is governed by **Section 33(2)** read with **Section 32** of the LLP Act, 2008.

**Whether contribution may be withdrawn:** The Act does not prohibit variation or withdrawal of contribution as between the partners; the obligation to contribute is a matter of the **LLP agreement** (Section 33(1)), which the partners may compromise inter se. So the internal compromise reducing Aarav's obligation to ₹5,00,000 is valid **between the partners**.

**Creditor's right to enforce the original obligation — Section 33(2):** A creditor of the LLP who **extends credit or otherwise acts in reliance on an obligation described in the LLP agreement**, and does so **without notice of any compromise of the obligation**, may enforce the **original obligation**.

Applying this to the facts:
1. The LLP agreement disclosed Aarav's contribution obligation of ₹20,00,000 (Section 32(2) requires such value to be accounted for and disclosed).
2. Sunrise Bank advanced ₹25,00,000 **in reliance** on that disclosed obligation.
3. The reduction to ₹5,00,000 and Aarav's withdrawal of ₹15,00,000 was a **subsequent compromise** of which the Bank had **no notice**.

Therefore the Bank is protected by Section 33(2): it may **enforce Aarav's original obligation of ₹20,00,000** notwithstanding the internal compromise and the withdrawal. Aarav must make good ₹20,00,000 (i.e., re-contribute the withdrawn ₹15,00,000 over and above the ₹5,00,000 he retained as reduced) to the extent required to satisfy the LLP's liability to the Bank.

**Note:** The compromise remains effective between Aarav and Meera; Aarav may seek adjustment from Meera per their agreement, but that does not defeat the Bank's statutory right.

**Conclusion:** The compromise/withdrawal is valid between the partners but **not against Sunrise Bank**, which relied on the disclosed obligation without notice; the Bank can enforce Aarav's **original contribution obligation of ₹20,00,000** under Section 33(2).

### Q73. Ch: The Limited Liability Partnership Act, 2008 — Assignment & Transfer of Partnership Interest (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain, with reference to Section 42 of the LLP Act, 2008, the nature and consequences of the transfer of a partner's economic rights in an LLP. Does an assignment of interest make the assignee a partner or entitle him to participate in management? What rights does the assignee acquire, and what is the effect of such transfer on the partner's status?

**Answer:**
**Transferable nature of partner's rights — Section 42(1):** The rights of a partner to a **share of the profits and losses** of the LLP and to **receive distributions** in accordance with the LLP agreement are **transferable**, either **wholly or in part**.

**Effect of transfer on partner's status — Section 42(2):** The transfer of any such right by a partner **does not by itself cause the disassociation of the partner** or a **dissolution and winding up** of the LLP. The assigning partner therefore **remains a partner**, retaining his non-economic rights and obligations.

**What the assignee does NOT acquire — Section 42(3):** The transfer of a right by a partner **does not, by itself, entitle the transferee (assignee) to:**
(a) **participate in the management or conduct** of the activities of the LLP, or
(b) **access information** concerning the transactions of the LLP.

**What the assignee acquires:** Only the **economic interest** — the assignor's right to receive the share of profits/losses and distributions that were transferred. The assignee steps into the financial entitlement but acquires **no management or information rights** and does **not become a partner**.

| Question | Position under Section 42 |
|---|---|
| Are economic rights transferable? | Yes, wholly or in part (Sec. 42(1)) |
| Does assignment dissociate the partner / dissolve LLP? | No, not by itself (Sec. 42(2)) |
| Does assignee become a partner? | No |
| Can assignee participate in management? | No (Sec. 42(3)(a)) |
| Can assignee access LLP information? | No (Sec. 42(3)(b)) |
| What does assignee get? | Only the assigned share of profits/losses and distributions |

**Conclusion:** Under Section 42 a partner may assign his economic rights wholly or partly; this neither makes the assignee a partner nor gives him any management or information rights, nor does it dissociate the assigning partner or dissolve the LLP — the assignee receives only the financial entitlement.

*(Full-marks tip: the three sub-sections of Sec. 42 map cleanly to three points — transferability, no dissociation/dissolution, and no management/information rights. Missing the "not by itself dissociation" limb of Sec. 42(2) is the usual deduction.)*

### Q75. Ch: The Limited Liability Partnership Act, 2008 — Cessation & Continuing Liability (Marks: 4) [Application]

(a) the person has **notice that the former partner has ceased** to be a partner of the LLP, **or**

- No notice of cessation had yet been delivered to the Registrar.

### Q77. Ch: The General Clauses Act, 1897 — "Immovable Property" and "Movable Property" (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Define "immovable property" and "movable property" as given in the General Clauses Act, 1897. Explain, with the statutory definitions and illustrations, how a thing is classified as movable or immovable, and state why these definitions matter for interpreting Central Acts.

**Answer:**
The General Clauses Act, 1897 supplies default definitions that apply to every Central Act and Regulation unless there is a repugnant subject or context (Section 3).

**"Immovable property" — Section 3(26):** "Immovable property" shall include **land, benefits to arise out of land, and things attached to the earth, or permanently fastened to anything attached to the earth.** The definition is **inclusive**, so it is not exhaustive.
- **Land** includes the surface of the earth and the space above and below.
- **Benefits to arise out of land** — e.g., the right to collect rent, a right of way, a right to catch fish (fishery), a right to collect dues at a market, or a right to the ferry — are immovable property though intangible.
- **Things attached to the earth or permanently fastened to anything so attached** — e.g., trees, buildings, and machinery embedded in a building for its permanent beneficial enjoyment.

**"Movable property" — Section 3(36):** "Movable property" shall mean **property of every description, except immovable property.** Thus the definition is by **exclusion** — whatever is not immovable property is movable.

| Item | Classification | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Right to collect rent / right of way / fishery | Immovable | "Benefit to arise out of land" — Sec. 3(26) |
| Standing timber, growing crops, grass (cut and severable) | Movable | Intended for severance; not permanently attached |
| Building, embedded machinery | Immovable | Attached to earth / permanently fastened |
| Government promissory note, shares, debts | Movable | Not immovable; falls under Sec. 3(36) |

**Why the definitions matter:** Since Central Acts frequently use these terms without separately defining them (e.g., in fiscal, registration and procedural statutes), Section 3(26)/(36) supply the operative meaning, ensuring uniform interpretation and, in particular, that intangible **benefits arising out of land** are treated as immovable property.

**Conclusion:** Under the General Clauses Act, immovable property is an **inclusive** category (land, benefits arising out of land, and things attached to/permanently fastened to the earth), while movable property is defined **residually** as all property except immovable property.

*(Full-marks tip: quote Sec. 3(26) and 3(36) exactly, stress that 3(26) is inclusive while 3(36) is by exclusion, and give the "benefits to arise out of land" examples — that is the discriminating content examiners look for.)*

### Q81. Ch: The General Clauses Act, 1897 — Effect of Repeal (Section 6) (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the general effect of the repeal of an enactment as laid down in Section 6 of the General Clauses Act, 1897. What obligations, liabilities and legal proceedings survive a repeal, and subject to what condition does Section 6 apply?

**Answer:**
**Statutory basis and the governing condition — Section 6:** Where any Central Act or Regulation repeals any enactment, then, **unless a different intention appears**, the repeal shall **not** produce the consequences set out in the section. Section 6 thus preserves the past operation of the repealed law and applies only where the repealing Act does not disclose a contrary intention.

**Consequences that a repeal shall NOT have (Section 6(a)–(e)):**
(a) **Revive** anything not in force or existing at the time the repeal takes effect;
(b) Affect the **previous operation** of any enactment so repealed or **anything duly done or suffered** thereunder;
(c) Affect any **right, privilege, obligation or liability acquired, accrued or incurred** under any enactment so repealed;
(d) Affect any **penalty, forfeiture or punishment** incurred in respect of any offence committed against any enactment so repealed;
(e) Affect any **investigation, legal proceeding or remedy** in respect of any such right, privilege, obligation, liability, penalty, forfeiture or punishment; and any such investigation, legal proceeding or remedy **may be instituted, continued or enforced**, and any such penalty, forfeiture or punishment **may be imposed**, **as if the repealing Act or Regulation had not been passed.**

**Explanation:** The object is to prevent a repeal from disturbing rights and liabilities already crystallised. Accrued rights and incurred liabilities survive, and proceedings to enforce them may be begun or continued as though the old law still stood. This statutory saving operates automatically, so a repealing Act need not contain its own saving clause.

**Condition:** All the above operate only **"unless a different intention appears"** in the repealing enactment. If the new Act shows an intention to obliterate accrued rights, Section 6 yields to that intention.

**Conclusion:** Under Section 6, a repeal — absent a contrary intention — does not revive the dead, does not disturb the past operation of the repealed law, and does not extinguish accrued rights, incurred liabilities, penalties or pending proceedings, which may still be instituted, continued and enforced as if the repeal had not occurred.

*(Full-marks tip: reproduce clauses (a)–(e) of Sec. 6 and expressly state the controlling phrase "unless a different intention appears." Omitting the "revive anything not in force" clause (a) or the contrary-intention condition is a common deduction.)*

### Q82. Ch: The General Clauses Act, 1897 — Repeal and Re-enactment (Section 24) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** An old Central Act "A" empowered the Central Government to make rules, under which the "Rules, 2015" were framed and notifications issued. In 2026 Act "A" is **repealed and re-enacted** by a new Central Act "B" which contains a substantially similar rule-making power but does not itself re-issue any rules. Examine, under Section 24 of the General Clauses Act, 1897, whether the "Rules, 2015" and the notifications made under the repealed Act "A" continue in force under the new Act "B", and subject to what conditions.

**Answer:**
The issue is governed by **Section 24** of the General Clauses Act, 1897 — "Continuation of orders, etc., issued under enactments repealed and re-enacted."

**Rule — Section 24:** Where any Central Act or Regulation is, after the commencement of the Act, **repealed and re-enacted** with or without modification, then, **unless it is otherwise expressly provided**, any **appointment, notification, order, scheme, rule, form or bye-law** made or issued under the repealed Act or Regulation shall, **so far as it is not inconsistent with the provisions re-enacted**, **continue in force** and be **deemed to have been made or issued under the provisions so re-enacted**, unless and until it is **superseded** by any appointment, notification, order, scheme, rule, form or bye-law made or issued under the provisions so re-enacted.

**Application to the facts:**
1. Act "A" has been **repealed and re-enacted** by Act "B" — the trigger for Section 24 is satisfied.
2. Act "B" contains a **substantially similar rule-making power**, so the "Rules, 2015" and the notifications relate to a re-enacted provision.
3. Provided the Rules/notifications are **not inconsistent** with Act "B", and Act "B" does not **expressly provide otherwise**, the "Rules, 2015" and the notifications **continue in force** and are **deemed to have been made under Act "B".**
4. This continuance lasts **until they are superseded** by fresh rules/notifications made under Act "B".

Thus the absence of freshly re-issued rules under Act "B" is immaterial; Section 24 supplies statutory continuity so that governance is not interrupted by the repeal-and-re-enactment.

**Conclusion:** The "Rules, 2015" and the notifications **continue in force** and are **deemed to have been made under the re-enacted Act "B"**, so long as (i) Act "B" does not expressly provide otherwise, (ii) they are not inconsistent with Act "B", and (iii) they are not superseded by fresh rules/notifications under Act "B".

### Q83. Ch: The General Clauses Act, 1897 — Power to Make Rules and Commencement (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** With reference to Sections 21 and 22 of the General Clauses Act, 1897, explain (i) the power to add to, amend, vary or rescind orders, rules or bye-laws, and (ii) the power to make rules or bye-laws and to fix their commencement **before** the enactment comes into force. Also state, under Section 5, when a Central Act comes into operation.

**Answer:**
**(i) Power to amend, vary or rescind — Section 21:** Where, by any Central Act or Regulation, a power to **issue notifications, orders, rules or bye-laws** is conferred, then that power **includes a power, exercisable in the like manner and subject to the like sanction and conditions (if any), to add to, amend, vary or rescind** any notifications, orders, rules or bye-laws so issued. The rationale is that the authority which can make a piece of subordinate legislation can also modify or undo it, following the same procedure — the power to create implies the power to alter and repeal.

**(ii) Power to make rules/bye-laws before commencement — Section 22:** Where, by any Central Act or Regulation which is **not to come into force immediately** on its passing, a power is conferred to make rules or bye-laws, or to issue orders with respect to the application of the Act/Regulation, or with respect to the establishment of any court or office or the appointment of any officer, or otherwise for the purpose of giving effect to the Act/Regulation, that power may be **exercised at any time after the passing of the Act/Regulation**; **but** rules, bye-laws or orders so made or issued shall **not take effect till the commencement of the Act or Regulation.** This enables the machinery of the law to be readied in advance while ensuring nothing operates before the parent Act commences.

**When a Central Act comes into operation — Section 5:** Where any Central Act is not expressed to come into operation on a particular day, it shall come into operation on the day on which it **receives the assent** — of the Governor-General (for Acts before the Constitution) or of the **President** (for Acts made after the commencement of the Constitution).

**Conclusion:** Section 21 makes the rule-making power include the power to amend, vary or rescind by the same procedure; Section 22 permits rules/bye-laws to be made after passing but before commencement, though they take effect only on commencement; and, under Section 5, a Central Act with no stated date comes into force on the day it receives the President's assent.

*(Full-marks tip: the discriminating content is Sec. 22's rule that pre-commencement rules "shall not take effect till the commencement of the Act", coupled with Sec. 5 on assent. Pair it with Sec. 21's "like manner, like sanction and conditions" phrase.)*

### Q84. Ch: The General Clauses Act, 1897 — Commencement of Rules Made Before Enactment (Application) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** The "Digital Payments Regulation Act, 2026" was passed and received the President's assent on 10th May 2026, but Section 1 provides that the Act "shall come into force on such date as the Central Government may, by notification, appoint." On 15th June 2026 the Central Government notified 1st July 2026 as the date of commencement of the Act. A company argues that the Rules made on 1st June 2026 are invalid because the parent Act was not yet in force. Decide, citing the General Clauses Act, 1897.

**Answer:**
The dispute turns on **Section 22** (power to make rules before commencement) read with **Section 5** and **Section 3(13)** ("commencement") of the General Clauses Act, 1897.

**Legal position — Section 22:** Where a Central Act is **not to come into force immediately** on its passing but confers a power to make rules/bye-laws or issue orders for giving effect to the Act, that power **may be exercised at any time after the passing of the Act**; however, the rules or bye-laws so made **shall not take effect until the commencement of the Act.** "Commencement," under Section 3(13), means the day on which the Act comes into force.

### Q85. Ch: Interpretation of Statutes — Internal Aid: Proviso (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** "A proviso is added to an enactment to qualify or create an exception to what is in the enactment." Explain the function of a proviso as an internal aid to construction, distinguishing it from an exception and an explanation.

**Answer:** The normal function of a proviso is to except and deal with a case which would otherwise fall within the general language of the main enactment, and its effect is confined to that case alone. A proviso does not travel beyond the provision to which it is appended; it carves out something which, but for the proviso, would have been within the operative part of the section.

The following well-settled propositions govern the construction of a proviso:
1. A proviso must be construed with reference to the preceding parts of the clause to which it is appended, and it operates as an exception only to the main enactment and not to the whole statute.
2. Where the language of the main enactment is clear, the proviso cannot be so interpreted as to enlarge the scope of the section; it must be read down to be confined to the field covered by the main provision.
3. A proviso is not to be construed as stating a general rule; ordinarily it excepts, qualifies or restrains the enacting clause and does not add a new and independent provision — though on rare occasions, as an exception, a proviso may be read as a substantive provision if the legislature has clearly so intended.

The distinction between the three internal aids is as follows:

| Basis | Proviso | Exception | Explanation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Qualifies/carves out a case from the main enactment | Exempts something that would otherwise be covered | Clarifies the meaning of the main provision |
| Placement | Introduced by "Provided that" | Usually worded "except", "nothing in this section" | Introduced by "Explanation" |
| Effect | Confined to the enactment it is appended to | Excludes a specified class from operation | Neither widens nor narrows; it explains and may fill a gap |

**Conclusion:** A proviso is a qualifying or excepting clause whose operation is limited to the enactment to which it is attached, and it must not be construed so as to nullify or enlarge the main provision.

*(Full-marks tip: examiners reward the rule "a proviso is confined to the enactment it is appended to" plus the table distinguishing proviso/exception/explanation; a common deduction is treating a proviso as a general substantive rule without noting it is only an exception "on rare occasions".)*

### Q86. Ch: Interpretation of Statutes — Internal Aid: Explanation (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the objects and the rules of construction of an "Explanation" appended to a section. Can an Explanation override the main provision?

**Answer:** An Explanation is appended to a section with the purpose of explaining the meaning and intendment of the section itself. It is a well-recognised internal aid to construction and is added to remove doubt or ambiguity, or to make the intention of the legislature clear.

The objects and rules of construction of an Explanation are:
1. The object of an Explanation is to explain the meaning and effect of the main provision and to clear up any doubt or ambiguity in it, so that the section may be read harmoniously with the Explanation.
2. An Explanation may be added to include something within, or to exclude something from, the ambit of the main enactment or the connotation of some word occurring in it.
3. An Explanation does not enlarge the scope of the original section which it explains, but only tries to remove confusion and to make the meaning of the enactment clear beyond doubt.
4. The meaning to be given to an Explanation must depend upon its terms, and it must be read so as to harmonise with, and to clear up any ambiguity in, the main section — it should not be so construed as to widen the ambit of the section or to defeat its purpose.

Whether an Explanation can override the main provision: An Explanation, being subordinate and explanatory in character, cannot in the ordinary course override or nullify the main provision. It has to be read as a whole with the main provision to which it is appended and cannot be construed to take away a right conferred, or an obligation imposed, by the substantive provision. Where, however, the Explanation is worded in wide terms and there is a genuine conflict, the Court will attempt a harmonious construction so that both stand together.

**Conclusion:** An Explanation is intended to explain, clarify and harmonise the main provision; it neither enlarges nor curtails the section and cannot override the substantive enactment to which it is attached.

*(Full-marks tip: state all four rules and the harmonious-construction point; marks are lost if the student confuses an Explanation with a proviso — an Explanation clarifies, a proviso excepts.)*

### Q87. Ch: Interpretation of Statutes — Internal Aid: Illustrations (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Discuss the value of "Illustrations" appended to a section as an internal aid to interpretation. What is the position when there is a conflict between the section and an illustration appended to it?

**Answer:** Illustrations are examples appended to a section by the legislature for the purpose of showing how the language of the section applies in practice. They form part of the statute and are of considerable value in the construction of the text of the sections, being regarded as a legislative exposition of the enacting words.

The rules relating to Illustrations as an internal aid are:
1. Illustrations attached to sections are part of the statute and are useful in explaining and elucidating the provisions of law; they help to make the meaning of the section clear by giving practical examples of its operation.
2. Illustrations should not be read as extending or curtailing the ambit of the section which they merely illustrate; they cannot have the effect of modifying the language of the section and they can neither restrict nor enlarge the scope of the section.
3. It is the duty of a Court of law to accept, if it can, the illustration and to try to reconcile it with the language of the section — an effort should always be made to read the section and the illustration harmoniously.

Position on conflict: In the event of a real and irreconcilable conflict between the section and the illustration appended to it, the enacting words of the section will prevail over the illustration. This is because the illustration is only an example of the working of the section and cannot control or override the plain language of the substantive enactment. However, such a conflict is rarely permitted, and every attempt is first made to harmonise the two.

**Conclusion:** Illustrations are a valuable internal aid that exemplify the operation of a section, but where they cannot be reconciled with the clear language of the section, the section prevails.

*(Full-marks tip: the key marks lie in "illustration is part of the statute but the section prevails in case of irreconcilable conflict"; do not confuse illustrations with explanations.)*

### Q88. Ch: Interpretation of Statutes — Internal Aid: Marginal Notes & Headings (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** "Marginal notes and headings are of limited value as aids to construction, whereas the marginal notes to Articles of the Constitution stand on a different footing." Examine this statement and also state the value of a "Heading" prefixed to a section or group of sections.

**Answer:** Marginal notes are the notes printed at the side (margin) of a section of a statute, purporting to summarise the effect of that section, while a heading is prefixed to a section or a group or set of sections to indicate the subject-matter dealt with.

**Marginal notes:** The general rule is that marginal notes appended to a section cannot be used for construing the section. This is because marginal notes are ordinarily inserted by the drafter or printer and are not the product of legislative deliberation; they do not form part of the statute and cannot control the plain meaning of the words of the enactment. Hence, they are of little or no value as aids to construction of an ordinary statute.

However, marginal notes appended to the Articles of the Constitution stand on a different footing. They have been enacted and adopted by the Constituent Assembly as part of the constitutional text, and therefore they may be relied upon as furnishing a clue to, or throwing light on, the meaning and purpose of the Article to which they are attached. Even so, they cannot be used to control the plain language of the Article.

**Headings:** A heading is prefixed to a section or a group of sections and is treated as a preamble to those provisions. The following rules apply:
1. A heading may be regarded as giving the key to the interpretation of the clauses ranged under it, and may be treated as a preamble to those provisions.
2. Where the plain meaning of the enacting words is clear, a heading cannot control, override or cut down that plain meaning; the heading can be resorted to only when the section is ambiguous.
3. The weight of a heading depends on the facts, and a heading is not to be given the same weight as the operative part of the section.

| Aid | Ordinary statute | Constitution |
|---|---|---|
| Marginal note | Of little/no value; cannot control the section | May be used to interpret the Article (enacted by Constituent Assembly) |
| Heading | A preamble/key to the grouped sections; used only if ambiguous | Same principle applies |

**Conclusion:** Marginal notes to an ordinary statute are a weak aid and cannot control the section, though constitutional marginal notes may aid interpretation; a heading serves as a preamble to the sections grouped under it and is resorted to only where the enacting words are ambiguous.

### Q89. Ch: Interpretation of Statutes — External Aid: Dictionary (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** When and to what extent can a dictionary be used as an external aid in interpreting a word used in a statute? Explain the precautions a Court must observe.

**Answer:** A dictionary is an external aid to construction. When a word is not defined in the statute itself, it is permissible to refer to dictionaries to find out the general sense in which that word is understood in common parlance. This is a recognised external aid where the statute does not supply its own definition.

The rules and precautions governing the use of a dictionary are:
1. Dictionaries may be consulted only where the statute does not define the word and where the ordinary/popular meaning of the word is sought; they are useful for ascertaining the general sense of a word.
2. However, in selecting one out of the several meanings given in a dictionary, regard must always be had to the context in which the word is used in the statute. The Court must choose that meaning which fits the context and the object of the enactment, and not any meaning merely because it appears in the dictionary.
3. A dictionary meaning cannot be applied mechanically. The particular meaning of a word depends upon the subject-matter and the setting; a word may have one meaning in ordinary usage and quite another in a technical or statutory context.
4. Where a word has acquired a special or technical meaning, or has been the subject of judicial interpretation, the dictionary meaning yields to that special or judicially settled meaning.

**Conclusion:** A dictionary is a legitimate external aid to ascertain the ordinary meaning of an undefined word, but the meaning selected must accord with the context, subject-matter and object of the statute, and cannot be applied in a mechanical or acontextual manner.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner rewards the caution "meaning must fit the context, not mechanically picked"; simply saying "a dictionary can be used" without the context-caveat loses marks.)*

### Q90. Ch: Interpretation of Statutes — External Aid: Historical Setting & Foreign Decisions (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the following external aids to interpretation of a statute: (i) Historical setting / surrounding circumstances; and (ii) Use of foreign decisions. To what extent may each be relied upon?

**Answer:** External aids are those matters which lie outside the four corners of the statute but which may be looked at to ascertain the intention of the legislature where the statute is ambiguous.

**(i) Historical setting / surrounding circumstances:** The surrounding circumstances and situation which led to the passing of the statute — that is, the state of affairs existing at the time, the history of the legislation, the mischief which the statute was intended to remedy and the object which it sought to achieve — may be taken into account as an external aid to construction. The Court may consider the previous state of the law, the defects which the earlier law failed to cure, and the remedy which Parliament resolved to provide (the "mischief rule" in Heydon's case). This historical background helps the Court to appreciate the purpose behind the enactment and to construe ambiguous words in the light of that purpose. It is, however, an aid to be used only for resolving ambiguity and cannot be used to control the plain and unambiguous language of the statute.

**(ii) Use of foreign decisions:** Decisions of foreign Courts, particularly of countries following the same system of jurisprudence as India (such as England, the United States, Canada and Australia), may be used as an external aid where the Indian statute is in pari materia with the foreign statute, or where the principle involved is a general one. The following cautions apply:
1. Foreign decisions have persuasive value only and are not binding on Indian Courts.
2. Greater weight is given to Indian decisions than to foreign decisions; a foreign decision is to be followed only to the extent that it lays down a sound and applicable principle.
3. Prime importance must be given to the language of the Indian statute, the social conditions of India and the Indian background; a foreign ruling cannot be applied where it conflicts with the express provisions of Indian law or with Indian conditions.

**Conclusion:** Both the historical setting and foreign decisions are legitimate external aids of a persuasive character, to be used only where the statute is ambiguous, and neither can be permitted to override the plain language of the Indian enactment.

*(Full-marks tip: mention Heydon's mischief rule under historical setting and "persuasive, not binding; Indian language and conditions prevail" under foreign decisions — these earn the higher marks.)*

### Q98. Ch: FEMA, 1999 — Adjudication, Appeals & Compounding (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the scheme of adjudication and appeals under FEMA, 1999, covering the Adjudicating Authority, the Special Director (Appeals), the Appellate Tribunal and the High Court. State the time limits for filing appeals.

**Answer:** The Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999 provides a structured mechanism for adjudication of contraventions and for appeals against the orders passed. The scheme, contained in Sections 16 to 35, is as follows:

**1. Adjudicating Authority (Section 16):** For the purpose of adjudication under Section 13, the Central Government appoints Adjudicating Authorities. On a complaint being made, the Adjudicating Authority holds an inquiry after giving the person a reasonable opportunity of being heard, and if satisfied that the person has committed the contravention, may impose the penalty. The person is entitled to appear in person or through a legal practitioner or chartered accountant. The Adjudicating Authority is required to dispose of the complaint, as far as possible, within **one year**.

**2. Appeal to Special Director (Appeals) (Section 17):** Any person aggrieved by an order of the Adjudicating Authority (being an Assistant Director of Enforcement or a Deputy Director of Enforcement) may prefer an appeal to the **Special Director (Appeals)** within **forty-five days** from the date of receipt of the order. The Special Director (Appeals) may confirm, modify or set aside the order after giving the parties a reasonable opportunity of being heard.

**3. Appeal to the Appellate Tribunal (Section 19):** Any person aggrieved by an order of the Adjudicating Authority (other than one appealable to the Special Director (Appeals)) or of the Special Director (Appeals) may appeal to the **Appellate Tribunal** within **forty-five days** from the date of receipt of the order. The appeal must be accompanied by the prescribed fee. The Tribunal may entertain an appeal after the expiry of the period if it is satisfied that there was sufficient cause for the delay. Notably, where the appeal is against an order imposing a penalty, the appellant shall **deposit the amount of the penalty** with such authority; the Tribunal may, however, dispense with such deposit, subject to conditions, to avoid undue hardship. The Tribunal is required to dispose of the appeal, as far as possible, within one year.

**4. Appeal to the High Court (Section 35):** Any person aggrieved by any decision or order of the Appellate Tribunal may file an appeal to the **High Court** within **sixty days** from the date of communication of the decision or order, but only on a **question of law** arising out of such order. The High Court may allow a further period not exceeding sixty days if it is satisfied that the appellant was prevented by sufficient cause from filing within time.

| Stage | Forum | Time limit for appeal |
|---|---|---|
| Adjudication | Adjudicating Authority (Sec 16) | — (disposal within 1 year) |
| First appeal (minor officers' orders) | Special Director (Appeals) (Sec 17) | 45 days |
| Appeal | Appellate Tribunal (Sec 19) | 45 days (extendable for sufficient cause) |
| Final appeal (question of law) | High Court (Sec 35) | 60 days (+ 60 days extendable) |

**Conclusion:** FEMA provides a graded appellate structure — Adjudicating Authority → Special Director (Appeals)/Appellate Tribunal → High Court — with 45 days for appeals to the Special Director (Appeals) and the Tribunal, and 60 days on a question of law to the High Court.

### Q99. Ch: FEMA, 1999 — Residential Status under Section 2(v) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Determine the residential status under FEMA, 1999 (Section 2(v)) of the following persons for the financial year 2025-26:
| Case | Facts |
|---|---|
| (a) | Mr. Alok left India for the first time on 1st August 2025 for employment in Dubai |
| (b) | Ms. Farah, a foreign national, came to India on 1st May 2025 to take up employment with an Indian company |
| (c) | Mr. Rao, an Indian citizen, went abroad on 15th June 2025 for a two-month holiday and returned |
| (d) | A company incorporated in India |

**Answer:** The residential status of a person under FEMA is determined by Section 2(v) of the Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999. A "person resident in India" means, inter alia, a person residing in India for **more than 182 days** during the course of the **preceding financial year**, but does **not** include:
(A) a person who has gone out of India, or who stays outside India, for or on taking up employment outside India, or for carrying on a business or vocation outside India, or for any other purpose indicating his intention to stay outside India for an uncertain period; and
(B) a person who has come to or stays in India, otherwise than for or on taking up employment, business or vocation, or for any other purpose indicating his intention to stay in India for an uncertain period.

Thus, under FEMA, the "purpose/intention" test overrides the mere day-count in the year of arrival or departure; a person acquires or loses residency based on the purpose of his stay, not merely on the number of days.

**(a) Mr. Alok — left for employment abroad:** Although Mr. Alok resided in India for more than 182 days in the preceding financial year, he left India on 1st August 2025 for taking up employment outside India. Under the exclusion in Section 2(v)(A), a person who leaves India for taking up employment abroad ceases to be a person resident in India from the date of his departure. Hence, Mr. Alok becomes a **person resident outside India** with effect from 1st August 2025.

**(b) Ms. Farah — came to India for employment:** A foreign national who comes to India for taking up employment becomes a person resident in India from the date of her arrival, because she has come to India for the purpose of employment (covered by the intention test). Hence, Ms. Farah is a **person resident in India** from 1st May 2025, notwithstanding that she is a foreign citizen and stays less than 182 days in the year of arrival.

**(c) Mr. Rao — went abroad for a holiday:** Mr. Rao went abroad only for a two-month holiday, which is not employment, business, vocation, or a purpose indicating an intention to stay outside India for an uncertain period. His departure is temporary and for a specified purpose. Hence, he continues to be a **person resident in India**.

**(d) Company incorporated in India:** Under Section 2(v)(iii), any person or body corporate registered or incorporated in India is a person resident in India. Hence, the company is a **person resident in India**.

**Conclusion:** (a) Resident outside India from 01.08.2025; (b) Resident in India from 01.05.2025; (c) Resident in India; (d) Resident in India.

*(Full-marks tip: the key FEMA insight is that "purpose/intention" trumps the 182-day count in the year of arrival/departure — leaving for employment makes one non-resident immediately, and coming for employment makes one resident immediately; a day-count-only answer loses marks.)*

### Q100. Ch: FEMA, 1999 — Capital vs Current Account Transactions & Section 6 (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between a "capital account transaction" and a "current account transaction" under FEMA, 1999. Explain the regulatory approach of Section 6 towards capital account transactions and give examples of each type of transaction.

**Answer:** The Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999 classifies foreign exchange transactions into two categories — capital account transactions and current account transactions — and adopts a different regulatory approach to each.

**Capital account transaction — Section 2(e):** A "capital account transaction" means a transaction which alters the assets or liabilities, including contingent liabilities, outside India of persons resident in India, or the assets or liabilities in India of persons resident outside India. In short, it affects the asset-liability position (the capital account) of the person concerned. It includes transactions specified under Section 6(3).

**Current account transaction — Section 2(j):** A "current account transaction" means a transaction other than a capital account transaction, and includes, without limitation: (i) payments due in connection with foreign trade, other current business, services, and short-term banking and credit facilities in the ordinary course of business; (ii) payments due as interest on loans and as net income from investments; (iii) remittances for living expenses of parents, spouse and children residing abroad; and (iv) expenses in connection with foreign travel, education and medical care of parents, spouse and children.

**Regulatory approach of Section 6:** Section 6 draws a fundamental distinction in the treatment of the two categories:
- **Current account transactions (Section 6 general rule read with Section 5):** These are **freely permitted**, and any person may sell or draw foreign exchange to or from an authorised person for a current account transaction, subject only to reasonable restrictions imposed by the Central Government in public interest (the Current Account Transaction Rules, 2000).
- **Capital account transactions (Section 6(2), 6(2A) etc.):** These are **permitted only to the extent allowed** by the RBI/Central Government. The Reserve Bank/Central Government may, in consultation with each other, specify the classes of permissible capital account transactions and the limits up to which foreign exchange may be drawn. A capital account transaction not so permitted is not allowed. The rule-making power now enables the Central Government to prescribe permissible capital account transactions (including through the Non-Debt Instruments Rules and Debt Instruments Regulations).

| Basis | Capital account transaction | Current account transaction |
|---|---|---|
| Definition section | Section 2(e) | Section 2(j) |
| Effect | Alters assets/liabilities outside India of a resident, or in India of a non-resident | Does not alter such assets/liabilities |
| Regulatory rule | Permitted only to the extent allowed (Section 6) | Freely permitted (Section 5), save reasonable restrictions |
| Examples | Investment in shares/property abroad, borrowing/lending in forex, acquisition of immovable property abroad, ECBs | Import/export payments, travel, education, medical expenses, interest payments |

**Conclusion:** A capital account transaction alters the asset-liability position and is permitted only to the extent specifically allowed under Section 6, whereas a current account transaction is any other transaction and is freely permitted under Section 5, subject only to reasonable public-interest restrictions.

*(Full-marks tip: cite Section 2(e) and 2(j) for the definitions and the "freely permitted vs permitted only to the extent allowed" contrast under Sections 5 and 6; the comparison table with correct examples of each carries the bulk of the marks.)*
