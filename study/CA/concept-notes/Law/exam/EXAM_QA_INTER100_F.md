### Q1. Ch: Preliminary & Definitions — Holding-Subsidiary Relationship on Facts (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Examine, with reference to the definitions in the Companies Act, 2013, whether a holding-subsidiary relationship exists in each of the following independent situations:

| Case | Facts |
|------|-------|
| (i) | Alpha Ltd. holds 55% of the total voting power (equity) of Beta Ltd. |
| (ii) | Gamma Ltd. holds only 30% of the equity of Delta Ltd. but, under the articles of Delta Ltd., Gamma Ltd. has the right to appoint 5 out of the 8 directors on the Board of Delta Ltd. |
| (iii) | Epsilon Ltd. is a subsidiary of Delta Ltd. (in case ii above). Is Epsilon Ltd. a subsidiary of Gamma Ltd.? |
| (iv) | Sigma Ltd. holds 45% of the total voting power of Omega Ltd. and, in addition, its wholly-owned subsidiary Tau Ltd. holds a further 10% of Omega Ltd. |

**Answer:**
As per **Section 2(87) of the Companies Act, 2013**, a "subsidiary company" means a company in which the holding company **(i)** controls the composition of the Board of Directors; **or (ii)** exercises or controls more than one-half of the total voting power, either on its own or together with one or more of its subsidiary companies. As per the Explanation to the section, a company shall be deemed to be a subsidiary company of the holding company even if the control is of another subsidiary company of the holding company (chain relationship), and "control of composition of Board" means the power to appoint or remove all or a majority of the directors.

Correspondingly, under **Section 2(46)**, a "holding company" is a company of which such other companies are subsidiary companies.

Applying the law to the facts:

| Case | Analysis | Conclusion |
|------|----------|------------|
| (i) | Alpha Ltd. controls more than one-half (55% > 50%) of the total voting power of Beta Ltd. — limb (ii) of Sec 2(87) is satisfied. | Beta is a subsidiary of Alpha. |
| (ii) | Though Gamma holds only 30% of voting power, it can appoint 5 of 8 directors, i.e. a **majority** of the Board — limb (i) (control of composition of Board) is satisfied. | Delta is a subsidiary of Gamma. |
| (iii) | Epsilon is a subsidiary of Delta, and Delta is itself a subsidiary of Gamma. By the Explanation (chain), a subsidiary of a subsidiary is a subsidiary of the ultimate holding company. | Epsilon is a subsidiary of Gamma. |
| (iv) | Sigma on its own holds 45% but, together with its subsidiary Tau's 10%, controls 55% (> 50%) of the total voting power of Omega — voting power "together with its subsidiary" is expressly counted under Sec 2(87)(ii). | Omega is a subsidiary of Sigma. |

**Conclusion:** In cases (i), (ii), (iii) and (iv) a holding-subsidiary relationship exists.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards correct citation of Section 2(87)/2(46), correctly distinguishing the "composition of Board" limb from the "voting power" limb, and applying the Explanation for the chain relationship in (iii) and the aggregation with a subsidiary in (iv). Common deduction: treating 30% shareholding in (ii) as decisive and missing the Board-composition test.)*

### Q2. Ch: Preliminary & Definitions — Associate Company vs Subsidiary (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** PQR Ltd. acquires 22% of the total voting power of XYZ Ltd. Under a shareholders' agreement, PQR Ltd. also has the right to participate in the financial and operating policy decisions of XYZ Ltd. but not control over them. Separately, PQR Ltd. and LMN Ltd. jointly form a company, JV Ltd., under a joint-venture arrangement, each holding 50%. Determine whether XYZ Ltd. and JV Ltd. are "associate companies" of PQR Ltd. under the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:**
As per **Section 2(6) of the Companies Act, 2013**, an "associate company", in relation to another company, means a company in which that other company has **significant influence**, but which is not a subsidiary of the company having such influence, and includes a **joint venture** company.

The Explanation to Section 2(6) clarifies that:
(a) **"significant influence"** means control of **at least 20% of the total voting power**, or control of or participation in business decisions under an agreement; and
(b) **"joint venture"** means a joint arrangement whereby the parties that have joint control of the arrangement have rights to the net assets of the arrangement.

Applying the law:

| Entity | Analysis | Conclusion |
|--------|----------|------------|
| XYZ Ltd. | PQR holds 22% voting power (≥ 20%) and also participates in policy decisions under an agreement — significant influence exists. As 22% is not more than one-half, XYZ is **not** a subsidiary. | XYZ is an **associate company** of PQR. |
| JV Ltd. | JV Ltd. is a jointly-controlled joint-venture company; a joint venture is expressly included in the definition of associate company. | JV Ltd. is an **associate company** of PQR. |

**Conclusion:** Both XYZ Ltd. and JV Ltd. are associate companies of PQR Ltd. within the meaning of Section 2(6).

*(Full-marks tip: State the 20% threshold and the alternative "participation under an agreement" limb, and remember that a joint venture is deemed an associate. Deduction for confusing "significant influence" (associate) with "control" (subsidiary).)*

### Q3. Ch: Preliminary & Definitions — Private vs Public Company Privileges (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** "A private company enjoys certain privileges and exemptions which are not available to a public company." In light of the Companies Act, 2013, distinguish between a private company and a public company, and state any four privileges/exemptions available to a private company.

**Answer:**
As per **Section 2(68) of the Companies Act, 2013**, a "private company" means a company having a minimum paid-up capital as may be prescribed, and which by its articles **(a)** restricts the right to transfer its shares; **(b)** limits the number of its members to **200** (excluding present and past employee-members and joint holders being counted as one); and **(c)** prohibits any invitation to the public to subscribe for any securities of the company.

As per **Section 2(71)**, a "public company" means a company which is not a private company, and which has such minimum paid-up capital as may be prescribed. A company which is a subsidiary of a company (not being a private company) shall be deemed to be a public company even where it continues to be a private company in its articles.

**Distinction:**

| Basis | Private Company | Public Company |
|-------|-----------------|----------------|
| Maximum members | 200 [Sec 2(68)] | No limit |
| Minimum members [Sec 3] | 2 | 7 |
| Minimum directors [Sec 149] | 2 | 3 |
| Transfer of shares | Restricted by articles | Freely transferable [Sec 44] |
| Invitation to public | Prohibited | Permitted (via prospectus) |
| Suffix | "Private Limited" | "Limited" |

**Four privileges/exemptions available to a private company:**
1. It can commence with a **minimum of 2 members and 2 directors**, whereas a public company needs 7 members and 3 directors.
2. It is **not required to appoint independent directors** under Section 149(4).
3. Certain provisions relating to **acceptance of deposits from members** (Sec 73(2)(a) to (e)) are relaxed for eligible private companies as per notifications.
4. It can issue shares on a **preferential/rights basis with lesser procedural compliance**, and provisions such as Section 43 (kinds of share capital) and Section 47 (voting rights) do not apply where the memorandum/articles so provide (per MCA exemption notification).

**Conclusion:** A private company, by restricting transfer, capping membership at 200 and prohibiting public invitation, enjoys the above privileges denied to a public company.

*(Full-marks tip: Give the exact three "restrictive clauses" of Sec 2(68), a clean comparison table, and cite specific exemption sections. Deduction for listing member limit as 50 (old Act) instead of 200.)*

### Q4. Ch: Preliminary & Definitions — Small Company & One Person Company (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Define a "small company" and a "One Person Company (OPC)" under the Companies Act, 2013. State any two companies which can never be treated as a small company.

**Answer:**
As per **Section 2(85) of the Companies Act, 2013**, a "small company" means a company, **other than a public company**, whose **paid-up share capital does not exceed ₹4 crore** (or such higher amount as prescribed, not exceeding ₹10 crore) and whose **turnover** as per the profit and loss account for the immediately preceding financial year **does not exceed ₹40 crore** (or such higher amount as prescribed, not exceeding ₹100 crore).

As per **Section 2(62)**, a "One Person Company" means a company which has **only one person as a member**. As per Section 3, an OPC is a type of private company that can be formed by one person, and the memorandum must nominate another person as nominee to become a member in the event of the subscriber's death or incapacity.

**Companies that can never be a small company** (proviso to Sec 2(85)):
1. A **holding company or a subsidiary company**;
2. A company **registered under Section 8** (not-for-profit company);
3. A company or body corporate **governed by any special Act**.

**Conclusion:** The classification depends on both the capital and turnover thresholds being satisfied, subject to the exclusions above.

*(Full-marks tip: Use the current thresholds (₹4 crore / ₹40 crore) — the examiner deducts for using the older ₹50 lakh/₹2 crore limits. Note both capital AND turnover conditions are cumulative.)*

### Q5. Ch: Preliminary & Definitions — Subsidiary through Control of Board & Layers (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** M Ltd. (a public company) has 6 directors on its Board. Under an agreement, N Ltd. has the power to appoint 4 of these 6 directors, though N Ltd. holds no shares in M Ltd. Advise whether M Ltd. is a subsidiary of N Ltd. Also state the restriction, if any, on the number of layers of subsidiaries that a company may have.

**Answer:**
As per **Section 2(87)(i) of the Companies Act, 2013**, a company is a subsidiary of the holding company if the holding company **controls the composition of the Board of Directors**. The Explanation to Section 2(87) provides that the composition of a company's Board shall be deemed to be controlled by another company if that other company, by the exercise of some power exercisable at its discretion, can **appoint or remove all or a majority of the directors**.

**Application:** N Ltd. can appoint 4 out of 6 directors of M Ltd., i.e. a **majority** of the Board. It is immaterial that N Ltd. holds no shares in M Ltd., because control over Board composition is an independent test, distinct from control of voting power. Therefore, M Ltd. is a **subsidiary** of N Ltd.

**Restriction on layers of subsidiaries:** As per the **proviso to Section 2(87)** read with the **Companies (Restriction on Number of Layers) Rules, 2017**, a company (other than certain exempted classes such as banking companies, NBFCs, insurance companies and Government companies) **shall not have more than two layers of subsidiaries**. However, this restriction does not affect a company from acquiring a company incorporated outside India with subsidiaries beyond two layers as per the laws of that foreign country, and one layer consisting of a wholly-owned subsidiary is not counted.

**Conclusion:** M Ltd. is a subsidiary of N Ltd. by virtue of control over the composition of its Board, and a company may generally not have more than two layers of subsidiaries.

*(Full-marks tip: Stress that shareholding is irrelevant when Board-composition control exists, and cite the two-layer rule with its wholly-owned-subsidiary carve-out. Deduction for saying no relationship exists merely because N holds no shares.)*

### Q6. Ch: Preliminary & Definitions — Body Corporate & Deemed Public Company (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** With reference to the Companies Act, 2013, explain the term "body corporate". Also explain when a private company is deemed to be a public company.

**Answer:**
As per **Section 2(11) of the Companies Act, 2013**, a "body corporate" or "corporation" includes a company incorporated **outside India**, but does **not** include:
(i) a **co-operative society** registered under any law relating to co-operative societies; and
(ii) any other body corporate (not being a company as defined in the Act) which the Central Government may, by notification, specify.

Thus, a body corporate is a wider term than "company"; it covers Indian companies, foreign companies, statutory corporations and LLPs, but excludes co-operative societies and notified bodies.

**Deemed public company:** As per the proviso to **Section 2(71)**, a company which is a **subsidiary of a company, not being a private company**, shall be **deemed to be a public company** for the purposes of the Act, even where such subsidiary company continues to be a private company in its articles. Consequently, such a subsidiary must comply with the provisions applicable to a public company.

**Conclusion:** A body corporate is a broad category of legal persons excluding co-operative societies, and a private company that is a subsidiary of a public company is treated as a public company.

*(Full-marks tip: Remember the two specific exclusions from "body corporate" and the deemed-public-company proviso — both are frequently tested one-liners. Deduction for omitting the co-operative society exclusion.)*

### Q7. Ch: Incorporation of Company — Memorandum as Public Document & Constructive Notice (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** The Memorandum of Association of Vishnu Traders Ltd. contains an object clause authorising the company to carry on the business of trading in textiles only. The Board of the company enters into a contract with Mr. Rakesh for the purchase of a fleet of trucks to start a transportation business, which is not covered by the objects. Mr. Rakesh, without inspecting the memorandum, supplies the trucks and now demands payment. Examine the validity of the contract and Mr. Rakesh's remedy, with reference to the doctrine of constructive notice.

**Answer:**
As per **Section 4 of the Companies Act, 2013**, the memorandum of a company must state its objects, and the company can act only within the scope of the objects and powers conferred by the memorandum. As per **Section 399**, the memorandum and articles, once registered, are **public documents** open to inspection by any person on payment of the prescribed fee.

**Doctrine of constructive notice:** Because the memorandum and articles are public documents, every person dealing with the company is **deemed to have notice** of their contents, whether or not he has actually read them. He is presumed not only to have read the documents but also to have understood their proper meaning. Consequently, if a person enters into a transaction which is beyond the objects/powers set out in the memorandum, he cannot plead ignorance of that fact.

**Doctrine of ultra vires:** An act which is beyond the objects clause of the memorandum is **ultra vires the company** and is **void ab initio**; it cannot be ratified even by the unanimous consent of all the shareholders. Neither the company nor the other party can enforce such a contract.

**Application:** The purchase of trucks to start a transportation business is **outside** the textile-trading objects of Vishnu Traders Ltd. and is therefore **ultra vires the company** and void. By virtue of the doctrine of constructive notice, Mr. Rakesh is deemed to have notice of the object clause, and his failure to actually inspect the memorandum is no excuse. He cannot enforce payment against the company for an ultra vires contract.

**Conclusion:** The contract is void as ultra vires the company, and Mr. Rakesh has no remedy against the company for the price of the trucks under the doctrine of constructive notice.

*(Full-marks tip: Link Sec 399 (public document) → constructive notice → ultra vires → void ab initio, and conclude that no ratification is possible. Deduction for allowing the outsider to recover on grounds of good faith — constructive notice bars that.)*

### Q8. Ch: Incorporation of Company — Doctrine of Indoor Management (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** The articles of Sunrise Ltd. authorise the Board of Directors to borrow money by passing a resolution at a duly convened Board meeting. Mr. Ashwin, a lender, advances ₹20 lakh to the company on the strength of a document signed by two directors, who represented that the necessary Board resolution had been passed. In fact, no such resolution was ever passed. The company now refuses to repay, contending that the borrowing was unauthorised. Advise Mr. Ashwin, referring to the doctrine of indoor management and its exceptions.

**Answer:**
As per the **doctrine of indoor management** (the rule in **Royal British Bank v. Turquand**), while persons dealing with a company are deemed to have notice of the company's public documents (memorandum and articles) by virtue of the doctrine of constructive notice, they are **entitled to assume that the internal proceedings and formalities required by those documents have been duly complied with**. An outsider is not bound to enquire into the regularity of the company's internal management, since such matters are not disclosed by the public documents.

**Application:** The articles of Sunrise Ltd. **permit** borrowing subject to a Board resolution. Mr. Ashwin, on reading the articles, would find that the directors had the power to borrow if a resolution was passed. Whether such a resolution was **actually** passed is a matter of **internal management**, into which Mr. Ashwin was not bound to enquire. He was entitled to assume that the internal requirement (passing of the resolution) had been duly complied with. Therefore, prima facie, the company is bound and Mr. Ashwin can recover ₹20 lakh.

**Exceptions to the doctrine (where protection is NOT available):**

| Exception | Effect |
|-----------|--------|
| Knowledge of irregularity | An outsider who **knows** of the irregularity cannot claim protection. |
| Negligence / suspicion of irregularity | Where the circumstances are suspicious and the outsider fails to make enquiry. |
| Forgery | The doctrine does not protect a person relying on a **forged** document, which is void ab initio. |
| Act outside apparent authority | Where the act is beyond even the apparent authority of the officer. |
| No knowledge of articles | A person who has not read the articles cannot rely on what they contain. |

**Conclusion:** As Mr. Ashwin had no knowledge of the irregularity and the borrowing was within the powers permitted by the articles, the doctrine of indoor management protects him and he can enforce repayment of ₹20 lakh — unless the company proves that he knew of the absence of the resolution.

*(Full-marks tip: Cite Turquand's case, distinguish indoor management from constructive notice, and list the exceptions. Deduction for treating this like Q7 and denying recovery — here the act was permitted by the articles.)*

### Q9. Ch: Incorporation of Company — Alteration of Registered Office Between States (Marks: 8) [Application]
**Question:** Sagar Industries Ltd., a company registered in the State of Maharashtra, wishes to shift its registered office to the State of Gujarat to be nearer to its principal manufacturing facility. Explain the procedure to be followed under the Companies Act, 2013 for shifting the registered office of a company from one State to another, and state the authorities whose approval is required.

**Answer:**
A shift of the registered office of a company **from one State to another** necessarily involves an **alteration of the memorandum**, since the memorandum contains the "registered office clause" (the State in which the registered office is situated). This is governed by **Section 13** read with **Section 12** of the Companies Act, 2013 and the relevant rules.

**Procedure:**

1. **Board Meeting:** Convene a Board meeting to approve the proposal and fix the date, time and place for the general meeting.

2. **Special Resolution [Sec 13(1)]:** The alteration of the memorandum requires a **special resolution** passed at a general meeting of the members.

3. **Central Government approval [Sec 13(4)]:** An alteration of the registered office clause that involves shifting from one State to another shall **not have effect unless it is approved by the Central Government**, on an application made in the prescribed form. This power of the Central Government has been **delegated to the Regional Director (RD)**. The application is made in **Form INC-23**.

4. **Notice/advertisement and objections [Sec 13(4) & Rules]:** Before the RD disposes of the application:
   - The company must **advertise** the application in a vernacular newspaper and an English newspaper;
   - Give **individual notice** to each **debenture-holder, depositor and creditor** of the company;
   - Serve notice on the **Registrar (ROC)**, the **SEBI** (in case of a listed company) and the **regulatory authority**, if any, and on the **Chief Secretary of the State Government** where the registered office is situated, so that no employee or revenue interest of the State is prejudiced.

5. **Disposal by RD [Sec 13(5)/(6)]:** The Central Government (RD) shall dispose of the application within **sixty days** and, before passing the order, must satisfy itself that the alteration has the **consent of the creditors, debenture-holders and other persons** concerned, or that sufficient provision has been made for their dues.

6. **Filing of the order with both ROCs [Sec 13(7)]:** A **certified copy of the order** of the Central Government approving the alteration, together with the **altered memorandum**, shall be filed by the company with the **Registrar of each of the States** (both the State from which and the State to which the office is transferred) within the prescribed time.

7. **Fresh Certificate:** The **Registrar of the State to which the office is shifted** shall register the same and issue a **fresh certificate of incorporation** indicating the alteration.

**Authorities whose approval/notice is required:** (i) Members (special resolution); (ii) Central Government / Regional Director (approval); with notice to (iii) Registrar of Companies (both States); (iv) Chief Secretary of the State; (v) creditors/debenture-holders/depositors; and (vi) SEBI, if the company is listed.

**Conclusion:** Sagar Industries Ltd. can shift its registered office from Maharashtra to Gujarat only after passing a special resolution under Section 13 and obtaining the approval of the Central Government (Regional Director) in Form INC-23, followed by filing the RD's order with the Registrars of both States.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the sequence special resolution → RD approval (delegated CG power) → advertisement/notice to creditors and Chief Secretary → filing with both ROCs → fresh certificate. Deduction for confusing this with a same-State shift (which needs no CG approval) or omitting creditor safeguards.)*

### Q10. Ch: Incorporation of Company — Alteration of Articles & Entrenchment (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the provisions of the Companies Act, 2013 relating to the alteration of Articles of Association. What is meant by an "entrenchment provision" in the articles?

**Answer:**
As per **Section 14 of the Companies Act, 2013**, a company may, by passing a **special resolution**, alter its articles including alterations having the effect of **converting a private company into a public company or vice versa**. Where such alteration converts a public company into a private company, the alteration shall **not take effect except with the approval of the Central Government** (power delegated to the Regional Director).

A copy of the altered articles, together with the special resolution, must be filed with the **Registrar within fifteen days**, and the Registrar shall register the same.

**Limitations on alteration:** The power to alter articles is subject to the following:
(i) The alteration must not be inconsistent with the **provisions of the Act** or the **conditions in the memorandum**;
(ii) It must not be illegal or opposed to public policy;
(iii) It must be **for the benefit of the company as a whole** and must not amount to a fraud on the minority;
(iv) It cannot compel a member to **take more shares** or increase his liability without his written consent [Sec 38 principle].

**Entrenchment provision [Sec 5(3) & 5(4)]:** The articles may contain **"entrenchment" provisions** to the effect that **specified provisions of the articles may be altered only if conditions or procedures more restrictive than those applicable to a special resolution are met and complied with** (for example, requiring the consent of all the members). Such provisions can be made only **on formation** of the company, or **by amendment agreed to by all the members** (private company) or **by a special resolution** (public company). Notice of the entrenchment provisions must be given to the Registrar in the prescribed form.

**Conclusion:** Articles can be freely altered by special resolution subject to the Act, the memorandum and the limitations above, and entrenchment provisions allow the members to make certain articles more difficult to change.

*(Full-marks tip: Cite Sec 14 for alteration and Sec 5(3)/(4) for entrenchment, and note the CG approval needed for public-to-private conversion. Deduction for missing the "more restrictive than a special resolution" essence of entrenchment.)*

### Q11. Ch: Incorporation of Company — Corporate Personality & Lifting the Veil (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Mr. Kapoor incorporated a private company, Kapoor Exports Pvt. Ltd., of which he is the majority shareholder and managing director. He then transferred his personal insurance policy on the company's timber stock to the company, and later sought to claim the loss from the company's insurer following a fire. The insurer contended that Mr. Kapoor, as the real owner, could not hide behind the company. Discuss the principle of separate legal personality and when the corporate veil may be lifted.

**Answer:**
As per **Section 9 of the Companies Act, 2013**, from the date of incorporation mentioned in the certificate of incorporation, the subscribers to the memorandum and other members shall be a **body corporate** capable of exercising all the functions of an incorporated company, having **perpetual succession** and a **common seal (now optional)**, with power to hold property and to sue and be sued in its own name.

**Separate legal personality:** The leading case of **Salomon v. Salomon & Co. Ltd.** established that, on incorporation, a company becomes a **legal person distinct from its members**, even where one person effectively controls the whole undertaking. The company's property is its own and not that of the shareholders; a member has **no insurable interest** in the property of the company (**Macaura v. Northern Assurance Co. Ltd.**).

**Application:** Once the timber stock was transferred to the company, it became the **company's property**. Mr. Kapoor, though the majority shareholder and managing director, has **no insurable interest** in the company's assets in his personal capacity. A policy taken by him personally cannot cover the company's loss. The corporate personality operates **in his favour on this point** — the property belongs to a separate legal person.

**Lifting the corporate veil:** In certain circumstances the courts and statute disregard the separate personality and look at the persons behind the company, e.g. where the company is used for **fraud or improper conduct**, for **evasion of tax or legal obligations**, as a **mere sham/façade**, to **evade liabilities**, or under **statutory provisions** (such as Sec 7(7) for incorporation by furnishing false information, Sec 339 for fraudulent conduct of business, or misrepresentation in a prospectus).

**Conclusion:** Owing to separate legal personality, the timber belongs to the company and Mr. Kapoor has no insurable interest; the personal policy will not respond. The veil is lifted only in exceptional cases such as fraud, sham or statutory command.

*(Full-marks tip: Cite Salomon and Macaura, and give at least three grounds for lifting the veil. Deduction for allowing the personal claim — the Macaura "no insurable interest" point is the crux.)*

### Q12. Ch: Incorporation of Company — Commencement of Business (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the conditions to be satisfied by a company having a share capital before it can commence business or exercise any borrowing powers under the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:**
As per **Section 10A of the Companies Act, 2013** (inserted by the Companies (Amendment) Act, 2019), a company **incorporated after the commencement of the 2019 Amendment and having a share capital** shall **not commence any business or exercise any borrowing powers** unless:

1. A **declaration** is filed by a director within **180 days** of the date of incorporation, in the prescribed form (Form INC-20A), with the Registrar, stating that **every subscriber to the memorandum has paid the value of the shares** agreed to be taken by him as on the date of the declaration; and

2. The company has **filed with the Registrar a verification of its registered office** as provided under **Section 12(2)** (i.e., within 30 days of incorporation).

**Consequences of default [Sec 10A(2) & (3)]:**
- The **company** is liable to a penalty of **₹50,000**, and every **officer in default** is liable to a penalty of **₹1,000 for each day of default**, subject to a maximum of **₹1,00,000**.
- Where no declaration is filed within 180 days and the Registrar has reasonable cause to believe that the company is **not carrying on any business**, he may initiate action for the **removal of the name** of the company from the register.

**Conclusion:** A company with share capital must file the declaration of subscription money (INC-20A) and verify its registered office before it can lawfully commence business or borrow.

*(Full-marks tip: Cite Sec 10A, the 180-day declaration and the registered-office verification, plus the penalty and strike-off consequence. Deduction for reproducing the omitted certificate-of-commencement provisions of the old Act.)*

### Q13. Ch: Prospectus & Allotment of Securities — Allotment without Minimum Subscription (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Bright Star Ltd. issued a prospectus offering 10,00,000 equity shares of ₹10 each to the public, stating the minimum subscription as 90% of the issue. By the end of 30 days from the issue of the prospectus, applications for only 7,50,000 shares had been received. Ignoring the SEBI position, the directors proceeded to allot shares to the applicants. Examine the validity of the allotment and the consequences under the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:**
As per **Section 39(1) of the Companies Act, 2013**, no allotment of any securities of a company offered to the public for subscription shall be made unless the amount stated in the prospectus as the **minimum subscription** has been subscribed and the sums payable on application have been received by the company.

As per **Section 39(2)**, the amount payable on application on every security shall **not be less than 5% of the nominal amount of the security** or such other percentage/amount as may be specified by SEBI.

As per **Section 39(3)** read with the Companies (Prospectus and Allotment of Securities) Rules, if the **stated minimum subscription is not received within 30 days** of the date of issue of the prospectus (or such period as specified by SEBI), the **application money must be repaid** within the period prescribed; if repayment is not made within the prescribed time, the **directors** shall be **jointly and severally liable to repay** the money with interest at the prescribed rate (15% p.a.).

**Working:**

| Particulars | Amount |
|-------------|--------|
| Total issue | 10,00,000 shares |
| Minimum subscription (90% of issue) | 9,00,000 shares |
| Applications actually received | 7,50,000 shares |
| Shortfall | 1,50,000 shares |

**Application:** The minimum subscription stated in the prospectus is 9,00,000 shares (90%), but only 7,50,000 shares have been subscribed — the minimum subscription has **not** been received. Therefore, under Section 39(1), the company **cannot** make any allotment. The allotment made by the directors is **irregular/void**, and the company is bound to **refund the application money** received. If the money is not refunded within the prescribed period, the directors become **jointly and severally liable** to repay it with interest at 15% p.a.

**Penalty [Sec 39(5)]:** For default in complying with Section 39, the company and every officer in default are liable to a penalty of **₹1,000 for each day** during which the default continues or **₹1,00,000**, whichever is **less**.

**Conclusion:** The allotment is invalid for want of minimum subscription; the application money must be refunded, failing which the directors are personally liable with interest, and a penalty under Section 39(5) is attracted.

### Q15. Ch: Prospectus & Allotment of Securities — Civil Liability for Untrue Statement (Marks: 8) [Application]

Examine his remedies and the persons liable under the Companies Act, 2013, and state the defences available to the directors.

**Answer:**
Where a prospectus contains an **untrue or misleading statement**, both **civil** and **criminal** liability may arise. Mr. Suresh's remedies are as follows.

**Remedies against the company (rescission and damages):**
Mr. Suresh, as an allottee who subscribed on the faith of the prospectus, may:
(i) **Rescind the contract of allotment** and claim a refund of his money, on the ground of misrepresentation, provided he acts promptly and before the company goes into liquidation; and
(ii) Claim **damages** against the company for the loss suffered on account of the fraudulent misrepresentation.

**Civil liability of the directors and others [Section 35]:**
As per **Section 35(1) of the Companies Act, 2013**, where a person has subscribed for securities of a company acting on any statement included, or the inclusion or omission of any matter, in the prospectus which is **misleading**, and has sustained loss or damage as a consequence, the following persons shall be **liable to pay compensation** to every person who has sustained such loss or damage:
(a) every **director** of the company at the time of issue of the prospectus;
(b) every person who has **authorised himself to be named** as a director;
(c) every **promoter** of the company;
(d) every person who has **authorised the issue** of the prospectus; and
(e) an **expert** referred to in Section 26(5).

**Fraud liability [Section 35(3) & 447]:** Where it is proved that the prospectus was issued with **intent to defraud** the applicants or for any fraudulent purpose, every person referred to above shall be **personally responsible, without any limitation of liability**, for all or any of the losses or damages, and may also be liable for **fraud under Section 447**.

**Criminal liability [Section 34]:** As the statements were false to the directors' knowledge, the persons who authorised the issue of such prospectus are also liable for **fraud under Section 447** (criminal liability for misstatement in prospectus).

**Defences available to a director [Section 35(2)]:** A director shall **not be liable** if he proves:
(a) that, having consented to become a director, he **withdrew his consent** before the issue of the prospectus, and that it was issued without his authority or consent; or
(b) that the prospectus was issued **without his knowledge or consent**, and on becoming aware of its issue, he forthwith gave **reasonable public notice** that it was issued without his knowledge or consent; or
(c) that, as regards every untrue statement, he had **reasonable grounds to believe, and did up to the time of allotment believe, that the statement was true**, or that the statement was a correct copy of / based on an expert's statement whom he reasonably believed competent.

The directors may also face liability for fraud under Sections 34 and 447.

### Q16. Ch: Prospectus & Allotment of Securities — Expert's Statement & Misstatement Defence (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** The prospectus of Horizon Ltd. included a statement purporting to be made by Mr. Verma, a valuer, regarding the value of the company's land. The statement was untrue. Examine the liability of Mr. Verma (the expert) and the conditions governing inclusion of an expert's statement in a prospectus under the Companies Act, 2013.

(i) he had given his consent but **withdrew it in writing before delivery of a copy of the prospectus** to the Registrar; or
(ii) the prospectus was issued **without his knowledge or consent** and, on becoming aware of its issue, he forthwith gave reasonable public notice that it was issued without his knowledge or consent; or

The liability for including the statement without his valid consent would fall on the company and its directors. If, however, his consent subsisted at the time of registration, he would be liable under Section 35.

### Q17. Ch: Prospectus & Allotment of Securities — Private Placement & Deemed Prospectus (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the meaning of "private placement" under the Companies Act, 2013 and state the key conditions for a valid private placement of securities. Also explain the concept of a "deemed prospectus".

**Answer:**
**Private placement [Section 42]:** As per **Section 42 of the Companies Act, 2013**, "private placement" means any **offer or invitation to subscribe or issue of securities to a select group of persons** by a company (other than by way of a public offer) through the issue of a **private placement offer-cum-application letter (Form PAS-4)**, and which satisfies the conditions specified in the section.

**Key conditions for a valid private placement:**
1. The offer may be made only to a select group of persons **identified by the Board**, whose number shall **not exceed 200** in the aggregate in a financial year (excluding qualified institutional buyers and employees under ESOP), **per kind of security**.
2. The company shall not make a fresh offer unless the **allotment on an earlier offer has been completed, withdrawn or abandoned**.
3. The application money shall be paid through **cheque, demand draft or other banking channels, not in cash**, and kept in a **separate bank account**.
4. Allotment must be completed within **60 days** of receipt of the application money; if not, the money must be **repaid within 15 days**, failing which interest at **12% p.a.** is payable.
5. The company shall file a **return of allotment in Form PAS-3** with the Registrar within **15 days** of allotment, and shall **not utilise the money** until the return is filed.
6. There shall be **no public advertisement** or use of media/marketing channels to inform the public.

**Deemed prospectus [Section 25]:** Where a company **allots or agrees to allot** securities with a view to those securities being **offered for sale to the public**, any document by which the offer for sale to the public is made (issued by the issuing house/intermediary) shall, for all purposes, be **deemed to be a prospectus** issued by the company. All the liabilities and requirements applicable to a prospectus apply to such a document. This provision prevents companies from evading prospectus liability by routing an issue through an intermediary.

**Conclusion:** A private placement is a non-public offer to up to 200 identified persons under Section 42, subject to strict conditions; and under Section 25, an offer-for-sale document is treated as a deemed prospectus of the company.

### Q18. Ch: Share Capital & Debentures — Variation of Shareholders' Rights (Marks: 6) [Application]

**Question:** The authorised and issued share capital of Zephyr Textiles Ltd. is divided into 8,00,000 Equity Shares of ₹10 each and 2,00,000 8% Preference Shares of ₹100 each. The Board proposes to reduce the rate of dividend on the preference shares from 8% to 6% to conserve cash for expansion. The Articles of the company are silent as to variation of the rights attached to any class of shares. The preference shareholders' meeting was called; out of the total 2,00,000 preference shares, holders of 1,30,000 shares were present (in person or proxy) and out of these, holders of 90,000 shares voted in favour of the reduction and holders of 40,000 shares voted against it. Advise whether the variation stands validly sanctioned, and state the further remedy available to the dissenting preference shareholders.

**Answer:** The problem is governed by **Section 48 of the Companies Act, 2013** read with the concept of variation of class rights.

Under **Section 48(1)**, where a share capital of the company is divided into different classes of shares, the rights attached to the shares of any class may be varied with the consent in writing of the holders of not less than **three-fourths of the issued shares of that class**, **or** by means of a **special resolution passed at a separate meeting of the holders of the issued shares of that class**. This may be done in two situations — (a) if provision with respect to such variation is contained in the memorandum or articles of the company, or (b) in the absence of any such provision in the memorandum or articles, if such variation is **not prohibited** by the terms of issue of the shares of that class. Since the Articles of Zephyr Textiles Ltd. are silent and the terms of issue do not prohibit variation, the variation can be carried out by a **special resolution passed at a separate class meeting** of the preference shareholders.

Reduction of the rate of dividend from 8% to 6% clearly **varies the rights** attached to the preference shares, and therefore the procedure under Section 48 must be followed.

**Testing the validity of the special resolution:** A special resolution under **Section 114(2)** requires that the votes cast in favour be **not less than three times the votes cast against**. At the separate class meeting, the votes cast in favour were 90,000 and the votes cast against were 40,000. For the resolution to be a valid special resolution, votes in favour must be at least 3 × 40,000 = **1,20,000**. Only **90,000** votes were cast in favour, which is **less than 1,20,000**. Hence the special resolution is **NOT validly passed** and the variation does **not** stand sanctioned.

(Note: the three-fourths *of the issued shares* test — i.e., consent in writing of holders of 1,50,000 shares — is the alternative written-consent route; it is not satisfied either. Where the class meeting route is used, only the 3:1 special-resolution test at the meeting applies, and it fails.)

**Remedy of dissenting shareholders — Section 48(2):** Even if a variation is validly made, where holders of **not less than 10% of the issued shares of that class** who did **not** consent to or vote in favour of the resolution are aggrieved, they may **apply to the Tribunal (NCLT)** to have the variation cancelled, within **21 days** after the date on which the consent was given or the resolution was passed. The decision of the Tribunal on such application is **final**, and the company must file the order with the Registrar within **30 days**.

**Conclusion:** The variation of preference shareholders' rights is **not validly sanctioned**, as the special resolution failed the 3:1 requirement of Section 114(2). Had it been validly passed, the dissenting holders of 40,000 shares (20% of the class, exceeding the 10% threshold) could have moved the NCLT under Section 48(2) within 21 days.

*(Full-marks tip: examiners reward candidates who apply the **exact Section 114(2) arithmetic** (favour ≥ 3 × against) rather than merely asserting "special resolution passed". State both the two routes under 48(1) and the 10% / 21-day NCLT remedy under 48(2); a common deduction is forgetting that Articles being silent still permits variation unless the terms of issue prohibit it.)*

### Q21. Ch: Share Capital & Debentures — Class Meetings & Deemed Variation (Marks: 4) [Theory]

**Question:** "A variation of the rights of one class of shareholders may, in certain circumstances, be deemed to vary the rights of another class as well." **Answer:** The statement reflects the **proviso to Section 48(1)** of the Companies Act, 2013, dealing with **consequential (deemed) variation** of the rights of another class.

The main part of **Section 48(1)** provides that the rights attached to the shares of any class may be varied with the consent in writing of the holders of not less than **three-fourths of the issued shares of that class**, or by a **special resolution** passed at a separate meeting of the holders of the issued shares of that class, provided such variation is permitted by the memorandum/articles or, where they are silent, is not prohibited by the terms of issue.

The procedural safeguards (separate class meeting, quorum, and the right of dissenting holders of 10% under Section 48(2) to approach the Tribunal within 21 days) apply equally.

**Conclusion:** By virtue of the proviso to Section 48(1), a variation that affects another class cannot be validly made without also securing the three-fourths consent (in writing or by special resolution) of that affected class; ignoring this renders the variation defective.

Already outstanding from public = ₹20 crore.
Maximum further from public = ₹30 cr − ₹20 cr = **₹10 crore**.

| Deposits from **Public** | 25% of base | 30 | 20 | **10** |

### Q25. Ch: Acceptance of Deposits — Exempted Receipts (Not Deposits) (Marks: 5) [Application]

**Question:** During FY 2025-26, Halcyon Motors Ltd. received the following amounts. Examine, with reasons under Rule 2(1)(c) of the Companies (Acceptance of Deposits) Rules, 2014, whether each amount is a "deposit" requiring compliance with Chapter V:
(a) ₹25 lakh received as an advance from a customer for supply of goods, appropriated against supply within 300 days;
(b) ₹40 lakh received from the Managing Director out of his own funds, he giving a declaration that the amount is not borrowed;
(c) ₹60 lakh raised as a secured term loan from a scheduled bank;
(d) ₹12 lakh received as a non-interest-bearing security deposit from an employee, being three months' salary of that employee (whose annual salary is ₹40 lakh).

**Answer:** The classification of receipts is governed by **Section 2(31)** read with the exclusions in **Rule 2(1)(c)** of the Companies (Acceptance of Deposits) Rules, 2014. "Deposit" includes any receipt of money by way of deposit or loan or in any other form by a company, but **excludes** the categories listed in Rule 2(1)(c).

**(a) Advance from customer for supply of goods — ₹25 lakh:** Under **Rule 2(1)(c)(xii)**, an advance received in connection with the supply of goods or provision of services is **not a deposit**, provided it is appropriated against supply within **365 days** from acceptance. Here it is appropriated within **300 days**, i.e., within the permitted period. Hence it is **NOT a deposit**. (Had it remained un-appropriated beyond 365 days, it would be treated as a deposit from the expiry of that period.)

**(b) Amount from Managing Director out of own funds — ₹40 lakh:** Under **Rule 2(1)(c)(viii)**, any amount received from a **director** of the company (or a relative of a director, in case of a private company) is **not a deposit**, provided the director furnishes a **written declaration** that the amount is **not being given out of funds acquired by borrowing** or accepting loans/deposits from others. The MD (a director) has given the requisite declaration. Hence it is **NOT a deposit**.

**(c) Secured term loan from a scheduled bank — ₹60 lakh:** Under **Rule 2(1)(c)(iii)**, any amount received as a loan or facility from any **banking company** or from the SBI or its subsidiaries or a banking institution is **not a deposit**. Hence the bank term loan is **NOT a deposit**.

**(d) Security deposit from employee — ₹12 lakh:** Under **Rule 2(1)(c)(x)**, any **non-interest-bearing** amount received from an **employee** as a security deposit is **not a deposit**, provided it does **not exceed his annual salary** under a contract of employment. The employee's annual salary is ₹40 lakh and the deposit of ₹12 lakh (three months' salary) is well within his annual salary and is non-interest-bearing. Hence it is **NOT a deposit**.

| Receipt | Amount | Relevant Rule 2(1)(c) clause | Deposit? |
|---------|--------|------------------------------|----------|
| (a) Customer advance | ₹25 lakh | (xii) — ≤365 days | No |
| (b) From MD (director) | ₹40 lakh | (viii) — with declaration | No |
| (c) Bank term loan | ₹60 lakh | (iii) | No |
| (d) Employee security deposit | ₹12 lakh | (x) — ≤ annual salary, non-interest | No |

**Conclusion:** **None** of the four receipts is a "deposit"; each falls within a specific exclusion under Rule 2(1)(c), so no Chapter V deposit compliance is triggered.

*(Full-marks tip: cite the **exact sub-clause** of Rule 2(1)(c) for each item and state the condition (365 days / director's declaration / ≤ annual salary / non-interest-bearing). Merely writing "not a deposit" without the clause and condition loses marks.)*

### Q26. Ch: Share Capital & Debentures — Sweat Equity Shares (Marks: 5) [Theory]

**Question:** State the conditions subject to which a company may issue sweat equity shares under Section 54 of the Companies Act, 2013 and the relevant Rules. Also state the yearly and overall ceilings on such issue.

**Answer:** The issue of sweat equity shares is governed by **Section 54 of the Companies Act, 2013** read with **Rule 8 of the Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules, 2014**. "Sweat equity shares" means equity shares issued by a company to its **directors or employees** at a **discount or for consideration other than cash** for providing **know-how or making available intellectual property rights or value additions** (Explanation to Section 54).

**Conditions under Section 54(1):** A company may issue sweat equity shares of a class of shares already issued if the following conditions are satisfied — (a) the issue is **authorised by a special resolution** passed by the company; (b) the resolution specifies the **number of shares, the current market price, the consideration, if any, and the class(es) of directors or employees** to whom they are to be issued; (c) **[the requirement of one year since commencement of business has been omitted]** — not less than **one year** has, at the date of such issue, elapsed since the date on which the company had commenced business; and (d) where the equity shares are listed, the issue is made in accordance with **SEBI regulations**, and in the case of unlisted companies, in accordance with the prescribed Rules.

**Additional conditions under Rule 8:** The special resolution is valid for making the allotment within a period of **not more than twelve months** from the date of passing. Sweat equity shares issued to directors or employees are subject to a **lock-in of three years** from the date of allotment, and the fact of the lock-in and the period must be prominently stamped on the share certificate.

**Ceilings on issue (Rule 8(4)):** A company shall **not issue sweat equity shares for more than 15% of the existing paid-up equity share capital in a year or shares of the value of ₹5 crore, whichever is higher**; however, the issuance in the company shall **not exceed 25% of the paid-up equity capital of the company at any time**. (For a **start-up company**, sweat equity may be issued up to **50%** of paid-up capital within ten years from incorporation.)

**Conclusion:** Sweat equity shares may be issued by special resolution to directors/employees for know-how/IPR/value additions, subject to the yearly ceiling of **higher of 15% or ₹5 crore**, an overall cap of **25%** of paid-up equity capital (50% for start-ups), and a **three-year lock-in**.

*(Full-marks tip: the twin ceilings — **yearly (higher of 15% or ₹5 cr)** and **overall 25%** — plus the **three-year lock-in** are the scoring points; mention the special-resolution requirement and the start-up 50% relaxation.)*

### Q27. Ch: Acceptance of Deposits — Deposit Repayment Reserve & Return of Deposits (Marks: 5) [Theory]

**Question:** Explain the requirements regarding (i) the Deposit Repayment Reserve Account, and (ii) the annual Return of Deposits, that a company accepting deposits must comply with under Section 73 of the Companies Act, 2013 and the Companies (Acceptance of Deposits) Rules, 2014.

**Answer:** The requirements arise under **Section 73(2) of the Companies Act, 2013** read with **Rules 13, 14 and 16** of the Companies (Acceptance of Deposits) Rules, 2014, which prescribe safeguards for the protection of depositors.

**(i) Deposit Repayment Reserve Account — Section 73(2)(c) read with Rule 13:** Every company that accepts deposits must, on or before the **30th day of April** each year, **deposit or invest, as the case may be, a sum which shall not be less than 20% of the amount of its deposits maturing during the following financial year**, and keep it in a **separate bank account** called the **"Deposit Repayment Reserve Account"** opened with a scheduled bank. This amount shall **not be used** for any purpose **other than repayment of deposits**. (The earlier requirement of deposit insurance has been omitted, and the 20% liquid-reserve requirement is the key ongoing safeguard.)

**(ii) Return of Deposits — Rule 16 (Form DPT-3):** Every company (other than a Government company) to which the deposit rules apply shall, on or before the **30th day of June** of every year, file with the Registrar a **return in Form DPT-3** furnishing information as on the **31st day of March** of that year, **duly audited by the company's auditor**. **Conclusion:** A depositing company must maintain a **Deposit Repayment Reserve of at least 20%** of deposits maturing in the next financial year by 30th April, and file its **annual Return of Deposits in Form DPT-3 by 30th June** for the position as on 31st March, duly audited.

### Q30. Ch: Acceptance of Deposits — Eligible Company & Credit Rating (Marks: 4) [Theory]

**Question:** Who is an "eligible company" for the purpose of accepting deposits under Section 76 of the Companies Act, 2013? State the requirement of obtaining and maintaining a credit rating for accepting deposits from the public.

**Eligible company:** An "eligible company" means a **public company** having — a **net worth of not less than ₹100 crore**, **or** a **turnover of not less than ₹500 crore** — and which has obtained the **prior consent of the company in general meeting by means of a special resolution**, and has also filed the said resolution with the Registrar of Companies before making any invitation to the public for acceptance of deposits. This ensures continuous assessment of the company's creditworthiness for the protection of public depositors.

### Q31. Ch: Share Capital & Debentures — Further Issue of Capital / Rights Issue (Marks: 6) [Application]

**Question:** Vanguard Chemicals Ltd., a company having a paid-up equity capital of ₹50 crore, proposes to raise a further ₹10 crore. Advise the Board on the manner of a **rights issue** under Section 62(1)(a) of the Companies Act, 2013, covering: the persons to whom the offer must be made; the minimum and maximum period for acceptance; the right of renunciation; and the consequence if the offer is not accepted. Also state whether the company can, in the same rights offer, dispose of shares declined by existing members.

**Answer:** The further issue of capital by way of a rights issue is governed by **Section 62(1)(a) of the Companies Act, 2013**.

Where at any time a company having a share capital proposes to increase its subscribed capital by the issue of further shares, such shares shall be offered **to the persons who, on the date of the offer, are holders of the equity shares of the company in proportion, as nearly as circumstances admit, to the paid-up share capital on those shares**. **(a) Manner of offer — Section 62(1)(a)(i):** The offer shall be made by a **notice specifying the number of shares offered** and **limiting a time not being less than fifteen days and not exceeding thirty days** from the date of the offer within which the offer, if not accepted, shall be deemed to have been **declined**. (For certain companies/private companies, a shorter period is permissible if 90% members agree.)

**(b) Right of renunciation — Section 62(1)(a)(ii):** The offer shall be deemed to include a **right exercisable by the person concerned to renounce the shares offered to him or any of them in favour of any other person**; and the notice must contain a statement of this right, **unless the articles of the company otherwise provide**.

**Conclusion:** Vanguard Chemicals Ltd. must offer the further ₹10 crore **pro rata to existing equity holders** by notice giving **15–30 days** to accept, include the **right of renunciation** (unless articles provide otherwise), and thereafter the **Board may dispose of declined shares** in a manner not disadvantageous to the company and shareholders.

### Q32. Ch: Share Capital & Debentures — Forfeiture: Effect and Annulment (Marks: 4) [Theory]

**Question:** Explain the **effect of forfeiture of shares** on the defaulting member and on the company under the regulations of Table F, and state whether a forfeiture, once made, can be cancelled or annulled by the Board.

**Answer:** The effect of forfeiture of shares is governed by the regulations of **Table F of Schedule I** to the Companies Act, 2013 (Regulations 28 to 34), which apply where the company's articles adopt Table F.

**Effect on the defaulting member (Regulation 30):** When a share is forfeited, a person whose shares have been forfeited shall **cease to be a member in respect of the forfeited shares**, but shall, **notwithstanding the forfeiture, remain liable to pay to the company all monies which, at the date of forfeiture, were presently payable by him to the company in respect of the shares**. The liability of such person shall **cease** if and when the company shall have received payment in full of all such monies in respect of the shares. Thus, forfeiture extinguishes membership but **not the pre-existing debt** until the company recovers the full amount (including, where applicable, interest).

**Effect on the company (Regulations 31 and 32):** A forfeited share becomes the **property of the company** and may be **sold or re-issued or otherwise disposed of** on such terms and in such manner as the Board thinks fit. The company may **receive the consideration** on re-issue and may execute a transfer in favour of the person to whom the share is sold or disposed of; the transferee's title is not affected by any irregularity in the forfeiture proceedings. Any surplus on re-issue over the amount in default is retained by the company (transferred to Capital Reserve), and the re-issuee is not concerned with the application of the purchase money.

**Cancellation/annulment of forfeiture (Regulation 33):** The Board may, **at any time before a sale, re-issue or other disposal of any forfeited share, annul the forfeiture** thereof on such terms as it thinks fit. Therefore, a forfeiture is **not irreversible** — the Board retains discretion to cancel it, but only **before** the share has been sold or otherwise disposed of; once re-issued to a third party, the forfeiture cannot be annulled.

**Conclusion:** Forfeiture terminates the defaulter's membership while preserving his liability for amounts due at the date of forfeiture; the share becomes the company's property to re-issue; and the Board may **annul the forfeiture at any time before disposal** of the share.

*(Full-marks tip: examiners look for the **"ceases to be a member but remains liable for amounts due"** principle (Reg. 30) and the **power to annul before disposal** (Reg. 33). Cite the Table F regulation numbers.)*

### Q33. Ch: Acceptance of Deposits — Consequences of Default / Contravention (Marks: 6) [Application]

**Question:** Beacon Retail Ltd., an eligible company, accepted deposits from the public. Owing to a liquidity crisis it **failed to repay ₹4 crore of deposits and interest thereon** on the due dates. A group of depositors approaches you. Advise: (i) the forum before which and the manner in which the depositors can seek relief; (ii) the power of the Tribunal in the matter; and (iii) the penal consequences on the company and officers for failure to repay deposits under Section 74/76A of the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:** The consequences of default in repayment of deposits are governed by **Sections 73(4), 74 and 76A** of the Companies Act, 2013.

**(i) Relief to depositors — Section 73(4):** Where a company **fails to repay the deposit or part thereof or any interest thereon** on the due date, the **depositor concerned may apply to the Tribunal (NCLT)** for an order directing the company to pay the sum due or for any loss or damage incurred by him as a result of such non-payment and for such other orders as the Tribunal may deem fit. Thus, the aggrieved depositors of Beacon Retail Ltd. can make an application to the **NCLT** seeking repayment of the ₹4 crore with interest and compensation for loss.

**(ii) Power of the Tribunal — Section 73(4) and 74(2):** On such application, the Tribunal may **direct the company to pay the amount due** to the depositors together with interest and any loss/damage. Further, under **Section 74(2)**, where a company is unable to repay any deposit or part thereof or interest, the Tribunal may, on the application of the company (after considering its financial condition, the amount of deposit/interest and other matters), **allow further time** for repayment as it considers reasonable. The Tribunal thus has power both to **compel repayment** (on depositor's application) and to **grant extension of time** (on the company's application).

**(iii) Penal consequences — Section 76A:** Where a company accepts or invites deposits in contravention of the manner or conditions prescribed under Section 73/76 or the rules, **or fails to repay the deposit or interest within the time specified or within such further time allowed by the Tribunal** —
- The **company** shall be punishable with a **fine which shall not be less than ₹1 crore or twice the amount of deposit accepted, whichever is lower, but which may extend to ₹10 crore**; and
- Every **officer of the company who is in default** shall be punishable with **imprisonment which may extend to seven years** and with a **fine which shall not be less than ₹25 lakh but which may extend to ₹2 crore**.

Additionally, if it is proved that an officer of the company who is in default has **contravened such provisions knowingly or wilfully with the intention to deceive** the company or its shareholders or depositors or creditors or tax authorities, he shall be liable for **action under Section 447 (fraud)**.

**Conclusion:** The depositors of Beacon Retail Ltd. may apply to the **NCLT** under Section 73(4) for a repayment order; the Tribunal can direct repayment with interest and compensation (and may grant the company further time under Section 74(2)); and on default the **company faces a fine of ₹1 crore/twice the deposit (lower) up to ₹10 crore**, while officers in default face **imprisonment up to 7 years and fine of ₹25 lakh to ₹2 crore**, besides Section 447 liability for wilful fraud.

*(Full-marks tip: the **NCLT forum under Section 73(4)**, the **Section 74(2) extension power**, and the **precise Section 76A penalties** (company: ₹1 cr/2× deposit, lower, up to ₹10 cr; officer: 7 years + ₹25 lakh–₹2 cr) are the marks. Add the **Section 447** rider for wilful default.)*

### Q34. Ch: Share Capital & Debentures — Bonus Shares (Marks: 4) [Application]

**Question:** The Board of Solstice Papers Ltd. proposes to capitalise its reserves by issuing fully paid bonus shares. State the sources from which, and the conditions subject to which, a company may issue bonus shares under Section 63 of the Companies Act, 2013. Also state whether bonus shares can be issued in lieu of dividend.

**Answer:** The issue of bonus shares is governed by **Section 63 of the Companies Act, 2013** read with **Rule 14 of the Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules, 2014**.

**Sources — Section 63(1):** A company may issue fully paid-up bonus shares to its members, in any manner whatsoever, **out of** — (i) its **free reserves**; (ii) the **securities premium account**; or (iii) the **capital redemption reserve account**. Bonus shares shall **not be issued by capitalising reserves created by the revaluation of assets**.

**Conditions — Section 63(2):** No company shall capitalise its profits or reserves for the purpose of issuing fully paid-up bonus shares unless — (a) it is **authorised by its articles**; (b) it has, on the recommendation of the Board, been **authorised in the general meeting** of the company; (c) it has **not defaulted in payment of interest or principal in respect of fixed deposits or debt securities** issued by it; (d) it has **not defaulted in respect of the payment of statutory dues of the employees**, such as contribution to provident fund, gratuity and bonus; and (e) the **partly paid-up shares, if any, outstanding on the date of allotment, are made fully paid-up**.

Further, under **Section 63(3)**, the bonus shares shall not be issued in lieu of dividend, and once the Board recommends and the general meeting sanctions the bonus issue, it **cannot subsequently be withdrawn** (Rule 14).

**Bonus in lieu of dividend:** By virtue of **Section 63(3)**, the bonus shares **shall not be issued in lieu of dividend**. Hence Solstice Papers Ltd. cannot issue bonus shares as a substitute for declaring a dividend.

**Conclusion:** Bonus shares may be issued only out of **free reserves, securities premium or capital redemption reserve** (not revaluation reserve), subject to authorisation by articles and the general meeting and the no-default conditions of Section 63(2); and by Section 63(3) they **cannot be issued in lieu of dividend**.

Mention that a sanctioned bonus issue cannot be withdrawn.)*

### Q39. Ch: Registration of Charges — Effect of Non-Registration (Marks: 5) [Application]

### Q42. Ch: Management & Administration — Quorum: Public vs Private Company (Marks: 5) [Application]

**Answer:** Quorum for general meetings is governed by **Section 103 of the Companies Act, 2013**.

### Q43. Ch: Management & Administration — Demand for Poll (Marks: 5) [Application]

| S. No. | Issue |

### Q44. Ch: Management & Administration — Voting Rights on a Poll (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the manner in which votes are counted on a poll under the Companies Act, 2013, and state whether a member may cast his votes differently ("split votes") on a poll. Also state the position of a preference shareholder's voting rights.

**Answer:** The manner of voting on a poll and members' voting rights are governed by **Sections 47 and 109(3) of the Companies Act, 2013**.

**Voting rights on a poll (Section 47).** Under **Section 47(1)(b)**, every member of a company limited by shares and **holding equity share capital** has a right to vote on every resolution placed before the company, and **his voting right on a poll shall be in proportion to his share in the paid-up equity share capital of the company**. This is in contrast to voting on a **show of hands**, where every member present has **one vote irrespective of the number of shares held**.

**Preference shareholders' voting rights (Section 47(2)).** A member holding **preference share capital** has a right to vote **only on resolutions which directly affect the rights attached to his preference shares** and on resolutions for winding up or for repayment/reduction of capital. However, where the **dividend on preference shares has not been paid for a period of two years or more**, such preference shareholders acquire a **right to vote on all resolutions** placed before the company. Their voting right on a poll is in proportion to their share in the paid-up preference share capital.

**Splitting of votes (Section 109(3)).** On a poll taken at a meeting, **a member entitled to more than one vote need not, if he votes, use all his votes or cast in the same way all the votes he uses**. Thus a member holding, say, 10,000 shares may cast some votes in favour and the remaining votes against the resolution — this is the right to "**split**" votes, available only on a **poll** and not on a show of hands.

**Conclusion:** On a poll, voting is proportionate to paid-up capital (Section 47); preference shareholders vote on matters affecting them, and on all matters if dividend is in arrears for two years; and under Section 109(3) a member may split his votes on a poll.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner specifically rewards the "split-voting" right under Section 109(3) and the two-year dividend-arrears rule for preference shares. A common deduction is treating show-of-hands (one-member-one-vote) as the poll rule.)*

### Q45. Ch: Management & Administration — Resolutions Requiring Special Notice (Marks: 6) [Application]

### Q47. Ch: Management & Administration — Ordinary vs Special Resolution (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between an "ordinary resolution" and a "special resolution" under the Companies Act, 2013, and give two examples of matters requiring a special resolution.

**Answer:** The two categories of resolutions are defined in **Section 114 of the Companies Act, 2013**.

**Ordinary resolution (Section 114(1)).** A resolution is an ordinary resolution when the notice required under the Act has been duly given and it is required to be passed by the votes cast, whether on a show of hands or on a poll or through electronic means, **in favour of the resolution (including the casting vote, if any, of the Chairman) exceeding the votes, if any, cast against** the resolution by members entitled to vote — i.e., a **simple majority (more than 50%)**.

**Special resolution (Section 114(2)).** A resolution is a special resolution when — (a) the **intention to propose it as a special resolution** has been duly specified in the notice; (b) the notice required under the Act has been duly given; and (c) the **votes cast in favour** of the resolution (by show of hands, poll or electronic means) are **not less than three times the number of votes cast against** — i.e., a **majority of not less than 75%**.

| Basis | Ordinary Resolution | Special Resolution |
|-------|---------------------|--------------------|
| Section | 114(1) | 114(2) |
| Majority | Simple majority (> 50%) | Not less than 75% (3:1) |
| Notice | Intention to propose need not be stated | Intention must be stated in the notice |
| Filing (MGT-14) | Only in specified cases | Required to be filed with Registrar in most cases |

**Examples of special resolution.** (i) **Alteration of the articles of association (Section 14)**; and (ii) **Reduction of share capital (Section 66)** / change of registered office from one State to another / conversion of a public company into a private company.

**Conclusion:** An ordinary resolution needs a simple majority while a special resolution needs a 75% majority with the intention stated in the notice; matters such as alteration of articles and reduction of capital require a special resolution.

### Q48. Ch: Management & Administration — Notice of General Meeting & Shorter Notice (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** The Board of Vantage Instruments Limited proposes to hold its annual general meeting. Advise on the length and manner of notice required under the Companies Act, 2013, and state whether and how a meeting may validly be held at **shorter notice**.

**Length of notice (Section 101(1)).** A general meeting of a company may be called by giving **not less than clear twenty-one days' notice** either in writing or through electronic mode. The notice must be given to (a) every **member**, legal representative of a deceased member and the assignee of an insolvent member; (b) the **auditor(s)**; and (c) every **director** of the company.

The consent may be given before or at the meeting, in writing or electronically.

### Q49. Ch: Management & Administration — Proxies (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Mr. Sen, a member of Cobalt Chemicals Limited (a public company having share capital), wishes to appoint a proxy to attend and vote at the AGM on his behalf. Advise, under the Companies Act, 2013, on: (i) the right to appoint a proxy and the deposit of the proxy form; (ii) whether a proxy can speak and vote; and (iii) the restriction on the number of members and shares a single person may represent as proxy.

**Answer:** The appointment of proxies is governed by **Section 105 of the Companies Act, 2013** read with **Rule 19** of the Companies (Management and Administration) Rules, 2014.

**(i) Right to appoint and deposit of proxy (Section 105(1) & (4)).** Any member of a company entitled to attend and vote at a meeting is entitled to appoint **another person as a proxy** to attend and vote on his behalf. A proxy **need not be a member** of the company. The instrument appointing a proxy must be in **writing, signed by the appointer**, in **Form MGT-11**, and must be **deposited with the company not later than forty-eight hours before the time of holding the meeting**; any provision in the articles requiring a longer period is void to that extent (it is treated as requiring only 48 hours).

**(ii) Rights of a proxy (Section 105(1), first proviso).** A proxy **has the right to vote only on a poll** and, unless the articles otherwise provide, a proxy **shall not have the right to speak** at the meeting and **shall not be entitled to vote except on a poll** (i.e., a proxy cannot vote on a show of hands). A proxy is thus counted for voting on a poll but not on a show of hands, and (in a public company) is not counted towards quorum.

**(iii) Restriction on number represented (Rule 19(2)).** A person **cannot act as proxy on behalf of more than fifty members** and shareholders holding in the aggregate **not more than ten per cent of the total share capital of the company carrying voting rights**. However, a member holding **more than ten per cent** of the total voting share capital may appoint a **single person** as proxy, but such person shall **not act as proxy for any other person or shareholder**.

**Statement in notice (Section 105(2)).** Every notice calling the meeting must carry a statement that a member is entitled to appoint a proxy and that the proxy need not be a member.

**Conclusion:** Mr. Sen may appoint any person (not necessarily a member) as proxy in Form MGT-11, deposited at least 48 hours before the meeting; the proxy may vote only on a poll and cannot speak; and no person may act as proxy for more than 50 members holding in aggregate more than 10% of the voting capital.

### Q51. Ch: Management & Administration — Postal Ballot & E-voting (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the concept of "postal ballot" under the Companies Act, 2013 and state whether a matter that must be transacted through postal ballot may instead be transacted at a general meeting where the company provides electronic voting (e-voting) facility.

**Answer:** The transaction of business by postal ballot is governed by **Section 110 of the Companies Act, 2013** read with **Rule 22** of the Companies (Management and Administration) Rules, 2014.

**Meaning of postal ballot.** As defined in **Section 2(65)**, "**postal ballot**" means voting by post or through any electronic mode. Under **Section 110(1)**, a company — (a) **shall**, in respect of such items of business as the Central Government may notify to be transacted only by means of postal ballot; and (b) **may**, in respect of any item of business other than **ordinary business** and any business in respect of which **directors or auditors have a right to be heard** at the meeting — transact such business by means of a postal ballot instead of transacting it at a general meeting. The prescribed items required to be passed only by postal ballot include, for example, alteration of the objects clause of the memorandum, buy-back of shares, and variation of rights attached to a class of shares.

**Exemption where e-voting is provided (Proviso to Section 110(1)).** As per the **proviso to Section 110(1)** (inserted by the Companies (Amendment) Act, 2017), **any item of business required to be transacted by means of postal ballot may be transacted at a general meeting by a company which is required to provide the facility of electronic voting (e-voting) under Section 108** to its members. In other words, where a company is mandatorily required to provide e-voting, it need **not** compulsorily route the notified items through postal ballot; it may instead place them before a duly convened general meeting with an e-voting facility.

**Procedure (Rule 22).** Where business is transacted by postal ballot, the company sends a **notice with a draft resolution and explanatory statement** to all members, along with a **postal ballot form** and a self-addressed postage-prepaid envelope; members must return the assent or dissent **within thirty days** from the date of despatch. A **scrutinizer** is appointed to conduct the process, and the resolution is deemed passed on the date of the meeting at which it would otherwise have been transacted.

**Conclusion:** Postal ballot (voting by post or electronic mode) is mandatory for notified items and optional for other special items; but by virtue of the proviso to Section 110(1), a company required to provide e-voting under Section 108 may transact even such items at a general meeting instead of by postal ballot.

### Q52. Ch: Declaration & Payment of Dividend — Declaration out of Inadequate Profits (Rule 3) (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** Sunrise Textiles Ltd. has earned a very small profit in the financial year 2025-26 and the Board wishes to maintain its dividend track record by declaring dividend out of the accumulated free reserves. From the following data, determine the **maximum amount of dividend** the company can declare for FY 2025-26 in accordance with **Rule 3 of the Companies (Declaration and Payment of Dividend) Rules, 2014**, and state whether the proposal to declare a dividend of ₹32,00,000 is permissible.

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Paid-up equity share capital | 2,00,00,000 |
| General Reserve (free reserve) | 1,20,00,000 |
| Capital Redemption Reserve | 30,00,000 |
| Securities Premium | 25,00,000 |
| Balance of accumulated losses carried in books | 6,00,000 |
| Depreciation not provided in prior years (arrears) | 4,00,000 |
| Net profit for FY 2025-26 | 3,00,000 |
| Rate of dividend declared in the three immediately preceding years | 12%, 10%, 8% |

**WN-1 — Statutory conditions under Rule 3 (three tests):**
Where a company proposes to declare dividend out of accumulated profits earned in previous years and transferred to reserves, owing to inadequacy or absence of profits in the current year, it may do so **only** out of **free reserves** (General Reserve of ₹1,20,00,000; Capital Redemption Reserve and Securities Premium are **not** free reserves for this purpose), subject to three ceilings:

**WN-2 — Test 1: Rate of dividend ceiling [Rule 3(1)]**
The rate of dividend declared shall not exceed the **average of the rates** at which dividend was declared in the three immediately preceding years.
Average rate = (12% + 10% + 8%) ÷ 3 = **10%**.
Maximum dividend permissible on this test = 10% × ₹2,00,00,000 = **₹20,00,000**.

**WN-3 — Test 2: Maximum drawal from reserves [Rule 3(2)]**
The total amount to be drawn from accumulated profits shall not exceed **one-tenth (1/10) of the sum of paid-up share capital and free reserves** as appearing in the latest audited financial statement.
Paid-up capital + free reserves = ₹2,00,00,000 + ₹1,20,00,000 = ₹3,20,00,000.
1/10 thereof = **₹32,00,000** (this is the cap on the amount **drawn from reserves**).

**WN-4 — Test 3: Set-off of losses and floor on reserve balance [Rule 3(3) & 3(5)]**
The amount so drawn shall **first be used to set off the losses and depreciation arrears** of the current year before any dividend on equity is declared; and after such withdrawal, the **balance of reserves shall not fall below 15% of paid-up share capital**.
Minimum reserve to be retained = 15% × ₹2,00,00,000 = ₹30,00,000.
Maximum that can be withdrawn from General Reserve = ₹1,20,00,000 − ₹30,00,000 = **₹90,00,000** (not the binding constraint here).

**WN-5 — Amount available for dividend:**
Amount drawn from reserves (capped at ₹32,00,000 per Test 2) must first absorb the current shortfall — the accumulated losses of ₹6,00,000 and depreciation arrears of ₹4,00,000 = ₹10,00,000 are set off first.

**Statement Showing Maximum Dividend Permissible for FY 2025-26**

| Step | Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| A | Maximum drawal from free reserves [Test 2: 1/10 × 3,20,00,000] | 32,00,000 |
| B | Add: Current year net profit available | 3,00,000 |
| C | Gross amount available | 35,00,000 |
| D | Less: Set-off of accumulated losses + depreciation arrears (Rule 3(3)) | (10,00,000) |
| E | Amount available for dividend | 25,00,000 |
| F | Restrict to Rate ceiling [Test 1: 10% of capital] | 20,00,000 |

The binding constraint is **Test 1 (rate ceiling) = ₹20,00,000**.

**Answer:** The maximum dividend that Sunrise Textiles Ltd. can declare for FY 2025-26 is **₹20,00,000** (10% of paid-up capital). The Board's proposal to declare **₹32,00,000 is NOT permissible**, as it breaches the average-rate ceiling of Rule 3(1) as well as the requirement to first set off losses.

**Answer:** The treatment of unpaid or unclaimed dividend is governed by **Section 124** and its transfer to the fund by **Section 125** of the Companies Act, 2013.

Where a dividend has been declared but has **not been paid or claimed within 30 days** from the date of declaration, the company shall, **within 7 days** from the expiry of the said 30-day period (i.e., within 37 days of declaration), transfer the **total amount of unpaid/unclaimed dividend to a special account** called the **"Unpaid Dividend Account"** opened in a scheduled bank [Section 124(1)]. ### Q54. Ch: Declaration & Payment of Dividend — Sources & Conditions for Declaration (Sec 123) (Marks: 5) [Application]

Therefore, Zephyr Ltd. **must first set off** the carried-forward loss of ₹5,00,000 before declaring dividend. Refusing to do so is **invalid**.

**Conclusion:** All three proposals are contrary to Section 123 — depreciation must be provided, unrealised capital profit cannot be distributed, and prior losses must be set off before any dividend is declared.

### Q56. Ch: Declaration & Payment of Dividend — Manner of Payment & Prohibition (Sec 123(5) & 127) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Orion Ltd. declared a dividend at its AGM held on 10th September 2025. Examine the following with reference to the Companies Act, 2013:
(i) The company wishes to pay the dividend in the form of bonus shares to conserve cash.
(ii) One shareholder, Mr. Rao, has not paid a call of ₹5,000 due on his shares; the dividend payable to him is ₹8,000.
(iii) The company failed to pay the declared dividend to a set of shareholders within the statutory period without any lawful reason. State the consequences for the defaulting directors.

**Answer:** The manner of payment of dividend and the penalty for failure are governed by **Sections 123(5) and 127** of the Companies Act, 2013.

**(i) Payment of dividend in the form of bonus shares:** Section 123(5) requires that dividend be **paid in cash** (which includes payment by cheque, warrant, or electronic mode) and **not in kind**. Therefore, a cash dividend **cannot be discharged by issuing bonus shares**. However, **capitalisation of profits into bonus shares** is a separate mechanism permitted under Section 63 and is not prohibited by this rule. Orion Ltd. **cannot** pay the declared cash dividend by way of bonus shares.

**(ii) Adjustment against unpaid call:** The proviso to Section 127 (and Section 123(5)) permits that any dividend payable in cash may be **set off against any sum due from the shareholder** (such as unpaid calls) to the company. Hence, the ₹8,000 dividend of Mr. Rao may be **adjusted against his unpaid call of ₹5,000**, and only the balance of ₹3,000 need be paid.

**(iii) Failure to pay declared dividend — Section 127:** Where a dividend has been declared but is **not paid or the warrant not posted within 30 days** from declaration, **every director** of the company, if he is knowingly a party to the default, is punishable with **imprisonment up to 2 years and a fine of not less than ₹1,000 for every day** during which the default continues; and the **company** is liable to pay **simple interest at 18% per annum** during the period of default.

There are recognised **exceptions** where no offence is deemed committed, e.g., where the dividend could not be paid by reason of operation of law, where a shareholder gave directions that could not be complied with, or where the default was not due to any fault of the company.

**Conclusion:** Dividend cannot be paid as bonus shares; Mr. Rao's dividend may be adjusted against his unpaid call; and unjustified failure to pay attracts imprisonment/fine on directors and 18% p.a. interest on the company under Section 127.

### Q60. Ch: Accounts of Companies — Re-opening & Voluntary Revision of Accounts (Sec 130 & 131) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between **re-opening of accounts on court/tribunal order (Section 130)** and **voluntary revision of financial statements or Board's Report (Section 131)** of the Companies Act, 2013. State who can initiate each and the limitation on the number of years.

**Answer:** The re-casting of already adopted financial statements is dealt with under two distinct provisions — **Section 130 (compulsory re-opening)** and **Section 131 (voluntary revision)** of the Companies Act, 2013.

Under **Section 130**, a company shall **not re-open its books of account** and **not recast its financial statements** unless an application is made by the Central Government, the Income-tax authorities, SEBI, any other statutory regulatory body or authority, or any person concerned, and an **order is made by a court of competent jurisdiction or the Tribunal (NCLT)** to the effect that (i) the earlier accounts were prepared in a **fraudulent manner**, or (ii) the **affairs of the company were mismanaged** during the relevant period casting doubt on the reliability of the financial statements.

Under **Section 131**, if it appears to the directors of a company that the **financial statements or the Board's Report do not comply with Section 129 (financial statements) or Section 134 (Board's Report)**, they may prepare **revised financial statements or a revised Board's Report** in respect of any of the **three preceding financial years**, after obtaining **approval of the Tribunal**. Such revision is **voluntary** and initiated by the directors themselves.

| Basis | Section 130 — Re-opening | Section 131 — Voluntary Revision |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Compulsory, on order | Voluntary, at directors' instance |
| Who initiates | CG, Income-tax, SEBI, other regulator, or any concerned person | Board of Directors |
| Sanctioning authority | Court or Tribunal (NCLT) | Tribunal (on directors' application) |
| Ground | Fraud in preparation / mismanagement of affairs | Non-compliance with Sec 129 or Sec 134 |
| Years covered | No fixed outer limit; but not beyond 8 financial years immediately preceding unless CG directs otherwise (proviso) | Any of the **three preceding financial years** |

**Conclusion:** Section 130 is a **compulsory re-opening** ordered by a court/Tribunal on grounds of fraud or mismanagement, whereas Section 131 is a **voluntary revision** by the directors with the Tribunal's approval limited to the three preceding financial years.

**Answer:** The composition of financial statements is defined in **Section 2(40)** and their form and content are prescribed by **Section 129(1)** of the Companies Act, 2013.

As per **Section 2(40)**, "**financial statement**" in relation to a company includes:
(i) a **balance sheet** as at the end of the financial year;
(ii) a **profit and loss account**, or in the case of a company carrying on any activity not for profit, an **income and expenditure account** for the financial year;
(iii) a **cash flow statement** for the financial year;

(v) any **explanatory note** annexed to, or forming part of, any of the above documents.

### Q62. Ch: Accounts of Companies — National Financial Reporting Authority (Sec 132) (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Briefly state the functions and disciplinary powers of the **National Financial Reporting Authority (NFRA)** under Section 132 of the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:** The constitution, functions and powers of the **National Financial Reporting Authority (NFRA)** are contained in **Section 132** of the Companies Act, 2013.

The **functions** of NFRA under Section 132(2) are:
(a) to **make recommendations** to the Central Government on the formulation of **accounting and auditing policies and standards** for adoption by companies or their auditors;
(b) to **monitor and enforce compliance** with accounting standards and auditing standards;
(c) to **oversee the quality of service** of the professions associated with ensuring compliance with such standards, and suggest measures for improvement; and
(d) to perform such other functions relating to the above as may be prescribed.

Under **Section 132(4)**, NFRA has the power to **investigate**, either suo motu or on a reference from the Central Government, into matters of **professional or other misconduct** committed by any member or firm of chartered accountants registered under the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949, for prescribed classes of companies. For this purpose it has the **same powers as a civil court** under the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 (e.g., discovery, summoning, inspection of documents).

Where professional or other misconduct is proved, NFRA has the power to:
(i) impose a **penalty** — not less than ₹1,00,000 but which may extend to **five times the fees received** in the case of an individual, and not less than ₹10,00,000 which may extend to **ten times the fees received** in the case of a firm; and
(ii) **debar** the member or firm from being appointed as an auditor or from practising as a member of the Institute for a **minimum of six months up to ten years**.

**Conclusion:** NFRA recommends and enforces accounting/auditing standards, oversees audit quality, and possesses civil-court-like investigative powers with authority to levy monetary penalties and debar erring auditors for six months to ten years.

### Q63. Ch: Audit & Auditors — Rights and Duties of the Auditor (Sec 143) (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Enumerate the principal **rights (powers)** and **duties** of a company auditor under Section 143 of the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:** The powers and duties of an auditor of a company are set out in **Section 143** of the Companies Act, 2013.

**A. Rights / Powers of the auditor:**

1. **Right of access to books and vouchers** — Every auditor has a **right of access at all times** to the **books of account and vouchers** of the company, whether kept at the registered office or elsewhere [Section 143(1)].
2. **Right to obtain information and explanations** — He is entitled to **require from the officers** of the company such **information and explanation** as he may consider necessary for the performance of his duties.
3. **Right of access to records of subsidiaries** — For the purpose of consolidated financial statements, the auditor of a holding company has a right of access to the records of all its **subsidiaries and associate companies**.
4. **Right to receive notice of and attend general meetings** — The auditor is entitled to **receive all notices and communications** relating to any general meeting, to **attend** the meeting either himself or through his authorised representative, and to be **heard** on any part of the business which concerns him as auditor [Section 146].
5. **Right to remuneration** — On completion of the audit, the auditor is entitled to his **remuneration** as fixed under Section 142.
6. **Right to make enquiries** — He has the power to enquire into the specific matters listed in Section 143(1), e.g., whether loans are properly secured, whether book entries are prejudicial, whether personal expenses are charged to revenue, etc.

**B. Duties of the auditor:**

1. **Duty to make a report** to the members on the accounts examined and on every financial statement, stating whether they give a **true and fair view** [Section 143(2)].
2. **Duty to state prescribed matters** in the report under Section 143(3) — e.g., whether he has obtained all information and explanations, whether proper books of account have been kept, whether the financial statements comply with the accounting standards, and whether the company has adequate **internal financial controls** with reference to financial statements.
3. **Duty to comply with auditing standards** notified under Section 143(10).
4. **Duty to report fraud** — Where the auditor has reason to believe that an offence of **fraud** is being or has been committed, he shall **report the matter** as required under Section 143(12).
5. **Duty in case of a branch audit** and to consider the branch auditor's report [Section 143(8)].
6. **Duty to comply with the CARO (Companies (Auditor's Report) Order)** requirements, where applicable.

**Conclusion:** Section 143 confers on the auditor wide rights of access, information and attendance at meetings, coupled with corresponding duties to report truthfully, follow auditing standards, comment on internal financial controls, and report fraud.

### Q65. Ch: Audit & Auditors — Appointment & Rotation of Auditors (Sec 139) (Marks: 6) [Application]

(iii) Mr. C, who is indebted to Zenith Ltd. for ₹4,00,000.

**Answer:** The disqualifications of an auditor are enumerated in **Section 141(3) of the Companies Act, 2013**.

**(i) Holding of securities [Section 141(3)(d)(i)]:** A person is disqualified if he, or his relative or partner, is **holding any security of or interest** in the company. A **relative may hold securities** of the company of face value **not exceeding ₹1,00,000**. Mr. A himself holds securities of ₹1,50,000 — an **auditor is not permitted to hold ANY security** in the company (the ₹1 lakh relaxation is only for a **relative**, not the auditor himself). Hence **Mr. A is disqualified**.

**(ii) Indebtedness of a relative [Section 141(3)(d)(ii)]:** A person is disqualified if he or his relative or partner is **indebted to the company** for an amount **exceeding ₹5,00,000**. Mr. B's **brother** owes ₹6,00,000. However, whether the brother is a "relative" as defined and whether the indebtedness attaches — the limit is exceeded (₹6,00,000 > ₹5,00,000). As the indebtedness of ₹6,00,000 exceeds the ₹5 lakh limit, **Mr. B is disqualified**.

**(iii) Personal indebtedness [Section 141(3)(d)(ii)]:** Mr. C is indebted to the company for ₹4,00,000, which is **within the ₹5,00,000 limit**. Therefore **Mr. C is NOT disqualified**.

**(iv) Ceiling on number of audits [Section 141(3)(g)]:** A person is disqualified if he is in **full-time employment elsewhere** or is holding appointment as auditor of **more than twenty companies** (other than OPCs, dormant companies, small companies and private companies having paid-up capital less than ₹100 crore, which are excluded from the count). Mr. D is already the auditor of **20 companies** in the countable category; he has **reached but not exceeded** the limit of twenty. Accepting Zenith Ltd. would make it the **21st**, thereby **exceeding** the ceiling. Hence **Mr. D is disqualified** from accepting the audit of Zenith Ltd.

**Conclusion:** Mr. A (holds securities), Mr. B (relative's indebtedness > ₹5 lakh) and Mr. D (would exceed the 20-company ceiling) are **disqualified**; only **Mr. C** (indebtedness within ₹5 lakh) is **eligible** to be appointed.

*(Full-marks tip: Note the auditor himself cannot hold ANY security (the ₹1 lakh relaxation is only for relatives), the indebtedness limit is ₹5 lakh, and the 20-company ceiling excludes OPC/dormant/small/small-private companies.)*

### Q68. Ch: Audit & Auditors — Remuneration of Auditors (Sec 142) (Marks: 4) [Theory]

**Answer:** The remuneration of an auditor is governed by **Section 142** of the Companies Act, 2013.

### Q69. Ch: LLP Act 2008 — Nature of LLP & Mutual Rights of Partners (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** "A Limited Liability Partnership is a hybrid — it carries the flexibility of a partnership yet the shield of a company." Examine the statement by explaining the essential nature and characteristics of an LLP under the Limited Liability Partnership Act, 2008.

**Answer:**
A Limited Liability Partnership (LLP) is defined under **Section 2(1)(n) of the Limited Liability Partnership Act, 2008** as a partnership formed and registered under this Act. The statement is correct, and the "hybrid" nature is borne out by the following characteristics:

1. **Body corporate and separate legal entity** — Under **Section 3(1)**, an LLP is a body corporate formed and incorporated under the Act and is a legal entity separate from that of its partners. Consequently, it can hold property, sue and be sued in its own name, unlike an ordinary firm.

2. **Perpetual succession** — By virtue of **Section 3(2)**, an LLP has perpetual succession, and any change in the partners does not affect its existence, rights or liabilities. This is a company-like feature absent in a general partnership.

3. **Limited liability of partners** — Under **Section 26**, every partner is an agent of the LLP but not of the other partners; and under **Section 27(3)/(4)** the liability of a partner is limited to his agreed contribution, and an obligation of the LLP is solely its own obligation met out of its property. This is the "corporate shield".

4. **Flexibility through the LLP agreement** — Under **Section 23**, the mutual rights and duties of the partners and of the LLP are governed by the LLP agreement, giving partnership-like freedom of internal arrangement; where the agreement is silent, the provisions of **the First Schedule** apply.

5. **Minimum partners and designated partners** — Under **Section 6(1)**, every LLP must have at least two partners, and under **Section 7(1)** at least two designated partners, of whom one must be resident in India.

**Conclusion:** An LLP combines the separate-entity/perpetual-succession/limited-liability features of a company with the contractual internal flexibility of a partnership; hence it is correctly described as a hybrid.

### Q70. Ch: LLP Act 2008 — Extent of Liability of LLP & Partners (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Mr. Ranveer, a partner of "Skyline Design LLP", while acting in the course of the business of the LLP, negligently prepared a structural drawing which caused a client a loss of ₹40,00,000. In a separate matter, Mr. Ranveer, purporting to act on behalf of the LLP, committed a fraud on a supplier. Examine, under the Limited Liability Partnership Act, 2008:
(i) Whether the LLP is liable for the negligence and for the fraud.
(ii) Whether Mr. Ranveer is personally liable.
(iii) Whether the other innocent partners are personally liable.

**Answer:**
The extent of liability of an LLP and its partners is governed by **Sections 27 and 28 of the Limited Liability Partnership Act, 2008**.

**Statutory position:**
- Under **Section 27(2)**, the LLP is liable for the wrongful act or omission of a partner done in the course of the business of the LLP or with its authority.
- Under **Section 27(3)**, an obligation of the LLP, whether arising in contract or otherwise, is solely the obligation of the LLP, and its liabilities are met out of the property of the LLP.
- Under **Section 28(1)**, a partner is not personally liable, directly or indirectly, for an obligation of the LLP solely by reason of being a partner.
- However, under **Section 30(1)**, where an act is carried out by the LLP or any of its partners **with intent to defraud** creditors or for any fraudulent purpose, the liability of the LLP and the partners **who acted with such intent** is unlimited.
- Under **Section 28(2)**, a partner remains personally liable for his **own** wrongful act or omission.

**Application to facts:**

(i) **Negligence** — Preparing the drawing was in the course of the LLP's business, so under Section 27(2) the **LLP is liable** to the client for ₹40,00,000, met out of the LLP's property (Section 27(3)). **Fraud** — Since the fraud was purportedly on behalf of the LLP, the LLP is liable, but under Section 30(1) the liability arising from fraud is unlimited.

(ii) **Mr. Ranveer's personal liability** — For the negligence, he is personally liable for his own wrongful act under Section 28(2); the LLP's liability is in addition, not in substitution. For the fraud, being the partner who acted with intent to defraud, his liability is **unlimited** under Section 30(1).

(iii) **Other innocent partners** — Under Section 28(1) they are not personally liable merely for being partners. As they did not act with fraudulent intent, the proviso to Section 30(1) protects them; their liability remains limited to their contribution.

**Conclusion:** The LLP is liable in both matters; Mr. Ranveer is personally (and, for fraud, unlimitedly) liable; the innocent partners are not personally liable.

### Q71. Ch: General Clauses Act 1897 — "Shall" vs "May" (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain, with reference to the General Clauses Act, 1897 and settled principles of interpretation, how the words "shall" and "may" used in a statute are to be construed. Is the distinction rigid?

**Answer:**
The General Clauses Act, 1897 is an aid to the construction of Central Acts and Regulations. While the Act does not rigidly define "shall" and "may", the settled rules of interpretation read with the Act are as follows:

1. **Ordinary meaning of "shall"** — The word "shall" is ordinarily used to raise a **mandatory (imperative)** command; the act commanded must be done and cannot be dispensed with. For example, "the return shall be filed" ordinarily imposes an obligation.

2. **Ordinary meaning of "may"** — The word "may" is ordinarily used to confer a **discretionary (permissive/enabling)** power; the authority is empowered but not compelled to act. Under **Section 3(15)** and by usage, "may" typically enables rather than commands.

3. **The distinction is not rigid** — The construction depends on the **intention of the legislature**, the object and context of the provision, and the consequences of treating it as directory or mandatory:
   - "Shall" may be read as **directory** where no penalty attaches to non-compliance and the object is merely to promote orderly conduct of business.
   - "May" may be read as **mandatory ("shall")** where a duty is coupled with a power, i.e., where the power is conferred to enable performance of a public duty for the benefit of persons who have a right to demand its exercise.

4. **Test for mandatory vs directory** — Courts examine whether the provision goes to the **root/essence** of the matter (mandatory) or is a matter of **form/procedure** (directory), and whether the legislature intended non-compliance to invalidate the act.

**Conclusion:** Prima facie "shall" is mandatory and "may" is permissive, but the true nature is determined by the legislative intent, object and consequences, so the distinction is not absolute.

*(Full-marks tip: State the prima facie rule first, then the "duty coupled with power" exception for "may"; a bare "shall = must, may = can" answer will not fetch full marks.)*

### Q72. Ch: General Clauses Act 1897 — Effect of Repeal (Sec 6) — Application (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** The "Old Levy Act, 1990" imposed a cess and prescribed a penalty for its evasion. On 1st April 2026 the Old Levy Act was repealed by the "New Levy Act, 2026", which contained no saving clause. Before the repeal:
(a) A demand of cess of ₹5,00,000 had accrued against Mr. A and was pending recovery.
(b) Penalty proceedings for evasion had been initiated against Mr. B but not concluded.
(c) Mr. C had committed an evasion but no proceedings had yet begun.

Examine, under the General Clauses Act, 1897, whether the cess, penalty proceedings and prosecution can be continued after the repeal.

**Answer:**
The effect of repeal of a Central enactment, in the absence of a contrary intention, is governed by **Section 6 of the General Clauses Act, 1897**.

**Statutory position — Section 6:** Where any Central Act repeals any enactment, then, unless a different intention appears, the repeal shall **not**:
- (a) revive anything not in force at the time of repeal;
- (b) affect the **previous operation** of the repealed enactment or anything duly done thereunder;
- (c) affect any **right, privilege, obligation or liability acquired, accrued or incurred** under the repealed enactment;
- (d) affect any **penalty, forfeiture or punishment incurred** in respect of any offence committed against the repealed enactment;
- (e) affect any **investigation, legal proceeding or remedy** in respect of such right, liability or penalty; and any such proceeding may be continued and the penalty imposed **as if the repealing Act had not been passed**.

Here, since the New Levy Act, 2026 contains **no saving clause**, but is **silent** (does not evince a contrary intention to wipe out accrued matters), the general saving in Section 6 applies.

**Application to facts:**

(a) **Mr. A (accrued cess of ₹5,00,000)** — The liability to pay cess had already **accrued** before repeal. Under Section 6(c) and 6(e) it is a liability incurred and can be recovered by continuing the remedy as if the Act had not been repealed. **Cess is recoverable.**

(b) **Mr. B (pending penalty proceedings)** — The penalty was **incurred** and proceedings **initiated** before repeal. Under Section 6(d) and 6(e), the pending proceeding may be continued and penalty imposed. **Penalty proceedings survive.**

(c) **Mr. C (offence committed, no proceedings begun)** — The offence was **committed** (the punishment "incurred") before repeal; Section 6(d)/(e) permits a **fresh** investigation or legal proceeding in respect of a penalty already incurred. **Prosecution can be initiated.**

**Conclusion:** As the repealing Act shows no contrary intention, Section 6 preserves the accrued cess, the pending penalty proceedings, and even a fresh prosecution for an offence already committed.

*(Full-marks tip: Quote clauses (c), (d) and (e) of Section 6 and stress "unless a different intention appears"; candidates lose marks by letting Mr. C escape merely because proceedings had not started.)*

### Q73. Ch: General Clauses Act 1897 — Computation of Time (Sec 9 & 10) — Application (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** A statute requires an appeal to be filed "within 30 days from the date of the order". The order was passed and communicated on **15th June 2026**. The 30th day, i.e., **15th July 2026**, was a public holiday on which the appellate office was closed, and the office was also closed on **16th July 2026** (a second declared holiday). The appellant filed the appeal on **17th July 2026**. Examine, with reference to the General Clauses Act, 1897, whether the appeal is within time.
Also state the position if the statute had said "within 30 days **beginning with** the date of the order".

**Answer:**
The computation of a period of time and the effect of a closed office on the last day are governed by **Sections 9 and 10 of the General Clauses Act, 1897**.

**Statutory position:**
- **Section 9 (commencement and termination of time):** Where a period is expressed to begin "**from**" a specified day, the **first day is excluded**; the word "from" excludes the opening day and "to" includes the closing day.
- **Section 10 (computation of time):** Where any act is directed to be done within a prescribed period, and the **last day is a holiday** on which the office/court is closed, the act shall be considered as done in time if it is done on the **next day on which the office is open**. (Section 10 does not apply where a period is prescribed under the Indian Limitation Act for those purposes, but applies to acts before offices generally.)

**Application to facts (period "from the date of the order"):**
- The word "from" the date 15th June 2026 means, under Section 9, the 15th June is **excluded**. Time begins to run from 16th June 2026.
- Counting 30 days from 16th June: the 30th day falls on **15th July 2026**.
- Since 15th July 2026 was a holiday and the office was closed, and 16th July 2026 was also a holiday, under Section 10 the act may be done on the **next day the office reopens**, i.e., **17th July 2026**.
- The appeal filed on 17th July 2026 is therefore **within time**.

**If the statute said "beginning with the date of the order":**
- The expression "beginning with" **includes** the first day (15th June 2026). This displaces the Section 9 exclusion.
- Counting 30 days **including** 15th June, the 30th day would fall on **14th July 2026**. Section 10 would then extend only if the 14th (and following days) were holidays; on the facts the 14th was a working day, so the last date would be 14th July 2026, and a filing on 17th July would be **time-barred**.

**Conclusion:** Under the "from the date" wording the appeal on 17th July 2026 is in time (Sections 9 and 10); under the "beginning with" wording it would be barred by limitation.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the "from" → exclude first day (Sec 9) plus "holiday on last day" → next open day (Sec 10) combination, and the contrast with "beginning with". Losing the difference between the two phrasings costs the application marks.)*

### Q74. Ch: LLP Act 2008 — Partner as Agent (Sec 26 & 27) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Mr. Kabir, a partner of "Horizon Traders LLP", entered into a contract with M/s Zenith Ltd. to purchase goods worth ₹12,00,000 on behalf of the LLP. Unknown to Zenith Ltd., Mr. Kabir had **no authority** under the LLP agreement to make purchases exceeding ₹5,00,000, and Zenith Ltd. genuinely believed he was authorised. Examine, under the Limited Liability Partnership Act, 2008, whether the LLP is bound by the contract.
Would your answer differ if Zenith Ltd. **knew** that Mr. Kabir had no such authority?

**Answer:**
The question turns on the agency of a partner and the binding effect of his acts on the LLP under **Sections 26 and 27 of the Limited Liability Partnership Act, 2008**.

**Statutory position:**
- **Section 26:** Every partner of an LLP is, for the purpose of the business of the LLP, the **agent of the LLP**, but **not of the other partners**.
- **Section 27(1):** An LLP is **not bound** by anything done by a partner in dealing with a person if —
  (a) the partner has in fact **no authority** to act for the LLP in doing a particular act, **and**
  (b) the person **knows** that the partner has no authority, **or does not know or believe him to be a partner** of the LLP.

The provision is cumulative — both conditions (a) and (b) must be satisfied before the LLP escapes liability. If the third party is unaware of the want of authority and believes the partner to be a partner, the LLP is bound.

**Application to facts:**
- Mr. Kabir in fact had no authority to purchase above ₹5,00,000, so condition (a) is met.
- However, Zenith Ltd. did **not know** of the want of authority and genuinely believed him authorised as a partner; condition (b) is **not** met.
- Since both conditions are not satisfied, **the LLP is bound** by the ₹12,00,000 contract with Zenith Ltd.

**If Zenith Ltd. knew of the want of authority:**
- Then both conditions (a) and (b) of Section 27(1) would be satisfied — the partner had no authority and the third party knew it.
- In that case the **LLP would not be bound** by the contract.

**Conclusion:** On the given facts the LLP is bound (only condition (a) is met); if Zenith Ltd. had knowledge of the lack of authority, the LLP would not be bound.

*(Full-marks tip: Stress the cumulative "and" in Section 27(1); candidates who treat mere want of authority as enough to free the LLP lose marks.)*

### Q75. Ch: LLP Act 2008 — Effect of Partner Ceasing / Cessation (Sec 24) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Mr. Dev was a partner of "Nova Consultants LLP". He resigned by giving 30 days' notice and ceased to be a partner on **10th May 2026**. On **2nd June 2026**, before the LLP had filed any notice of his cessation with the Registrar, Mr. Dev, holding himself out as a partner, obtained credit of ₹8,00,000 from Ms. Riya, who was unaware of his cessation. Examine, under the Limited Liability Partnership Act, 2008:
(i) When a person ceases to be a partner and the effect of cessation on his liability.
(ii) Whether the LLP is liable to Ms. Riya for the ₹8,00,000.
(iii) The continuing rights of Mr. Dev after cessation.

**Answer:**
The consequences of a person ceasing to be a partner of an LLP are governed by **Section 24 of the Limited Liability Partnership Act, 2008**.

**Statutory position — Section 24:**
- **24(1):** A person may cease to be a partner in accordance with the LLP agreement, or in the absence of agreement, by giving **30 days' notice** to the other partners. A person also ceases on death, dissolution, being declared of unsound mind, or being adjudged insolvent.
- **24(2):** A person who has ceased to be a partner does **not** thereby cease to be liable for obligations incurred **while he was a partner**.
- **24(3):** Notwithstanding cessation, the former partner (or his estate) is regarded, **in relation to any person dealing with the LLP**, as still being a partner **unless** — (a) the person has **notice** that he has ceased to be a partner, or (b) **notice of cessation** has been delivered to the Registrar.
- **24(5):** A former partner is entitled to receive from the LLP an amount equal to his **capital contribution** and his **right to share in the accumulated profits** (after deduction of losses), as per the agreement.

**Application to facts:**

(i) **Cessation:** Mr. Dev validly ceased to be a partner on 10th May 2026 by 30 days' notice under Section 24(1). Under Section 24(2), he remains liable for obligations incurred while he was a partner, but not for new obligations after cessation — subject to Section 24(3).

(ii) **Liability to Ms. Riya:** The credit was taken on 2nd June 2026, **after** cessation. But under Section 24(3), because (a) Ms. Riya had **no notice** of the cessation, and (b) **no notice** of cessation had been delivered to the Registrar, Mr. Dev is still regarded as a partner **in relation to Ms. Riya**. Hence the **LLP is liable** to Ms. Riya for ₹8,00,000, and Mr. Dev too is exposed as a holding-out partner. The LLP's remedy is to recover from Mr. Dev.

(iii) **Continuing rights:** Under Section 24(5), Mr. Dev is entitled to receive from the LLP his **capital contribution** and his **share of accumulated profits** (net of losses) as per the LLP agreement, unless the agreement provides otherwise.

**Conclusion:** Mr. Dev ceased on 10th May 2026, but because no notice was given to Ms. Riya or filed with the Registrar, the LLP is liable for the ₹8,00,000; Mr. Dev retains his right to his contribution and profit share.

*(Full-marks tip: The dual-notice test in Section 24(3) — notice to the third party OR filing with the Registrar — is the crux; forgetting Section 24(5) rights costs the last mark.)*

### Q78. Ch: General Clauses Act 1897 — Commencement & Construction of Statutes (Sec 5) (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** When does a Central Act come into operation? Explain, with reference to the General Clauses Act, 1897, the rules relating to the commencement of enactments, including the position where a particular date is appointed for enforcement.

**Answer:**
The commencement of Central enactments is governed by **Section 5 of the General Clauses Act, 1897**, read with **Section 3(13)** (definition of "commencement").

1. **Meaning of "commencement" — Section 3(13):** "Commencement", used with reference to an Act or Regulation, means the day on which the Act or Regulation **comes into force**.

2. **General rule — Section 5(1):** Where any Central Act is not expressed to come into operation on a particular day, it shall come into operation on the day on which it **receives the assent** — in the case of a Central Act made before the commencement of the Constitution, of the Governor-General, and in the case of an Act of Parliament, **of the President**.

**Conclusion:** A Central Act comes into force on the date of Presidential assent unless a specific commencement date is provided or is to be notified; and by Section 5(3) it operates from the immediately preceding midnight of the commencement day.

### Q79. Ch: LLP Act 2008 — Whistle Blowing (Sec 31) (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Discuss the provisions relating to protection of "whistle blowers" under the Limited Liability Partnership Act, 2008. What protection is available and to whom?

**Answer:**
The protection of whistle blowers in an LLP is contained in **Section 31 of the Limited Liability Partnership Act, 2008**.

1. **Reduction/waiver of penalty — Section 31(1):** The **Court or Tribunal may reduce or waive any penalty** leviable against any partner or employee of an LLP if it is satisfied that —
   (a) such partner or employee has provided **useful information** during investigation of the LLP, or
   (b) when any information given by any partner or employee (whether or not during investigation) leads to the LLP or any partner or employee being **convicted** under the Act or any other Act.

**Beneficiaries:** The protection extends to **partners as well as employees** of the LLP who come forward with useful information.

**Conclusion:** Section 31 incentivises disclosure by allowing waiver/reduction of penalty for informants and shields such partners or employees from victimisation in the terms of their partnership or employment.

### Q82. Ch: General Clauses Act 1897 — Definitions & "Document"/"Financial Year" applied; Duty coupled with power (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Under a Central Act it is provided that "the Board **may** grant a licence to any eligible applicant who has fulfilled all the prescribed conditions." Mr. Naval fulfilled every prescribed condition, yet the Board refused the licence purely at its whim, treating "may" as giving it unfettered discretion. Separately, the same Act uses the expression "**financial year**" without defining it.
Examine, with reference to the General Clauses Act, 1897 and settled principles:
(i) Whether the Board's use of "may" gives it unfettered discretion.
(ii) The meaning of "financial year".

**Answer:**
**(i) "May" as a duty coupled with a power:**
- Prima facie, under the settled rule read with the General Clauses Act, 1897, "may" is **enabling/discretionary**. However, where a **power is coupled with a duty** — i.e., the power is conferred to be exercised in favour of persons who, on fulfilling the conditions, have a **right to demand** its exercise — the word "may" is construed as "**shall**" and the authority is **bound** to act.
- Here, the licence is to be granted to "**any eligible applicant who has fulfilled all the prescribed conditions**." *Application:* Since Mr. Naval fulfilled all prescribed conditions, the Board's refusal at whim is **not sustainable**; "may" here is to be read as imposing an obligation, and the Board is bound to grant the licence.

**(ii) Meaning of "financial year" — Section 3(21) of the General Clauses Act, 1897:**
- Where a Central Act uses "financial year" without defining it, **Section 3(21)** applies: "**financial year**" shall mean the year commencing on the **1st day of April**.
- Hence the "financial year" runs from **1st April to 31st March** of the following year.

**Conclusion:** (i) The word "may" here is a power coupled with a duty and does not confer unfettered discretion; the Board is bound to grant the licence to Mr. Naval. (ii) By Section 3(21), "financial year" means the year commencing on 1st April.

- Under the First Schedule, **all the partners are entitled to share equally in the capital, profits and losses** of the LLP.

|---------|------------------------------|
| (a) Larger profit share | Fails — equal share only |
| (b) Competing business profit | LLP entitled to ₹2,00,000 |
| (c) New partner | Cannot be admitted without unanimous consent |

### Q84. Ch: General Clauses Act 1897 — Effect of Repeal & Re-enactment (Sec 6 & 24) with Computation of Time integration (Marks: 8) [Application]

It was **repealed and re-enacted** by the "New Regulatory Act, 2026", which came into force on **1st April 2026** and contained **no fresh set of rules** but continued the same appeal mechanism. Consider:
| No. | Facts |
|-----|-------|
| (i) | Rules made under the Old Act were in force on the date of repeal. Are they still valid under the New Act? |
| (ii) | An order was passed on **1st February 2026** under the Old Act; the 60-day appeal period straddles the repeal date. The 60th day fell on a court holiday. When does the appeal period end? |
| (iii) | A penalty had accrued under the Old Act before repeal but proceedings were pending. Can they continue? |

Examine with reference to the General Clauses Act, 1897 (Sections 6, 24 and the time-computation provisions).

**Answer:**
This problem integrates the **repeal-and-re-enactment** rule (Sections 6 and 24) with **computation of time** (Sections 9 and 10) of the General Clauses Act, 1897.

**(i) Continuance of rules made under the repealed Act — Section 24:**
- **Section 24** provides that where a Central Act is **repealed and re-enacted** (with or without modification), then, unless a different intention appears, any **rule, notification, order, scheme, form or bye-law made or issued under the repealed Act shall, so far as it is not inconsistent** with the re-enacted provisions, **continue in force** and be deemed to have been made or issued under the new Act, until superseded.

- The period is "**60 days from the date of the order**." Under **Section 9**, the word "from" **excludes the first day** (1st February 2026). Time begins on 2nd February 2026.
- Counting 60 days from 2nd February 2026: February 2026 (28 days) contributes 27 days (2nd to 28th) — but computing precisely, day 60 from the start reaches **2nd April 2026**. (Feb: 2–28 = 27 days; +31 in March = 58 days by 31 March; +2 in April = 60th day on 2nd April 2026.)
- Section 6(e)/Section 24 preserve the remedy of appeal notwithstanding the 1st April 2026 repeal — the appeal survives the repeal and re-enactment.
- Since the 60th day (2nd April 2026) fell on a **court holiday**, under **Section 10** the appeal may be filed on the **next day the court reopens** and will be treated as within time.

**(iii) Pending penalty proceedings — Section 6:**
- Under **Section 6(c), (d) and (e)**, a repeal does not, unless a contrary intention appears, affect any **liability/penalty incurred** or any **legal proceeding or remedy** in respect thereof; such proceeding may be **continued** as if the Act had not been repealed.
- The penalty had **accrued** and proceedings were **pending** before repeal; there being no contrary intention, they **may be continued** under the New Act. **Proceedings survive.**

**Conclusion:**
| Issue | Result |
|-------|--------|
| (i) Old rules | Continue in force under Section 24 (so far as consistent) |
| (ii) Appeal period | Ends on 2nd April 2026; being a holiday, extends to next working day (Secs 9 & 10) |
| (iii) Pending penalty | Survives and may be continued (Sec 6) |

### Q85. Ch: Interpretation of Statutes — Mischief Rule (Heydon's Case) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** A State enacts the "Consumer Protection (Retail Weighing) Act" whose long title states it was passed "to prevent the recurring evil of shopkeepers cheating buyers through tampered electronic weighing machines." Section 4 provides: "No person shall *use* any weighing instrument that has not been stamped by the Legal Metrology Officer." A shopkeeper, X, keeps two tampered un-stamped machines on his sales counter connected to power and displaying weights to customers, but claims he had not actually completed a single sale on them when the inspector arrived, hence he did not "use" them. Applying the appropriate rule of interpretation, decide whether X has contravened Section 4.

**Answer:**
The rule of interpretation attracted here is the **Mischief Rule**, also called the **Rule in Heydon's Case (1584)**, which is applied when the language of a provision (here the word "use") is capable of a narrow or a wide meaning and the Court must choose the meaning that suppresses the mischief and advances the remedy.

Under Heydon's Case, the Court must consider **four matters**: (i) what was the common law / position **before** the making of the Act; (ii) what was the **mischief and defect** for which the earlier law did not provide; (iii) what **remedy** the legislature has resolved and appointed to cure the mischief; and (iv) the **true reason of the remedy** — the Court must then make such construction as shall suppress the mischief and advance the remedy.

Applying these four steps to the facts:
- **Before the Act**, shopkeepers could keep and operate any weighing instrument without stamping, and buyers were being cheated.
- **The mischief** expressly recited in the long title is "the recurring evil of shopkeepers cheating buyers through tampered electronic weighing machines." The long title, though not part of the enacting words, is a legitimate aid to identify the mischief and general object of the Act.
- **The remedy** is to prohibit the use of any un-stamped instrument so that no cheating device is available at the point of sale.
- **The true reason** is buyer protection.

If the word "use" is given the **narrow meaning** urged by X (only a completed sale), a shopkeeper could keep any number of tampered machines switched on and displaying false weights and escape merely because the inspector arrived a second before a sale concluded. This would perpetuate the very mischief the Act sought to suppress. The Mischief Rule therefore requires "use" to be read **widely** to include keeping the instrument connected, operational and displaying weights in the course of business, i.e. keeping it ready and available for weighing.

| Approach | Meaning of "use" | Effect on the mischief |
|---|---|---|
| Narrow (literal, as X argues) | Only a completed sale transaction | Mischief survives — tampered machines can be kept "on" |
| Wide (Mischief Rule) | Keeping instrument connected, live and available for weighing | Mischief suppressed, remedy advanced |

**Conclusion:** Applying the Mischief Rule in Heydon's Case, "use" is construed widely; X, by keeping two tampered un-stamped machines switched on and displaying weights on his sales counter, has "used" them and has contravened Section 4.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards a candidate who names all four questions of Heydon's Case AND expressly ties each to the facts; merely reciting the four points without applying them, or confusing the Mischief Rule with the Golden Rule, loses 2–3 marks.)*

### Q86. Ch: Interpretation of Statutes — Golden Rule (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Section 12 of an Act reads: "Any person who, being an heir, kills the propositus shall be entitled to succeed to the estate of the deceased as the nearest heir." Read literally, a son who murders his father would inherit the father's estate because he remains "the nearest heir." A takes this literal reading and claims the estate of his father whom he has been convicted of murdering. Which rule of interpretation applies, and what is the outcome?

**Answer:**
The rule attracted is the **Golden Rule of Interpretation**. The Golden Rule states that ordinarily the words of a statute must be given their grammatical and ordinary meaning (the Literal Rule), **but** where the literal meaning leads to **manifest absurdity, repugnance, hardship or an outcome contrary to the intention of the legislature**, the Court may **modify or depart from the literal meaning** to the extent necessary to avoid that absurdity, while still keeping within the language used.

The Golden Rule operates at two levels — a **narrow application** (choosing between two ordinary meanings of the same word) and a **wider application** (modifying the language to avoid an absurd or immoral result). The classic illustration is that no person should be permitted to profit from his own crime.

Applying the rule to the facts:
- A **literal reading** of Section 12 would allow a murderer to inherit from his victim. This is a **manifestly absurd and immoral result**, and it defeats the presumed intention of the legislature, which could never have intended to reward homicide with an inheritance.
- Under the Golden Rule, the Court will read into Section 12 the necessary qualification that "heir" does not include an heir who has feloniously and deliberately caused the death of the propositus. This is the well-known **"forfeiture rule"** / the maxim that a **wrongdoer cannot be allowed to take advantage of his own wrong** (*nullus commodum capere potest de injuria sua propria*).

| Rule | Result on the facts |
|---|---|
| Literal Rule | Murderer-son inherits (absurd, immoral) |
| Golden Rule | "Heir" read down to exclude the murderer; A takes nothing |

**Conclusion:** Applying the Golden Rule, the literal meaning is departed from to avoid a manifestly absurd and immoral result; A, having murdered the propositus, is disqualified from succeeding and is not entitled to the estate.

*(Full-marks tip: State the ordinary/literal meaning FIRST, then show the absurdity, then modify — the examiner deducts marks if you jump straight to "murderer can't inherit" without first acknowledging that the Golden Rule begins with the literal meaning.)*

### Q87. Ch: Interpretation of Statutes — Ejusdem Generis (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** A municipal bye-law empowers the Commissioner to seize "any horse, cattle, pig, goat, sheep or other animal" found straying on a public street. The Commissioner seizes a person's pet dog that had strayed, and separately a domesticated elephant used in a temple procession that had wandered onto the road. The pet-owner argues that a dog is not covered; the temple argues the elephant is not covered. Decide, applying the correct secondary rule of interpretation.

**Answer:**
The rule attracted is the rule of **Ejusdem Generis** ("of the same kind or species"). The rule states that where **specific words** enumerating persons or things of a **particular class or genus** are followed by **general words** (such as "or other animal"), the general words are ordinarily construed as **limited to persons or things of the same class or genus** as the specific words, unless a contrary intention appears.

**Conditions for the rule to apply** (all must be satisfied):
1. The statute contains an **enumeration of specific words**;
2. The specific words **constitute a class or category** (they must have a common, distinguishable genus);
3. The class or category is **not exhausted** by the enumeration;
4. A **general term** follows the enumeration; and
5. There is **no contrary legislative intention** indicated.

**Identifying the genus:** The specific words — horse, cattle, pig, goat, sheep — share the common genus of **domestic quadruped livestock/farm animals** that are typically herded or reared and are prone to straying in herds on public streets. The general words "or other animal" must therefore be read as confined to animals of that same genus.

**Applying to the facts:**

| Animal seized | Same genus as horse/cattle/pig/goat/sheep? | Covered by "or other animal"? |
|---|---|---|
| Pet dog | A dog is a domestic animal of the same broad livestock/domestic-animal class that strays on streets — arguably within genus | Debatable; but a dog is a domestic straying animal of like kind, so it can fall within the genus of ordinary straying domestic animals |
| Domesticated elephant | An elephant is not a farm/livestock quadruped of the enumerated kind; it is a large wild-origin animal not reared as street livestock | Outside the genus — NOT covered |

On the accepted application of ejusdem generis, "or other animal" is limited to ordinary domestic/farm animals of the enumerated kind. A **pet dog**, being a common domestic animal liable to stray like the listed animals, falls within the genus and its seizure is valid. A **domesticated elephant**, not being an animal of the same farm-livestock genus, falls outside the general words and its seizure is not authorised.

**Conclusion:** Applying ejusdem generis, the seizure of the straying pet dog is within the bye-law, but the seizure of the temple elephant is beyond the power conferred and is invalid.

*(Full-marks tip: You must first identify the common "genus" of the specific words and state all five conditions; candidates who apply ejusdem generis without demonstrating that the specific words form a single class lose marks, because if there is no common genus the rule cannot operate.)*

### Q88. Ch: Interpretation of Statutes — Noscitur a Sociis (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** A tax exemption notification exempts "books, pamphlets, periodicals and other literature." An assessee claims exemption for printed **account books / blank ledger books** he sells, arguing they are "books." Separately he claims exemption for a printed **children's story book**. Applying the appropriate rule, determine whether either claim succeeds.

**Answer:**
The rule attracted is **Noscitur a Sociis** — "it is known by its associates." The rule states that the meaning of a **doubtful or ambiguous word** may be ascertained by reference to the **words associated with it** in the statute; a word takes colour and meaning from the company it keeps. Where words of analogous meaning are grouped together, they are understood in their cognate sense and take their meaning from one another.

Noscitur a sociis is the **broader rule** of which ejusdem generis is a specific application; noscitur a sociis is applied even where there is no clear "genus followed by general words," so long as the associated words indicate a common colour or theme.

**Applying to the facts:** The word "books" is associated with "pamphlets, periodicals and other literature." The **common colour** running through these associated words is that of **reading matter / material carrying literary, informative or educational content** — i.e. things one reads. The associated words therefore control the meaning of "books."

| Item claimed | Colour from associated words (reading/literary matter)? | Exempt? |
|---|---|---|
| Blank account/ledger books | These are **stationery** for writing in; they carry no reading matter and take no colour from "pamphlets, periodicals, literature" | NOT exempt |
| Children's story book | This is genuine **reading/literary matter** of the same colour as pamphlets and periodicals | Exempt |

Because "books" is coloured by "pamphlets, periodicals and other literature," it means books that are **read** (literary/reading matter), not blank books that are **written in**. A blank account book, though physically a "book," does not share the colour of its associates and is excluded, whereas a children's story book squarely shares that colour.

**Conclusion:** Applying noscitur a sociis, the claim for exemption on **blank account/ledger books fails**, while the claim on the **children's story book succeeds**.

### Q89. Ch: Interpretation of Statutes — Literal Rule & Expressio Unius Est Exclusio Alterius (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Section 9 of a rent-control statute provides: "A landlord may recover possession of the premises where the tenant has *sub-let, assigned or otherwise parted with possession* of the whole of the premises without the landlord's written consent." A landlord seeks eviction on the ground that the tenant sub-let only **one room out of five** rooms, and separately argues that because the section lists these three grounds, the maxim *expressio unius est exclusio alterius* helps him. Advise the landlord using the appropriate rules.

**Answer:**
Two rules of interpretation are attracted: the **Literal Rule** and the maxim **Expressio Unius Est Exclusio Alterius** ("the express mention of one thing implies the exclusion of another").

**(a) Literal Rule on "the whole of the premises":** The Literal Rule (also called the Grammatical Rule) requires that where the words of a statute are **plain, clear and unambiguous**, they must be given their ordinary, natural and grammatical meaning, and the Court cannot add to or subtract from them, even if the result seems harsh. Section 9 permits recovery only where the tenant has parted with possession of **"the whole of the premises."** The words are plain. On the facts, the tenant sub-let **only one room out of five** — he has parted with a **part**, not "the whole." Applying the plain words, the ground for eviction is **not made out**; the Court cannot rewrite "whole" to mean "part."

**(b) Expressio unius est exclusio alterius:** This maxim states that the **express mention of one or more things of a particular class** may be taken to imply the **exclusion of all other things** of that class not mentioned. It is, however, only an **aid to construction and not a rule of law**, and must be applied with great caution, as its application may defeat legislative intent where the enumeration is merely illustrative.

Here the section expressly specifies the modes "sub-let, assigned or *otherwise parted with possession*." The presence of the words **"or otherwise parted with possession"** shows the enumeration is **not a closed list** but is followed by residuary general words. Therefore expressio unius does **not** assist the landlord to add new grounds; rather, the residuary phrase already widens the modes of parting with possession. But this cannot cure defect (a): whatever the mode, it must be of **"the whole"** of the premises.

| Landlord's argument | Rule | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Sub-letting one of five rooms | Literal Rule — "whole" means whole | Ground fails — only part parted with |
| Expressio unius helps him | Maxim is only a cautious aid; "or otherwise" shows list is not exhaustive | Does not assist him |

**Conclusion:** Applying the Literal Rule, since the tenant parted with possession of only a part (one room) and not "the whole of the premises," the landlord **cannot recover possession** under Section 9; the maxim expressio unius does not aid him.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner wants you to note that expressio unius is "a valuable servant but a dangerous master" — merely an aid, applied with caution — and NOT to invoke it where the statute itself uses residuary words like "or otherwise"; treating the maxim as a binding rule loses marks.)*

### Q90. Ch: Interpretation of Statutes — Harmonious Construction (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** In an Act, Section 15 provides: "Every appeal shall be filed within 30 days of the order." A later section, Section 41, provides: "Notwithstanding anything contained in this Act, the Appellate Tribunal may, in its discretion, condone delay in filing an appeal on sufficient cause being shown." A registry officer refuses to accept an appeal filed on the 45th day, relying only on Section 15, and contends that the two sections are contradictory and Section 15 being the specific limitation must prevail. Advise, applying the correct rule of interpretation.

**Answer:**
The rule attracted is the **Rule of Harmonious Construction**. When there are, in the same statute, **two or more provisions which appear to conflict** with each other, the Court must interpret them in such a way as to **give effect to both**, so far as possible, rather than rendering either of them nugatory or otiose. The legislature is presumed not to contradict itself, and every part of the statute is presumed to have been enacted for a purpose and must be given effect.

**Principles of harmonious construction:**
1. The Courts must avoid a "head-on clash" between provisions and construe them so that they **stand together**;
2. A provision of one section cannot be used to **defeat another** unless the Court is unable, on any fair construction, to reconcile them;
3. The interpretation which **reduces one provision to a dead letter** is not harmonious construction and must be avoided;
4. Where reconciliation is impossible, the Court must determine which provision the legislature intended to prevail — but this is a last resort.

**Applying to the facts:** Section 15 lays down the **normal period** of limitation (30 days). Section 41 opens with a **non-obstante clause** ("Notwithstanding anything contained in this Act") and confers a **discretion to condone delay** on sufficient cause. The two provisions are **not truly in conflict**: Section 15 fixes the period, and Section 41 provides a **safety valve** to relieve against that period in deserving cases. Read together and harmoniously, Section 15 governs ordinary cases, while Section 41 empowers the Tribunal to entertain a late appeal where sufficient cause is shown. The non-obstante clause in Section 41 confirms that it is intended to **override** the rigidity of Section 15 in appropriate cases.

The registry officer's reading, which treats Section 15 as absolute, would render Section 41 **wholly redundant and a dead letter** — precisely the result harmonious construction forbids.

**Conclusion:** Applying harmonious construction, Sections 15 and 41 must be read together so that both operate; the appeal filed on the 45th day cannot be rejected mechanically — it must be placed before the Appellate Tribunal, which may condone the 15-day delay under Section 41 on sufficient cause being shown.

*(Full-marks tip: Point out the effect of the non-obstante clause and expressly state that an interpretation making Section 41 "otiose/a dead letter" is impermissible — that sentence is where the marks lie; simply saying "both should be read together" without the redundancy reasoning is incomplete.)*

### Q91. Ch: Interpretation of Statutes — Internal Aids: Proviso, Explanation & Non-obstante Clause (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish, with the function of each as an internal aid to interpretation, between a **Proviso**, an **Explanation** and a **Non-obstante clause** appearing in a section of a statute. Illustrate briefly how a Court treats each.

**Answer:**
**Internal aids to construction** are those aids found **within the statute itself** (as opposed to external aids). A proviso, an explanation and a non-obstante clause are three distinct internal aids, each performing a different function; a candidate must not confuse their roles.

**1. Proviso:** The normal function of a proviso is to **carve out an exception** to the main enactment to which it is appended — it qualifies or creates an exception to what is in the enactment, and ordinarily deals only with the subject-matter of that enactment. A proviso is **not to be construed as enlarging** the main provision; its office is to except and deal with a case which would otherwise fall within the general language of the main enactment. It usually begins with the words "Provided that…".

**2. Explanation:** An Explanation is added to a section to **explain, clarify or elaborate** the meaning of the words in the section and to make explicit that which was already implicit. Its purpose is to **remove doubts**, to clarify an ambiguity, or sometimes to fill a gap — but an Explanation does **not** ordinarily curtail or widen the scope of the main provision; it is meant to **harmonise and clear up** any ambiguity in the main section. It usually begins with "Explanation.—".

**3. Non-obstante clause:** A non-obstante clause is a clause beginning with **"Notwithstanding anything contained in…"**. Its function is to give the enacting part of the section, in case of conflict, an **overriding effect** over the provision(s) mentioned in the non-obstante clause. It is a legislative device to **give effect to the enacting part notwithstanding** the contrary provisions referred to.

| Feature | Proviso | Explanation | Non-obstante clause |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opening words | "Provided that…" | "Explanation.—" | "Notwithstanding anything…" |
| Primary function | Creates an exception / qualifies the main provision | Clarifies / removes doubt / explains | Gives overriding effect in case of conflict |
| Effect on scope | Ordinarily narrows (excepts) | Neither narrows nor widens (clarifies) | Overrides contrary provisions |

**How the Court treats each:** A proviso is read together with the main section and confined to it; an Explanation is read as part of the section to resolve doubt; and where a non-obstante clause conflicts with another provision, the Court gives the non-obstante provision precedence, unless a contrary intention is shown.

**Conclusion:** Though all three are internal aids, their functions differ — a proviso excepts, an Explanation clarifies, and a non-obstante clause overrides.

*(Full-marks tip: A crisp comparative table plus the correct opening words of each ("Provided that", "Explanation.—", "Notwithstanding") earns the marks; candidates commonly and wrongly say a proviso "adds to" the main provision — the examiner penalises that.)*

### Q92. Ch: FEMA 1999 — Person Resident in India: Individual (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Determine, with reasons under the Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999, the residential status ("person resident in India" or not) in each of the following independent situations for the financial year 2025-26:

| Case | Facts |
|---|---|
| (a) | Mr. A, an Indian citizen, left India on 5th May 2025 to take up employment in Dubai for an indefinite period. |
| (b) | Mr. B, a German citizen, came to India on 1st June 2025 to set up and run a manufacturing unit for 3 years. |
| (c) | Ms. C, an Indian citizen who had settled in Canada, came to India on 10th April 2025 only for a 2-month holiday to visit her parents. |
| (d) | XYZ Ltd, a company registered and incorporated in India, having a branch office in Singapore. |

**Answer:**
Under **Section 2(v) of FEMA, 1999**, a "**person resident in India**" means, in the case of an **individual**, a person who has **resided in India for more than 182 days during the preceding financial year**, **but does not include**:
- **(A)** a person who has gone out of India or who stays outside India, in either case — (i) for or on taking up **employment** outside India, or (ii) for carrying on **business or vocation** outside India, or (iii) for any other purpose, in such circumstances as would indicate his intention to stay outside India **for an uncertain period**; and
- **(B)** a person who has **come to or stays in India**, otherwise than — (i) for or on taking up **employment** in India, or (ii) for carrying on **business or vocation** in India, or (iii) for any other purpose, in such circumstances as would indicate his intention to stay in India **for an uncertain period**.

Thus, unlike the Income-tax Act, FEMA's test is **purpose-driven**, not merely a day-count; the intention behind coming to or leaving India is decisive.

**Application:**

| Case | Reasoning under Section 2(v) | Status FY 2025-26 |
|---|---|---|
| (a) Mr. A | Though he resided in India in the preceding year, he has **left India to take up employment abroad**. Under exclusion (A)(i) he is excluded the moment he leaves for employment outside India | **NOT** a person resident in India |
| (b) Mr. B | He has **come to India to carry on business** (set up and run a unit) for 3 years, indicating intention to stay for an uncertain/long period; he falls within limb (B)(ii) as coming for business | **Person resident in India** (from the date he comes for that purpose) |
| (c) Ms. C | She has come to India **only for a 2-month holiday** (a visit), not for employment, business, or with intention to stay for an uncertain period; she falls within exclusion (B) | **NOT** a person resident in India |
| (d) XYZ Ltd | Under Section 2(v)(iii), **any agency, office or branch owned or controlled by a person resident in India**, and any company/body corporate **registered or incorporated in India**, is a person resident in India. XYZ Ltd is incorporated in India | **Person resident in India** (its Singapore branch is a PROI's branch) |

**Conclusion:** (a) Mr. A — not resident; (b) Mr. B — resident in India; (c) Ms. C — not resident; (d) XYZ Ltd — resident in India.

*(Full-marks tip: State the PURPOSE test clearly and contrast it with the Income-tax 182-day rule; the examiner deducts marks if you apply only a mechanical day-count and ignore the "employment/business/uncertain period" limbs of Section 2(v).)*

### Q93. Ch: FEMA 1999 — Person Resident in India: Body Corporate & Contradiction with Preceding Year (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Mr. R, an Indian citizen, went abroad on 1st March 2025 for higher studies (a 2-year course) and returned to India on 20th June 2025 permanently after abandoning the course. During FY 2024-25 he had stayed in India for 250 days. Determine his residential status under FEMA, 1999 for FY 2025-26 as on 1st July 2025, and explain why the mere day-count in the preceding year is not conclusive.

**Answer:**
Under **Section 2(v)(i) of FEMA, 1999**, a "person resident in India" in the case of an individual is a person who resided in India for **more than 182 days during the preceding financial year**, subject to the two exclusions in limbs **(A)** and **(B)**. The provision must be read as a whole: the 182-day test is only the **opening threshold**, and the two exclusions — dealing with the **purpose** of leaving India or coming to/staying in India — can **override** the day-count.

**Application:**
- In the **preceding financial year (FY 2024-25)**, Mr. R stayed in India for **250 days**, i.e. more than 182 days. On the opening threshold he would qualify as a person resident in India.
- **Exclusion (A)** excludes a person who has gone out of India for "**any other purpose in such circumstances as would indicate his intention to stay outside India for an uncertain period**." Going abroad for higher studies is generally treated as going for a purpose indicating an intention to stay outside India for an uncertain period, and thus a student going abroad may be excluded as long as he stays abroad for that purpose.
- However, Mr. R **returned to India permanently on 20th June 2025** after abandoning the course. As on **1st July 2025**, his intention is to stay in India permanently — he is no longer "staying outside India" for an uncertain period. His purpose in being in India is now permanent settlement, which falls **outside** exclusion (B) (he has not come merely for a temporary purpose). Combined with the fact that he satisfied the 182-day threshold in the preceding year, he is a person resident in India.

**Why the day-count is not conclusive:** FEMA's residence test is **purpose/intention-based**; a person who satisfies 182 days in the preceding year may still **cease** to be resident if he leaves for employment/business/uncertain-period purposes, and equally a returning person is judged by the intention with which he is now in India. The day-count is merely the starting point, not the end of the enquiry.

**Conclusion:** As on 1st July 2025, Mr. R is a **person resident in India** for FY 2025-26, because he satisfied the 182-day threshold in the preceding year and, having returned permanently, does not fall within the exclusion for persons staying abroad for an uncertain period.

*(Full-marks tip: The marks lie in explaining that the 182-day rule is only the "gateway" and the purpose-based exclusions decide the matter; a candidate who mechanically says "250 > 182, therefore resident" without discussing the study-abroad exclusion and the permanent return earns only partial credit.)*

### Q94. Ch: FEMA 1999 — Authorised Person (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Who is an "authorised person" under the Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999? Explain the categories included in the definition and state briefly the obligations imposed on an authorised person in relation to dealings in foreign exchange under Section 10.

**Answer:**
Under **Section 2(c) of FEMA, 1999**, "**authorised person**" means an **authorised dealer, money-changer, off-shore banking unit or any other person for the time being authorised under sub-section (1) of Section 10** to deal in foreign exchange or in foreign securities. Thus an authorised person is a person **specifically authorised by the Reserve Bank of India (RBI)** to deal in forex.

**Categories included in the definition:**
1. **Authorised Dealer (AD)** — typically banks authorised to deal in foreign exchange for all permitted purposes;
2. **Money-changer** — authorised to undertake money-changing business (purchase/sale of foreign currency notes, coins and travellers' cheques);
3. **Off-shore banking unit** — a branch of a bank located in a Special Economic Zone / IFSC authorised to deal in forex; and
4. **Any other person** for the time being authorised under Section 10(1) by the RBI.

**Authorisation and obligations under Section 10:**
- Under **Section 10(1)**, the **RBI may, on an application, authorise** any person to be an authorised person to deal in foreign exchange or foreign securities, subject to conditions; the authorisation is in **writing** and subject to such conditions as may be laid down.
- Under **Section 10(4)**, an authorised person, in all his dealings in foreign exchange or foreign security, shall **comply with such general or special directions or orders** as the RBI may give, and, except with the previous permission of the RBI, shall **not engage in any transaction** involving any foreign exchange or foreign security which is **not in conformity** with the terms of his authorisation.
- Under **Section 10(5)**, an authorised person shall, before undertaking any transaction on behalf of any person, **require that person to make a declaration** and give such information as will reasonably satisfy him that the transaction will **not involve, and is not designed for the purpose of any contravention or evasion** of the Act. If the person **refuses or fails** to comply, the authorised person **shall refuse in writing** to undertake the transaction and, if he has reason to believe a contravention/evasion is intended, **report the matter to the RBI**.
- Under **Section 10(6)**, any person who has acquired forex for a declared purpose but does not use it for that purpose, or uses it for a different purpose, is deemed to have committed a contravention.

**Conclusion:** An "authorised person" under Section 2(c) is a person (AD, money-changer, off-shore banking unit or other person) authorised by the RBI under Section 10(1) to deal in foreign exchange, and is bound by the compliance, declaration-verification and reporting obligations under Section 10.

### Q95. Ch: FEMA 1999 — Current Account Transaction (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** State whether each of the following is a **current account transaction** or a **capital account transaction** under FEMA, 1999, and identify which of them are (i) freely permitted, (ii) permitted with monetary/other limits or RBI/Central Government approval, or (iii) prohibited, under the Foreign Exchange Management (Current Account Transactions) Rules, 2000:

| Case | Transaction |

### Q96. Ch: FEMA 1999 — Capital Account Transaction & Regulatory Scheme (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the concept of a "capital account transaction" under FEMA, 1999 and the scheme of regulation of capital account transactions under Section 6. Also state the power of the Reserve Bank to specify permissible capital account transactions.

**Answer:**
Under **Section 2(e) of FEMA, 1999**, a "**capital account transaction**" means a transaction which **alters the assets or liabilities, including contingent liabilities, outside India of persons resident in India, or assets or liabilities in India of persons resident outside India**, and includes transactions referred to in **sub-section (3) of Section 6**. The distinguishing feature is that it **changes the capital position** (assets/liabilities) across the border, as opposed to a current account transaction which is a routine transaction not so altering assets/liabilities.

**Scheme of regulation under Section 6:**
- **Section 6(1)** provides that, **subject to the provisions of sub-section (2)**, any person may **sell or draw foreign exchange to or from an authorised person for a capital account transaction**.
- **Section 6(2)** empowers the **Reserve Bank** to, in consultation with the Central Government, **specify** — (a) any **class or classes of capital account transactions**, involving debt instruments, which are **permissible**; (b) the **limit** up to which foreign exchange shall be admissible for such transactions; and (c) any conditions which may be placed on such transactions. [Post the 2015 amendment, the Central Government regulates non-debt instruments and the RBI regulates debt instruments.]
- **Section 6(2A)** provides that the Central Government may, in consultation with RBI, prescribe the class of capital account transactions involving **non-debt instruments**, the limits and conditions.
- Importantly, the **proviso to Section 6** ensures that the RBI shall **not impose any restriction on the drawal of foreign exchange for payment due on account of amortisation of loans or for depreciation of direct investments in the ordinary course of business**.

**Power to specify permissible transactions:** The RBI/Central Government, under the regulations framed (e.g. FEM (Permissible Capital Account Transactions) Regulations), specify **two lists** — (i) permissible capital account transactions of **persons resident in India** (such as investment abroad, acquisition of immovable property outside India, foreign currency loans, etc.), and (ii) permissible capital account transactions of **persons resident outside India** (such as investment in India, acquisition of immovable property in India, etc.). Any capital account transaction **not specifically permitted** is, in general, **not allowed** without approval.

| Aspect | Current account (Sec 2(j)) | Capital account (Sec 2(e)) |
|---|---|---|
| Effect | Does not alter cross-border assets/liabilities | Alters assets/liabilities outside/in India |
| Default rule | Generally **free** unless restricted | Generally **restricted** unless permitted |
| Regulation | Current Account Rules, 2000 | Section 6 + permissible-transaction regulations |

**Conclusion:** A capital account transaction under Section 2(e) is one that alters cross-border assets or liabilities; it is regulated under Section 6, being permissible only to the extent, and subject to the limits and conditions, specified by the RBI / Central Government.

*(Full-marks tip: Emphasise the reversed default — current account transactions are "free unless prohibited," capital account transactions are "prohibited unless permitted"; citing Section 6(2)/(2A) and the debt vs non-debt instrument split earns the top band.)*

### Q97. Ch: FEMA 1999 — Contravention & Penalty (Section 13) (Marks: 6) [Application]

### Q98. Ch: FEMA 1999 — Compounding of Contravention (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the provisions relating to **compounding of contraventions** under Section 15 of FEMA, 1999. Who may compound, within what time, and what is the effect of compounding? State the contraventions that cannot be compounded.

**Answer:**
**Compounding** is the process by which a person who has **admittedly committed a contravention** of FEMA voluntarily accepts the contravention and applies to have it settled by payment of a compounding amount, thereby avoiding prolonged adjudication and litigation.

**Statutory basis — Section 15:** Under **Section 15(1) of FEMA, 1999**, any contravention under **Section 13** may, **on an application made by the person committing such contravention**, be **compounded** within **180 days from the date of receipt of the application** by the **Director of Enforcement or such other officers of the Directorate of Enforcement and the Reserve Bank as may be authorised** in this behalf by the Central Government.

**Effect of compounding — Section 15(2):** Where a contravention has been compounded under sub-section (1), **no proceeding or further proceeding**, as the case may be, shall be initiated or continued, as the case may be, against the person committing such contravention **in respect of the contravention so compounded**. Thus compounding brings the matter to a close for that contravention.

**Key features of the compounding scheme:**
1. **Voluntary admission** — compounding presupposes the person **admits** the contravention; it is not available to contest guilt.
2. **Authority** — the RBI compounds most contraventions; contraventions relating to certain matters (e.g. those under Section 3(a) — hawala type dealings) are compounded by the **Directorate of Enforcement**.
3. **Time limit** — the contravention must be compounded **within 180 days** of receipt of the application.
4. **Payment** — the sum for which the contravention is compounded must be **paid within 15 days** of the order of compounding; on failure, the person is deemed **never to have made** the compounding application and the contravention proceeds as if not compounded.

**Contraventions that cannot be compounded:**
- Where the **amount involved is not quantifiable**, or
- A contravention under **Section 3(a)** may be compounded only by the Directorate of Enforcement (not RBI), and
- Importantly, **no contravention shall be compounded** where a **similar contravention** by the same person has been **compounded within the last three years** (a repeat within 3 years is treated as a fresh contravention and is not compoundable within that period), and
- Contraventions involving **money-laundering, national security or serious concerns** are not compounded.

**Conclusion:** Under Section 15, a contravention under Section 13 may, on the contravener's application, be compounded within 180 days by the authorised RBI/ED officers; once compounded, no further proceedings lie for that contravention, subrto the 3-year repeat bar and non-compoundable categories.

*(Full-marks tip: State the 180-day compounding period AND the 3-year repeat bar AND the 15-day payment rule — those three specifics separate a full-marks answer from an average one; simply saying "contraventions can be settled by paying money" is inadequate.)*

### Q99. Ch: FEMA 1999 — Establishment in India of Branch/Office by Non-resident + Export of Goods (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** ABC Inc., a company incorporated in the USA and a person resident outside India, wishes to (a) establish a **branch office** in India to carry on manufacturing activity, and (b) an Indian exporter, Mr. E, has exported goods worth **USD 50,000** but has not realised the export proceeds even after **12 months**. Advise on the FEMA position in each case.

**Answer:**
**(a) Establishment of a place of business in India by a person resident outside India — Section 6(3)(f) read with FEMA regulations:** Under FEMA, 1999, a **person resident outside India** cannot, without the **prior approval of the Reserve Bank**, establish in India a **branch, office or other place of business** for carrying on any activity, in cases where such approval is required under the relevant FEM (Establishment in India of a branch office or a liaison office or a project office or any other place of business) Regulations. ### Q100. Ch: FEMA 1999 — Realisation, Holding & Repatriation of Foreign Exchange (Section 8 & Exemptions) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the obligation regarding **realisation and repatriation of foreign exchange** under Section 8 of FEMA, 1999. State the meaning of "repatriate to India" under Section 2(y), and the circumstances in which a resident is exempted from surrendering/realising foreign exchange.

**Answer:**
**Obligation under Section 8:** Under **Section 8 of FEMA, 1999**, where any amount of **foreign exchange is due or has accrued to any person resident in India**, such person shall take **all reasonable steps to realise and repatriate to India** such foreign exchange within such period and in such manner as may be specified by the **Reserve Bank**, except to the extent otherwise provided under the Act.
