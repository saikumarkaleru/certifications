### Q1. Ch: Preliminary & Definitions — Associate Company via the 20% Voting-Power Test (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Sunrise Ltd. holds equity shares in three investee companies. The relevant data for the year ended 31st March, 2026 is given below. There are no preference shares carrying voting rights and no shareholders' agreement in any case. Examine, giving reasons, whether each investee is an "associate company" of Sunrise Ltd. within the meaning of Section 2(6) of the Companies Act, 2013.

| Investee | Total paid-up equity (₹) | Equity held by Sunrise Ltd. (₹) | Board seats controlled by Sunrise Ltd. |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alpha Ltd. | 1,00,00,000 | 18,00,000 | Nil |
| Beta Ltd. | 50,00,000 | 12,50,000 | Nil |
| Gamma Ltd. | 2,00,00,000 | 90,00,000 | Composition of Board controlled |

**Answer:**
Section 2(6) of the Companies Act, 2013 defines an "associate company", in relation to another company, as a company in which that other company has a **significant influence**, but which is not a subsidiary of the company having such influence, and includes a joint venture company. The Explanation to Section 2(6) clarifies that "significant influence" means **control of at least twenty per cent of the total voting power**, or control of or participation in business decisions under an agreement. "Total voting power" is defined in Section 2(89).

The determining line, therefore, is the **20% voting-power threshold** (there being no agreement in any case here), coupled with the negative condition that the investee must **not** be a subsidiary under Section 2(87).

**Alpha Ltd.:** Voting power held = 18,00,000 / 1,00,00,000 = **18%**. This is below the 20% threshold and there is no agreement conferring participation in business decisions. Hence Alpha Ltd. is **not** an associate company of Sunrise Ltd.

**Beta Ltd.:** Voting power held = 12,50,000 / 50,00,000 = **25%**. This equals or exceeds 20% and Sunrise Ltd. does not control Beta's Board (so Beta is not a subsidiary). Sunrise thus has significant influence and Beta Ltd. **is an associate company**.

**Gamma Ltd.:** Voting power held = 90,00,000 / 2,00,00,000 = 45%; more importantly, Sunrise Ltd. **controls the composition of Gamma's Board of Directors**. Under Section 2(87), controlling Board composition makes Gamma a **subsidiary**. Since Section 2(6) expressly excludes a subsidiary, Gamma Ltd. is **not** an associate company — it is a subsidiary company.

**Conclusion:** Beta Ltd. is the only associate company (25% voting power). Alpha Ltd. fails the 20% test, and Gamma Ltd. is a subsidiary and is therefore excluded from the definition of associate company.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards computing the exact percentage and then testing it against the 20% figure, and specifically catching that a subsidiary is carved out of Section 2(6). Marks are lost for treating a 45% holding as "associate" merely because it is below 50% — Board control makes it a subsidiary regardless of percentage.)*

### Q2. Ch: Preliminary & Definitions — Subsidiary by Total Voting Power vs. Layers (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Examine the following independent situations under Section 2(87) of the Companies Act, 2013 read with the relevant Rule:
(i) Vishal Ltd. holds 40% of the equity (each share carrying one vote) of Neel Ltd. and, in addition, another company Om Ltd. — which is itself a wholly-owned subsidiary of Vishal Ltd. — holds a further 15% of the equity of Neel Ltd. Is Neel Ltd. a subsidiary of Vishal Ltd.?
(ii) Preferred Ltd. controls the composition of the Board of Directors of Q Ltd. but holds only 8% of Q Ltd.'s share capital. Is Q Ltd. a subsidiary of Preferred Ltd.?

**Answer:**
Under **Section 2(87)**, a "subsidiary company", in relation to a holding company, means a company in which the holding company **(a) controls the composition of the Board of Directors; or (b) exercises or controls more than one-half of the total voting power** either at its own or together with one or more of its subsidiary companies. The Explanation provides that a company shall be deemed to be a subsidiary of the holding company even if the control is of a subsidiary of the holding company.

**(i)** The holding must be tested **on a combined basis** with subsidiaries. Vishal Ltd. directly controls 40% of the voting power of Neel Ltd. Om Ltd. is a wholly-owned subsidiary of Vishal Ltd. and holds a further 15%. Under Section 2(87)(b), voting power is counted "at its own or together with one or more of its subsidiary companies". Aggregating: 40% + 15% = **55%, which is more than one-half** of the total voting power. Therefore, Neel Ltd. **is a subsidiary** of Vishal Ltd.

**(ii)** The two limbs of Section 2(87) are **alternative, not cumulative** — either control of Board composition **or** more than half the voting power suffices. Although Preferred Ltd. holds only 8% of the capital, it **controls the composition of the Board of Q Ltd.** under limb (a). The percentage of capital held is irrelevant once Board control is established. Therefore, Q Ltd. **is a subsidiary** of Preferred Ltd.

**Conclusion:** In (i), Neel Ltd. is a subsidiary because the combined voting power (55%) of Vishal Ltd. and its subsidiary Om Ltd. exceeds one-half. In (ii), Q Ltd. is a subsidiary because control of Board composition — the first limb of Section 2(87) — is satisfied independently of the 8% shareholding.

*(Full-marks tip: State that the two tests are alternatives and that a subsidiary's holding is aggregated with the parent's. Deductions occur when a student adds up only if the parent alone crosses 50%, or insists on a majority shareholding when Board control alone is enough.)*

### Q3. Ch: Preliminary & Definitions — Small Company: Testing Both Thresholds and Exclusions (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** With reference to the definition of "small company" under Section 2(85) of the Companies Act, 2013 (as amended), determine whether each of the following companies qualifies as a small company for the financial year 2025-26:

| Company | Type | Paid-up capital (₹) | Turnover (₹) | Remark |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tiny Ltd. | Private | 3.5 crore | 38 crore | — |
| Modest Ltd. | Private | 3.5 crore | 45 crore | — |
| Public Well Ltd. | Public | 2 crore | 30 crore | — |
| Charity Foundation | Sec 8 company | 0.10 crore | 5 crore | Registered under Sec 8 |

**Answer:**
Under **Section 2(85)** of the Companies Act, 2013, a "small company" means a company, **other than a public company**, whose **(i) paid-up share capital does not exceed ₹4 crore** (as prescribed) **and (ii) turnover** as per the profit and loss account for the immediately preceding financial year **does not exceed ₹40 crore** (as prescribed). Both conditions must be satisfied simultaneously (the word is "and"). The **proviso** excludes from the definition: (a) a holding company or a subsidiary company; (b) a company registered under **Section 8**; and (c) a company or body corporate governed by any special Act.

**Tiny Ltd.:** Paid-up capital ₹3.5 crore (≤ ₹4 crore) and turnover ₹38 crore (≤ ₹40 crore). It is a private company and both limbs are satisfied. Tiny Ltd. **is a small company**.

**Modest Ltd.:** Paid-up capital ₹3.5 crore is within the limit, **but** turnover of ₹45 crore **exceeds** the ₹40 crore ceiling. Since both conditions must be met, Modest Ltd. **is not a small company**.

**Public Well Ltd.:** Although its capital and turnover are within the limits, it is a **public company**, which is expressly excluded by the opening words of Section 2(85). Hence it **is not a small company**.

**Charity Foundation:** It is a **Section 8 company**, which is excluded by the proviso to Section 2(85) irrespective of its capital and turnover. It **is not a small company**.

**Conclusion:** Only **Tiny Ltd.** qualifies as a small company. The others fail on turnover, public-company status, and the Section 8 exclusion respectively.

*(Full-marks tip: Quote both the ₹4 crore / ₹40 crore limits and the three-fold proviso exclusions. Common deduction: quoting the old ₹2 crore / ₹20 crore figures, or forgetting that a public company can never be a small company.)*

### Q4. Ch: Preliminary & Definitions — Dormant Company: Meaning, "Significant Accounting Transaction" (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** What is a "dormant company" under Section 455 of the Companies Act, 2013? Explain the concept of a "significant accounting transaction" in this context, and state which transactions are excluded from it.

**Answer:**
**Section 455(1)** provides that where a company is **formed and registered under the Act for a future project or to hold an asset or intellectual property and has no significant accounting transaction**, or where it is an **inactive company**, such a company may make an application to the Registrar in the prescribed manner for obtaining the status of a **dormant company**. On such application the Registrar shall issue a certificate allowing the status of a dormant company and maintain a register of dormant companies.

The **Explanation to Section 455(1)** defines the two key terms:
- An **"inactive company"** means a company which has **not been carrying on any business or operation, or has not made any significant accounting transaction during the last two financial years, or has not filed financial statements and annual returns during the last two financial years.**
- A **"significant accounting transaction"** means any transaction **other than** the following four excluded transactions:
  (i) **payment of fees** by a company to the Registrar;
  (ii) **payments made by it to fulfil the requirements of this Act or any other law**;
  (iii) **allotment of shares** to fulfil the requirements of this Act; and
  (iv) **payments for maintenance of its office and records.**

Thus, a company may remain "dormant" while still incurring these four categories of routine payments, because they are not treated as significant accounting transactions. A dormant company must have a **minimum number of directors, file such documents and pay such annual fee** as prescribed to retain its dormant status (Section 455(5)).

**Conclusion:** A dormant company is an inactive or future-project company that obtains dormant status from the Registrar; it can incur only the four excluded (non-significant) transactions without losing that status.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner looks for all four excluded transactions listed verbatim and the "last two financial years" test for inactivity. Deduction for confusing dormant status with striking off under Section 248.)*

### Q5. Ch: Preliminary & Definitions — Company Limited by Guarantee vs. by Shares (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between a "company limited by shares" and a "company limited by guarantee" under the Companies Act, 2013. Can a guarantee company also have a share capital?

**Answer:**
Under **Section 2(22)**, a **"company limited by shares"** means a company having the liability of its members limited by the memorandum to the amount, if any, **unpaid on the shares respectively held by them**. Under **Section 2(21)**, a **"company limited by guarantee"** means a company having the liability of its members limited by the memorandum to such amount as the members may respectively undertake, by the memorandum, **to contribute to the assets of the company in the event of its being wound up**.

The distinction may be tabulated as follows:

| Basis | Company limited by shares [Sec 2(22)] | Company limited by guarantee [Sec 2(21)] |
|---|---|---|
| Nature of liability | Amount unpaid on shares held | Amount guaranteed in the memorandum |
| When liability arises | At any time during the life of the company (on call) | Only in the event of winding up |
| Presence of share capital | Always has share capital | May or may not have share capital |
| Typical use | Trading/commercial companies | Clubs, trade associations, non-profit bodies |

As to whether a guarantee company may also have share capital — **yes**. A company can be limited by guarantee **and** have a share capital. In such a company the liability of members is two-fold: (i) the amount unpaid on shares held, and (ii) the amount guaranteed, payable only on winding up. Section 2(21) does not prohibit a share capital.

**Conclusion:** In a share company, liability is the unpaid amount on shares and is enforceable during the company's life; in a guarantee company, liability is the guaranteed amount enforceable only on winding up — and a guarantee company may additionally have share capital.

*(Full-marks tip: State clearly that guarantee-company liability crystallises only on winding up, and confirm that a guarantee company can also have share capital. Deduction for saying a guarantee company can never have shares.)*

### Q6. Ch: Preliminary & Definitions — Officer in Default (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain who is an "officer who is in default" under Section 2(60) of the Companies Act, 2013. Name any four categories of persons covered.

**Answer:**
**Section 2(60)** defines "officer who is in default", for the purpose of any provision in the Act which enacts that an officer of the company who is in default shall be liable to any penalty or punishment, as meaning **any of the following officers of the company**:

(i) **whole-time director**;
(ii) **key managerial personnel** (as defined in Section 2(51));
(iii) where there is no key managerial personnel, such **director or directors as specified by the Board** in this behalf and who has or have given his or their consent in writing to the Board to such specification, or **all the directors** if no director is so specified;
(iv) any person who, under the immediate authority of the Board or any key managerial personnel, is **charged with any responsibility** including maintenance, filing or distribution of accounts or records, and who **authorises, actively participates in, knowingly permits, or knowingly fails to take active steps** to prevent any default;
(v) any person in accordance with whose advice, directions or instructions the Board is accustomed to act (other than professional advice);
(vi) every director who is aware of a contravention (by virtue of Board proceedings or participation without objecting) or where the contravention took place with his consent or connivance;
(vii) in respect of the issue or transfer of securities, the **share transfer agents, registrars and merchant bankers** to the issue or transfer.

**Conclusion:** The concept fixes penal liability on those who are genuinely responsible for and connected with the default — principally whole-time directors, key managerial personnel, specified directors, and persons charged with the relevant responsibility.

*(Full-marks tip: List at least four categories with the correct statutory language ("knowingly permits", "authorises, actively participates"). Merely writing "the directors" without the specified-director/KMP structure loses marks.)*

### Q7. Ch: Incorporation of Company — Formation of a Section 8 Company; Licence and Conditions (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** A group of professionals wishes to form a company to promote education and research, intending to apply its profits only towards its objects and to prohibit payment of any dividend to its members. Advise them on the formation of a company under Section 8 of the Companies Act, 2013 — the conditions to be satisfied, the licence, and the privileges available.

**Answer:**
**Section 8(1)** empowers the Central Government (power delegated to the Registrar) to register as a **limited company**, a person or association of persons proposed to be registered under the Act for the promotion of **commerce, art, science, sports, education, research, social welfare, religion, charity, protection of environment or any such other object**, where the company:
(a) **intends to apply its profits, if any, or other income in promoting its objects**; and
(b) **intends to prohibit the payment of any dividend to its members.**

On being satisfied, the Central Government may, by **licence**, and subject to such conditions as it deems fit, permit the registration of such a company **without the addition of the words "Limited" or "Private Limited" to its name** (Section 8(1) proviso). The company enjoys **all the privileges and is subject to all the obligations of a limited company**.

**Privileges/relaxations** available to a Section 8 company include:
- Its **firm** may be a member (Section 8(3)).
- It need not use the suffix "Limited"/"Private Limited".
- It is exempt from certain procedural requirements (e.g. shorter notice for general meetings and relaxed rules on holding of meetings under the exemption notifications), and is **not required to have a minimum paid-up capital**.
- A Section 8 company is entitled to the tax and other benefits available to charitable institutions (subject to compliance with the applicable tax law).

**Key prohibitions/consequences:**
- A Section 8 company **cannot alter its memorandum or articles except with the previous approval of the Central Government** (Section 8(4)(i)).
- It **cannot** convert into any other kind of company except in compliance with the prescribed conditions (Section 8(4)(ii) and the Rules).
- If the company contravenes Section 8, the Central Government may **revoke the licence** (Section 8(6)); on revocation the words "Limited"/"Private Limited" must be added, and in serious cases the company may be wound up or amalgamated with another Section 8 company (Section 8(7)–(9)).

**Conclusion:** The professionals may form a Section 8 company on obtaining a licence, provided profits are applied only to the charitable objects and dividend is prohibited; the company then enjoys the privileges of a licensed non-profit company subject to the Section 8(4) restrictions.

*(Full-marks tip: Cover both conditions in Section 8(1)(a)/(b), the licence, the "no Limited suffix" relaxation, and the Section 8(4) restriction on altering MOA/AOA and converting. Deduction for omitting the revocation consequence under Section 8(6).)*

### Q8. Ch: Incorporation of Company — Conversion of a Section 8 Company into an Ordinary Company (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** "A company registered under Section 8 can convert itself into an ordinary company, but only under strict conditions." Explain the procedure and safeguards for such conversion under the Companies Act, 2013 and the relevant Rules.

**Answer:**
A Section 8 company enjoys its privileges precisely because it undertakes to apply profits only to its objects and not to pay dividends. Therefore, **Section 8(4)(ii)** provides that a Section 8 company **may convert itself into a company of any other kind only after complying with such conditions as may be prescribed**. The procedure is governed by **Rules 21 and 22 of the Companies (Incorporation) Rules, 2014**.

The principal safeguards and steps are:
1. **Special resolution:** The company must pass a **special resolution** at a general meeting for approving such conversion, and a copy is filed with the Registrar.
2. **Explanatory statement:** The notice of the general meeting must set out the reasons for conversion and the effect on members (including that the privileges of a Section 8 company will be lost).
3. **Application to the Regional Director:** The company must file an **application in the prescribed form to the Regional Director** for approval of conversion, along with the required documents (e.g. board and members' resolutions, a statement of assets and liabilities, and a certificate from a professional).
4. **Publication and notice:** The company must, before making the application, **publish a notice** in a prescribed newspaper (in English and vernacular) and send a copy of the notice to the relevant authorities (e.g. Chief Commissioner of Income Tax, Charity Commissioner, and any authority from which it obtained special benefits) so that objections may be raised.
5. **No distribution of accumulated assets:** Any **accumulated assets and income of the Section 8 company that were funded by donations, grants or special benefits cannot be distributed** to members on conversion; safeguards ensure such assets continue to be applied for the original objects/transferred appropriately.
6. **Approval and fresh certificate:** On being satisfied, the Regional Director approves the conversion; the company then complies with the alteration of its memorandum/articles and obtains a fresh certificate of incorporation reflecting its new status, and thereafter adds the suffix "Limited"/"Private Limited".

**Conclusion:** Conversion is permitted under Section 8(4)(ii) read with Rules 21–22, but only after a special resolution, a Regional Director's approval, public notice to affected authorities, and protection of the charitable assets — thereby ensuring the privileges once granted are not misused.

*(Full-marks tip: Anchor on Section 8(4)(ii) and Rules 21–22, and emphasise the Regional Director's approval, newspaper notice to authorities, and protection of accumulated charitable assets. Deduction for treating it as a simple Section 14 conversion.)*

### Q9. Ch: Incorporation of Company — Commencement of Business under Section 10A (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Zenith Traders Ltd. was incorporated as a public company on 1st April, 2025 with an authorised and subscribed capital that required the subscribers to pay ₹25,00,000 towards the shares agreed to be taken by them. The company started borrowing money and entered into a large supply contract on 20th June, 2025. As on that date, the subscribers had not paid the amount due on their shares and no declaration had been filed with the Registrar. Examine, with reference to Section 10A of the Companies Act, 2013:
(i) whether the company was entitled to commence business or exercise borrowing powers on 20th June, 2025;
(ii) the consequences of the default; and
(iii) the position if no declaration is filed within 180 days.

**Answer:**
**Section 10A(1)** provides that a company **incorporated after the commencement of the Companies (Amendment) Ordinance, 2018 and having a share capital shall not commence any business or exercise any borrowing powers unless**:
(a) a **declaration is filed by a director within 180 days of the date of incorporation** with the Registrar, in the prescribed form and verified in the prescribed manner, stating that **every subscriber to the memorandum has paid the value of the shares agreed to be taken by him** on the date of making of such declaration; and
(b) the company has **filed with the Registrar a verification of its registered office** as provided in Section 12(2).

**(i)** As on 20th June, 2025, the subscribers had **not paid** the ₹25,00,000 due on their shares and **no declaration had been filed**. Both mandatory pre-conditions of Section 10A(1) were unfulfilled. Therefore, Zenith Traders Ltd. was **not entitled** to commence business or exercise its borrowing powers on that date. The borrowing and the supply contract were entered into in contravention of Section 10A.

**(ii) Consequences of default:** Under **Section 10A(2)**, if a company makes default in complying with these requirements, the **company is liable to a penalty of ₹50,000** and **every officer who is in default is liable to a penalty of ₹1,000 for each day during which the default continues, subject to a maximum of ₹1,00,000.**

**(iii) Non-filing within 180 days:** Under **Section 10A(3)**, where **no declaration has been filed with the Registrar within 180 days** of incorporation and the Registrar has reasonable cause to believe that the company is not carrying on any business or operations, he **may initiate action for the removal of the name of the company** from the register of companies under the provisions relating to striking off (Section 248).

**Conclusion:** Zenith Traders Ltd. could not lawfully commence business or borrow on 20th June, 2025 as neither condition of Section 10A was met; the company and its officers face the penalties in Section 10A(2), and continued non-filing beyond 180 days exposes the company to removal of its name under Section 10A(3).

*(Full-marks tip: Quote both pre-conditions (paid-up declaration + registered-office verification), the exact penalty figures (₹50,000 / ₹1,000 per day up to ₹1,00,000), and the 180-day striking-off consequence. Deduction for confusing Section 10A with the old Section 11 certificate of commencement.)*

### Q10. Ch: Incorporation of Company — Registered Office and Change from One State to Another (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Horizon Ltd., registered in State X, wishes to shift its registered office to State Y for reasons of administrative convenience and proximity to its main market. Advise the company on the procedure to be followed under Section 13 of the Companies Act, 2013 for shifting the registered office from one State to another, and the role of the Central Government.

**Answer:**
The situation of the registered office is a clause of the **memorandum of association**. A change of registered office **from one State to another** alters the memorandum and therefore attracts **Section 13** of the Companies Act, 2013, in particular **Section 13(4) and 13(7)**.

The procedure is:
1. **Special resolution:** The company must pass a **special resolution** approving the alteration of the memorandum to change the State in which the registered office is situated (Section 13(1)).
2. **Central Government approval:** Under **Section 13(4)**, the alteration of the memorandum relating to the place of the registered office from one State to another **shall not have any effect unless it is approved by the Central Government on an application** in the prescribed form (the power is exercised by the Regional Director).
3. **Notice to affected persons and authorities:** The company must give advertisement/notice of the application and serve notice on **creditors, debenture-holders and every other person whose interest is likely to be affected**, and on the **Registrar and the Registrar of the State** to which the office is proposed to be shifted, so that objections (particularly of creditors) may be considered. The Regional Director must be satisfied that the change does not prejudice creditors or is with their consent.
4. **Filing of the confirmation order:** Under **Section 13(7)**, where the alteration involves a change of registered office from one State to another, a **certified copy of the order of the Central Government approving the alteration shall be filed by the company with the Registrar of each of the States**, who shall register the same; and the Registrar of the State where the office is newly situated **shall issue a fresh certificate of incorporation** indicating the alteration.
5. **Effect:** The change takes effect only on such registration.

**Conclusion:** Horizon Ltd. can shift its registered office to State Y by passing a special resolution and obtaining the Central Government's (Regional Director's) confirmation after protecting creditor interests, followed by filing the confirmation order with the Registrars of both States and obtaining a fresh certificate of incorporation.

*(Full-marks tip: Emphasise that inter-State shifting needs Central Government/Regional Director confirmation (unlike within-State shifting) and the protection of creditors. Deduction for omitting Section 13(7) filing with both Registrars and the fresh certificate.)*

### Q11. Ch: Incorporation of Company — Effect of Registration and Corporate Personality (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the effect of registration of a company under Section 9 of the Companies Act, 2013. In this light, briefly explain the significance of the certificate of incorporation under Section 7(2).

**Answer:**
**Section 9** provides that **from the date of incorporation mentioned in the certificate of incorporation, the subscribers to the memorandum and all other persons who may from time to time become members of the company, shall be a body corporate by the name contained in the memorandum, capable of exercising all the functions of an incorporated company** under the Act, and **having perpetual succession, with power to acquire, hold and dispose of property**, both movable and immovable, tangible and intangible, **to contract and to sue and be sued**, by the said name.

The effects of registration flowing from Section 9 are, therefore:
1. **Separate legal personality:** The company becomes a distinct legal person, separate from its members (the principle in *Salomon v. Salomon & Co. Ltd.*). Its property is its own; members are not co-owners of its assets.
2. **Perpetual succession:** The company continues to exist irrespective of changes in its membership — death, insolvency or exit of members does not affect its continuity.
3. **Capacity to contract, sue and be sued, and own property** in its own name.
4. **Limited liability** (where limited by shares or guarantee) of members.

**Certificate of incorporation — Section 7(2):** On and from the date of incorporation, the Registrar issues a **certificate of incorporation** in the prescribed form to the effect that the company is incorporated, and **allots a Corporate Identity Number (CIN)** which is a distinct identity for the company. The certificate is **conclusive evidence** that the company has been duly registered and that all the requirements of the Act in respect of registration have been complied with.

**Conclusion:** Registration under Section 9 confers on the company separate corporate personality with perpetual succession and full capacity to own property and contract, and the certificate of incorporation under Section 7(2) is conclusive proof of the company's valid formation.

*(Full-marks tip: Cite Section 9 for the effects and Section 7(2) for the CIN and conclusiveness of the certificate; the mention of *Salomon* earns credit. Deduction for omitting perpetual succession or the CIN.)*

### Q12. Ch: Incorporation of Company — Doctrine of Ultra Vires and the Object Clause (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the doctrine of "ultra vires" with reference to the object clause of the memorandum of association. What is the effect of an ultra vires act?

**Answer:**
The **memorandum of association** under **Section 4** must state, among other clauses, the **objects for which the company is proposed to be incorporated** (the object clause). A company can exercise **only those powers which are expressly conferred by the object clause and those which are reasonably incidental to the attainment of the stated objects.** Any act done or contract entered into by the company which is **beyond the scope of the object clause** is said to be **ultra vires** (beyond the powers of) the company. The doctrine was established in the leading case of *Ashbury Railway Carriage & Iron Co. Ltd. v. Riche*.

The effects of an ultra vires act are:
1. **Void ab initio:** An ultra vires act or contract is **void from the beginning** and is a **nullity**. It cannot be ratified even by the unanimous consent of all the members, because ratification cannot cure something the company itself had no power to do.
2. **No enforcement:** Neither the company nor the other party can enforce an ultra vires contract.
3. **Personal liability of directors:** Directors who apply the company's funds for ultra vires purposes may be held **personally liable** to make good the funds so applied.
4. **Ultra vires property/torts:** Property acquired under an ultra vires transaction is nonetheless protected, and the company remains liable for torts committed by its servants within the course of an intra vires activity.

**Conclusion:** An act outside the object clause of the memorandum is ultra vires the company, void ab initio and incapable of ratification, though the doctrine operates without prejudice to the personal liability of the directors responsible.

*(Full-marks tip: Cite the object clause under Section 4, the *Ashbury* case, and the "void, cannot be ratified" rule. Deduction for confusing ultra vires the company with ultra vires the directors, which can be ratified by members.)*

### Q13. Ch: Prospectus & Allotment — Shelf Prospectus and Information Memorandum (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the concept of a "shelf prospectus" under Section 31 of the Companies Act, 2013. How does the information memorandum operate in relation to a shelf prospectus, and for what maximum period does a shelf prospectus remain valid?

**Answer:**
**Section 31(1)** provides that any class or classes of companies, as the **Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI)** may provide by regulations, may file a **shelf prospectus** with the Registrar **at the stage of the first offer of securities** included therein, which shall indicate a **period not exceeding one year** as the period of validity of such prospectus from the date of opening of the first offer of securities under that prospectus. In respect of a second or subsequent offer of such securities issued during the period of validity of that prospectus, **no further prospectus is required.**

A **"shelf prospectus"** is thus a prospectus in respect of which the securities or class of securities included therein are issued for subscription in **one or more issues over a certain period without the issue of a further prospectus** (Explanation to Section 31).

**Information memorandum:** Under **Section 31(2)**, a company filing a shelf prospectus is required to file an **information memorandum** containing all **material facts relating to new charges created, changes in the financial position of the company** as have occurred between the first offer of securities/previous offer and the succeeding offer, and such other changes as may be prescribed, with the Registrar **within the prescribed time, prior to the issue of a second or subsequent offer** of securities under the shelf prospectus.

Under **Section 31(3)**, where a company or any other person has received applications for the allotment of securities along with advance payments before making a subsequent offer, and the applicant desires to withdraw after such subsequent offer on account of any information contained in the information memorandum, the company must refund the advance so received.

Under **Section 31(4)**, where an information memorandum is filed every time an offer of securities is made, such memorandum **together with the shelf prospectus shall be deemed to be a prospectus.**

**Conclusion:** A shelf prospectus, valid for a maximum of **one year** from the opening of the first offer, allows repeated issues without a fresh prospectus, the updating being done through an information memorandum which, together with the shelf prospectus, is deemed to be a prospectus for each subsequent offer.

### Q14. Ch: Prospectus & Allotment — Red-Herring vs. Abridged Prospectus (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between a "red-herring prospectus" (Section 32) and an "abridged prospectus" (Section 2(1) read with Section 33) of the Companies Act, 2013. State one situation in which each is used.

**Answer:**
**Red-herring prospectus — Section 32:** A "red-herring prospectus" means a prospectus which **does not include complete particulars of the quantum or price of the securities included therein** (Explanation to Section 32). A company proposing to make an offer of securities may issue a red-herring prospectus **prior to the issue of a prospectus**, and must file it with the Registrar **at least three days before the opening of the subscription list and the offer.** It carries the same obligations as a prospectus, and any variation between the red-herring prospectus and the final prospectus must be highlighted as variations in the prospectus. Upon closing of the offer, the company files a final prospectus with the Registrar and SEBI stating the total capital raised and the closing price of the securities.

**Abridged prospectus — Section 2(1) and Section 33:** An "abridged prospectus" means a **memorandum containing such salient features of a prospectus as may be specified by SEBI by regulations** (Section 2(1)). Under **Section 33(1)**, **no application form for the purchase of securities shall be issued unless it is accompanied by an abridged prospectus**, save in the excepted cases (e.g. underwriting or where the securities are not offered to the public). Its purpose is to give the investor a concise summary while keeping costs low.

| Basis | Red-herring prospectus [Sec 32] | Abridged prospectus [Sec 2(1)/33] |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Prospectus without complete particulars of quantum/price of securities | A memorandum of salient features of a prospectus |
| Purpose | Used in book-building where price is discovered later | Accompanies the application form to inform the investor briefly |
| Filing | Filed with Registrar at least 3 days before opening of offer | Not separately filed; issued with each application form |
| Completeness | Full prospectus minus price/quantum | Condensed summary of the full prospectus |

**Conclusion:** A red-herring prospectus is a near-complete prospectus lacking only the final price/quantum, used in book-built issues, whereas an abridged prospectus is a short summary that must accompany every application form under Section 33.

*(Full-marks tip: Give the "3 days before opening" filing rule for the red-herring and the "must accompany the application form" rule for the abridged prospectus. Deduction for describing the abridged prospectus as a price-less prospectus — that is the red-herring.)*

### Q15. Ch: Prospectus & Allotment — Minimum Subscription and Refund on Failure (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Bright Star Ltd. issued a prospectus on 1st May, 2025 offering 10,00,000 equity shares of ₹10 each (nominal value) to the public. The prospectus stated the minimum subscription. By 31st May, 2025 (i.e. within 30 days of the issue of the prospectus), the company had received valid applications for only 8,00,000 shares, and the application money received on each share was ₹0.40. Examine the following with reference to Section 39 of the Companies Act, 2013 and the relevant Rule:

| Particulars | Amount |
|---|---|
| Shares offered | 10,00,000 shares of ₹10 each |
| Applications received (within 30 days) | 8,00,000 shares |
| Application money per share | ₹0.40 |

(i) Was the minimum subscription received? (ii) Was the application money legally sufficient? (iii) What must the company do and what is the consequence of default?

**Answer:**
**Section 39(1)** provides that **no allotment of any securities of a company offered to the public for subscription shall be made unless the amount stated in the prospectus as the minimum amount has been subscribed and the sums payable on application for such amount have been paid to and received by the company.** As per SEBI norms, the "minimum subscription" for a public issue is generally **90% of the offer**. **Section 39(2)** provides that the amount payable on application on every security **shall not be less than five per cent of the nominal amount of the security** or such other percentage/amount as SEBI may specify.

**WN-1 — Minimum subscription test:**
Minimum subscription required = 90% of 10,00,000 shares = **9,00,000 shares.**
Applications received = 8,00,000 shares.
Since 8,00,000 < 9,00,000, the **minimum subscription has NOT been received.**

**WN-2 — Adequacy of application money (Section 39(2)):**
Minimum application money = 5% of nominal value = 5% × ₹10 = **₹0.50 per share.**
Application money actually received = ₹0.40 per share.
Since ₹0.40 < ₹0.50, the application money is **below the statutory minimum** and is therefore **legally insufficient.**

**(i)** The minimum subscription was not received — only 8,00,000 shares against the required 9,00,000 (see WN-1).
**(ii)** The application money of ₹0.40 per share is short of the required ₹0.50 (5% of ₹10) — hence insufficient (see WN-2).
**(iii)** Consequences under **Section 39(3)** read with **Rule 11 of the Companies (Prospectus and Allotment of Securities) Rules, 2014:**
- The company **cannot make any allotment**.
- Where the minimum amount has **not been subscribed and the sum payable on application is not received within 30 days** from the date of issue of the prospectus (or such period as specified by SEBI), the amount received **shall be returned within such time and manner as prescribed** — i.e. **within 15 days from the closure of the issue.**
- If the money is **not repaid within 15 days**, the **directors of the company who are officers in default shall jointly and severally be liable to repay that money with interest at the rate of 15% per annum** for the delayed period.
- In addition, **Section 39(5)** provides that in case of default under sub-section (3) or (4), the company and every officer in default shall be **liable to a penalty of ₹1,000 for each day of default or ₹1,00,000, whichever is less.**

**Conclusion:** Bright Star Ltd. **cannot allot** the shares — both the minimum subscription (9,00,000 shares) and the minimum application money (₹0.50 per share) requirements have failed. It must refund the application money within 15 days, failing which its directors are personally liable to repay it with 15% p.a. interest, besides the penalty under Section 39(5).

### Q16. Ch: Prospectus & Allotment — Allotment Irregularities: Application Money in a Separate Bank Account & Listing (Marks: 5) [Application]

**Section 40** governs securities offered to the public that are proposed to be listed. Its relevant requirements are:

1. **Prior listing application — Section 40(1):** Every company making a public offer shall, **before making such offer, make an application to one or more recognised stock exchanges and obtain permission for the securities to be dealt with in such stock exchange(s).** The prospectus must state the name(s) of the stock exchange(s) where the application has been made (Section 40(2)).

### Q17. Ch: Prospectus & Allotment — Criminal and Civil Liability for Mis-statement (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** The prospectus of Novel Ventures Ltd. contained a statement, attributed to an "expert" valuer, materially overstating the value of the company's land. Mr. Ravi, relying on this statement, subscribed for shares and suffered loss when the truth emerged. Two directors, A and B, were on the Board when the prospectus was issued. Director A had, before issue, **withdrawn his consent** to the prospectus and given public notice; Director B was **unaware** of the overstatement and had reasonable grounds to believe, and did believe, the expert statement to be true. Examine the liability of the company, the directors and the expert under Sections 34, 35 and 26 of the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:**
Where a prospectus contains a statement which is **untrue or misleading in form or context** or where any inclusion or omission is calculated to mislead, both **criminal** and **civil** liability may arise.

**Criminal liability — Section 34:** Where a prospectus issued, circulated or distributed under Chapter III **includes any statement which is untrue or misleading in form or context in which it is included, or where any inclusion or omission of any matter is likely to mislead**, every person who **authorises the issue** of such prospectus shall be liable under **Section 447** (fraud). The person is **not liable** if he proves that (a) the statement or omission was **immaterial**, or (b) he had **reasonable grounds to believe, and did up to the time of issue of the prospectus believe, that the statement was true** or the omission necessary.

**Civil liability — Section 35:** Where a person has **subscribed for securities** acting on any misleading statement or inclusion/omission in the prospectus and **sustained loss or damage as a consequence**, the **company and every person who: (a) is a director at the time of issue; (b) has authorised himself to be named as a director; (c) is a promoter; (d) has authorised the issue; and (e) is an expert referred to in Section 26(5)** shall be liable to **pay compensation** for the loss or damage. Under **Section 35(2)**, a person (other than the company) shall **not be liable** if he proves that:
- having consented to become a director, he **withdrew his consent before the issue of the prospectus, and it was issued without his authority or consent**; or
- the prospectus was issued **without his knowledge or consent**, and on becoming aware he gave reasonable public notice; or
- he had **reasonable grounds to believe, and did believe, that the statement was true** / the expert was competent and had given consent (in relation to expert statements).

**Application to facts:**
- **The company (Novel Ventures Ltd.):** Liable to pay compensation to Mr. Ravi under Section 35(1) — the company has **no "reasonable belief" defence**; it is liable for loss caused by the misleading prospectus.
- **Director A:** He **withdrew his consent before the issue** and gave public notice; he falls within the defence in Section 35(2). **Director A is not liable.**
- **Director B:** He was **unaware** of the overstatement and had **reasonable grounds to believe, and did believe, the expert statement to be true.** He is entitled to the defence of reasonable belief and is **not liable** under Section 35, nor under Section 34 (reasonable-belief defence).
- **The expert:** Under **Section 26(5)**, an expert's statement may be included only if he is competent, has given and not withdrawn his written consent, and a statement to that effect appears in the prospectus. If those conditions are met, the **expert is liable** under Section 35(1)(e) for the misleading valuation to the extent of his statement; if his consent was wrongly used, the position differs.

**Conclusion:** Mr. Ravi may recover compensation from the **company** (and from the **expert** in respect of the expert statement). **Director A escapes liability** because he withdrew consent before issue and gave notice, and **Director B escapes liability** because he had a reasonable and honest belief in the truth of the expert statement. Criminal liability under Section 34 attaches only to those who authorised the issue without the reasonable-belief defence.

*(Full-marks tip: Separate criminal (Sec 34 → Sec 447) from civil (Sec 35 compensation) liability, apply each director's defence individually, and bring in the expert's Section 26(5) consent. Deduction for giving the company a "reasonable belief" escape — only persons other than the company have defences.)*

### Q21. Ch: Share Capital & Debentures — Calls in Advance and Uniform Basis (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the provisions of the Companies Act, 2013 relating to (a) calls being made on a **uniform basis**, and (b) acceptance of **calls in advance**, including the position as to interest and voting.

**Answer:** **(a) Calls on a uniform basis — Section 49.** Where any calls for further share capital are made on shares, such calls shall be made **on a uniform basis on all shares falling under the same class**. Shares of the same nominal value on which different amounts have been paid up are **not** deemed to fall under the same class. The object is to ensure equality of treatment among shareholders of the same class.

**(b) Payment of calls in advance — Section 50.** A company may, **if so authorised by its Articles**, accept from any member the whole or a part of the amount remaining unpaid on any shares held by him, **even if no part of that amount has been called up**. However, under **Section 50(2)**, a member paying such advance money shall **not be entitled to any voting rights** in respect of the money so paid until that amount has been called up.

As regards **interest**, where the Articles adopt **Table F of Schedule I**, the company may pay interest on calls paid in advance at a rate **not exceeding 12% per annum** (or such rate as the members may determine), and such interest is payable even out of capital where profits are inadequate, since the advance is akin to a loan. Conversely, on **calls-in-arrear**, Table F permits the company to charge interest at a rate **not exceeding 10% per annum**.

**Conclusion:** Calls must be uniform within a class (Section 49); advance calls may be accepted if the Articles permit (Section 50) but carry no voting rights until called up, while attracting interest per the Articles/Table F.

### Q23. Ch: Share Capital & Debentures — Surrender vs Forfeiture of Shares (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between **surrender** of shares and **forfeiture** of shares. Is surrender of shares valid under the Companies Act, 2013?

**Answer:** **Forfeiture** of shares is the compulsory termination of membership as a penalty for non-payment of a call, carried out by the company in accordance with the authority in its Articles (as in Regulations 28–34 of **Table F, Schedule I**). **Surrender** of shares is the **voluntary handing back** of shares by a shareholder to the company. The Companies Act, 2013 contains **no express provision** authorising surrender; it is permissible only where the Articles so provide and **only in circumstances in which the shares could otherwise have been lawfully forfeited**, because a surrender that operates as a purchase by the company of its own shares or as an unauthorised reduction of capital would be void as offending **Section 66 (reduction of capital)** and the buy-back provisions.

| Basis | Forfeiture | Surrender |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Compulsory, penal action by the company | Voluntary act of the shareholder |
| Initiated by | Company (Board), for default in payment | Shareholder |
| Statutory backing | Recognised via Articles/Table F | No express provision; valid only in lieu of forfeiture |
| Effect on money paid | Amount paid is forfeited to the company | Company holds the surrendered shares |
| Validity condition | Strict compliance with Articles | Permitted only where forfeiture could have been enforced |

**Conclusion:** Surrender is valid **only** where the Articles authorise it and the shares are liable to forfeiture; a surrender used to escape liability or to reduce capital without following Section 66 is **void**.

### Q25. Ch: Share Capital & Debentures — Debenture Trustee: Appointment and Disqualification (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** State when a company must appoint a **debenture trustee**, who is **disqualified** from being appointed as a debenture trustee, and the principal **duties** of a debenture trustee under the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:** **When required — Section 71(5) and Rule 18(2).** A company shall appoint a debenture trustee **before issuing a prospectus or making an offer or invitation to the public or to its members exceeding 500** for the subscription of its debentures, and must obtain the written consent of the trustee. (i) beneficially holds shares in the company;
(ii) is a promoter, director or key managerial personnel or any other officer or an employee of the company or its holding, subsidiary or associate company;
(iii) is beneficially entitled to moneys which are to be paid by the company otherwise than as remuneration payable to the debenture trustee;
(iv) is indebted to the company, or its subsidiary or its holding or associate company or a subsidiary of such holding company;
(v) has furnished any guarantee in respect of the principal debts secured by the debentures or interest thereon;
(vi) has any pecuniary relationship with the company amounting to 2% or more of its gross turnover or total income, or ₹50 lakh or such higher amount as may be prescribed, whichever is lower, during the two immediately preceding financial years or during the current financial year;

Is the Secretary correct? Advise the company on the correct legal remedy, the authority involved and the procedure.

**Answer:**
The relevant provisions are Section 77, Section 87 and Section 400 of the Companies Act, 2013 read with the Companies (Registration of Charges) Rules, 2014.

Under **Section 77(1)**, every company creating a charge shall register the particulars with the Registrar within **thirty days** of creation. The **first proviso** allows the Registrar to permit registration within a further period of **thirty days** (i.e., days 31–60) on payment of ad valorem additional fees, and the **second proviso** allows a further period of **sixty days** (i.e., days 61–120) on payment of ad valorem fees, but this applies only to charges created **on or after** the commencement of the Companies (Amendment) Ordinance, 2019. Thus, the maximum period available at the **Registrar's** level is **120 days** from the date of creation.

In the present case, 135 days have already elapsed, so the Registrar can no longer register the charge under Section 77. However, the Secretary is **incorrect** in concluding that the charge is irretrievably lost. The remedy now lies with the **Central Government** (powers delegated to the Regional Director) under **Section 87**.

**Section 87** empowers the Central Government, on being satisfied that the omission to file the particulars of any charge within the time required, or the omission to register any charge, was **accidental or due to inadvertence or some other sufficient cause, or is not of a nature to prejudice the position of creditors or shareholders**, or that on other grounds it is **just and equitable to grant relief**, to order that the time for filing be **extended (condonation of delay)**. It is important to note that after the 2019 amendment, Section 87 covers **rectification in respect of omission or mis-statement of particulars, and delay in giving intimation of satisfaction** — but the condonation of delay in the **creation** of a charge beyond 120 days is dealt with by the Central Government's general rectification power together with Rule 12 of the Companies (Registration of Charges) Rules, 2014.

**Procedure:** The company shall file an application in **Form CHG-8** to the **Regional Director** (Central Government's delegate). The Regional Director, after hearing, may pass an order under Section 87 condoning the delay on such terms and conditions as it deems fit. A certified copy of the order is then filed with the Registrar in **Form INC-28**, whereupon the Registrar registers the charge in **Form CHG-1**.

| Time elapsed since creation | Authority | Form |
|---|---|---|
| Up to 30 days (normal) | Registrar | CHG-1 |
| 31 to 120 days (with additional/ad valorem fee) | Registrar (provisos to Sec 77) | CHG-1 |
| Beyond 120 days | Central Government / Regional Director (Sec 87) | CHG-8, then INC-28 |

**Conclusion:** The charge is not lost. Zenith Alloys Ltd. must apply to the Regional Director in Form CHG-8 for condonation of delay under Section 87; on the order being passed and filed in INC-28, the Registrar will register the charge.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards correctly identifying that 120 days is the Registrar's ceiling and that beyond it the authority shifts to the Central Government/RD under Section 87 — not the Tribunal. Mark deductions occur when students cite the Tribunal, or wrongly say the charge is void beyond 120 days.)*

### Q36. Ch: Registration of Charges — Priority of Charges & the "Notice" Question (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Cygnus Textiles Ltd. created two charges over the same immovable property (a factory building):
- First, a charge in favour of **Pragati Bank** for ₹1.20 crore, **created on 5th February 2026** but **registered with the Registrar on 2nd April 2026** (within the permitted period).
- Second, a charge in favour of **Sunrise Finance Ltd.** for ₹90 lakh, **created on 1st March 2026** and **registered on 12th March 2026**.

The company defaulted, and the factory building was sold for ₹1.60 crore. Both lenders claim priority. Sunrise Finance argues that because its charge was registered **first**, it must be paid **first**. Determine the priority between the two charge-holders and apportion the sale proceeds. Would your answer change if it is proved that Sunrise Finance had actual knowledge of Pragati Bank's charge at the time its charge was created?

**Answer:**
The governing law is **Section 77** and the settled principle of priority of charges under the Companies Act, 2013, read with the doctrine of **notice**.

**Registration is not the sole determinant of priority.** The primary rule is that among charges of the same nature over the same property, priority is generally governed by the **date of creation** of the charge — the maxim *qui prior est tempore potior est jure* ("he who is earlier in time is stronger in law"). Registration under Section 77 operates as **constructive notice** to the world (Section 80 provides that any person acquiring a charge shall be **deemed to have notice** of an already registered charge). Registration determines the **effect against a liquidator/creditor** and gives constructive notice, but as between two consensual charge-holders the **time of creation coupled with the state of notice** decides priority.

**Applying to facts (first situation):**
- Pragati Bank's charge was **created on 5th February 2026** — earlier in time.
- Sunrise Finance's charge was **created on 1st March 2026** — later in time.
- Although Sunrise registered first (12 March vs 2 April), Pragati's charge was registered **within the permitted statutory period** and therefore relates back / retains its priority as the **first-created charge**.

Because Pragati's charge was validly registered within time, its **earlier date of creation** prevails. Sunrise cannot claim priority merely because its registration date is earlier.

**Apportionment of ₹1.60 crore:**

| Charge-holder | Basis | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| Pragati Bank (first-created) | Paid in priority | 1,20,00,000 |
| Sunrise Finance (second) | Balance out of remaining ₹40 lakh | 40,00,000 |
| Sunrise shortfall (unsecured) | ₹90 L − ₹40 L | 50,00,000 (ranks as unsecured) |

**Second situation (actual notice):** If Sunrise Finance had **actual knowledge** of Pragati's charge at the time its own charge was created, the position is **reinforced** in favour of Pragati Bank. A subsequent charge-holder with actual (or constructive, via Section 80) notice of a prior charge cannot claim priority over it. Hence the answer does **not change** — Pragati Bank retains first priority in either case.

**Conclusion:** Pragati Bank (charge created 5 Feb 2026, registered in time) has priority and is paid ₹1.20 crore in full; Sunrise Finance receives the balance ₹40 lakh and ranks as an unsecured creditor for the shortfall of ₹50 lakh. Actual notice to Sunrise does not alter this outcome.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the clear statement that priority follows date of **creation**, not date of registration, and the use of Section 80 (constructive notice) and the *qui prior est tempore* maxim. A common deduction is mechanically awarding priority to the earlier-registered charge.)*

### Q37. Ch: Registration of Charges — Satisfaction of Charge & Extended Timeline u/s 82 (Marks: 4) [Application]

Advise, citing the exact provision.

### Q40. Ch: Management & Administration — Postal Ballot u/s 110 & Rule 22 (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Orbit Infrastructure Ltd., an unlisted public company with 4,200 members, proposes to alter the objects clause of its memorandum. The Company Secretary contends that this "business" **must** be transacted only by **postal ballot** and that it cannot be transacted at a general meeting at all. Separately, the company had, on the same notice, also proposed the ordinary business of **adoption of financial statements**, and directed that it too be decided by postal ballot to save cost. Examine both propositions with reference to the correct provisions. Also state whether members can demand e-voting instead of postal ballot.

**Answer:**
The relevant provisions are **Section 110** of the Companies Act, 2013 read with **Rule 22 of the Companies (Management and Administration) Rules, 2014** and **Section 108/Rule 20** (e-voting).

**Concept of postal ballot:** Postal ballot means voting by post or through electronic means. Under **Section 110(1)(a)**, the Central Government has, by rules (Rule 22), prescribed certain items of business — described as **"items to be transacted only by postal ballot"** — which **must** be transacted by means of postal ballot (except at an Annual General Meeting), rather than at a general meeting. Under **Section 110(1)(b)**, a company **may** transact any other item (other than ordinary business and business in respect of which directors/auditors have a right to be heard) by postal ballot at its option.

**Proposition 1 — alteration of objects clause:** Alteration of the objects clause is one of the items listed under **Rule 22(16)** that must ordinarily be transacted **only by postal ballot**. Hence the Secretary's contention is broadly correct. **However**, an important **proviso to Section 110(1)** (inserted by the 2017 amendment, effective from the Rule) provides that any item required to be transacted by postal ballot **may be transacted at a general meeting** by a company **which is required to provide e-voting** under Section 108. Thus the item is **not** absolutely incapable of being transacted at a general meeting where e-voting is provided.

**Proposition 2 — adoption of financial statements:** Adoption of financial statements is an item of **ordinary business** under **Section 102(2)**. **Section 110 expressly excludes ordinary business** from being transacted by postal ballot, and further excludes any business in respect of which **directors or auditors have a right to be heard** at the meeting. Therefore, the company **cannot** transact adoption of accounts by postal ballot; it must be placed before the general meeting. The Secretary's second direction is **invalid**.

**E-voting:** Under postal ballot (Rule 22), voting may be done **by post or through electronic means** — e-voting is itself a **mode of postal ballot** and also a facility under **Section 108/Rule 20** for meetings. Members of a company that provides e-voting can cast postal-ballot votes electronically; they cannot compel a separate "meeting-based" e-voting where the matter is being validly conducted by postal ballot, but if the company provides e-voting for a general meeting, listed/prescribed items may be moved to the meeting.

**Conclusion:** Alteration of the objects clause must normally be by postal ballot (Rule 22(16)), but may be transacted at a general meeting if the company provides e-voting (proviso to Sec 110). Adoption of financial statements is ordinary business and **cannot** be transacted by postal ballot.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the two carve-outs — (i) ordinary business/right-to-be-heard business is excluded from postal ballot, and (ii) the e-voting proviso letting postal-ballot items be moved to a general meeting. Missing either nuance costs marks.)*

### Q41. Ch: Management & Administration — Quorum Failure & Adjournment u/s 103 (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Polaris Trading Ltd., a public company, has 1,900 members. Its Annual General Meeting was called for 11:00 a.m. on Monday, 4th May 2026. At 11:00 a.m., only **9 members** were personally present. Advise on the following, with reference to the exact provision:
(a) Was the quorum present?
(b) What is the consequence if quorum is not present within half an hour, where the meeting was called **on the requisition of members**?
(c) In an ordinary AGM (not requisitioned), what happens at the adjourned meeting if quorum is again absent?

**Answer:**
The relevant provision is **Section 103** of the Companies Act, 2013.

**(a) Quorum required:** Under **Section 103(1)(a)**, unless the articles provide for a larger number, the quorum for a meeting of a **public company** is:
- **5 members** personally present if the number of members as on the date of meeting is **not more than 1,000**;
- **15 members** personally present if the number of members is **more than 1,000 but up to 5,000**;
- **30 members** personally present if the number of members **exceeds 5,000**.

Polaris Trading Ltd. has **1,900 members** (i.e., more than 1,000 but up to 5,000), so the required quorum is **15 members** personally present. Only **9** were present. Hence, **quorum was NOT present**.

**(b) Meeting called on requisition of members:** Under the **proviso to Section 103(2)(a)**, if within half an hour from the appointed time a quorum is not present, and the **meeting was called by requisitionists** under Section 100, the meeting shall stand **cancelled** (dissolved), not merely adjourned.

**(c) Ordinary (non-requisitioned) meeting — adjournment:** Under **Section 103(2)(a)**, if quorum is not present within half an hour, the meeting shall stand **adjourned to the same day in the next week at the same time and place**, or to such other day, time and place as the Board may determine. Under **Section 103(3)**, if at the **adjourned meeting** also a quorum is not present within half an hour, the **members present shall be the quorum** — i.e., whatever number is present can validly transact business.

**Conclusion:** Quorum (15) was not present. Had the AGM been requisitioned, it would stand cancelled; being an ordinary AGM, it stands adjourned to the same day next week, and at that adjourned meeting the members then present will themselves constitute the quorum.

*(Full-marks tip: Apply the exact 1,000/5,000 slabs of Section 103(1)(a) to the given member count, and distinguish the requisitioned-meeting cancellation (proviso) from the ordinary-meeting adjournment. A common deduction is using the private-company quorum of 2.)*

### Q42. Ch: Management & Administration — Minutes: Contents, Non-inclusion & Evidentiary Value u/s 118 (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** With reference to Section 118 of the Companies Act, 2013, answer: (i) the time limit for preparing and signing minutes; (ii) the matters that the chairman may, at his discretion, exclude from the minutes; (iii) the evidentiary value of minutes; and (iv) whether minutes may be maintained in loose-leaf form and the applicability of the Secretarial Standard.

**Answer:**
The relevant provision is **Section 118** of the Companies Act, 2013 read with **Rule 25** of the Companies (Management and Administration) Rules, 2014 and **Secretarial Standard SS-2** (General Meetings) / SS-1 (Board Meetings).

**(i) Time limit:** Under **Section 118(1)** read with the rules, every company shall prepare and keep minutes of every general meeting and every meeting of the Board/committees, and the minutes must be **entered in the minutes book within thirty days** of the conclusion of the meeting. Each page must be initialled/signed and the last page dated and signed — for **Board meetings, by the chairman of that meeting or the next meeting**, and for **general meetings, by the chairman of the same meeting** within the aforesaid period (or in the event of his death/inability, by a director duly authorised by the Board).

Crucially, **Section 118(10)** makes it mandatory for every company to **observe Secretarial Standards SS-1 and SS-2** specified by the ICSI and approved by the Central Government with respect to Board and general meetings.

**Conclusion:** Minutes must be entered within 30 days; the chairman has absolute discretion to exclude defamatory, irrelevant or company-detrimental matter; minutes are evidence of proceedings and raise a presumption of regularity; and observance of Secretarial Standards SS-1 and SS-2 is mandatory under Section 118(10).

### Q44. Ch: Registration of Charges — Consequence of Non-registration & Certificate as Conclusive Evidence u/s 77 (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Galaxy Steels Ltd. created a charge on its stock-in-trade in favour of Apex Bank for ₹65 lakh on 1st April 2026 but never registered it. On 20th July 2026, the company went into liquidation. Apex Bank claims to be a **secured creditor** entitled to priority over unsecured creditors on the strength of its charge instrument. Meanwhile, another lender, Vista Finance, whose charge on the **same stock** was registered on time, also claims the stock. Advise: (i) Can Apex Bank enforce its charge in liquidation? (ii) Is the underlying loan still recoverable? (iii) What is the evidentiary effect of the certificate of registration issued to Vista Finance?

**Answer:**
The relevant provision is **Section 77(3)** and **Section 77(4)** of the Companies Act, 2013 read with **Rule 6(2)**.

**(i) Effect of non-registration on the charge:** Under **Section 77(3)**, notwithstanding anything contained in any other law, **no charge created by a company shall be taken into account by the liquidator appointed under this Act or the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, or any other creditor, unless it is duly registered** under Section 77 and a certificate of registration is issued. Consequently, an **unregistered charge is void against the liquidator and other creditors**. Apex Bank, having failed to register, **cannot enforce its charge** in the liquidation and **cannot claim the status of a secured creditor** — it ranks merely as an **unsecured creditor** for its dues.

**(ii) Recovery of the underlying money:** Non-registration voids the **security**, but not the **debt** itself. **Section 77(3)** is expressly saved by **Section 77(4)**, which provides that nothing in Section 77(3) shall prejudice any contract or obligation for the **repayment of the money secured** by the charge. Therefore, the loan of ₹65 lakh **remains recoverable** from the company as a **debt**, and indeed becomes **immediately payable**. Apex Bank can prove in the liquidation as an unsecured creditor for ₹65 lakh.

**(iii) Certificate of registration — conclusive evidence:** Under **Section 77(2)**, where a charge is registered, the Registrar shall issue a **certificate of registration** in **Form CHG-2** (or CHG-3 for modification), and such certificate shall be **conclusive evidence** that the requirements of Chapter VI (registration of charges) have been complied with. Hence Vista Finance's certificate is conclusive proof of valid registration, and its charge over the stock is enforceable in priority to unsecured creditors, including Apex Bank.

**Conclusion:** Apex Bank cannot enforce its unregistered charge and ranks as an unsecured creditor, though the ₹65 lakh debt remains recoverable (Section 77(4)). Vista Finance's charge, evidenced by a conclusive certificate under Section 77(2), takes priority over the stock.

### Q45. Ch: Management & Administration — Class Meetings & Variation of Shareholders' Rights (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Ecliptic Motors Ltd. has 10,00,000 equity shares and 2,00,000 **8% preference shares**. The Board proposes to **vary the rights** attached to the preference shares by reducing the dividend rate to 6%. The company's articles permit variation with the consent of the class affected. Advise on: (i) the consent required from the preference shareholders and the meeting to be held; (ii) the majority needed; and (iii) the remedy available to dissenting preference shareholders. Cite the exact provision.

**Answer:**
The relevant provision is **Section 48** of the Companies Act, 2013 (variation of shareholders' rights), read together with the provisions on class meetings.

**(i) Consent and class meeting:** Under **Section 48(1)**, where a share capital of the company is divided into **different classes of shares**, the rights attached to the shares of any class may be **varied with the consent** of the holders of **not less than three-fourths of the issued shares of that class**, either — (a) in **writing** from such holders, **or** (b) by means of a **special resolution passed at a separate meeting of the holders of that class** (a **class meeting**). Therefore, Ecliptic Motors Ltd. must obtain the consent of the **preference shareholders as a class** — either by written consent of 75% of them or by a special resolution at a **separate class meeting** of the preference shareholders. Additionally, where the variation of one class's rights affects the rights of **another class**, the consent of that other class (three-fourths) is also required.

The decision of the Tribunal is **binding on the shareholders**, and the variation shall **not have effect** unless and until it is confirmed by the Tribunal.

Dissenting holders of at least 10% of that class may apply to the Tribunal within 21 days under Section 48(2) to cancel the variation.

The Board did **not** proceed to call the meeting. Advise: (i) the threshold for a valid requisition; (ii) within what time the Board must call the meeting and by when it must be held; (iii) what the requisitionists may do if the Board fails; and (iv) recovery of expenses. Cite the exact sub-sections.

**Answer:**
The relevant provision is **Section 100** of the Companies Act, 2013.

### Q47. Ch: Registration of Charges — Modification of Charge u/s 79 (Marks: 4) [Application]
**Question:** Comet Packaging Ltd. had a registered charge in favour of Syndicate Bank securing ₹1 crore. On 5th May 2026, the terms were varied to **increase the secured amount to ₹1.5 crore** and to add additional machinery as security. The Managing Director believes that since a charge already stands registered, no further filing is needed for a mere modification. Advise on whether modification requires registration, the time limit, the form, and the effect of failure. Cite the provision.

**Answer:**
The relevant provision is **Section 79** of the Companies Act, 2013 read with **Rule 6** of the Companies (Registration of Charges) Rules, 2014.

**Modification requires registration:** Under **Section 79**, the provisions of Section 77 relating to **registration of charges** shall apply, *mutatis mutandis*, to — (a) a company **modifying** the terms or conditions or the extent or operation of any charge registered under Section 77; and (b) the acquisition of any property subject to a charge. A **modification** includes any variation in the terms of the charge — such as a **change in the amount secured, the rate of interest, the terms of repayment, or the property charged**. An increase in the secured amount from ₹1 crore to ₹1.5 crore and the addition of machinery clearly constitute a **modification**. Hence the MD's belief is **incorrect**.

### Q48. Ch: Management & Administration — Annual General Meeting: Timing, Extension & First AGM u/s 96 (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Stellar Foods Ltd. was **incorporated on 12th June 2025**. Its first financial year ended on **31st March 2026**. The Board seeks advice on: (i) the last date by which it must hold its **first** AGM; (ii) whether the "not more than fifteen months gap" rule applies to a first AGM; (iii) the maximum extension the Registrar may grant and whether it applies to a first AGM; and (iv) the permissible day, time and place of an AGM. Cite the exact provision.

**Answer:**
The relevant provision is **Section 96** of the Companies Act, 2013.

**(i) First AGM timing:** Under the **first proviso to Section 96(1)**, in the case of the **first annual general meeting**, the company shall hold it within a period of **nine months from the date of closing of the first financial year**. The first financial year closed on **31st March 2026**; nine months therefrom expires on **31st December 2026**. Hence the first AGM must be held **on or before 31st December 2026**.

**(ii) The 15-month gap rule:** For **subsequent** AGMs, Section 96(1) requires that the meeting be held within **six months from the close of the financial year**, and that **not more than fifteen months** shall elapse between the date of one AGM and that of the next. However, the same first proviso expressly states that **if a company holds its first AGM within nine months, it need not hold any AGM in the year of its incorporation**. Thus, for the **first** AGM, only the nine-month rule applies; the "not more than fifteen months gap" condition does **not** govern the first meeting.

**(iii) Extension by Registrar:** Under the **third proviso to Section 96(1)**, the Registrar may, for **any special reason**, extend the time within which any AGM (other than the first AGM) shall be held, by a period **not exceeding three months**. This power **does not apply to the first AGM** — no extension is available for the first AGM.

**(iv) Day, time and place (Section 96(2)):** Every AGM shall be called during **business hours**, that is, between **9 a.m. and 6 p.m.**, on **any day that is not a National Holiday**, and shall be held either at the **registered office** of the company or at some other place **within the city, town or village** in which the registered office is situate. (An unlisted company may hold its AGM anywhere in India if consent is given in advance by all members.)

**Conclusion:** Stellar Foods Ltd. must hold its first AGM on or before **31st December 2026** (nine months from 31 March 2026). The 15-month gap rule and the three-month Registrar extension do **not** apply to a first AGM, which must be held during business hours on a non-National-Holiday at/near the registered office.

### Q50. Ch: Registration of Charges — Charges Requiring Registration & Excluded Charges (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** "Every charge created by a company must be registered, and there are no exceptions." Examine the correctness of this statement with reference to Section 77 of the Companies Act, 2013 and the Companies (Registration of Charges) Rules, 2014. Discuss the meaning of "charge", the kinds of charges required to be registered, and the charges that are **exempt** from registration.

**Answer:**
The relevant provisions are **Section 2(16)**, **Section 77** of the Companies Act, 2013 and the Companies (Registration of Charges) Rules, 2014.

**Meaning of "charge" (Section 2(16)):** A **"charge"** means an **interest or lien created on the property or assets of a company or any of its undertakings, or both, as security**, and **includes a mortgage**. A charge may be **fixed** (on specific, identifiable, ascertained property) or **floating** (hovering over a class of assets that changes in the ordinary course of business, crystallising on default).

**Scope of registration (Section 77(1)):** Section 77(1) is deliberately wide. It requires **every company creating a charge** — (a) within or outside India, (b) on its property or assets or any of its undertakings, (c) whether **tangible or otherwise**, and (d) situated in or outside India — to register the particulars of the charge with the Registrar within thirty days. This covers charges created to secure debentures, mortgages on land/buildings, hypothecation of movables/stock, charges on book debts, goodwill, patents, trademarks, etc. Thus the general rule is indeed that **most charges must be registered**.

**Exceptions (Rule 3 of the 2014 Rules / statutory position):** The statement is **not wholly correct**, because certain charges are **excluded** from the registration requirement. The charges **not** requiring registration include:

| Charge / interest | Reason for exclusion |
|---|---|
| A pledge (as opposed to hypothecation/mortgage) — a possessory security | Possession passes; not a registrable "charge" in the strict sense |
| A lien arising by operation of law (e.g., an unpaid seller's lien, banker's lien) | Not "created" by the company; arises by law |
| A charge created by operation of law (statutory charge) | Not created by act of the company |
| Certain charges specified as exempt by the Central Government | By notification |

Additionally, only charges **created by the company** by its own act attract Section 77; charges/interests **arising by operation of law** (which the company does not create) fall outside it.

**Conclusion:** The statement is **not fully correct**. While Section 77 casts a very wide net requiring registration of almost every charge a company creates on its assets (tangible or intangible, in or outside India), charges arising by operation of law, and certain interests such as a pledge or a common-law lien, are **not** required to be registered.

*(Full-marks tip: Define "charge" under Sec 2(16), stress the width of Sec 77(1) (property tangible/intangible, in/outside India), and then list the exceptions (operation of law, pledge, lien). Answering "no exceptions" earns nothing; the exceptions carry the marks.)*

### Q51. Ch: Management & Administration — Proxies: Rights, Restrictions & Aggregate Limit u/s 105 (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** At the general meeting of Horizon Cements Ltd. (a company having share capital), the following situations arose. Advise on each, citing Section 105 and the relevant Rule:
(a) Mr. A, a proxy, wanted to **speak** at the meeting and to **vote on a show of hands**.
(b) Mr. B was appointed as proxy by **60 members** holding, in aggregate, 8% of the total share capital carrying voting rights.
(c) A proxy form was **deposited 30 hours** before the meeting; the articles required 48 hours' deposit.
(d) A **member of a company not having share capital** (a Section 8 company) wished to appoint a proxy.

**Answer:**
The relevant provision is **Section 105** of the Companies Act, 2013 read with **Rule 19** of the Companies (Management and Administration) Rules, 2014.

**General right (Section 105(1)):** Any member entitled to attend and vote at a meeting may appoint another person as a **proxy** to attend and **vote** on his behalf. However, unless the articles otherwise provide, a proxy shall **not have the right to speak** at the meeting, and shall **not be entitled to vote except on a poll**.

**(a) Right to speak and vote on show of hands:** Applying Section 105(1), Mr. A, as a proxy, **cannot speak** at the meeting and **cannot vote on a show of hands** — a proxy is entitled to vote **only on a poll**. Hence his demand is **not permissible**.

**(b) Aggregate limit on proxies (Rule 19(2)):** A person can act as proxy on behalf of members **not exceeding fifty (50) members** and holding in the aggregate **not more than ten per cent of the total share capital** carrying voting rights. Where a single member holds more than 10% of the total voting share capital, such member may appoint a single person as proxy who shall **not** act as proxy for any other person. Here, Mr. B is proxy for **60 members** — this **exceeds the ceiling of 50 members**, even though the 8% aggregate holding is within the 10% limit. Therefore, Mr. B's appointment is **invalid to the extent it exceeds 50 members**; he can validly act for a maximum of 50 of those members.

**(c) Time of deposit (Section 105(4)):** Under **Section 105(4)**, the instrument appointing a proxy must be deposited with the company **not less than forty-eight hours before the time of the meeting**. Any provision in the articles requiring a **longer** period is void; the maximum notice that can be required is 48 hours. A proxy deposited only **30 hours** before the meeting is deposited **short of the 48-hour requirement** and is therefore **invalid / may be rejected**. (Conversely, if the articles had demanded more than 48 hours, that excess would be disregarded.)

**(d) Company not having share capital (proviso to Section 105(1)):** Under the **first proviso to Section 105(1)** read with the rules, a member of a company **not having a share capital** shall **not be entitled to appoint a proxy** unless the articles provide otherwise. A **Section 8 company** is one such prescribed class where a member may appoint a proxy **only if the articles so permit**, and even then, a member who is not a member cannot be a proxy. Hence, absent an enabling article, the Section 8 company member **cannot** appoint a proxy.

**Conclusion:** (a) Mr. A cannot speak or vote on a show of hands — only on a poll; (b) Mr. B's proxy is valid only up to 50 members (60 exceeds the cap); (c) the proxy deposited 30 hours before the meeting is invalid, as 48 hours' deposit is mandatory; (d) the Section 8 company member can appoint a proxy only if the articles so permit.

*(Full-marks tip: Cite the twin limits of Rule 19(2) — 50 members AND 10% capital — and the 48-hour deposit rule of Section 105(4) (articles cannot demand longer). Missing the "50 members" cap while noting only the 10% is the frequent error.)*

### Q52. Ch: Declaration & Payment of Dividend — Dividend out of Free Reserves (Rule 3) (Marks: 6) [Problem]

**Question:** Zenith Metals Ltd. earned inadequate profit in the year ended 31st March, 2025 and proposes to declare dividend out of its accumulated free reserves. The Board seeks your advice on the maximum dividend that may be declared under Rule 3 of the Companies (Declaration and Payment of Dividend) Rules, 2014. The relevant data is given below. Compute the maximum permissible dividend and state whether the proposed dividend of ₹22 lakh is in order.

| Particulars | Amount |
|---|---|
| Paid-up equity share capital | ₹200 lakh |
| Free reserves as on 01.04.2024 | ₹150 lakh |
| Rate of dividend declared in immediately preceding three years | 12%, 10%, 8% |
| Net loss for the year ended 31.03.2025 (after tax) | ₹6 lakh |
| Accumulated losses carried in balance sheet | Nil |
| Proposed dividend | ₹22 lakh |

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Applicable conditions of Rule 3.** Where a company proposes to declare dividend out of accumulated profits earned in previous years and transferred to free reserves owing to inadequacy/absence of profits in the current year, Rule 3 of the Companies (Declaration and Payment of Dividend) Rules, 2014 imposes four limits — (a) the rate of dividend shall not exceed the **average of the rates of the three immediately preceding years**; (b) the total amount drawn shall not exceed **one-tenth of the sum of paid-up capital plus free reserves**; (c) the amount so drawn shall **first be used to set off the current year's loss**; and (d) the balance of reserves after such withdrawal shall **not fall below 15% of paid-up share capital**.

**WN-2: Condition (a) — Average rate ceiling.**
Average rate = (12% + 10% + 8%) ÷ 3 = 30% ÷ 3 = **10%**.
Maximum dividend by rate = 10% × ₹200 lakh = **₹20 lakh**.

**WN-3: Condition (b) — One-tenth of (capital + free reserves).**
= 1/10 × (₹200 lakh + ₹150 lakh) = 1/10 × ₹350 lakh = **₹35 lakh**.

**WN-4: Condition (c) — Set-off of current year loss.**
Amount to be withdrawn from reserves must first absorb the current year loss of ₹6 lakh. Available for dividend = withdrawal − current loss.
Ceiling from this condition = ₹35 lakh − ₹6 lakh = **₹29 lakh** (i.e. total draw of ₹35 lakh less ₹6 lakh set-off).

**WN-5: Condition (d) — Minimum residual reserve (15% of capital).**
Balance of free reserves must not fall below 15% × ₹200 lakh = ₹30 lakh.
Maximum drawable from reserves = ₹150 lakh − ₹30 lakh = ₹120 lakh; after setting off the ₹6 lakh loss, this is not the binding constraint here.

**Statement Showing Maximum Permissible Dividend — Zenith Metals Ltd.**

| Test under Rule 3 | Ceiling on dividend |
|---|---|
| (a) Average-rate limit (10% of ₹200 lakh) | ₹20 lakh |
| (b) One-tenth of (capital + reserves) less loss set-off | ₹29 lakh |
| (d) Residual reserve ≥ 15% of capital | ₹120 lakh (not binding) |
| **Maximum permissible (lowest of the above)** | **₹20 lakh** |

The binding constraint is the average-rate limit. Maximum dividend that may be declared = **₹20 lakh**.

**Answer:** The maximum dividend Zenith Metals Ltd. may declare out of free reserves under Rule 3 is **₹20 lakh**. The proposed dividend of ₹22 lakh **exceeds this ceiling by ₹2 lakh and is NOT in order**; the Board must restrict the dividend to ₹20 lakh.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards testing ALL four conditions separately and picking the lowest; students routinely lose marks by forgetting the loss set-off in condition (c) or by applying the 15% residual test to capital instead of computing the drawable reserve. State that the average-rate limit binds, with the arithmetic shown.)*

### Q53. Ch: Declaration & Payment of Dividend — Interim Dividend (Sec 123(3)) (Marks: 5) [Application]

**Question:** The Board of Directors of Aravali Cements Ltd. wishes to declare an interim dividend on 10th December, 2024 for the current financial year 2024-25. | Loss up to preceding quarter (present case) | Proviso to Sec 123(3) | Average of preceding 3 years = 15% |

### Q54. Ch: Declaration & Payment of Dividend — Unpaid Dividend & IEPF Transfer (Sec 124) (Marks: 6) [Application]

Also state the disclosure the company must make on its website.

**(i) Transfer to Unpaid Dividend Account [Sec 124(1)]:** Where a dividend has been declared but has not been paid or claimed within **thirty days** from the date of declaration, the company shall, within **seven days** from the expiry of the said thirty-day period, transfer the total amount of unpaid or unclaimed dividend to a special account called the **"Unpaid Dividend Account"** opened in a scheduled bank. However, any claimant of shares so transferred shall be entitled to **claim the transfer of shares back from the IEPF Authority** in accordance with the prescribed procedure.

|---|---|---|

| After transfer | Website statement of unpaid amounts | Within 90 days |

**Question:** After Meghdoot Textiles Ltd. transferred its unpaid dividend to the Unpaid Dividend Account, an employee of the company mistakenly withdrew ₹1.5 lakh from that account for the company's working-capital use. Answer, with reference to the Companies Act, 2013: (a) Is the company liable to pay interest on the amount wrongly retained/utilised, and at what rate and for whose benefit? (b) Who is entitled to claim payment out of the Unpaid Dividend Account?

**Answer:** The point is governed by **sub-sections (3) and (4) of Section 124 of the Companies Act, 2013**, which regulate money lying in the Unpaid Dividend Account.

**(a) Interest for default [Sec 124(3)]:** If any default is made in transferring the total amount, or any part thereof, to the Unpaid Dividend Account, the company shall pay, from the date of such default, **interest on the amount not transferred at the rate of twelve per cent per annum**, and the **interest accruing on such amount shall enure to the benefit of the members** of the company in proportion to the amount remaining unpaid to them. Thus, where the company has wrongly utilised ₹1.5 lakh instead of keeping it in the Unpaid Dividend Account, it must bear interest at **12% p.a.** on that amount, and such interest belongs to the members concerned, not to the company.

**(b) Right to claim payment [Sec 124(4)]:** Any person claiming to be entitled to any money transferred to the Unpaid Dividend Account of the company may **apply to the company for payment of the money claimed**. The company is bound to pay the rightful claimant out of that account. (Only after the seven-year period, when the money is transferred to the IEPF, does the claimant apply to the IEPF Authority instead.)

**Conclusion:** (a) Yes — the company must pay interest at **12% per annum** on the amount not kept in / withdrawn from the Unpaid Dividend Account, and this interest enures to the benefit of the members. (b) The shareholder, being a person entitled to the money, may **apply to the company** and the company must pay her out of the Unpaid Dividend Account.

### Q56. Ch: Declaration & Payment of Dividend — Punishment for Failure to Distribute Dividend (Sec 127) (Marks: 5) [Application]

**Question:** Nilgiri Foods Ltd. declared a dividend at its AGM on 20th August, 2024 but failed to pay or dispatch the dividend warrants to several shareholders even by 30th September, 2024. The defaulting directors contend that no offence is made out because the shareholders had not furnished updated bank details. Examine the liability of the company and its directors under the Companies Act, 2013, and state the circumstances in which no offence is deemed to have been committed.

**Answer:** The matter is governed by **Section 127 of the Companies Act, 2013**, which prescribes punishment for failure to distribute dividends. Where a dividend has been declared but is **not paid or the warrant in respect thereof is not posted within thirty days** from the date of declaration to any shareholder entitled to the payment of the dividend, every director of the company, if he is knowingly a party to the default, shall be **punishable with imprisonment which may extend to two years and with fine of not less than one thousand rupees for every day** during which such default continues; and the **company shall be liable to pay simple interest at the rate of eighteen per cent per annum** during the period for which such default continues.

For the dividend declared on 20th August, 2024, the thirty-day period expires on **19th September, 2024**; non-payment/non-dispatch beyond that date attracts Section 127.

**Exceptions — where no offence is deemed committed:** The proviso to Section 127 lists situations in which no offence shall be deemed to have been committed, including inter alia — (a) where the dividend could not be paid **by reason of the operation of any law**; (b) where a shareholder has given **directions to the company regarding payment** which could not be complied with and the same has been communicated to him; (c) where there is a **dispute regarding the right to receive the dividend**; (d) where the dividend has been **lawfully adjusted** by the company against any sum due to it from the shareholder; and (e) where, for **any other reason, the failure to pay the dividend was not due to any default on the part of the company**.

**Application:** If shareholders had not furnished updated bank details and the company had communicated the position, the situation may fall within exception (b)/(e). However, this exemption applies only where the failure is genuinely **not due to any default of the company** and the shareholder's directions could not be complied with despite communication. The directors cannot escape liability merely by asserting missing bank details if the company took no steps to communicate or to dispatch warrants to the last known address.

**Conclusion:** Unless the company proves that the non-payment falls within one of the statutory exceptions (particularly that the failure was not due to its own default and the shareholders were duly informed), the company is liable to pay interest at **18% p.a.** and the knowingly-defaulting directors face imprisonment up to two years and a daily fine of not less than ₹1,000 under Section 127.

### Q57. Ch: Declaration & Payment of Dividend — Deposit of Dividend Amount in Separate Bank Account (Sec 123(4)) (Marks: 4) [Theory]

In the light of the Companies Act, 2013, explain the requirement to deposit the declared dividend in a separate bank account, the applicability of this requirement, and the manner in which dividend must be paid to shareholders.

**Answer:** The proposition flows from **Section 123(4) and Section 123(5) of the Companies Act, 2013**, which ring-fence the declared dividend for the benefit of shareholders.

**Separate bank account [Sec 123(4)]:** The amount of the dividend, including interim dividend, shall be **deposited in a scheduled bank in a separate account within five days from the date of declaration** of such dividend. Once dividend is declared, it becomes a debt due to the shareholders and the company can no longer use those funds for its own purposes; the mandatory deposit in a separate account ensures the money is set apart exclusively for distribution.

Dividend may be paid by cheque or warrant or in any electronic mode to the shareholder entitled to the payment. Dividend is payable only to the **registered shareholder, or to his order, or to his banker.**

**Non-applicability:** Nothing in Section 123 shall apply to a **Government company in which the entire paid-up share capital is held by the Central Government, or by any State Government or Governments, or partly by the Central Government and partly by one or more State Governments**, where a dividend is declared or paid by such company — such companies are exempt from the separate-account requirement (subject to the notification).

**Conclusion:** By virtue of Section 123(4), a company must transfer the declared dividend to a separate scheduled-bank account within five days, and pay it only in cash to the registered shareholder, thereby ensuring the declared dividend is preserved solely for the members and is not diverted to the company's operations.

### Q58. Ch: Accounts of Companies — Re-opening of Accounts on Court/Tribunal Order (Sec 130) (Marks: 6) [Application]

**Question:** The Serious Fraud Investigation Office (SFIO) discovers that the accounts of Kaveri Infra Ltd. for the financial years 2019-20 and 2020-21 were prepared in a fraudulent manner, understating certain liabilities. It seeks to have those accounts re-opened. The Board of Kaveri Infra Ltd. contends that (i) accounts once adopted at the AGM are final and cannot be reopened, and (ii) in any case, a company may voluntarily reopen accounts of any past year at its discretion. Examine both contentions with reference to Section 130 of the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:** The re-opening of accounts is governed by **Section 130 of the Companies Act, 2013**, which permits re-opening only on the order of a competent authority and lays down strict safeguards.

**Who may order re-opening [Sec 130(1)]:** A company shall **not re-open its books of account and not recast its financial statements** unless an application in this regard is made by (a) the Central Government, (b) the Income-tax authorities, (c) the Securities and Exchange Board (SEBI), (d) any other statutory regulatory body or authority, or (e) any person concerned, and an **order is made by a court of competent jurisdiction or the Tribunal (NCLT)** to the effect that — (i) the **relevant earlier accounts were prepared in a fraudulent manner**, or (ii) the **affairs of the company were mismanaged** during the relevant period, casting a doubt on the reliability of the financial statements.

**Notice to authorities [Proviso to Sec 130(1)]:** The court or the Tribunal, before passing any such order, shall give notice to the **Central Government, the Income-tax authorities, SEBI or any other statutory regulatory body/authority concerned or any other person concerned** and shall take into consideration their representations.

**Finality of re-opened accounts [Sec 130(2)]:** The accounts so revised or re-cast pursuant to the order **shall be final**.

**Time-limit [Sec 130(3)]:** No order shall be made in respect of re-opening of books of account relating to a period **earlier than eight financial years immediately preceding the current financial year**, unless the Central Government has, under Section 128(5), directed a longer period of preservation, in which case the books may be ordered to be re-opened within such longer period.

**Application to the contentions:**
- **Contention (i)** — that adopted accounts are final and can never be reopened — is **incorrect.** Where a court or the Tribunal, on the application of an authority such as the SFIO/Central Government, is satisfied that the earlier accounts were prepared fraudulently, it may order re-opening under Section 130 notwithstanding earlier adoption at the AGM. The accounts of FY 2019-20 and 2020-21 fall well within the eight-year window and can be reopened.
- **Contention (ii)** — that a company may voluntarily reopen accounts of any past year at its own discretion — is also **incorrect.** Section 130 permits re-opening **only pursuant to a court/Tribunal order** on the specified grounds; a company has no independent right to reopen and recast its books voluntarily under this section. (Voluntary revision of financial statements is separately and narrowly permitted under Section 131, not Section 130.)

**Conclusion:** Both contentions of the Board are untenable. The accounts of Kaveri Infra Ltd. can be re-opened and recast under Section 130 pursuant to an order of the court/Tribunal on the SFIO's application, and the company cannot reopen the accounts of its own accord.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the two structural safeguards — (a) re-opening ONLY on a court/Tribunal order on the two specified grounds, and (b) the eight-financial-year limit. Contrast Sec 130 (compulsory, order-driven) with Sec 131 (voluntary, self-initiated) to earn the application marks.)*

### Q59. Ch: Accounts of Companies — Voluntary Revision of Financial Statements (Sec 131) (Marks: 6) [Application]

**Question:** After the financial statements and Board's report of Vindhya Pharma Ltd. for FY 2023-24 were adopted at its AGM, the directors realise that the financial statements do not comply with a particular Accounting Standard and that the Board's report is deficient. The directors wish to revise both. Advise them on the procedure, restrictions and limits under Section 131 of the Companies Act, 2013. Can they revise the statements for more than one earlier year, and how many times?

**Answer:** The matter is governed by **Section 131 of the Companies Act, 2013**, which provides for **voluntary revision of financial statements or Board's report** by the directors themselves — as distinct from compulsory re-opening under Section 130.

**Grounds and approval [Sec 131(1)]:** If it appears to the directors of a company that (a) the **financial statement of the company, or** (b) the **report of the Board**, do **not comply with the provisions of Section 129** (financial statements/AS) **or Section 134** (Board's report), they may prepare a **revised financial statement or a revised report** in respect of **any of the three preceding financial years** after obtaining **approval of the Tribunal (NCLT)** on an application made by the company. A copy of the Tribunal's order shall be filed with the Registrar.

**Notice to authorities [Proviso to Sec 131(1)]:** The Tribunal shall give **notice to the Central Government and the Income-tax authorities** and take into consideration their representations, before passing any such order.

**Limited scope [Second proviso to Sec 131(1)]:** Such revised financial statement or report shall **not be prepared or filed more than once in a financial year.** Further, the **detailed reasons for revision** shall be disclosed in the Board's report in the relevant financial year in which such revision is being made.

**Confined revision [Sec 131(2)]:** Where copies of the previous financial statement or report have been sent out to members, delivered to the Registrar or laid before the company in general meeting, the **revisions must be confined to** (a) the correction in respect of which the previous statement/report **did not comply** with Section 129 or 134, and (b) the making of any **necessary consequential alteration.**

**Application:** Vindhya Pharma Ltd.'s directors may voluntarily revise both the non-compliant financial statements (AS default under Section 129) and the deficient Board's report (Section 134) for FY 2023-24, provided they obtain the **approval of the Tribunal**, file the order with the Registrar, and confine the revision to the non-compliance and its consequential effects. They may revise in respect of **any of the three preceding financial years**, but such revision can be made **only once in a financial year.**

**Conclusion:** The directors can voluntarily revise the financial statements and Board's report under Section 131, but only for up to the three preceding financial years, only with Tribunal approval, only once in a financial year, and only to the extent of correcting the non-compliance and its consequential alterations.

*(Full-marks tip: The three high-value points are (i) "three preceding financial years", (ii) "Tribunal approval + notice to Central Government and Income-tax authorities", and (iii) "not more than once in a financial year". Distinguish this self-initiated route from the order-driven Section 130.)*

### Q60. Ch: Accounts of Companies — National Financial Reporting Authority (Sec 132) (Marks: 6) [Theory]

**Question:** Explain the constitution and the principal functions and powers of the National Financial Reporting Authority (NFRA) under Section 132 of the Companies Act, 2013. In particular, state its role in relation to accounting and auditing standards, its disciplinary powers over auditors, and the bar on appearing before other authorities.

**Answer:** The **National Financial Reporting Authority (NFRA)** is constituted under **Section 132 of the Companies Act, 2013** as an independent regulator to oversee compliance with accounting and auditing standards and to monitor the quality of audits.

**Constitution [Sec 132(1) & (3)]:** The Central Government may, by notification, constitute NFRA to provide for matters relating to accounting and auditing standards under the Act. NFRA shall consist of a **chairperson**, who shall be a person of eminence and having expertise in accountancy, auditing, finance or law, and such **other members not exceeding fifteen**, as may be prescribed, appointed by the Central Government.

**Functions [Sec 132(2)]:** NFRA shall — (a) **make recommendations** to the Central Government on the formulation and laying down of accounting and auditing policies and standards for adoption by companies or their auditors; (b) **monitor and enforce compliance** with accounting standards and auditing standards; (c) **oversee the quality of service** of the professions associated with ensuring compliance with such standards and suggest measures for improvement; and (d) perform such other functions relating to the above as may be prescribed.

**Powers of investigation [Sec 132(4)]:** Notwithstanding anything in any other law, NFRA shall have the power to **investigate**, either suo motu or on a reference by the Central Government, into matters of **professional or other misconduct** committed by any member or firm of chartered accountants registered under the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949, in respect of prescribed classes of companies. Where NFRA has initiated an investigation, **no other institute or body shall initiate or continue any proceedings** in such matters of misconduct. For its investigation, NFRA has the **same powers as are vested in a civil court** under the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, in respect of discovery and production of documents, summoning and examining persons on oath, and inspection of books and records.

**Disciplinary powers [Sec 132(4)(c)]:** Where professional or other misconduct is proved, NFRA has the power to make an order for — (A) **imposing penalty** of not less than ₹1 lakh, extending up to five times the fees received, in case of individuals, and not less than ₹5 lakh, extending up to ten times the fees received, in case of firms; and (B) **debarring the member or the firm** from being appointed as an auditor or internal auditor or from undertaking any audit in respect of financial statements or internal audit of the functions and activities of any company or body corporate, for a **minimum period of six months up to a maximum of ten years**.

**Appeal and bar [Sec 132(5) & (4)(d)]:** Any person aggrieved by an order of NFRA may prefer an **appeal before the Appellate Tribunal.** Further, a person on whom penalty has been imposed shall not be eligible to be appointed as auditor for the debarred period.

**Conclusion:** NFRA is an independent oversight body that recommends and enforces accounting and auditing standards, monitors audit quality, and exercises exclusive investigative and disciplinary jurisdiction — including penalties and debarment — over auditors of prescribed classes of companies, with appeals lying to the Appellate Tribunal.

*(Full-marks tip: The penalty figures (₹1 lakh/₹5 lakh; up to 5x/10x fees) and the debarment range (6 months to 10 years) are the discriminating marks. Also state the exclusivity rule — once NFRA acts, no other body (e.g. ICAI) may proceed on the same misconduct.)*

### Q64. Ch: Audit & Auditors — Rotation of Auditors & Cooling-off (Sec 139(2)) (Marks: 6) [Application]

Deep & Co.) that has **two partners common** with the outgoing Prakash & Associates **cannot be appointed** as the auditor of Godavari Steels Ltd. for the five-year cooling-off period, since it shares partners with the firm whose tenure has expired.

Deep & Co., having common partners with the outgoing firm, is likewise **ineligible** for appointment during that period. Godavari Steels Ltd. must appoint a genuinely different audit firm.

### Q65. Ch: Audit & Auditors — Appointment of First Auditor & Casual Vacancy (Sec 139(6) & 139(8)) (Marks: 5) [Application]

**Question:** Examine, with reference to the Companies Act, 2013, the following independent situations relating to Tapti Chemicals Ltd. (a non-Government company): (a) The Board of Directors failed to appoint the first auditor within 30 days of registration; what is the consequence and who then appoints? ### Q67. Ch: Audit & Auditors — Auditor Not to Render Certain Services (Sec 144) (Marks: 5) [Theory]

**Question:** "The independence of the statutory auditor is protected by prohibiting certain non-audit services." In the light of Section 144 of the Companies Act, 2013, enumerate the services which a statutory auditor shall not render to the company, and explain the meaning of rendering services "directly or indirectly" and the transition period allowed for existing arrangements.

**Answer:** The prohibition on rendering certain services is contained in **Section 144 of the Companies Act, 2013**, enacted to safeguard auditor independence by preventing self-review and conflicts of interest.

**Prohibited services [Sec 144]:** An auditor appointed under the Act shall provide to the company only such **other services as are approved by the Board of Directors or the audit committee**, as the case may be, but which shall **not include** any of the following services (whether such services are rendered directly or indirectly to the company or its holding company or subsidiary company), namely —

(b) **internal audit**;
(c) **design and implementation of any financial information system**;
(d) **actuarial services**;
(e) **investment advisory services**;
(f) **investment banking services**;
(g) **rendering of outsourced financial services**;
(h) **management services**; and
(i) **any other kind of services as may be prescribed.**

**Meaning of "directly or indirectly" [Explanation to Sec 144]:** The term "directly or indirectly" shall include rendering of services by the auditor —

Separately, the auditor also detects a suspected fraud of ₹80 lakh. **Answer:** The auditor's duty to report fraud is governed by **Section 143(12) of the Companies Act, 2013** read with **Rule 13 of the Companies (Audit and Auditors) Rules, 2014.**

**Statutory duty [Sec 143(12)]:** If an auditor of a company, in the course of performance of his duties as auditor, has **reason to believe that an offence of fraud** involving such amount as may be prescribed **is being or has been committed** in the company by its officers or employees, the auditor shall report the matter as follows:

**Threshold and reporting channel [Rule 13]:**
- **Fraud involving an amount of ₹1 crore or above:** The auditor shall report the matter to the **Central Government** in the following manner — (a) he shall **forward his report to the Board or the Audit Committee**, as the case may be, seeking their reply or observations **within forty-five days**; (b) on receipt of such reply/observations, he shall forward his report, the reply/observations and his comments thereon, to the **Central Government within fifteen days** of receipt of such reply; (c) if he **fails to receive any reply/observations within forty-five days**, he shall forward his report to the Central Government along with a note containing the details of his report that was earlier forwarded to the Board/Audit Committee for which he has not received any reply. **Consequence of non-reporting:** If any auditor does not comply with Section 143(12), he shall be liable to a **penalty** as prescribed.

**Conclusion:** The auditor must report the ₹1.20 crore fraud to the **Central Government in Form ADT-4** through the Board/Audit-Committee procedure (45 days + 15 days), whereas the ₹80 lakh fraud, being below the ₹1 crore threshold, is reported to the **Audit Committee/Board and disclosed in the Board's report** — it is not reported to the Central Government.

*(Full-marks tip: The ₹1 crore dividing line and the "45 days to Board/Audit Committee + 15 days to Central Government in Form ADT-4" sequence are the marks. Handle BOTH frauds separately — reporting the sub-₹1 crore fraud to the Central Government (or vice versa) is the standard error.)*

### Q72. Ch: General Clauses Act 1897 — Distance & Time Computation Applied (Marks: 5) [Application]

Two provisions of the **General Clauses Act, 1897** apply.

**(1) Computation of the limitation period — Section 9 and Section 10.**
Under **Section 9**, in computing a period of time, the word "**from**" excludes the **first** day (the day of the order, 5th September, is excluded) and the word "**to**" includes the **last** day. So the 30-day period, counted from 6th September, expires on **5th October 2025**.

| Effect of last day being a holiday | Sec 10(1) | Act valid if done on next working day → 7th Oct valid |
| When is postal service complete | Sec 27 | On proper addressing, pre-paying & posting; deemed delivered in ordinary course unless contrary proved |

**Conclusion:** The appeal filed on **7th October 2025 is within time** under **Sections 9 and 10** of the General Clauses Act, 1897, and the registry was wrong to reject it. Service by post is deemed complete under **Section 27** on correctly addressing, pre-paying and posting the letter.

### Q76. Ch: LLP Act 2008 — DPIN and Designated Partner Consent (Marks: 5) [Application]

A body corporate as such, therefore, cannot be a designated partner.

| Query | Provision | Answer |

| Ceiling on LLPs | No provision | No maximum limit |

**Conclusion:** Mr. T must **hold a DPIN and give prior consent** (Sec 7(6), 7(3)); the LLP must file his particulars **within 30 days** (Sec 7(4)); there is **no ceiling** on the number of LLPs; and a **body corporate cannot be a designated partner** (Sec 7(1)), though it may nominate an individual.

### Q77. Ch: General Clauses Act 1897 — Effect of Repeal (Marks: 6) [Application]

Also, the repealing Act itself is repealed later — does the Old Act revive? Examine under the General Clauses Act 1897.

**Answer:**
The consequences of repeal are governed by **Section 6 (effect of repeal)** and **Section 7 (revival of repealed enactments)** of the General Clauses Act, 1897.

**Section 6 — Effect of repeal.** Where any Central Act repeals any enactment, then, **unless a different intention appears**, the repeal shall **not** —
(a) revive anything not in force at the time of repeal;
(b) affect the **previous operation** of the repealed enactment or anything duly done or suffered thereunder;
(c) affect any **right, privilege, obligation or liability acquired, accrued or incurred** under the repealed enactment;
(d) affect any **penalty, forfeiture or punishment incurred** in respect of any offence committed against the repealed enactment;
(e) affect any **investigation, legal proceeding or remedy** in respect of such right, obligation, liability, penalty or forfeiture — and any such proceeding may be **continued and enforced** as if the repealing Act had not been passed.

**Conclusion:** Under **Section 6**, Mr. K's offence, the pending penalty proceeding, and the licence granted are all **saved** despite the repeal. ### Q78. Ch: LLP Act 2008 — Extent of Partner's Liability & Designated Partner (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** In "Crest Engineering LLP", partner M, while acting in the course of the LLP's business, negligently causes a loss of ₹8,00,000 to a client. Separately, partner N commits a fraud on a supplier for ₹3,00,000 without the knowledge of the other partners. The client and the supplier both seek to recover from the LLP and from the individual partners. Advise, with reference to Sections 26 to 30 of the LLP Act 2008, the extent of liability of the LLP, of M, of N, and of the innocent partners.

**Answer:**
The liability of an LLP and its partners for the acts of a partner is governed by **Sections 26 to 30 of the LLP Act 2008**.

**Partner as agent — Section 26.** Every partner is, **for the purpose of the business of the LLP, the agent of the LLP**, but **not of the other partners**. This is the foundation of LLP liability.

**LLP's liability for a partner's wrongful act — Section 27(2).** The LLP is **liable** if a partner, **in the course of the business of the LLP or with its authority**, does any wrongful act or omission causing loss or injury to a third person. Accordingly, **M's negligence in the course of business** binds the LLP; the client can recover ₹8,00,000 from the LLP.

**Obligation met out of LLP property — Section 27(4).** The liability of the LLP is met **out of the property of the LLP**.

**Limited liability of partners — Section 28.** A partner is **not personally liable**, directly or indirectly, for an obligation of the LLP solely by reason of being a partner. However, **Section 28(2)** preserves personal liability of a partner **for his own wrongful act or omission**. Thus M remains personally liable for his own negligence, but the innocent partners are **not** personally liable for M's act.

**Unlimited liability in case of fraud — Section 30.** Where the LLP or any of its partners acts **with intent to defraud** creditors or for any fraudulent purpose, the **liability of the LLP and of the partners who acted with intent to defraud is unlimited** for all debts/obligations. But the proviso protects partners who were **unaware** — an LLP is **not liable** if the partner's fraudulent act was **without the knowledge or authority** of the LLP; liability then falls **on the defrauding partner personally**.

| Party | Provision | Extent of liability |
|---|---|---|
| LLP for M's negligence | Sec 27(2), 27(4) | Liable, met out of LLP property (₹8,00,000) |
| M personally | Sec 28(2) | Personally liable for his own wrongful act |
| Innocent partners for M's act | Sec 28(1) | Not personally liable |
| N (fraud without LLP's knowledge) | Sec 30 proviso | Unlimited personal liability of N (₹3,00,000) |
| LLP for N's fraud | Sec 30 proviso | LLP not liable, as fraud was without its knowledge/authority |

**Conclusion:** For **M's negligence** the LLP is liable (Sec 27) out of LLP property, and M is personally liable (Sec 28(2)); the innocent partners are not. For **N's fraud committed without the LLP's knowledge**, N alone bears **unlimited personal liability** under Section 30, and neither the LLP nor the innocent partners are liable.

*(Full-marks tip: Separate the negligence limb (Sec 27/28) from the fraud limb (Sec 30) — examiners reward showing that fraud without the LLP's knowledge attaches unlimited liability only to the wrongdoer. Citing Sec 26 (agent of LLP, not of partners) sets the frame.)*

### Q81. Ch: General Clauses Act 1897 — Powers Conferred Include Power to Amend/Rescind (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain, with reference to the General Clauses Act 1897, the principle that a power to make includes a power to add, amend, vary or rescind. Also state the rule regarding exercise of powers and performance of duties more than once.

Two allied provisions of the **General Clauses Act, 1897** apply.

| Section 14 | Power/duty may be exercised or performed **from time to time**, as occasion requires |

**Conclusion:** Under **Section 21**, a power to make rules, orders or notifications carries the implied power to **amend, vary or rescind** them in the like manner; and under **Section 14**, a statutory power or duty may be **exercised or performed repeatedly, from time to time**, and is not exhausted by one exercise.

### Q82. Ch: LLP Act 2008 — Cessation & Continuing Liability of Designated Partner (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Mr. D was a designated partner of "Summit Textiles LLP". He resigned on 30th June 2025 and gave a notice of his resignation to the LLP. The LLP, however, did not file the notice of his cessation with the Registrar. On 20th August 2025 the LLP entered into a contract with a supplier, "FabricWorld", who dealt with the LLP believing Mr. D was still a designated partner (as the Registrar's records still showed him). FabricWorld now seeks to hold Mr. D liable. Advise, under Sections 24 and 25 of the LLP Act 2008, (a) how and when a partner ceases to be a partner, (b) the effect of failure to file the cessation notice, and (c) whether Mr. D remains liable to FabricWorld.

**Answer:**
The cessation of a partner and its consequences are governed by **Sections 24 and 25 of the LLP Act 2008**.

**(a) How and when a partner ceases — Section 24.** A person may cease to be a partner of an LLP **in accordance with the LLP agreement**, or **in the absence of agreement, by giving 30 days' notice** in writing to the other partners of his intention to resign (Section 24(1)). A person also ceases to be a partner on **death, dissolution of the LLP, being declared of unsound mind, or being adjudged insolvent** (Section 24(2)). Mr. D, having given notice, ceases to be a partner accordingly.

**(b) Registration and notice of cessation — Section 25.** Under **Section 25(1)**, a person who ceases to be a partner is **obliged to inform the Registrar**, and under **Section 25(2)** the LLP must **file a notice of the cessation with the Registrar within 30 days**. Crucially, **Section 25(2) proviso** provides that where a partner ceases to be a partner, notice may be **filed by that former partner himself** if he has reasonable cause to believe the LLP will not do so.

**Effect on third parties — Section 25(2).** The cessation of a partner **does not by itself discharge him** from liability to third parties for acts done **before** his cessation. Further, a former partner **continues to be regarded, in relation to any person dealing with the LLP, as still being a partner** **unless** —
(a) the person has **notice** that the former partner has ceased to be a partner; **or**
(b) **notice of cessation has been delivered to the Registrar**.

**(c) Application to Mr. D.** Here Mr. D resigned on 30th June 2025 and gave notice to the LLP. However, **no notice of cessation was delivered to the Registrar**, and FabricWorld dealt with the LLP on 20th August 2025 **without notice** of his cessation, relying on the Registrar's records. Under Section 25(2), Mr. D is therefore **still to be regarded as a partner qua FabricWorld**.

However, the proviso to Section 25(2) gave Mr. D the right to **file the cessation notice himself**. Since he failed to protect himself and the third party had neither actual notice nor constructive notice via the Registrar, he **remains exposed** to FabricWorld.

| Issue | Provision | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Cessation valid? | Sec 24(1) | Yes, on notice |
| Duty to notify Registrar | Sec 25(2) & proviso | LLP within 30 days; former partner may file himself |
| Liability to FabricWorld | Sec 25(2) | Yes — no notice to third party and no filing with Registrar |

**Conclusion:** Mr. D validly ceased to be a partner under **Section 24**, but because **no notice of cessation was filed with the Registrar** and FabricWorld had **no notice** of the change, Mr. D **continues to be regarded as a partner** in relation to FabricWorld under **Section 25(2)** and **remains liable**. He should have exercised his right under the proviso to file the cessation himself.

*(Full-marks tip: The decisive point is Sec 25(2) — a former partner stays liable to a third party until either the third party has notice OR the Registrar has been notified. Mention the proviso letting the ex-partner file himself; that is the fresh angle examiners reward.)*

The questions are answered by **Sections 5, 3 and the general scheme of the General Clauses Act, 1897**.

|---|---|---|
| (a) Commencement when no date fixed | Sec 5(1) | On date of President's assent → 15th July 2025 |
| (b) "may" vs "shall" | Rule of construction | "may" = permissive/discretionary; "shall" = mandatory (subject to context) |
| (c) Reading a defined term | Sec 3 / interpretation | Same defined meaning throughout, unless repugnant to context |

**Conclusion:** Under **Section 5**, the Coastal Zones Act, 2025 comes into force on **15th July 2025** (date of assent), as no commencement date was fixed. ### Q85. Ch: Interpretation of Statutes — Ejusdem Generis (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** A State "Entertainment Tax Act" levies tax on admission to "any theatre, cinema hall, amusement park, circus or other place of public entertainment." The Revenue Department sought to tax entry tickets to a public library reading-room that charged a nominal fee. The library contended that the general words "or other place of public entertainment" must take their colour from the specific words preceding them and cannot cover a library. Examine the correctness of the library's contention with reference to the rule of Ejusdem Generis and state the conditions for its application.

**Answer:**
The rule of **Ejusdem Generis** (meaning "of the same kind or nature") is a rule of interpretation which lays down that where specific words are followed by general words, the general words are construed as limited to things of the **same kind (genus)** as those specified by the preceding particular words. The general words are not given their widest possible meaning but are restricted to persons or things belonging to the same category or class denoted by the specific words.

The rule proceeds on the presumption that the legislature, having enumerated specific words which form a distinct class, intended the general words that follow to be confined to that same class, otherwise the specific enumeration would be rendered meaningless.

**Conditions for application of Ejusdem Generis** — the rule applies only when **all** of the following are satisfied:
1. The statute contains an **enumeration of specific words**.
2. The specific words constitute a **class or category** (i.e., they have a common genus).
3. That class or category is **not exhausted** by the enumeration.
4. The general words follow the specific words (i.e., general words come **after**, not before, the specific ones).
5. There is **no contrary legislative intention** indicating that the general words were meant to be given a wider meaning.

**Application to the facts:** The specific words — "theatre, cinema hall, amusement park, circus" — form a distinct genus, namely **places of public amusement, diversion or recreation** where people gather to be entertained. The class is not exhausted (e.g., a magic show hall or gaming arcade could fall in it). Therefore the general words "or other place of public entertainment" must be read *ejusdem generis* and confined to places of the same amusement/recreation character. A **public library reading-room**, whose object is study, reference and quiet reading, does **not** partake of the character of amusement or diversion and therefore falls outside the genus.

| Aspect | Specific words | Genus derived | Whether library fits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nature | Theatre, cinema, amusement park, circus | Places of amusement/recreation | No — object is study, not amusement |
| Effect of general words | "other place of public entertainment" | Restricted to amusement places | Library excluded |

**Conclusion:** The library's contention is **correct**. Applying the rule of Ejusdem Generis, the general words "or other place of public entertainment" are limited to places of amusement of the same genus as theatre, cinema, amusement park and circus; a public library reading-room is not such a place, and the levy of entertainment tax on it is unsustainable.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards listing ALL the conditions — especially that the class must NOT be exhausted and that general words must FOLLOW specific words; a common deduction is forgetting the "no contrary intention" condition and merely stating the meaning of the maxim.)*

### Q86. Ch: Interpretation of Statutes — Noscitur a Sociis (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between the rule of "Noscitur a Sociis" and the rule of "Ejusdem Generis". Illustrate how Noscitur a Sociis operates where a single word of doubtful meaning is placed among associated words.

**Answer:**
**Noscitur a Sociis** literally means "it is known by its associates" (or "the meaning of a word is to be judged by the company it keeps"). Under this rule, when a word or phrase is **ambiguous or of doubtful meaning**, its meaning may be ascertained by reference to the **words associated with it** in the statute. Words grouped together are understood to take colour from one another, so that the general drift of the connected words fixes the sense of the doubtful word.

**Ejusdem Generis** is in fact only a **specific application** of the broader Noscitur a Sociis principle. However, the two are distinct in their operation and requirements, as shown below:

| Basis | Noscitur a Sociis | Ejusdem Generis |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | A word is known by its associates | General words follow the same genus as specific words |
| Requires a genus/class? | Not necessary; simply looks at neighbouring words | A distinct genus of specific words is essential |
| Structure needed | Word of doubtful meaning found among associated words | Specific words followed by general words |
| Scope | Wider / general rule | Narrower; a particular species of Noscitur a Sociis |
| Effect | Colours the doubtful word by its company | Restricts general words to the enumerated class |

**Illustration:** In an entry taxing "cushions, mattresses, pillows and other similar articles," the doubtful ambit of "articles" is coloured by its associates (soft furnishing bedding) so that it would not extend to, say, a car seat. Where an Act refers to "books, pamphlets, periodicals and leaflets," the word "leaflets" takes colour from its companions and is confined to reading matter of a like nature, not to advertising handbills of a purely commercial character.

**Conclusion:** While Ejusdem Generis is a narrower rule requiring a genus and a specific-to-general structure, Noscitur a Sociis is the wider principle that a doubtful word takes its meaning from the accompanying words; Ejusdem Generis is a species of Noscitur a Sociis.

*(Full-marks tip: State clearly that Ejusdem Generis is a species/application of Noscitur a Sociis — examiners look for this relationship; the distinction table earns the marks.)*

**Question:** A taxing statute enacted in 1962 used the expression "computing machine." Since 1962, and consistently for four decades, the administrative authorities had understood and applied this expression to cover only mechanical calculators and never modern electronic computers. In 2005 the Department reversed its stand and sought to tax electronic computers under the same expression for years dating back to 1962. Discuss the applicability of the maxim "Contemporanea Expositio est Optima et Fortissima in Lege" and state its limitations.

**Answer:**
The maxim **"Contemporanea Expositio est Optima et Fortissima in Lege"** means that the **best and most powerful way to interpret a document (or statute) is to read it as it would have been read at the time it was made** — i.e., contemporaneous exposition is the best and strongest in law. Under this rule, the meaning attributed to a statutory expression by those who **administered it soon after its enactment**, and the usage/practice that has been **consistently followed over a long period**, furnishes strong evidence of the true meaning of the words, particularly for **old statutes**.

The rationale is that the **contemporary understanding**, and the long, uniform administrative practice built upon it, reflect how the words were understood by the community and the officials at the time of enactment, and courts are slow to disturb a settled construction on which rights and transactions have been founded.

**Limitations of the rule:**
1. It applies with force chiefly to **ancient / old statutes**, and **not** to **modern statutes** which the court can construe itself.
2. It is used only where the language is **ambiguous**; it cannot override **plain and unambiguous** words.
3. Administrative construction, however long, **cannot bind the court** and must yield if it is clearly **erroneous** or contrary to the statute.

**Application to the facts:** The statute is of 1962 (a fairly old enactment). If the expression "computing machine" is regarded as ambiguous, the **uniform administrative understanding for four decades** — that it covered only mechanical calculators — is strong contemporaneous evidence of its meaning as understood in 1962. The Department's **sudden reversal in 2005** to include electronic computers, and its attempt to apply the new meaning retrospectively to 1962, runs contrary to the settled contemporaneous exposition. The rule supports the taxpayers who relied on the long-standing construction.

**Conclusion:** Applying Contemporanea Expositio, the long and consistent administrative understanding of "computing machine" as mechanical calculators is the best guide to its 1962 meaning; the Department cannot, subject to the limitation that the words are ambiguous, unsettle four decades of settled practice by a fresh construction applied backwards.

### Q88. Ch: Interpretation of Statutes — Presumption Against Retrospectivity (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** A newly inserted Section in a fiscal statute, notified on 1st August 2025, provides: "Every dealer shall, in respect of any assessment, furnish reconciliation statements in the prescribed manner." A separate amendment on the same date enhances the rate of penalty for concealment from 100% to 300% of tax. The Department seeks (a) to apply the reconciliation-statement requirement to a pending assessment for an earlier year, and (b) to levy the enhanced 300% penalty for a concealment committed in June 2025. Examine both actions in the light of the presumption against retrospective operation of statutes and the distinction between substantive and procedural law.

**Answer:**
The **presumption against retrospectivity** is a well-settled rule of interpretation which states that a statute is **presumed to be prospective** and not retrospective, unless it is made retrospective **expressly or by necessary implication**. The rule rests on the principle *nova constitutio futuris formam imponere debet non praeteritis* — a new law ought to regulate what is to follow, not the past. It protects **vested rights** and guards against the injustice of changing the legal consequences of acts already done.

The critical distinction is between **substantive** and **procedural** law:

| Basis | Substantive law | Procedural / adjectival law |
|---|---|---|
| Concerns | Creates, defines or takes away rights, liabilities, penalties | Governs the manner/machinery of enforcement (evidence, forms, procedure) |
| Presumption | Strong presumption **against** retrospectivity | Generally **retrospective** — no person has a vested right in procedure |
| Rationale | Protects vested rights | A litigant has no vested right in a particular form of procedure |

Thus, a purely **procedural** amendment ordinarily applies to **pending proceedings** as well, because no one has a vested right in a mode of procedure. But a provision creating or **enhancing a penalty is substantive** — it affects vested rights and imposes a heavier liability — and therefore attracts the presumption against retrospectivity; a penalty is governed by the law **as it stood on the date the offence/default was committed**.

**Application to the facts:**
- **(a) Reconciliation-statement requirement:** This merely prescribes the **manner of furnishing information** in an assessment — it is **procedural / machinery** in character and creates no new liability. Being procedural, it can validly be applied to a **pending assessment** of an earlier year. The Department's action here is **correct**.
- **(b) Enhanced penalty (100% → 300%):** Enhancement of penalty is **substantive**, since it increases the burden for the wrongdoing. The concealment was committed in **June 2025**, when the penalty was 100%. The enhanced rate notified on 1st August 2025, absent express retrospective words, applies **prospectively**. Levying 300% for a June 2025 concealment would be an impermissible retrospective imposition of a heavier penalty. The Department's action here is **incorrect** — only 100% penalty can be levied.

**Conclusion:** Action (a) is valid because the reconciliation requirement is procedural and applies to pending assessments; action (b) is invalid because enhancement of penalty is substantive and, in the absence of express retrospectivity, the penalty is governed by the law in force when the concealment was committed (100%).

*(Full-marks tip: The marks turn on correctly classifying each provision as substantive or procedural and stating that procedure is presumptively retrospective while penalty/liability is presumptively prospective — decide each limb separately and conclude on each.)*

### Q89. Ch: Interpretation of Statutes — Internal Aid: Proviso (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the function of a "Proviso" as an internal aid to interpretation. How does a proviso differ from an "Exception" and from an "Explanation"?

**Answer:**
A **Proviso** is an internal aid to construction which is appended to a main enactment (usually beginning with the words "Provided that…"). The normal function of a proviso is to **carve out an exception** to the main provision to which it is attached — that is, it **qualifies or limits** the generality of the main enactment by withdrawing something which, but for the proviso, would have fallen within the main provision.

The settled rules regarding a proviso are:
1. A proviso is construed in relation to the **main section** to which it is a proviso; it must be read together with the substantive provision.
2. As a rule, a proviso **cannot enlarge** the scope of the main enactment; it deals only with the subject matter of the main provision.
3. The proper function of a proviso is to **except and deal with a case** which would otherwise fall within the general language of the main provision.

The distinction between a Proviso, an Exception and an Explanation is as follows:

| Basis | Proviso | Exception | Explanation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Qualifies/carves out an exception to the main provision | Exempts something that would otherwise be within the enactment | Explains, clarifies or removes doubt about the main provision |
| Typical opening | "Provided that…" | "Except…" / "Nothing… shall apply to…" | "For the removal of doubts…" / "It is hereby clarified…" |
| Effect on scope | Restricts the main provision | Takes a case out of the main provision | Does not ordinarily add to or subtract from the provision; clarifies it |

**Conclusion:** A proviso qualifies the main enactment by carving out an exception from it and must be read with the main provision; it differs from an exception (which exempts) and from an explanation (which merely clarifies without ordinarily curtailing or enlarging the provision).

*(Full-marks tip: State that a proviso cannot normally ENLARGE the main section and must be read with it; the three-way distinction table between proviso/exception/explanation is the mark-scoring core.)*

### Q90. Ch: Interpretation of Statutes — Mischief Rule (Heydon's Case) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** A statute was enacted to curb the "mischief" of unregulated money-lending to farmers at usurious rates. It provided that "no money-lender shall recover interest exceeding the principal." A financier advanced money to a farmer and, styling the transaction as a "sale of standing crop with a right of re-purchase," sought to recover an amount far exceeding the principal, arguing that he was not a "money-lender" and the transaction was not a "loan." Applying the Mischief Rule laid down in Heydon's Case, how should the court construe the statute? State the four considerations under the rule.

**Answer:**
The **Mischief Rule** (also called the rule in **Heydon's Case, 1584**, or the rule of purposive construction) directs the court, when the language of a statute is capable of more than one meaning, to adopt the construction which **suppresses the mischief and advances the remedy** intended by the legislature. The court looks beyond the literal words to the **purpose and object** of the enactment.

Under Heydon's Case, for the true interpretation of a statute **four things** are to be considered:
1. **What was the common law (or the law) before the making of the Act** — i.e., the state of the law before the statute.
2. **What was the mischief and defect** for which the existing law did not provide.
3. **What remedy the legislature has resolved and appointed** to cure the mischief.
4. **The true reason of the remedy** — and the court's duty is then to make such construction as shall **suppress the mischief and advance the remedy**, suppressing subtle inventions and evasions for the continuance of the mischief.

**Application to the facts:**
- *Law before the Act:* Money could be lent to farmers at any rate, with no ceiling on interest recovery.
- *Mischief:* Usurious lending to farmers and their exploitation by recovery of exorbitant interest exceeding principal.
- *Remedy:* A statutory bar on recovery of interest exceeding the principal.
- *True reason:* To protect farmers from being crushed by compounding usurious charges.

The financier's device of dressing up the loan as a "sale of standing crop with re-purchase" is precisely the kind of **subtle invention and evasion** aimed at continuing the mischief. Applying the mischief rule, the court should look at the **substance** of the transaction — which is in reality a secured loan — and construe "money-lender" and "loan" so as to **cover the disguised transaction**, thereby suppressing the mischief and advancing the remedy. The financier cannot recover interest exceeding the principal.

**Conclusion:** Applying the rule in Heydon's Case, the court should treat the transaction according to its true substance (a loan) rather than its assumed form, hold the financier bound by the ceiling, and defeat the device designed to evade the statute — for the rule requires suppression of the mischief and advancement of the remedy.

### Q91. Ch: Interpretation of Statutes — External Aid: Dictionary & Foreign Decisions (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** "External aids may be resorted to only when the language of the statute is not clear." In this light, explain the use and limitations of (i) Dictionaries, and (ii) Foreign decisions, as external aids to the interpretation of a statute.

**Answer:**
**External aids** to construction are materials **outside the four corners of the statute** which the court may consult to ascertain the meaning of statutory words. The settled rule is that external aids are resorted to only where the words of the statute are **ambiguous or not clear**; where the language is plain and unambiguous, effect must be given to it and external aids are not called for.

**(i) Dictionaries:** When a word is not defined in the Act, and its ordinary meaning is required, the court may refer to a **dictionary** to find the general sense in which the word is understood in common parlance. However, the use of dictionaries is subject to important limitations:
- A dictionary gives **all the shades of meaning** of a word, and the court must select the one that fits the **context** and the object of the statute.
- The meaning must be that which the word bears **in the particular context**, not necessarily its widest dictionary meaning.
- In a taxing/commercial statute, the word is generally understood in its **popular or commercial sense** as understood by persons dealing in the trade, rather than its scientific or dictionary sense.

**(ii) Foreign decisions:** Decisions of foreign courts (especially of countries following the same system of jurisprudence, such as England, USA, Australia, Canada) may be referred to for their **persuasive value** and the reasoning they contain. Their limitations are:
- They have **only persuasive value** and are **not binding** on Indian courts.
- They must be applied with **great caution**, as they turn on the language of foreign statutes and different social/constitutional conditions.
- **Indian law and Indian statutory language must always prevail**; a foreign decision cannot override the plain provisions of the Indian statute or binding decisions of Indian courts.

**Conclusion:** Both dictionaries and foreign decisions are legitimate external aids usable only when the statutory language is ambiguous; a dictionary meaning must be chosen to suit the context (often the popular sense), and foreign decisions carry only persuasive, non-binding value to be used with caution.

*(Full-marks tip: Stress that dictionary meaning must fit the context / commercial sense and that foreign decisions are only persuasive and not binding — bald statements without these limitations lose marks.)*

### Q92. Ch: Interpretation of Statutes — Rule of Harmonious Construction (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Two Sections of the same Act appear to conflict. Section X confers on a Tribunal "exclusive jurisdiction to decide all disputes arising under this Act." Section Y provides that "any person aggrieved by an order of the Registrar may appeal to the Civil Court." In a dispute concerning an order of the Registrar, one party invokes Section X to oust the Civil Court, while the other invokes Section Y to approach it. Explain and apply the Rule of Harmonious Construction to resolve the apparent conflict.

**Answer:**
The **Rule of Harmonious Construction** is a rule of interpretation which requires that where there are, in a statute, **two or more provisions which appear to conflict** with each other, they should be interpreted, as far as possible, in such a manner as to **give effect to both** and to avoid a construction that renders either of them **otiose, nugatory or dead**. The presumption is that the legislature does not contradict itself, and that every provision has been enacted for a purpose.

The principles governing harmonious construction are:
1. The court must try to **reconcile** the conflicting provisions and give effect to **both**.
2. A construction which reduces one of the provisions to a **"dead letter"** is not harmonious construction, and should be avoided.
3. Where reconciliation is truly impossible, effect should be given to the provision that reflects the **dominant purpose**; but every attempt must first be made to harmonise.
4. The provision which is **more specific** on a matter generally prevails over the general provision on that matter (*generalia specialibus non derogant*).

**Application to the facts:** Section X is a **general** provision conferring exclusive jurisdiction on the Tribunal over "all disputes arising under this Act." Section Y is a **specific** provision dealing with a particular category — **orders of the Registrar** — for which it provides an appeal to the Civil Court. A harmonious reading gives effect to both: the Tribunal exercises exclusive jurisdiction over the **general body of disputes** under the Act, **except** the specific class of "orders of the Registrar," which the legislature has specially carved out for appeal to the Civil Court under Section Y. Reading Section X to swallow up Section Y would render Section Y a **dead letter**, which harmonious construction forbids.

**Conclusion:** Applying the Rule of Harmonious Construction (aided by *generalia specialibus non derogant*), both provisions are given effect: general disputes go to the Tribunal under Section X, but an order of the Registrar is appealable to the Civil Court under the specific Section Y. Hence the party invoking Section Y is entitled to approach the Civil Court.

*(Full-marks tip: The key marks lie in stating that no provision should be reduced to a dead letter and in using the specific-prevails-over-general (generalia specialibus) principle to reconcile — not merely picking one section.)*

### Q93. Ch: FEMA 1999 — Person Resident in India (Marks: 6) [Application]

| Case | Facts |
|---|---|
| (a) | Mr. A, an Indian citizen, left India on 5th May 2025 to take up permanent employment in Dubai. During FY 2024-25 he had been in India for the whole year. |
| (b) | Mr. B, a German citizen, came to India on 1st June 2025 to take up employment with an Indian company for 3 years. He was in India for only 40 days during FY 2024-25. |
| (c) | XYZ Ltd., a company incorporated in India, has its entire management and operations in Singapore. |
| (d) | A branch, in London, of an Indian company registered in Mumbai. |

**Answer:**
Under **Section 2(v) of FEMA, 1999**, "person resident in India" means, in the case of an **individual**, a person **residing in India for more than 182 days during the course of the preceding financial year**, but does **not** include:
- (A) a person who has gone out of India or who stays outside India, in either case — (i) for or on taking up **employment** outside India, or (ii) for carrying on **business or vocation** outside India, or (iii) for any other purpose, in such circumstances as would indicate his intention to stay outside India for an **uncertain period**; and
- (B) a person who has come to or stays in India, otherwise than — (i) for or on taking up **employment** in India, (ii) for carrying on business/vocation in India, or (iii) in circumstances indicating intention to stay in India for an **uncertain period**.

Section 2(v) further provides that "person resident in India" includes:
- any **person or body corporate registered or incorporated in India**;
- an **office, branch or agency in India** owned or controlled by a person resident outside India; and
- an **office, branch or agency outside India** owned or controlled by a person resident in India.

The test therefore has **two limbs** — the **182-days-in-the-preceding-year** threshold *and* the **purpose/intention** test, which can override the day-count.

**Application:**

| Case | Analysis | Status FY 2025-26 |
|---|---|---|
| (a) Mr. A | Though he was in India for more than 182 days in the preceding FY 2024-25, he has **left India to take up employment abroad**, which falls in the excluding clause (A). His going abroad for employment makes him a person resident **outside** India from the date of departure. | **Non-resident** |
| (b) Mr. B | He was in India only 40 days in preceding FY 2024-25 (fails 182-day test), **but** he has **come to India to take up employment** and intends to stay for 3 years — the purpose/intention limb makes him a person resident in India. | **Resident in India** |
| (c) XYZ Ltd. | It is a **company incorporated in India**; the inclusive part of Section 2(v) makes any body corporate registered/incorporated in India a person resident in India, irrespective of where it is managed. | **Resident in India** |
| (d) London branch | It is an **office/branch outside India owned or controlled by a person resident in India** (the Mumbai company); expressly included as person resident in India. | **Resident in India** |

**Conclusion:** (a) Non-resident; (b) Resident in India; (c) Resident in India; (d) Resident in India.

### Q94. Ch: FEMA 1999 — Person Resident: Deemed 182 Days (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Mr. R, an Indian citizen who had been continuously resident in India, went to Canada on 10th April 2025 to take up a specific 6-month project assignment (a contract of fixed duration), with a firm intention to return to India upon its completion. He returned to India on 15th October 2025. He seeks your advice on his residential status under FEMA, 1999 for the financial year 2025-26, and the treatment of a person who leaves India for a fixed short assignment as distinguished from one who leaves for indefinite employment. Advise him.

Under **Section 2(v)(i) of FEMA, 1999**, an individual is a "person resident in India" if he resided in India for **more than 182 days during the preceding financial year**, subject to the exclusions in sub-clause (A). Sub-clause (A) excludes a person who has gone out of / stays outside India **(i)** for or on taking up employment outside India, **(ii)** for carrying on business or vocation outside India, or **(iii)** for any other purpose, **in such circumstances as would indicate his intention to stay outside India for an uncertain period**.

The decisive factor under FEMA (unlike the mechanical day-count under the Income-tax Act) is the **purpose of, and intention behind, leaving India**. A person who leaves India for a purpose **other than** the three excluding purposes, or who leaves for a **fixed / definite period** with a **clear intention to return**, does **not** fall within the exclusion and continues to be a person resident in India. The exclusion bites only where the departure is for employment/business abroad, or for a purpose indicating an intention to stay away for an **uncertain period**.

- In the preceding financial year (FY 2024-25) Mr. R was continuously in India (more than 182 days) — the basic residence limb is satisfied.

| Basis | Left for indefinite/permanent employment abroad | Left for a fixed short assignment with intent to return |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Employment/business abroad; uncertain-period stay | Definite period; certain return |
| Effect under Sec 2(v)(A) | Becomes person resident OUTSIDE India | Remains person resident IN India |

**Conclusion:** Mr. R **remains a person resident in India** for FY 2025-26, because he left only for a fixed, definite assignment with an intention to return, and did not go abroad for employment/business or for an uncertain period. ### Q97. Ch: FEMA 1999 — Contravention & Penalty (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** M/s Global Traders, a person resident in India, contravened a provision of FEMA, 1999. The **amount involved in the contravention is quantifiable at ₹8,00,000**. The contravention **continued for 30 days** after the initial default. The Adjudicating Authority is examining the quantum of penalty imposable under Section 13(1) of FEMA, 1999. Compute the **maximum penalty** that can be levied and state the position regarding **confiscation** and **imprisonment/civil imprisonment** under FEMA.

**Answer:**
Under **Section 13(1) of FEMA, 1999**, if any person **contravenes** any provision of the Act, or any rule, regulation, notification, direction or order issued in exercise of the powers under the Act, or contravenes any condition subject to which an authorisation is issued by the RBI, he shall, upon adjudication, be liable to a **penalty**:
- **up to thrice the sum involved** in such contravention, **where such amount is quantifiable**; or
- **up to ₹2,00,000**, where the amount is **not quantifiable**; and
- where the contravention is a **continuing one**, a **further penalty which may extend to ₹5,000 for every day** after the first day during which the contravention continues.

**Confiscation — Section 13(2):** Any Adjudicating Authority adjudging any contravention under Section 13(1) may, if it thinks fit, in addition to the penalty, direct that **any currency, security or any other money or property in respect of which the contravention has taken place shall be confiscated** to the Central Government. "Property" here includes deposits, foreign exchange, immovable property and things into which such property has been converted.

**Nature of FEMA — no imprisonment for contravention:** FEMA is a **civil law** (unlike the erstwhile FERA, which was criminal in character). A contravention under FEMA attracts a **monetary penalty** and **not imprisonment**. Imprisonment enters only at the **enforcement/recovery stage**: under **Section 14**, if a person fails to make **full payment of the penalty** within **90 days** from the date of the notice of demand, he is liable to **civil imprisonment** (arrest and detention by the Adjudicating Authority) — this is a coercive measure to recover the penalty, not a punishment for the original contravention.

**Working Note — Computation of maximum penalty:**

| Component | Basis (Sec 13(1)) | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| WN-1: Base penalty (amount quantifiable) | Up to 3 × ₹8,00,000 | 24,00,000 |
| WN-2: Continuing-contravention penalty | ₹5,000 × 30 days | 1,50,000 |
| **Maximum penalty** | WN-1 + WN-2 | **25,50,000** |

Formula: Maximum penalty = (3 × sum involved) + (₹5,000 × number of continuing days) = (3 × 8,00,000) + (5,000 × 30) = 24,00,000 + 1,50,000 = **₹25,50,000**.

**Conclusion:** The **maximum penalty** imposable is **₹25,50,000**. In addition, the Adjudicating Authority may order **confiscation** under Section 13(2). FEMA prescribes **no imprisonment** for the contravention itself; only **civil imprisonment** under Section 14 may follow if the penalty is not paid within 90 days of the demand notice.

### Q98. Ch: FEMA 1999 — Compounding of Contravention (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the provisions relating to "Compounding of Contravention" under Section 15 of FEMA, 1999. Who may compound, within what time must the compounding be concluded, and what is the position regarding a contravention that has been compounded once and thereafter repeated? Also state the effect of compounding on the initiation of proceedings.

**Answer:**
**Compounding** is a mechanism by which a person who has **admitted** to having contravened a provision of FEMA may settle the matter by **paying a specified sum**, thereby avoiding prolonged adjudication and litigation. It is a **voluntary** process initiated on an **application** by the contravener.

Under **Section 15(1) of FEMA, 1999**, **any contravention under Section 13** may be **compounded** by the **Director of Enforcement** or such other officers of the Directorate of Enforcement and officers of the Reserve Bank of India as may be authorised, on an application made by the person committing the contravention. The compounding is done **within 180 days from the date of receipt of the application**.

Under **Section 15(2)**, where a contravention has been **compounded**, **no proceeding or further proceeding**, as the case may be, shall be **initiated or continued** against the person in respect of the contravention **so compounded**. Thus, once a contravention is compounded and the compounded sum is paid, the matter is **closed** and the person cannot be proceeded against for that contravention.

Key features of the compounding scheme (read with the Foreign Exchange (Compounding Proceedings) Rules):
1. **Who may compound:** The Director of Enforcement / authorised officers of the Enforcement Directorate and RBI, on an application by the contravener.
2. **Time limit:** The contravention shall be compounded **within 180 days** of receipt of the application.
3. **Effect:** No proceeding or further proceeding shall be initiated or continued for the compounded contravention (Section 15(2)).
4. **Repeat contravention:** Where a person commits a **similar contravention within a period of 3 years** from the date on which a **similar contravention was previously compounded**, the **second (repeat) contravention shall be treated as a first contravention only if the earlier compounding was more than 3 years ago**; if it is committed **within 3 years**, it is **not compoundable** and shall be dealt with under the adjudication provisions. (Contraventions occurring after 3 years are treated as fresh first contraventions and may be compounded.)

**Conclusion:** A contravention under Section 13 may be compounded, on application, by the Director of Enforcement or authorised RBI/ED officers **within 180 days**; on compounding, **no proceeding is initiated or continued** for that contravention. However, a **similar contravention repeated within 3 years** of an earlier compounding is **not compoundable** and must go through adjudication.

*(Full-marks tip: State the 180-day time limit, the "within 3 years = not compoundable" rule for repeat contraventions, and the Section 15(2) bar on further proceedings — these three points carry the marks; naming the compounding authorities (ED/RBI) rounds it off.)*

### Q99. Ch: FEMA 1999 — Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Mr. K, a resident individual, wishes to make the following remittances during the financial year 2025-26 under the Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS):

| Purpose | Amount (USD) |
|---|---|
| Gift to his non-resident nephew abroad | 40,000 |
| Investment in shares of a foreign company | 60,000 |
| Purchase of a residential property abroad | 1,20,000 |
| Total | 2,20,000 |

Advise Mr. K whether all these remittances can be made under the LRS during FY 2025-26, the overall annual limit, and whether prior approval of the RBI is required. Also state two purposes for which remittance under the LRS is **prohibited**.

**Answer:**
The **Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS)** is a facility, framed by the RBI under FEMA, 1999, under which **all resident individuals**, including minors, are permitted to freely remit up to a specified limit **per financial year** for any **permissible current or capital account transaction**, or a combination of both, **without prior approval of the RBI**.

**Annual limit:** Under the LRS, a resident individual may remit up to **USD 2,50,000 per financial year** (April–March). This overall ceiling applies to the **aggregate** of all permissible remittances (both current and capital account) made by the individual during the year. Remittances **within** this limit do **not** require prior RBI approval; any remittance **exceeding** the limit requires **prior approval of the RBI**.

**Permissible capital account transactions** under the LRS include: opening foreign currency accounts abroad, purchase of **immovable property abroad**, making **investments abroad** (acquisition of shares/securities), and extending loans/gifts to relatives, etc. **Gifts and donations** are permitted **within** the overall LRS limit.

**Purposes prohibited under LRS (any two):**
1. Remittance for purposes **specifically prohibited under Schedule I** of the FEM (Current Account Transactions) Rules — e.g., purchase of **lottery tickets, banned/prohibited magazines**, sweepstakes, etc.
2. Remittance for **margins or margin calls to overseas exchanges/counterparties**.
3. Remittance for the purchase of **Foreign Currency Convertible Bonds (FCCBs)** issued by Indian companies in the overseas secondary market.
4. Remittance for **trading in foreign exchange abroad**, or to countries identified by FATF as **non-cooperative**, or to persons posing terrorism-financing risk.

**Working Note — Aggregation under LRS for FY 2025-26:**

| Purpose | Nature | Amount (USD) |
|---|---|---|
| Gift to non-resident nephew | Permissible (within limit) | 40,000 |
| Investment in foreign shares | Permissible capital a/c | 60,000 |
| Purchase of residential property abroad | Permissible capital a/c | 1,20,000 |
| **Total remittance sought** | | **2,20,000** |
| **LRS annual limit** | | **2,50,000** |

**Application:** All three remittances are for **permissible purposes** under the LRS. Their **aggregate is USD 2,20,000**, which is **within the annual ceiling of USD 2,50,000**. Since the total does not exceed the limit and none of the purposes is prohibited, Mr. K can make **all the remittances under the LRS without prior RBI approval**. He has an unutilised headroom of **USD 30,000** (2,50,000 − 2,20,000) for the year.

**Conclusion:** Mr. K may make **all three remittances (aggregating USD 2,20,000) under the LRS in FY 2025-26 without prior RBI approval**, as they are permissible and within the USD 2,50,000 annual limit; any excess over USD 2,50,000 would have required prior RBI approval.

*(Full-marks tip: Aggregate ALL remittances against the single USD 2,50,000 ceiling (the limit is per individual per year, not per transaction), state that no prior RBI approval is needed within the limit, and give two genuinely prohibited purposes from Schedule I — listing a permitted purpose as prohibited loses marks.)*

### Q100. Ch: FEMA 1999 — Current vs Capital Account Transactions (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Classify each of the following transactions as a "Current Account Transaction" or a "Capital Account Transaction" under FEMA, 1999, giving the statutory basis, and state the general regulatory approach of the Act towards each category:

| Item | Transaction |
|---|---|
| (a) | Payment of interest on a loan taken from a person resident outside India |
| (b) | Acquisition of immovable property outside India by a resident |
| (c) | Remittance for education of a child studying abroad |
| (d) | Borrowing in foreign exchange by a resident from a non-resident (ECB) |
| (e) | Payment towards import of goods |

**Answer:**
**Capital Account Transaction** is defined in **Section 2(e) of FEMA, 1999** as a transaction which **alters the assets or liabilities, including contingent liabilities, outside India of persons resident in India, or assets or liabilities in India of persons resident outside India**, and includes transactions referred to in Section 6(3).

**Current Account Transaction** is defined in **Section 2(j)** as a transaction **other than a capital account transaction**, and (without prejudice to the generality) **includes**: (i) payments due in connection with **foreign trade**, other current business, services, and short-term banking and credit facilities in the ordinary course of business; (ii) payments due as **interest on loans** and as net income from investments; (iii) remittances for **living expenses** of parents, spouse and children residing abroad; and (iv) expenses in connection with foreign travel, **education** and medical care of parents, spouse and children.

**General regulatory approach:**

| Category | Approach under FEMA |
|---|---|
| Current account transactions | **Freely permitted** (Section 5) — any person may sell or draw foreign exchange for a current account transaction, subject only to reasonable restrictions imposed by the Central Government (FEM (Current Account Transactions) Rules — Schedule I prohibited, Schedule II requiring Govt approval, Schedule III within limits) |
| Capital account transactions | **Regulated** (Section 6) — permitted only to the extent, and subject to conditions, specified by the RBI; those not permitted are prohibited |

**Classification of the transactions:**

| Item | Classification | Reason / basis |
|---|---|---|
| (a) Interest on loan from non-resident | **Current account transaction** | Sec 2(j) expressly includes "payments due as interest on loans" |
| (b) Acquisition of immovable property abroad by resident | **Capital account transaction** | Alters assets outside India of a resident — Sec 2(e) r/w Sec 6(3) |
| (c) Remittance for education of child abroad | **Current account transaction** | Sec 2(j) expressly includes expenses for education of children |
| (d) ECB — foreign-currency borrowing by resident from non-resident | **Capital account transaction** | Creates/alters a liability outside India of a resident — Sec 2(e) |
| (e) Payment for import of goods | **Current account transaction** | Payment in connection with foreign trade — Sec 2(j) |

**Conclusion:** (a), (c) and (e) are **current account transactions** (freely permitted under Section 5, subject to restrictions), while (b) and (d) are **capital account transactions** (regulated and permitted only as specified by the RBI under Section 6).

*(Full-marks tip: Anchor each classification to the exact clause of Section 2(j)/2(e), and pair it with the correct regulatory treatment — current = free under Sec 5, capital = regulated under Sec 6; simply labelling without the statutory basis loses half the marks.)*
