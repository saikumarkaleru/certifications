# Chapter 15 — SM: Functional & Digital Strategy

## Snapshot
A corporate/business strategy is only a promise; it becomes real only when translated into concrete plans by each function (marketing, finance, operations, HR, R&D) and those plans are mutually consistent. Strategy is a cascade (down = intent/goals; up = feasibility/constraints), not a one-way memo. In the modern economy, this same discipline must extend to digital strategy — going digital is a corporate/business choice that must cascade into every function, not an IT purchase.

Four failure modes of the cascade: Translation failure (grand words never become measurable targets), Alignment failure (functions contradict each other), Resource failure (money/people/machines never actually reallocated), Feedback failure (top never hears the plan is infeasible).

## Core concepts

Hierarchy of strategy (three levels — each serves and constrains the level below):
- Corporate — "What businesses should we be in?" (scope, portfolio; directs)
- Business (SBU) — "How do we win in each business?" (cost or differentiation; positions)
- Functional — "What must each department do to deliver the win?" (executes)
Rule: if it changes which industry's profit pool you fish in = corporate; how you beat rivals in the same pool = business; how a department supports the fight = functional. Single-business firms collapse corporate and business into one. Below functional sits the operational/tactical layer (day-to-day schedules) — strategy allocates resources across a horizon; operations executes within an allocation already made.

Why the hierarchy exists: (1) different questions need different decision-makers (decentralisation); (2) scope/reversibility differs by level; (3) resources are committed inside functions; (4) consistency must be engineered — each function's default is to optimise its own local metric, and the sum of local optima ≠ global optimum; (5) technology changes constraints (digital can create new strategic options). Not writing one giant plan because of bounded rationality and information cost.

SBU (Strategic Business Unit) — three defining characteristics: (i) a single or related set of businesses plannable separately; (ii) its own set of competitors; (iii) a manager responsible for strategic planning and profit. Test: "Could it be sold off and survive as a standalone business?"

Grand strategy (master/corporate strategy) — Expansion, Stability, Retrenchment, Combination (Glueck & Jauch). Directional, not operational; each function translates it into a functional strategy. Functional strategy is a derivative of the grand strategy.

## Key frameworks / models

Grand strategy → functional posture (change the grand strategy and every arrow flips):

| Function | Expansion | Stability | Retrenchment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Marketing | New markets/products, aggressive promotion | Defend share, refine mix | Prune weak products, harvest |
| Finance | Raise capital, accept leverage, reinvest (low dividend) | Steady, moderate payout | Conserve cash, cut capex, sell assets |
| Operations | Add capacity, new plants/tech | Optimise existing capacity | Close/consolidate plants, cut inventory |
| HR | Hire/train fast, scale | Stable headcount, develop staff | Downsize, redeploy, VRS |
| R&D | Product development, widen range | Incremental improvement | Cut/defer projects, focus on essentials |

The five functional strategies:
- Marketing (demand-side): STP (Segmentation → Targeting → Positioning) precedes the 4 Ps. 4 Ps = Product, Price, Place, Promotion (McCarthy); for services add People, Process, Physical evidence = 7 Ps (Booms & Bitner). Pricing: Skimming (high price; use when demand inelastic, novel/differentiated, imitation hard, recover R&D fast) vs Penetration (low price, volume; use when demand elastic, scale economies large, network effects, deter entry). Push (through channel, trade incentives) vs Pull (end-customer demand, advertising/branding). Must be consistent with business strategy.
- Finance (resource-supply): capital structure (debt-equity mix), financing decisions, investment/capital budgeting, dividend policy, working capital management. Enabler + scorekeeper (ROI, ROCE, EVA). Value check: EVA = NOPAT − (Capital × WACC). Growth is not automatically good — only growth earning above the cost of capital creates value.
- Operations (supply-side): capacity, process choice (job → batch → mass → continuous), facilities/layout, supply chain (make vs buy), quality (TQM, Six Sigma), inventory (JIT vs buffer). Efficiency↔flexibility trade-off (moving right = more efficient, less flexible). Order-winner vs order-qualifier (Hill): qualifier = minimum to be considered; order-winner = what clinches the sale. Where cost and quality are born.
- HR (people plan): manpower planning, recruitment/selection, training/development, performance appraisal & rewards, retention/culture/change management. Strategy-culture bridge; usually the binding constraint on speed (skills/values can't be installed overnight); reward system is the sharpest lever ("what gets measured and paid gets done").
- R&D (future-supply): innovation posture — first mover/pioneer vs follower/imitator; product vs process R&D; make vs buy technology; R&D intensity. First-mover advantages: technological leadership, pre-emption of scarce assets, buyer switching costs, set industry standards. Disadvantages: high R&D/market-education cost, uncertainty, free-rider imitators. Rule: pioneer where advantage is defensible (patents, network effects, standards); follow where imitation is cheap and market proven.

Three fits (alignment test):
1. Vertical fit — advances the business/grand strategy above.
2. Horizontal fit — consistent with other functions (marketing's delivery dates match operations' capacity).
3. Internal fit — sub-decisions consistent (premium price + cheap packaging = mismatch).

McKinsey 7S (Peters, Waterman & Pascale) — total-alignment diagnostic:
- Hard Ss: Strategy, Structure, Systems (tangible, easier to change).
- Soft Ss: Style, Staff, Skills, Shared Values (cultural, hard to change, decide execution).
- Shared Values at the centre (anchors the other six; change last). Managers over-invest in hard Ss, under-invest in soft — why restructurings fail. Cannot change strategy without changing supporting functional Ss.

Digital depth ladder (data → process → business model):
- Digitisation — analog information → digital data (paper records → database).
- Digitalisation — digital tech improves an existing process (online order form replaces phone).
- Digital transformation — digital reinvents the business model/value proposition (selling scooters → battery-swap-as-a-service).

Drivers of digital transformation (why adapt): (1) changing customer expectations; (2) competitive pressure/disruption from digital-natives (Kodak problem); (3) technology availability & falling cost (cloud/AI/analytics rentable); (4) data as a strategic asset; (5) globalisation/connectivity; (6) operational efficiency/agility. Deep reason: digital changes the basis of competition — old advantages become liabilities.
Barriers (why hard): (i) legacy systems; (ii) cultural resistance/fear of job loss; (iii) skill gaps in data/AI talent; (iv) cybersecurity/data-privacy risk; (v) high upfront investment, uncertain payback; (vi) change-management fatigue.

E-business models:
- By parties: B2C, B2B, C2C, C2B, B2G/B2A.
- By revenue logic: e-tailer/online store (owns inventory, product margin); marketplace/platform (connects buyers-sellers, commission, network effects, neutral venue, seller's brand shows); subscription/SaaS (recurring fee); advertising/freemium; aggregator (one common brand + set quality standards, e.g. Ola/Oyo/Zomato); on-demand/gig; brokerage/transaction fee.
- Aggregator vs marketplace: both asset-light intermediaries. Marketplace = neutral venue, seller's own brand shows. Aggregator = fragmented providers under one common brand and quality standard; providers are partners not employees, aggregator doesn't own assets, sets quality/pricing, single unified brand.
- Network effects → winner-takes-most moat; new rivals face the cold-start (chicken-and-egg) problem; spend early to reach critical mass.

Emerging technologies as strategic levers (cross-functional, not IT-only):

| Technology | What it does | Strategic use |
|---|---|---|
| Cloud computing | Rent computing on demand | Capex → opex; instant scale; global reach |
| Big Data & Analytics | Insight from large data | Demand prediction, personalisation, risk mgmt |
| AI/ML | Machines that learn & decide | Automation, forecasting, chatbots, fraud detection; learns/improves with data |
| IoT | Connected sensors on assets | Real-time monitoring, predictive maintenance, pay-per-use |
| Blockchain | Distributed tamper-proof ledger | Trust without intermediaries, supply-chain traceability |
| Robotics/RPA | Repetitive rule-based work | Lower cost, higher speed; RPA does NOT learn |
| Mobile & Social | Ubiquitous connected customers | Direct relationships, new channels, community |

Why strategic: change the cost curve, change customer expectations, create compounding advantages (data/AI flywheel), destroy old advantages. RPA vs AI: RPA follows fixed rules for structured tasks (no learning); AI/ML handles unstructured/judgemental tasks and improves with data.

## Exam traps & must-remember
- Confusing the three levels — "advertise on Instagram?" is functional, not corporate.
- Digital transformation ≠ buying software/an app (that's digitalisation); transformation changes the business model.
- Digitisation vs digitalisation vs digital transformation — data → process → business model.
- Forgetting horizontal fit — state cross-function consistency explicitly.
- 4 Ps are Product, Price, Place, Promotion (McCarthy) — NOT "Packaging". Services' extra three = People, Process, Physical evidence (Booms & Bitner).
- 7S soft Ss = Style, Staff, Skills, Shared Values; hard = Strategy, Structure, Systems; Shared Values at centre.
- Emerging tech is cross-functional, not R&D-only (AI serves marketing, operations, finance, HR).
- Marketplace (no inventory, commission, seller's brand) ≠ e-tailer (owns inventory, margin) ≠ aggregator (one common brand, set quality). C2B ≠ B2C — direction matters.
- Cascade is a loop, not a one-way waterfall — functions feed feasibility back up.
- "Why adapt?" answered weakly — name the strategic drivers AND barriers; deep reason = digital changes the basis of competition.
- Don't praise digital/growth without a value check — test EVA > 0 (return beats WACC).
- Two computed options (skimming vs penetration, e-tailer vs marketplace) — marks are in the strategic reasoning, not the bigger number.
- Grand strategy flips every functional arrow — don't give expansion-style plans for a retrenchment scenario.
- Rule-based automation (RPA) is not AI — RPA doesn't learn.

Connections: Functional strategy = Porter's generic strategies made operational; maps onto Porter's Value Chain (primary + support activities); Ansoff's market/product development lands on marketing/R&D; 7S links to implementation & structure; finance-as-scorekeeper links to strategic control (Balanced Scorecard: financial, customer, internal-process, learning); BCG portfolio decisions become each SBU's financial constraint; financial strategy is the clearest SM↔FM bridge.

## One-line recall
- Corporate directs, business positions, functional executes — cascade down (intent), feedback up (feasibility).
- Five functional strategies: Marketing (STP then 4/7 Ps), Finance (structure/budgeting/dividend/WC; EVA), Operations (efficiency↔flexibility, order-winner), HR (binding constraint, reward lever), R&D (pioneer vs follower).
- Three fits = vertical + horizontal + internal; 7S hard = Strategy/Structure/Systems, soft = Style/Staff/Skills/Shared Values (centre).
- Digital ladder: digitisation (data) → digitalisation (process) → digital transformation (business model).
- E-models: B2C/B2B/C2C/C2B/B2G; e-tailer vs marketplace vs aggregator; network effects → winner-takes-most.
- Change the grand strategy and every functional arrow flips; digital transformation must itself cascade and clear the cost-of-capital hurdle.
