# SM Chapter 13 — Strategic Choices

## Snapshot
Finite resources force a firm to choose a direction — every "yes" is many implicit "no"s (strategy = what NOT to do). Two nested questions: (1) Corporate level = scope (which businesses; grow/hold/shrink); (2) Business level = positioning (how to beat rivals in one industry). Below sits (3) Functional level (how each department implements the business choice). The three levels must be vertically consistent. Frameworks are graded by a single risk-vs-familiarity gradient: the more that stays known, the lower the risk.

## Core concepts

**Three levels of strategy:** Corporate (which businesses) → Business (how to compete in each) → Functional (marketing/finance/operations/HR/R&D support the chosen way of competing). A "marketing head" question = functional. Must be vertically consistent (differentiation + starved R&D = contradiction).

**Four grand corporate directions:**
- **Stability** — maintain current position; least risky; NOT "doing nothing" (a conscious status-quo choice; firm still improves and defends). Danger = complacency. Sub-types: no-change (settled cruising, monitor); profit/harvesting (sacrifice long-term for short-term profit, often pre-exit); pause/proceed-with-caution (deliberate temporary rest AFTER fast growth, to consolidate before growing again — a comma, not a full stop).
- **Expansion (Growth)** — substantial growth in products/markets/scale. Reasons: survival (static share erodes in growing industry), profitability & drive, prestige/psychological (empire-building — dangerous), economies of scale. Trap: uncontrolled growth beyond capacity ("over-trading") causes later distress.
- **Retrenchment** — pull back to a defensible core; chosen under distress; NOT defeat (surgery to save the patient). Escalating forms:
  - **Turnaround** — stay in business, radically overhaul to reverse decline (intention = recovery). Elements: change leadership, redefine focus, divest/curtail unwanted assets, improve profitability of retained ops, careful complementary acquisitions. Warning signs: negative cash flow, falling margins/share, adverse debt-equity, deteriorating working capital, high employee turnover, mounting inventory.
  - **Divestment** — sell/spin off a unit as a going concern (failed turnaround often ends here).
  - **Liquidation** — close down, sell assets piecemeal; last resort; LEAST attractive (admits failure, damages reputation, destroys going-concern value). Divestment preferred whenever a buyer exists.
- **Combination** — different postures across different units, simultaneous or sequential (e.g. grow software, hold steady in steel, retrench telecom). Only available to multi-business/multi-region/multi-product firms. If a case gives several SBUs different treatment → label = combination.

**Expansion modes (separate axis from Ansoff direction):** organic; merger; acquisition/takeover; joint venture (jointly-owned new entity, share risk / local partner abroad); strategic alliance (looser, non-equity); internationalisation (really market development elevated by added political/currency/cultural risk). Direction (which products/markets) vs mode (alone vs partner) are two different axes.

**Diversification (4th Ansoff cell):**
- **Related (concentric)** — new business shares a link (technology, production skills, distribution, customers, brand). Logic = synergy (2+2=5). Lower risk, more defensible.
- **Unrelated (conglomerate)** — no operational link; enter attractive industry. Logic = financial (spread risk, deploy surplus cash, general management skill). Higher risk. Often value-destroying.
- Four synergy types: **Marketing** (brand, distribution, sales force, advertising); **Operating** (shared plants, technology, procurement, spread fixed costs); **Investment** (shared plant, machinery, R&D, inventories, tooling); **Management** (know-how transferred to similar problems).
- Sharp critique: pure risk-spreading is NOT a valid corporate justification — investors diversify more cheaply themselves (conglomerate discount = head-office cost, cross-subsidy, opacity). Diversification must add what the investor cannot replicate.

**Integration (subset of expansion — grow along industry's own value system):**
- **Vertical** — into own supply chain. Backward/upstream = towards supplier (secure inputs, control quality, capture margin). Forward/downstream = towards customer (control route to market, capture retailer margin, customer info).
- **Horizontal** — acquire/merge same-stage rivals (market share, scale, less competition, wider line).
- Costs/risks: raises fixed costs & reduces flexibility (locked into in-house supplier); demands new competences (can be unrelated diversification in disguise); dulls incentives (captive supplier complacent); balancing problem (upstream/downstream scales mismatch). Counter-trend = outsourcing / de-integration. Horizontal integration triggers competition (anti-trust) law scrutiny — India: Competition Commission clearance above thresholds ("appreciable adverse effect on competition").

## Key frameworks / models

**Ansoff Product-Market Matrix (Igor Ansoff, 1957)** — where to grow, rising risk:

| | Existing market | New market |
|---|---|---|
| **Existing product** | Market Penetration (lowest risk) | Market Development |
| **New product** | Product Development | Diversification (highest risk) |

- Penetration: sell more of same to same (usage frequency, win rivals' customers, convert non-users, price, advertising). Has a natural ceiling → forces firms up the gradient.
- Market Development: existing product to new geographies/segments/channels/uses (NOT just "going abroad").
- Product Development: new products/variants to existing loyal customers.
- Diversification: new on both axes, highest risk.
- Market vs product development both "moderate"; relative risk depends on where the firm's competence and environment's uncertainty lie.

**Porter's Generic Strategies (Michael Porter, 1980)** — how a single business beats rivals; 2 choices: source (low cost vs differentiation) × scope (broad vs narrow):

| | Broad market | Narrow market |
|---|---|---|
| **Low cost** | Cost Leadership | Cost Focus |
| **Uniqueness** | Differentiation | Differentiation Focus |

- **Cost Leadership** — lowest-COST producer (not necessarily lowest price; cost is the weapon). Sources: economies of scale, tight cost control, efficient ops, low-cost inputs, standardisation, experience curve. Defends vs 5 forces: survive price wars (rivals); buyers push only to next-efficient rival's floor; volume gives supplier power; scale = entry barrier; low price beats substitutes. Requirement: product must stay acceptable/comparable to buyers.
- **Differentiation** — perceived unique across industry (quality, design, brand, features, service, technology) → price premium → loyalty, lower price sensitivity. Economic condition: profitable ONLY if premium buyers will pay EXCEEDS extra cost of distinctiveness. Failure modes: over-differentiation (more than buyers pay for); uniqueness buyers can't perceive/verify. Vulnerable to imitation and downturns.
- **Focus (Niche)** — narrow segment served exceptionally well, via cost focus OR differentiation focus (not both). Works because broad rivals serve the "average" customer, leaving segments under- or over-served. Evaporates if niche needs converge with mainstream or niche too small.
- **Stuck in the Middle** — try both cost + differentiation broadly → achieve neither → below-average profit (beaten on price by cost leader, on value by differentiator; underlying activities structurally conflict). Exception (Porter conceded): a few firms briefly achieve both (e.g. process innovation cheaper AND better) — usually temporary. A focuser can also get stuck-in-the-middle within its niche.

**BCG Growth-Share Matrix (Boston Consulting Group, 1970)** — which multi-business units to fund/milk/cut. Axes: market growth rate (vertical = cash NEEDED, high/low line ≈ 10%) × relative market share (horizontal = cash GENERATED, line at 1.0×).
- **RMS = SBU's own share ÷ largest rival's share.** >1.0 = leader (high side); <1.0 = follower. Reversing the ratio flips every quadrant.

| Quadrant | Growth / Share | Strategy |
|---|---|---|
| **Star** | Hi / Hi | Invest to hold leadership (tomorrow's cash cow) |
| **Cash Cow** | Lo / Hi | Milk; surplus funds Stars & Question Marks |
| **Question Mark** (Problem Child) | Hi / Lo | Invest selectively to build into Star, or divest |
| **Dog** | Lo / Lo | Divest or liquidate |

- Cash-flow story: cows fund stars & selected question marks; cut dogs. Dynamic: success sequence (QM → Star → Cash Cow); disaster sequence (Star → QM → Dog, or over-milked cow leaves only dogs). Balance across the life cycle, not just today's cash.
- Limitations: only 2 crude variables; share alone drives profitability; "market" ambiguous; "Dog" over-condemns.

**GE/McKinsey Nine-Cell Matrix (GE & McKinsey, early 1970s)** — same decision, richer axes. Industry (market) Attractiveness (growth, size, profitability, competitive intensity, regulation) × Business (competitive) Strength (share, brand, cost position, technology, quality). Each axis = weighted composite (weights sum to 1.00; rate each factor 1–5; weight × rating, sum). 3×3 grid scored High/Med/Low.
- Green zone (attractive + strong) = Invest/Grow; yellow diagonal = Selectivity/Hold/Earn; red (weak + unattractive) = Harvest/Divest.
- BOTH axes must be high to justify aggressive investment (an attractive industry alone does not). Weakness: subjective weighting.

| Feature | BCG | GE/McKinsey |
|---|---|---|
| Cells | 4 (2×2) | 9 (3×3) |
| Vertical axis | Market growth (single) | Industry attractiveness (composite) |
| Horizontal axis | Relative market share (single) | Business strength (composite) |
| Driver | Cash flow / experience curve | Attractiveness × ability to compete |
| Strength | Simple, vivid cash logic | Richer, finer resolution |
| Weakness | 2 crude variables; Dog over-condemns | Subjective weights; judgement-heavy |

## Exam traps & must-remember
1. Stability ≠ doing nothing (active status-quo choice). Distinguish pause (rest before more growth) from no-change (settled cruising).
2. Don't confuse levels: "compete against a rival" → Porter; "which businesses to invest in" → BCG/GE; marketing manager's strategy → functional.
3. Cost leadership = lowest COST, not necessarily lowest price; product must stay acceptable.
4. Integration (own value system) ≠ diversification (new business). Backward/forward = vertical; same-stage = horizontal. Backward into a very different upstream can be unrelated diversification in disguise.
5. Ansoff risk order: Penetration (lowest) → Market/Product Development (moderate) → Diversification (highest). Market development includes new segments/channels/uses, not just abroad.
6. "Stuck in the middle" = trying to be both cost leader AND differentiator broadly → neither (activities conflict); not merely "a weak firm." Focusers can get stuck too.
7. BCG axes: vertical = growth (cash needed); horizontal = relative market share (cash generated). Dog = low-growth AND low-share.
8. RMS = own share ÷ LARGEST rival's share, line at 1.0×. Not raw share, not rival÷yours. 18% own share can be a low-share follower (rival 30% → 0.60×).
9. BCG (4 cells, single-factor axes) vs GE (9 cells, weighted multi-factor composites). "Weights/ratings/nine cells" → GE.
10. Related diversification's rationale = synergy (name the type); unrelated = financial. Don't attribute operational synergy to unrelated diversification.
11. Retrenchment ≠ failure. Order: turnaround → divestment → liquidation (least attractive, last resort).
12. Attractive industry alone does NOT justify investment on GE — own business strength must also be high (else Selectivity/Hold, not green).
13. BCG is dynamic, not static (success vs disaster sequence). A portfolio of only cash cows with no stars/question marks is strategically bankrupt.
14. Diversification is one Ansoff cell / one expansion mode, not a synonym for growth.

## One-line recall
- Corporate directions (4): Stability, Expansion, Retrenchment (turnaround → divestment → liquidation), Combination.
- Ansoff (1957): Penetration → Market Dev → Product Dev → Diversification (rising risk); related diversification = synergy (marketing/operating/investment/management).
- Porter (1980): Cost Leadership / Differentiation / Focus; refuse to choose = stuck in the middle. Differentiation pays only if premium > added cost AND uniqueness survives imitation.
- BCG (1970): Star (invest) / Cash Cow (milk) / Question Mark (selective/exit) / Dog (divest); RMS = own ÷ largest rival, line 1.0×; cows fund stars & question marks.
- GE/McKinsey (early 1970s): Industry Attractiveness × Business Strength (weighted composites), 9 cells; BOTH must be high to invest aggressively.
- Spine: finite resources force a choice → corporate scope + business positioning → refusing to choose loses.
