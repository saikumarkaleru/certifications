# Chapter 12 — SM: Analysis of the Internal Environment

## Snapshot

External analysis tells you what is *attractive*; internal analysis tells you what is *achievable*. Two firms facing the same environment pick different strategies because of what lies inside them. This chapter is the **Resource-Based View (RBV)** half of strategy: advantage comes from *what you own* (resources rivals cannot copy), not just *where you sit* (positioning school). The two schools are **complements, not rivals** — the RBV exists because positioning could not explain why two firms in identical positions earn differently. Payoff = the *inside-out* view of sustainable competitive advantage.

Three sub-questions: (1) What do we have? (2) Which of these is genuinely special? (3) Where in our activities is value created or destroyed?

## Core concepts

**Resource** = any asset (tangible or intangible) a firm owns/controls and can *deploy* to conceive and execute strategy. Definition = deployability toward strategy, NOT accounting recognition (reputation is a resource but off the balance sheet).

Three families:
- **Tangible** — physical, on balance sheet: plant, machinery, land, cash, raw-material stock, distribution hardware.
- **Intangible** — non-physical, often off balance sheet: brand/reputation, patents & trademarks, proprietary technology, culture, customer relationships, know-how.
- **Human** — skills/knowledge in people: managerial talent, engineering expertise, worker productivity, tacit skills.

Key point: **intangibles are usually the stronger source of advantage** — invisible, hard to value, cannot be bought, must be built over years; that inimitability is what makes them precious. **Financial resources (cash)** are a legitimate tangible resource (deployable to buy others) but rarely a source of advantage — capital is mobile and non-rare ⇒ usually **Valuable but not Rare = parity**.

**Capability (competence)** = capacity to deploy resources in a *coordinated* way to a desired end. Resides in routines, processes, organisational glue — not a single asset. Built by learning and repetition; cross-functional. Resources = nouns; capabilities = verbs; advantage lives in the verbs. An idle resource earns nothing.

- **Threshold capabilities** — minimum needed just to be in the game (bank clears cheques). Ceiling = parity; absence = fatal weakness.
- **Distinctive / core capabilities** — let the firm *outperform* rivals. Advantage lives only here. Mistake = over-investing in threshold capabilities.
- **Dynamic capability** — higher-order ability to *reconfigure, renew, rebuild* ordinary capabilities as the environment shifts (sense, seize, transform). Answer to the core-rigidity trap. If a stem says "turbulent / rapidly changing environment," this is the concept being tested.

**Core rigidity** — yesterday's core competence hardens into today's trap when the world changes (Kodak film chemistry blocked its digital pivot). The *more* inimitable the competence, the *harder* to abandon. So internal analysis is a continuous audit, never a one-time verdict.

## Key frameworks / models

### Core competence — Prahalad & Hamel (HBR, 1990), 3 tests
1. **Access to a wide variety of markets** — transferable, underpins many products (Honda engines → cars, bikes, generators, mowers).
2. **Significant contribution to perceived customer benefits** — customers value it.
3. **Difficult for competitors to imitate** — complex, tacit, built over time.

Tree metaphor: **core competencies = roots; core products = trunk; business units = branches; end products = leaves/fruit.** Think of the firm as a *portfolio of competencies*, not just businesses. "Core" = strategic label (central + rare + inimitable + transferable), NOT a synonym for "essential/important" (payroll is important, not core).

**Ladder of narrowing scope:**
| Term | Meaning | Test |
|---|---|---|
| Competence / capability | Any coordinated ability to deploy resources | Just needs to exist |
| Core competence | Central, transferable, valued, hard to imitate | Prahalad & Hamel's 3 tests |
| Distinctive competence | Core competence performed *better than all rivals* | Core + superiority |
| Competitive advantage | Superior value/profit the market rewards | Above-average returns |

Anchor: *a core competence becomes distinctive when it beats rivals; a distinctive competence becomes competitive advantage when the market rewards it.*

### VRIO / VRIN — Jay Barney (analytical heart of RBV)
Original = **VRIN** (Valuable, Rare, Inimitable, Non-substitutable); Barney later reframed the 4th filter as **Organisation** ⇒ **VRIO**. Cumulative gates — must clear each in turn:

| Filter | Question | If NO ⇒ |
|---|---|---|
| **V — Valuable** | Exploits opportunity / neutralises threat, improves efficiency or effectiveness? | Competitive **disadvantage** |
| **R — Rare** | Held by only a few (ideally one) rivals? | Competitive **parity** |
| **I — Inimitable** (costly to imitate) | Would rivals face significant cost disadvantage to obtain/develop it? | **Temporary** advantage |
| **O — Organised** to exploit (VRIO) / **N — Non-substitutable** (VRIN) | Firm has structure/systems/processes to capture value? / No equivalent substitute exists? | Unrealised / substitutable |

**All pass ⇒ Sustained competitive advantage.**

**Why inimitability is the linchpin** (isolating mechanisms):
- **Unique historical conditions** — path in time that cannot be rerun (first-mover, founder's vision).
- **Causal ambiguity** — even the firm cannot articulate why it is good; rivals can't copy what nobody can specify (culture).
- **Social complexity** — rests on interpersonal relationships, trust, reputation.

**O** = "so what — can you actually use it?" Structure, controls, culture, rewards must convert potential into realised advantage. **N** guards against a rival reaching the *same customer benefit by a different route* (inimitable canal beaten by a railway). Modern practice: quote **VRIO** primary, name **non-substitutability** if the question raises substitutes.

**Action per verdict:**
| Verdict | Exits at | Do |
|---|---|---|
| Disadvantage | Fails V | Fix, outsource, or exit |
| Parity | Passes V, fails R | Maintain at efficient cost; don't over-invest |
| Temporary advantage | Passes V,R; fails I | Exploit fast; build a durable asset before rivals copy |
| Unrealised advantage | Passes V,R,I; fails O | Fix structure/systems, not the resource |
| Sustained advantage | Passes all | Protect and deepen |

**V is not permanent** — re-test against the *current* environment. Kodak's film mastery stayed rare and inimitable but the V-gate flipped to No ⇒ straight to disadvantage. Formal RBV statement of core rigidity.

### Value Chain — Michael Porter (Competitive Advantage, 1985)
VRIO says *whether* you have advantage; the value chain says *where* it comes from. Disaggregates the firm into **nine strategically relevant activities**. **Margin = total value created − total cost of activities.**

**Primary activities** (create & deliver the product):
| Activity | Covers |
|---|---|
| Inbound logistics | Receiving, storing, distributing inputs; material handling, warehousing, inventory |
| Operations | Transform inputs into product; machining, assembly, packaging |
| Outbound logistics | Store & distribute finished goods; order processing, delivery |
| Marketing & sales | Inform buyers, induce purchase; advertising, pricing, channels, sales force |
| Service | Maintain/enhance value after sale; installation, repair, training, spares |

**Support (secondary) activities:**
| Activity | Covers |
|---|---|
| Firm infrastructure | General management, planning, finance, accounting, legal, quality |
| Human resource management | Recruiting, training, developing, compensating personnel |
| Technology development | R&D, process automation, product design, know-how |
| Procurement | The *function* of purchasing inputs (distinct from inbound logistics = physical handling) |

**How to use:** (1) identify each activity + cost; (2) diagnose sources of advantage; (3) examine **linkages** (interdependencies — better inspection in Operations cuts Service cost later; one fix heals two wounds); (4) benchmark vs rivals' chains. The chain sits inside a larger **value system** (suppliers ↔ firm ↔ channels/buyers); advantage often lives in managing interfaces (JIT).

**Two lenses on one chain:** *cost drivers* (scale, capacity utilisation, linkages, location, timing) for **cost leadership**; *uniqueness drivers* for **differentiation**. Cannot maximise both in the same activity ⇒ "stuck in the middle" warning. **Margin ≠ accounting profit** — it is buyer's willingness-to-pay minus total activity cost. For **service firms**, keep the nine categories but *re-interpret* them (Operations = core service delivery; inbound/outbound light or informational).

### SWOT and TOWS
- **Strengths / Weaknesses** = *internal* (output of resource/capability/competence/value-chain analysis). Test = ownership + origin (does the firm control it, inside its boundary), NOT good-vs-bad.
- **Opportunities / Threats** = *external* (arise in the environment, apply to all players).

Worked test: "government EV subsidy" = **Opportunity** (external, open to all); "our EV battery patents" = **Strength** (internal, ours). A fact can be a strength AND a weakness at once (deep specialisation = world-class competence + dangerous dependence/rigidity).

SWOT is a *list, not a strategy*. **TOWS Matrix — Heinz Weihrich** (name reverses SWOT) *pairs* internal with external to generate options:

| | Strengths (S) | Weaknesses (W) |
|---|---|---|
| **Opportunities (O)** | **SO — Maxi-Maxi**: strengths seize opportunities (aggressive growth) | **WO — Mini-Maxi**: overcome weaknesses to grab opportunities (turnaround) |
| **Threats (T)** | **ST — Maxi-Mini**: strengths counter threats (defend/diversify) | **WT — Mini-Mini**: minimise weaknesses, avoid threats (defensive/retrench) |

**SWOT limitations:** static snapshot; unweighted/unprioritised; same item argued into different boxes; long lists that paralyse. TOWS fixes "list not strategy" but still doesn't rank which family to pursue first.

### Framework roll-call (author + tool)
| Framework | Author | Answers |
|---|---|---|
| Resource-Based View | Wernerfelt; developed by Barney | *Why* look inside |
| Core competence | Prahalad & Hamel (1990) | Which capabilities *define* the firm |
| VRIO / VRIN | Jay Barney | *Whether* a resource yields sustainable advantage |
| Value Chain | Michael Porter (1985) | *Where* value & cost arise |
| SWOT | Traditional (Learned/Andrews) | *Collate* internal + external |
| TOWS Matrix | Heinz Weihrich | *Combine* into strategies |

### Examinable pipeline
Resources (tangible/intangible/human) → Capabilities & competencies → Core competence (Prahalad-Hamel 3 tests) → VRIO/VRIN screen → Value chain map (locate advantage & leaks) → List S & W → Combine with O & T in TOWS → feasible strategy. **Self-check: every strategy must trace back to a specific VRIO-passing resource matched to a real external factor.**

## Exam traps & must-remember

1. **Resource vs capability** — resource = thing you *have*; capability = something you can *do*. "Skilled engineers" (resource) vs "design a car in 18 months" (capability).
2. **Not every strength is an advantage** — most are only *parity*. Always run through VRIO first.
3. **Imitable resource ⇒ only temporary advantage** (patents expire, tech reverse-engineered). State the *type* of advantage.
4. **Never drop the O** in VRIO — un-exploited advantage yields nothing.
5. **VRIN vs VRIO** — N = Non-substitutability; O = Organisation. Barney's later, more-cited version = VRIO.
6. **Procurement (support = buying function) ≠ Inbound logistics (primary = physical handling).**
7. **Firm infrastructure, HRM, Technology development, Procurement = support**, even Technology development in a tech firm (develops know-how; Operations uses it).
8. **Don't stop at SWOT** — marks are in the TOWS SO/ST/WO/WT combination.
9. **Don't swap TOWS cells** — SO = Maxi-Maxi (aggressive); WT = Mini-Mini (defensive).
10. **Attribute the right author** — Core competence = Prahalad & Hamel; VRIO/VRIN = Barney; Value Chain = Porter; TOWS = Weihrich.
11. **Internal vs external misfiling** — test = origin & control, not favourable/unfavourable. A subsidy = external Opportunity; your patent on it = internal Strength.
12. **"Core" ≠ "important"** — must be central + rare + inimitable + transferable (passes the 3 tests).
13. **"Valuable" is not permanent** — re-test V against current environment; disruptive tech stem ⇒ former strength fails V and demands dynamic capabilities.
14. **Threshold vs distinctive** — a threshold capability can never be an edge no matter how well done.
15. **Don't force Porter's manufacturing chain on a service firm** — re-interpret the nine activities.
16. **Margin ≠ P&L gross margin** — it is willingness-to-pay minus total activity cost.

## One-line recall

- Same industry, different profits ⇒ the gap is internal ⇒ RBV; positioning (outside) and resource-based (inside) are complements.
- Resources (tangible/intangible/human) → capabilities → core competencies (roots of the tree); intangibles are the stronger source of advantage.
- VRIO cumulative gates: fail V = disadvantage, fail R = parity, fail I = temporary, fail O/N = unrealised; all pass = sustained advantage. Inimitability from unique history, causal ambiguity, social complexity.
- Value chain = 5 primary (Inbound → Operations → Outbound → Marketing & sales → Service) + 4 support (Infrastructure, HRM, Technology dev, Procurement); Value − Cost = Margin; watch linkages.
- SWOT collates internal S/W + external O/T; TOWS (Weihrich) combines into SO/ST/WO/WT — the matching, not the listing, is the strategy.
- Advantage is perishable: keep re-testing V and hold the dynamic capability to rebuild the resource base. Inside-out view.
