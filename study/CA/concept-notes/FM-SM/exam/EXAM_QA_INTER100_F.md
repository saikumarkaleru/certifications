### Q1. Ch: Ratio Analysis — Reverse Working of Financial Statements (Marks: 10) [Problem]
**Question:** From the following ratios and information relating to M/s Sunrise Traders for the year ended 31st March, 2026, you are required to prepare the **Balance Sheet** in the traditional (T) format.

| Particulars | Value |
|---|---|
| Current Ratio | 2.5 |
| Liquid (Quick) Ratio | 1.5 |
| Fixed Assets to Proprietor's Funds | 0.75 |
| Working Capital | ₹ 6,00,000 |
| Reserves & Surplus to Share Capital | 0.5 |
| Bank Overdraft (part of current liabilities) | ₹ 1,00,000 |
| There is no long-term loan or fictitious asset. All current liabilities other than the bank overdraft are trade creditors. | |

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Current Assets and Current Liabilities**
Let Current Liabilities = x. Current Ratio 2.5 ⇒ Current Assets = 2.5x.
Working Capital = CA − CL = 2.5x − x = 1.5x = ₹ 6,00,000 ⇒ x = ₹ 4,00,000.
Therefore Current Liabilities = ₹ 4,00,000 and Current Assets = 2.5 × 4,00,000 = **₹ 10,00,000**.

**WN-2: Stock (Inventory)**
Quick Ratio = Quick Assets / Current Liabilities = 1.5 ⇒ Quick Assets = 1.5 × 4,00,000 = ₹ 6,00,000.
Stock = Current Assets − Quick Assets = 10,00,000 − 6,00,000 = **₹ 4,00,000**.

**WN-3: Proprietor's Funds (Net Worth)**
Fixed Assets to Proprietor's Funds = 0.75 ⇒ Fixed Assets = 0.75 × Proprietor's Funds.
Since there is no long-term loan, Total Assets = Proprietor's Funds + Current Liabilities.
Total Assets = Fixed Assets + Current Assets = 0.75 PF + 10,00,000.
Also Total Assets = PF + 4,00,000.
⇒ 0.75 PF + 10,00,000 = PF + 4,00,000 ⇒ 0.25 PF = 6,00,000 ⇒ **PF = ₹ 24,00,000**.

**WN-4: Fixed Assets**
Fixed Assets = 0.75 × 24,00,000 = **₹ 18,00,000**. (Check: 18,00,000 + 10,00,000 = 28,00,000 = 24,00,000 + 4,00,000 ✓)

**WN-5: Share Capital and Reserves**
Reserves to Share Capital = 0.5 ⇒ let Share Capital = S, Reserves = 0.5S; PF = 1.5S = 24,00,000 ⇒ S = **₹ 16,00,000**; Reserves = **₹ 8,00,000**.

**WN-6: Trade Creditors**
Current Liabilities = ₹ 4,00,000; less Bank Overdraft ₹ 1,00,000 ⇒ Trade Creditors = **₹ 3,00,000**.

**Balance Sheet of M/s Sunrise Traders as at 31st March, 2026**

| Liabilities | ₹ | Assets | ₹ |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Share Capital | 16,00,000 | Fixed Assets | 18,00,000 |
| Reserves & Surplus | 8,00,000 | Current Assets: | |
| Current Liabilities: | | &nbsp;&nbsp;Stock | 4,00,000 |
| &nbsp;&nbsp;Trade Creditors | 3,00,000 | &nbsp;&nbsp;Quick Assets (Debtors + Cash/Bank) | 6,00,000 |
| &nbsp;&nbsp;Bank Overdraft | 1,00,000 | | |
| **Total** | **28,00,000** | **Total** | **28,00,000** |

**Answer:** Balance Sheet total = **₹ 28,00,000**; Proprietor's Funds = ₹ 24,00,000 (Share Capital ₹ 16,00,000 + Reserves ₹ 8,00,000).

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards clearly showing each ratio as an equation and solving stepwise. The frequent deduction is treating bank overdraft as a quick asset offset or forgetting it is already inside current liabilities — quick assets = current liabilities × quick ratio directly.)*

### Q2. Ch: Cost of Capital — Cost of Redeemable Debt: Approximation vs YTM (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** Zenith Ltd. issued 12% redeemable debentures of face value ₹ 100 each at a discount of 5%. Flotation cost is 2% of face value. The debentures are redeemable at a premium of 10% at the end of 5 years. The applicable tax rate is 30%. You are required to compute the **cost of debt (Kd)** using (a) the **approximation (short-cut) formula**, and (b) the **Yield to Maturity (YTM) method** using interpolation between 8% and 10%.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Net proceeds and Redemption value (per ₹ 100 debenture)**
Net Sale Proceeds (NP) = Face 100 − Discount 5 − Flotation 2 = **₹ 93**.
Redemption Value (RV) = 100 + 10% premium = **₹ 110**.
Annual Interest = 12% × 100 = ₹ 12; after tax = 12 × (1 − 0.30) = **₹ 8.40**.

**WN-2: (a) Approximation Formula**
$$K_d = \frac{I(1-t) + \dfrac{RV - NP}{n}}{\dfrac{RV + NP}{2}}$$
$$K_d = \frac{8.40 + \dfrac{110 - 93}{5}}{\dfrac{110 + 93}{2}} = \frac{8.40 + 3.40}{101.50} = \frac{11.80}{101.50} = 0.1163 = \mathbf{11.63\%}$$

**WN-3: (b) YTM by Interpolation — Cash flows (per debenture)**
After-tax interest ₹ 8.40 p.a. for years 1–5; Redemption ₹ 110 at year 5; Outflow (NP) ₹ 93 at year 0.

**At 8% discount rate:**

| Year | Cash flow (₹) | PVF @ 8% | PV (₹) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1–5 | 8.40 | 3.993 | 33.54 |
| 5 | 110.00 | 0.681 | 74.91 |
| | | **Total PV** | **108.45** |

NPV @ 8% = 108.45 − 93 = **+15.45**.

**At 10% discount rate:**

| Year | Cash flow (₹) | PVF @ 10% | PV (₹) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1–5 | 8.40 | 3.791 | 31.84 |
| 5 | 110.00 | 0.621 | 68.31 |
| | | **Total PV** | **100.15** |

NPV @ 10% = 100.15 − 93 = **+7.15**.

Both NPVs are positive, so extend to 12%:

| Year | Cash flow (₹) | PVF @ 12% | PV (₹) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1–5 | 8.40 | 3.605 | 30.28 |
| 5 | 110.00 | 0.567 | 62.37 |
| | | **Total PV** | **92.65** |

NPV @ 12% = 92.65 − 93 = **−0.35**.

**WN-4: Interpolation between 10% and 12%**
$$K_d = 10\% + \frac{7.15}{7.15 - (-0.35)} \times (12\% - 10\%) = 10\% + \frac{7.15}{7.50} \times 2\% = 10\% + 1.91\% = \mathbf{11.91\%}$$

**Answer:** Cost of debt = **11.63% (approximation)** and **≈ 11.91% (YTM)**. The YTM is marginally higher because it discounts the large terminal redemption value more heavily; the approximation formula understates Kd when redemption value exceeds proceeds.

*(Full-marks tip: State PVF sources and show NPV at each rate. A common deduction is applying tax to the redemption premium — only the interest is tax-deductible, never the capital redemption premium/discount.)*

### Q3. Ch: Cost of Capital — WACC with a New Project of Different Risk (Marks: 10) [Application]
**Question:** Aster Ltd.'s existing capital structure and current market data are given below. The company is evaluating a new project that is **riskier** than its existing business. The firm's overall (existing) cost of equity is based on a beta of 1.2; the new project has a beta of 1.8. Risk-free rate is 6% and the market return is 14%. The tax rate is 30%.

| Source | Book Value (₹) | Market Value (₹) | Pre-tax cost |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Equity Share Capital (₹10 shares) | 40,00,000 | 96,00,000 | (via CAPM) |
| 10% Preference Shares | 20,00,000 | 18,00,000 | 10% (irredeemable) |
| 9% Debentures | 40,00,000 | 36,00,000 | 9% |

Required: (i) Compute the existing WACC using **market-value weights**. (ii) Compute the **risk-adjusted cost of equity and WACC** to be used for the new project (keeping the same capital-structure weights). (iii) State whether the firm should use the existing WACC or the risk-adjusted rate to evaluate the new project, and why.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Cost of Equity via CAPM**
Existing: Ke = Rf + β(Rm − Rf) = 6% + 1.2(14% − 6%) = 6% + 9.6% = **15.6%**.
New project: Ke = 6% + 1.8(14% − 6%) = 6% + 14.4% = **20.4%**.

**WN-2: Cost of Preference and Debt**
Preference (irredeemable): Kp = 10% (preference dividend is not tax-deductible) = **10%**.
Debentures (after tax): Kd = 9% × (1 − 0.30) = **6.3%**.

**WN-3: Market-Value Weights**
Total MV = 96,00,000 + 18,00,000 + 36,00,000 = ₹ 1,50,00,000.
Equity = 96/150 = 0.64; Preference = 18/150 = 0.12; Debt = 36/150 = 0.24.

**WN-4: (i) Existing WACC**

| Source | Weight | Cost (%) | Weight × Cost |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Equity | 0.64 | 15.60 | 9.984 |
| Preference | 0.12 | 10.00 | 1.200 |
| Debentures | 0.24 | 6.30 | 1.512 |
| **WACC (existing)** | | | **12.696 ≈ 12.70%** |

**WN-5: (ii) Risk-Adjusted WACC (new project Ke = 20.4%)**

| Source | Weight | Cost (%) | Weight × Cost |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Equity | 0.64 | 20.40 | 13.056 |
| Preference | 0.12 | 10.00 | 1.200 |
| Debentures | 0.24 | 6.30 | 1.512 |
| **WACC (risk-adjusted)** | | | **15.768 ≈ 15.77%** |

**(iii) Conclusion:** The firm must evaluate the new project at the **risk-adjusted rate of 15.77%**, NOT the existing 12.70% WACC. The existing WACC reflects the risk of existing operations (β = 1.2). Using it as the hurdle rate for a riskier project (β = 1.8) would understate the required return, accept projects that do not compensate for their systematic risk, and destroy shareholder value.

**Answer:** Existing WACC = **12.70%**; risk-adjusted WACC for the new project = **15.77%**; use the **15.77%** hurdle rate.

*(Full-marks tip: Examiners specifically reward the reasoning in part (iii) — that a firm-wide WACC is valid only for projects of average risk. Do not tax-adjust the preference dividend, and use market-value (not book-value) weights when both are given.)*

### Q4. Ch: Cost of Capital — Adjusting Cost of Equity for Flotation Cost (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** Meridian Ltd.'s equity shares currently sell at ₹ 160 in the market. The company has just paid a dividend of ₹ 12 per share, which is expected to grow at 8% per annum indefinitely. To raise fresh equity, the company must incur flotation costs of 10% of the issue price. Compute (a) the cost of **existing** equity, and (b) the cost of **new (external) equity** after adjusting for flotation cost.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Expected Dividend (D1)**
D1 = D0 (1 + g) = 12 × 1.08 = **₹ 12.96**.

**WN-2: (a) Cost of Existing Equity (Dividend Growth Model)**
$$K_e = \frac{D_1}{P_0} + g = \frac{12.96}{160} + 0.08 = 0.081 + 0.08 = 0.161 = \mathbf{16.10\%}$$

**WN-3: (b) Cost of New Equity — flotation cost adjusts the net proceeds**
Net proceeds per share = P0 (1 − f) = 160 (1 − 0.10) = **₹ 144**.
$$K_e^{new} = \frac{D_1}{P_0(1-f)} + g = \frac{12.96}{144} + 0.08 = 0.09 + 0.08 = 0.17 = \mathbf{17.00\%}$$

**Answer:** Cost of existing equity = **16.10%**; cost of new equity (net of flotation) = **17.00%**.

*(Full-marks tip: Flotation cost is deducted from the issue price to arrive at net proceeds in the denominator — it is NOT deducted from the dividend and NOT added to g. A frequent error is applying flotation as `Ke × (1 − f)`, which is wrong.)*

### Q5. Ch: Ratio Analysis — Inter-Firm Comparison: Limitations (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** "Inter-firm comparison of accounting ratios is a useful analytical tool, but it must be used with caution." Explain any five limitations of inter-firm (cross-sectional) ratio comparison.

**Answer:**
Inter-firm comparison involves comparing the ratios of one enterprise with those of another firm in the same industry (or with the industry average) to judge relative performance. While informative, the technique suffers from the following limitations, each of which can distort the comparison:

1. **Different accounting policies:** Firms may adopt different methods of depreciation (SLM vs WDV), inventory valuation (FIFO vs weighted average) and treatment of expenditure (capital vs revenue). Ratios computed on non-uniform policies are not strictly comparable.

2. **Differences in size and scale:** Firms differing widely in size enjoy different economies of scale, bargaining power and cost structures, so a direct comparison of their ratios may be misleading even within the same industry.

3. **Price-level (inflation) differences:** Assets acquired at different points of time are recorded at different historical costs. A firm with older assets shows lower book values, inflating its return and turnover ratios relative to a firm with recently acquired assets.

4. **Different definitions of the ratio:** There is no universal definition — e.g., whether "profit" is before or after tax, whether "capital employed" includes or excludes intangibles. Unless the numerator and denominator are defined identically, the comparison is invalid.

5. **Diversified product mix / different functions:** Two firms classified in the same industry may operate in different market segments, have different degrees of vertical integration, or different risk profiles, making their ratios inherently non-comparable.

6. **Window dressing:** A firm may present a favourable position at the year-end (e.g., delaying purchases, accelerating collections) so that its reported ratios do not reflect the true year-round position.

**Conclusion:** Ratios should be compared only after adjusting for differences in accounting policies and definitions, and should be supplemented by trend (time-series) analysis; a single-period inter-firm comparison in isolation can lead to erroneous conclusions.

*(Full-marks tip: Give five distinct, well-labelled limitations with a one-line explanation each — the examiner marks per valid point. Merely listing headings without explanation loses half the marks.)*

### Q6. Ch: Ratio Analysis — Preparing Statements from Ratios (Trading & P&L) (Marks: 10) [Problem]
**Question:** From the following information relating to Orbit Manufacturers for the year ended 31st March, 2026, prepare a **Trading and Profit & Loss Account** (in statement form).

| Particulars | Value |
|---|---|
| Gross Profit Ratio | 25% |
| Net Profit Ratio | 10% |
| Stock Turnover Ratio (on cost of goods sold) | 6 times |
| Sales | ₹ 40,00,000 |
| Closing Stock is ₹ 2,00,000 more than Opening Stock | |
| Non-operating income | ₹ 50,000 |
| Non-operating expenses | ₹ 1,20,000 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Gross Profit and Cost of Goods Sold**
Gross Profit = 25% × 40,00,000 = **₹ 10,00,000**.
Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) = Sales − GP = 40,00,000 − 10,00,000 = **₹ 30,00,000**.

**WN-2: Average Stock and Opening/Closing Stock**
Stock Turnover = COGS / Average Stock = 6 ⇒ Average Stock = 30,00,000 / 6 = **₹ 5,00,000**.
Let Opening Stock = O; Closing = O + 2,00,000. Average = (O + O + 2,00,000)/2 = O + 1,00,000 = 5,00,000 ⇒ **Opening Stock = ₹ 4,00,000**, **Closing Stock = ₹ 6,00,000**.

**WN-3: Purchases (balancing figure in Trading A/c)**
COGS = Opening Stock + Purchases − Closing Stock ⇒ 30,00,000 = 4,00,000 + Purchases − 6,00,000 ⇒ Purchases = **₹ 32,00,000**.

**WN-4: Net Profit and Operating Expenses**
Net Profit = 10% × 40,00,000 = **₹ 4,00,000**.
Net Profit = Gross Profit + Non-operating income − Non-operating expenses − Operating (other) expenses.
4,00,000 = 10,00,000 + 50,000 − 1,20,000 − Operating expenses ⇒ Operating expenses = **₹ 5,30,000**.

**Trading and Profit & Loss Account of Orbit Manufacturers for the year ended 31st March, 2026**

| Particulars | ₹ | Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| To Opening Stock | 4,00,000 | By Sales | 40,00,000 |
| To Purchases | 32,00,000 | By Closing Stock | 6,00,000 |
| To Gross Profit c/d | 10,00,000 | | |
| **Total** | **46,00,000** | **Total** | **46,00,000** |
| To Operating Expenses | 5,30,000 | By Gross Profit b/d | 10,00,000 |
| To Non-operating Expenses | 1,20,000 | By Non-operating Income | 50,000 |
| To Net Profit | 4,00,000 | | |
| **Total** | **10,50,000** | **Total** | **10,50,000** |

**Answer:** Gross Profit = ₹ 10,00,000; **Net Profit = ₹ 4,00,000**; Purchases = ₹ 32,00,000; Opening Stock ₹ 4,00,000, Closing Stock ₹ 6,00,000.

*(Full-marks tip: State whether stock turnover is on cost or on sales — here it is on COGS, so average stock = COGS ÷ turnover. Deriving purchases as the balancing figure and reconciling both sides of the account is where marks are earned.)*

### Q7. Ch: Cost of Capital — Cost of Redeemable Preference Shares (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** Falcon Ltd. issued 5,000, 11% Preference Shares of ₹ 100 each at a premium of 4%, redeemable at a premium of 6% at the end of 8 years. The cost of issue is ₹ 3 per share. Compute the **cost of redeemable preference capital** using the approximation formula. (Preference dividend is not tax-deductible.)

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Net Proceeds and Redemption Value (per share of ₹ 100)**
Net Proceeds (NP) = 100 + 4 (premium) − 3 (issue cost) = **₹ 101**.
Redemption Value (RV) = 100 + 6% premium = **₹ 106**.
Annual Preference Dividend (PD) = 11% × 100 = **₹ 11** (no tax adjustment).

**WN-2: Cost of Redeemable Preference (approximation formula)**
$$K_p = \frac{PD + \dfrac{RV - NP}{n}}{\dfrac{RV + NP}{2}} = \frac{11 + \dfrac{106 - 101}{8}}{\dfrac{106 + 101}{2}}$$
$$= \frac{11 + 0.625}{103.50} = \frac{11.625}{103.50} = 0.11232 = \mathbf{11.23\%}$$

**Answer:** Cost of redeemable preference capital = **11.23%**.

*(Full-marks tip: Never apply a (1 − t) tax factor to preference dividends — unlike interest, they are paid out of post-tax profits. Include the securities premium received in net proceeds and the redemption premium in RV.)*

### Q8. Ch: Ratio Analysis — Reverse Working of Current Assets Composition (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** The following particulars relate to Pinnacle Ltd. for the year ended 31st March, 2026. Compute the amounts of **Debtors, Closing Stock, and Cash** and hence verify the current and quick ratios.

| Particulars | Value |
|---|---|
| Current Ratio | 3.0 |
| Quick Ratio | 1.75 |
| Current Liabilities | ₹ 4,00,000 |
| Debtors' Velocity (Debtors Collection Period) | 2 months |
| Credit Sales (all sales are on credit) | ₹ 24,00,000 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Current Assets and Quick Assets**
Current Assets = Current Ratio × CL = 3.0 × 4,00,000 = **₹ 12,00,000**.
Quick Assets = Quick Ratio × CL = 1.75 × 4,00,000 = **₹ 7,00,000**.
Closing Stock = CA − Quick Assets = 12,00,000 − 7,00,000 = **₹ 5,00,000**.

**WN-2: Debtors**
Debtors = Credit Sales × (Collection period in months / 12) = 24,00,000 × (2/12) = **₹ 4,00,000**.

**WN-3: Cash & Bank (balancing quick asset)**
Quick Assets = Debtors + Cash ⇒ 7,00,000 = 4,00,000 + Cash ⇒ Cash = **₹ 3,00,000**.

**Verification:**
Current Ratio = (Stock 5,00,000 + Debtors 4,00,000 + Cash 3,00,000) / 4,00,000 = 12,00,000 / 4,00,000 = **3.0** ✓
Quick Ratio = (4,00,000 + 3,00,000) / 4,00,000 = 7,00,000 / 4,00,000 = **1.75** ✓

**Answer:** Debtors = **₹ 4,00,000**; Closing Stock = **₹ 5,00,000**; Cash & Bank = **₹ 3,00,000**.

*(Full-marks tip: Convert the debtors' velocity of 2 months into the fraction 2/12 of annual credit sales; stock is the gap between current and quick assets, and cash is the residual quick asset after debtors. Always close with a verification of both ratios.)*

### Q9. Ch: Cost of Capital — Overall WACC with Retained Earnings vs New Equity (Marks: 10) [Problem]
**Question:** Crest Ltd. plans to raise ₹ 50,00,000 for a new expansion. Its target capital structure and cost details are given below. Retained earnings available are ₹ 12,00,000; the balance of the equity portion will be raised by a fresh issue that carries a flotation cost of 5%. Compute the **marginal (weighted average) cost of capital** for the ₹ 50,00,000 to be raised.

| Source | Proportion in structure | Details |
|---|---|---|

| Preference | 20% | 12% redeemable, cost (Kp) = 12.5% |

Equity portion = 50% × 50,00,000 = ₹ 25,00,000.
Of this, Retained Earnings = ₹ 12,00,000 and New Equity issue = 25,00,000 − 12,00,000 = ₹ 13,00,000.

**WN-3: Cost of New Equity (flotation 5%)**
Net proceeds = 200 (1 − 0.05) = ₹ 190.
$$K_e = \frac{20}{190} + 0.06 = 0.1053 + 0.06 = 0.1653 = \mathbf{16.53\%}$$

**WN-4: Cost of Debt (after tax)**
Kd = 10% × (1 − 0.30) = **7.00%**.

**WN-5: Rupee weights and weighted cost**

| Source | Amount (₹) | Weight | Cost (%) | Weight × Cost |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Retained Earnings | 12,00,000 | 0.24 | 16.00 | 3.840 |
| New Equity | 13,00,000 | 0.26 | 16.53 | 4.298 |
| Preference | 10,00,000 | 0.20 | 12.50 | 2.500 |
| Debt | 15,00,000 | 0.30 | 7.00 | 2.100 |
| **Total** | **50,00,000** | **1.00** | | **12.738 ≈ 12.74%** |

*(Full-marks tip: Retained earnings carry no flotation cost, so they are cheaper than fresh equity — the examiner rewards splitting the equity block into Kr and Ke separately. Preference (₹10,00,000) and debt (₹15,00,000) come from 20% and 30% of ₹50,00,000.)*

### Q10. Ch: Ratio Analysis — DuPont Analysis of Return on Equity (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Using the DuPont framework, analyse the **Return on Equity (ROE)** of Vertex Ltd. from the following data and comment on the drivers of its return.

| Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---:|
| Net Profit after Tax | 6,00,000 |
| Sales | 60,00,000 |
| Total Assets | 40,00,000 |
| Shareholders' (Equity) Funds | 24,00,000 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Net Profit Margin**
= Net Profit / Sales = 6,00,000 / 60,00,000 = 0.10 = **10%**.

**WN-2: Total Asset Turnover**
= Sales / Total Assets = 60,00,000 / 40,00,000 = **1.5 times**.

**WN-3: Equity Multiplier (Financial Leverage)**
= Total Assets / Equity Funds = 40,00,000 / 24,00,000 = **1.667**.

**WN-4: DuPont ROE**
$$ROE = \text{Net Profit Margin} \times \text{Asset Turnover} \times \text{Equity Multiplier}$$
$$= 0.10 \times 1.5 \times 1.667 = 0.25 = \mathbf{25\%}$$

**Cross-check:** ROE = Net Profit / Equity = 6,00,000 / 24,00,000 = **25%** ✓

**Comment:** The 25% ROE is driven by a moderate net margin (10%), efficient asset utilisation (1.5×) and a financial leverage of 1.667 (i.e., 40% of assets are debt-financed). The leverage magnifies the return on assets (10% × 1.5 = 15% ROA) up to 25% ROE; a decline in margin or an increase in interest cost would compress this return.

**Answer:** ROE = **25%** = 10% margin × 1.5 asset turnover × 1.667 equity multiplier.

*(Full-marks tip: The three-factor decomposition and the cross-check against direct ROE are both expected. Marks are lost if the equity multiplier is inverted or if the product is not reconciled with the direct ratio.)*

### Q11. Ch: Cost of Capital — CAPM vs Dividend Growth Model for Ke (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** For Nimbus Ltd., compute the cost of equity under **both** (a) the Capital Asset Pricing Model and (b) the Dividend Growth Model, and reconcile the difference.

| Particulars | Value |
|---|---|
| Risk-free rate | 7% |
| Market return | 15% |
| Beta of equity | 1.25 |
| Current market price per share | ₹ 125 |
| Dividend just paid (D0) | ₹ 8 |
| Expected growth rate | 9% |

**Solution:**

**WN-1: (a) CAPM**
$$K_e = R_f + \beta (R_m - R_f) = 7\% + 1.25(15\% - 7\%) = 7\% + 1.25 \times 8\% = 7\% + 10\% = \mathbf{17\%}$$

**WN-2: (b) Dividend Growth Model**
D1 = D0 (1 + g) = 8 × 1.09 = ₹ 8.72.
$$K_e = \frac{D_1}{P_0} + g = \frac{8.72}{125} + 0.09 = 0.06976 + 0.09 = 0.15976 = \mathbf{15.98\%}$$

**WN-3: Reconciliation**
CAPM gives 17% versus 15.98% under the growth model — a difference of about 1.02%. The two models rest on different inputs: CAPM prices only **systematic (market) risk** through beta and is forward-looking on market premium, whereas the dividend growth model is derived from the **current market price and the firm's own dividend expectations**. The gap may arise because the market currently prices the share to yield less than its CAPM-implied required return (i.e., the share may be marginally overvalued), or because the assumed growth rate/beta differs from the market's consensus.

**Answer:** Ke = **17% (CAPM)** and **15.98% (Dividend Growth Model)**.

### Q15. Ch: Cost of Capital — Concept & Importance (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the concept of **"Cost of Capital"** and state its significance in financial decision-making.

**Answer:**
The **cost of capital** is the **minimum rate of return** that a firm must earn on its investments so as to maintain the market value of its equity shares and satisfy the expectations of the suppliers of funds (equity shareholders, preference shareholders and lenders). It represents the composite (weighted average) cost of the various sources of finance used by the firm and is expressed as a percentage. It comprises three notional components — the return at zero risk, a premium for business risk, and a premium for financial risk.

**Significance in financial decision-making:**
1. **Capital budgeting (investment) decisions:** It is the **discount rate** used to compute the net present value and the **cut-off/hurdle rate** for the internal rate of return — a project is accepted only if its return exceeds the cost of capital.
2. **Capital structure (financing) decisions:** By comparing the specific costs of debt, preference and equity, the firm designs an optimal capital structure that **minimises the overall cost of capital** and thereby maximises firm value.
3. **Evaluation of financial performance:** Actual returns are compared against the cost of capital to judge whether management has created value for shareholders.
4. **Other decisions:** It aids in dividend policy, working-capital policy and lease-versus-buy decisions.

**Conclusion:** The cost of capital is the central link between the investment and financing decisions of a firm and acts as the benchmark rate for value creation.

*(Full-marks tip: Define it as the MINIMUM required rate of return, then list at least three distinct uses. Naming the capital-budgeting hurdle-rate role and the optimal-capital-structure role secures full marks.)*

### Q18. Ch: Capital Structure & Leverage — Operating BEP vs Financial BEP and Margin of Safety in Leverage Terms (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** The following data relate to Sunrise Manufacturing Ltd. for the year ended 31st March 2026. You are required to compute (i) the Operating Break-Even Point (in units and ₹), (ii) the Financial Break-Even Point (EBIT and units), (iii) the Degree of Operating, Financial and Combined Leverage, and (iv) verify the Margin of Safety through the operating leverage.

| Particulars | Amount |
|---|---|
| Selling price per unit | ₹200 |
| Variable cost per unit | ₹120 |
| Fixed operating cost (per annum) | ₹16,00,000 |
| Interest on debentures (per annum) | ₹4,00,000 |
| Preference dividend (per annum) | ₹1,50,000 |
| Present output / sales | 30,000 units |
| Tax rate | 25% |

Contribution per unit = ₹200 − ₹120 = ₹80.

**WN-2: Present income statement**

| Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Sales (30,000 × ₹200) | 60,00,000 |
| Less: Variable cost (30,000 × ₹120) | 36,00,000 |
| **Contribution** | **24,00,000** |
| Less: Fixed operating cost | 16,00,000 |

Operating BEP (₹) = 20,000 × ₹200 = **₹40,00,000**.

**WN-4: Financial Break-Even Point**
Financial BEP is the level of EBIT at which earnings available to equity shareholders is zero, i.e. EBIT that just covers interest and the grossed-up preference dividend.
Financial BEP (EBIT) = Interest + [Preference dividend ÷ (1 − t)] = ₹4,00,000 + (₹1,50,000 ÷ 0.75) = ₹4,00,000 + ₹2,00,000 = **₹6,00,000**.

**Answer:** Operating BEP = 20,000 units (₹40,00,000); Financial BEP = EBIT ₹6,00,000 (27,500 units); DOL = 3, DFL = 4, DCL = 12; Margin of Safety = 33.33% (confirmed by 1/DOL).

### Q22. Ch: Capital Structure & Leverage — Business Risk vs Financial Risk (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between "Business Risk" and "Financial Risk", and explain how each is measured through leverage.

**Answer:**
Business risk refers to the variability or uncertainty in a firm's operating profit (EBIT) that arises from the nature of its business operations — it exists even if the firm uses no debt at all. It is caused by factors such as demand fluctuations, price volatility, input cost changes and, importantly, the proportion of fixed operating costs in the cost structure. A higher proportion of fixed operating costs magnifies the effect of a change in sales on EBIT; this magnification is measured by the **Degree of Operating Leverage (DOL) = Contribution ÷ EBIT**. A higher DOL indicates higher business risk.

Financial risk refers to the additional variability imposed on the earnings available to equity shareholders (and the risk of insolvency) on account of the use of fixed-charge sources of finance such as debt and preference capital. It arises only when the firm employs a levered capital structure. Its magnitude is measured by the **Degree of Financial Leverage (DFL) = EBIT ÷ EBT** (adjusted for the grossed-up preference dividend where preference capital exists). A higher DFL indicates higher financial risk.

| Basis | Business Risk | Financial Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Operating activities / cost structure | Financing decision / capital structure |
| Arises from | Fixed operating costs | Fixed financial charges (interest, preference dividend) |
| Exists without debt? | Yes | No |
| Measured by | Degree of Operating Leverage (DOL) | Degree of Financial Leverage (DFL) |
| Affects | Variability of EBIT | Variability of EPS |

The combined effect of both risks on EPS for a given change in sales is captured by the **Degree of Combined Leverage (DCL) = DOL × DFL**.

**Conclusion:** Business risk is inherent in operations and measured by DOL, while financial risk is a consequence of the financing mix and measured by DFL; a firm with high business risk should generally keep financial risk (debt) low to avoid an unacceptably high combined leverage.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner expects the two measurement formulae (DOL and DFL) and the crucial distinction that business risk exists even in an all-equity firm whereas financial risk is entirely a product of the financing decision.)*

### Q28. Ch: Capital Budgeting — Superiority of NPV over IRR and the Multiple-IRR Problem (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** "The Net Present Value method is theoretically superior to the Internal Rate of Return method." Explain, and briefly state the problem of multiple IRRs.

**Answer:**
The Net Present Value (NPV) method computes the present value of a project's cash flows at the firm's cost of capital and expresses the result as an absolute rupee addition to shareholders' wealth, whereas the Internal Rate of Return (IRR) is the discount rate at which the NPV becomes zero. NPV is regarded as theoretically superior for the following reasons:

1. **Reinvestment assumption:** NPV assumes that intermediate cash flows are reinvested at the cost of capital, which is realistic, whereas IRR assumes reinvestment at the project's own IRR, which may be unrealistically high.
2. **Wealth maximisation:** NPV measures the absolute increase in shareholders' wealth, consistent with the goal of financial management, while IRR gives only a percentage and may favour a smaller project.
3. **Ranking of mutually exclusive projects:** Where projects differ in size or timing of cash flows, IRR can give a ranking that conflicts with wealth maximisation; NPV always gives the wealth-maximising choice.
4. **Multiple IRR problem:** When a project has non-conventional cash flows (i.e., the sign of the net cash flow changes more than once over the life of the project), the IRR equation can yield more than one rate (multiple IRRs) or no real IRR at all, making the IRR ambiguous. NPV does not suffer from this defect, as it gives a single unambiguous value at any given discount rate.

**Conclusion:** Because NPV rests on a realistic reinvestment assumption, directly measures wealth addition, ranks projects correctly, and avoids the multiple-IRR ambiguity, it is theoretically superior to the IRR method.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner specifically rewards the reinvestment-rate argument and the non-conventional-cash-flow cause of multiple IRRs — a sign change in cash flows more than once. Merely stating "NPV is in rupees, IRR is in %" earns only partial credit.)*

### Q29. Ch: Capital Structure & Leverage — Theories of Capital Structure (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Briefly explain the four theories of capital structure and state, under each, the effect of increasing financial leverage on the value of the firm and on the overall cost of capital.

**Answer:**
The relationship between the capital structure (the mix of debt and equity) and the value of the firm is explained by four principal theories:

1. **Net Income (NI) Approach:** This approach holds that capital structure is relevant. As the firm employs more low-cost debt, the overall (weighted average) cost of capital (Ko) falls and the value of the firm rises. The optimum capital structure is reached at the point where Ko is minimum and firm value is maximum (theoretically at maximum debt).

2. **Net Operating Income (NOI) Approach:** This approach holds that capital structure is irrelevant. The overall cost of capital (Ko) and the value of the firm remain constant at every level of leverage, because the benefit of cheaper debt is exactly offset by the rise in the cost of equity (Ke) demanded by shareholders for the increased financial risk. There is no unique optimum capital structure.

3. **Traditional Approach:** A compromise between NI and NOI. Up to a moderate level of debt, the use of leverage reduces Ko and increases firm value; beyond an optimum point, the rising costs of both debt and equity outweigh the benefit, Ko begins to rise and firm value falls. Thus a definite optimum capital structure exists at the minimum-Ko point.

4. **Modigliani–Miller (MM) Approach:** In the absence of taxes, MM support the NOI view through the arbitrage process — firm value is independent of capital structure, and any pricing difference between a levered and an unlevered firm is eliminated by investors' personal (homemade) leverage arbitrage. With corporate taxes, however, because interest is tax-deductible, the value of the levered firm exceeds that of the unlevered firm by the present value of the interest tax shield, so higher debt increases firm value.

| Approach | Ko as debt rises | Firm value | Optimum structure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Net Income | Falls | Rises | Yes (max debt) |
| Net Operating Income | Constant | Constant | No |
| Traditional | Falls then rises | Rises then falls | Yes (min Ko) |
| MM (no tax) | Constant | Constant | No |
| MM (with tax) | Falls | Rises | Towards max debt |

**Conclusion:** The relevance of capital structure to firm value depends on the assumptions adopted — the Traditional approach, recognising an optimum, is the most widely accepted in practice, while the MM approach with taxes provides the theoretical basis for the tax advantage of debt.

*(Full-marks tip: A comparative table of the effect on Ko and firm value under each theory is what the examiner scans for. Note the crucial distinction between MM without tax (irrelevance via arbitrage) and MM with tax (relevance via the interest tax shield).)*

### Q30. Ch: Capital Structure & Leverage — Factors Determining Capital Structure (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State and briefly explain any six factors that a finance manager should consider while designing the capital structure of a company.

**Answer:**
The capital structure of a company should be planned so as to maximise the wealth of the shareholders while keeping financial risk within acceptable limits. The important factors to be considered are:

1. **Cost of capital:** Debt is generally cheaper than equity (owing to the tax-deductibility of interest and lower risk to lenders); a structure that minimises the overall cost of capital is preferred.
2. **Risk (financial risk):** Higher use of fixed-cost debt increases financial risk and the danger of insolvency; the structure must keep the degree of financial leverage within safe limits, especially where business risk is already high.
3. **Control:** Raising funds through equity dilutes the control of existing shareholders, whereas debt does not; promoters wishing to retain control may prefer debt.
4. **Flexibility:** The structure should retain the capacity to raise additional funds in future (unused debt capacity) and to redeem or refinance without undue strain.
5. **Nature and stability of earnings (EBIT):** A company with stable and predictable operating profits can service a higher level of debt safely, whereas volatile earnings call for a more conservative, equity-oriented structure.
6. **Cash flow ability to service debt (solvency):** The firm must be able to meet interest and principal repayment obligations from its cash flows; adequate debt-service coverage is essential.

(Other relevant factors include trading on equity/EPS effect, capital market conditions, size of the company, purpose of financing, floatation costs and legal/regulatory requirements.)

**Conclusion:** An appropriate capital structure balances the twin objectives of minimising the cost of capital and containing financial risk, while preserving flexibility, control and the ability to service the debt.

*(Full-marks tip: Give six DISTINCT factors, each with a one-line justification tying it to cost, risk or control — a bare list without explanation earns only partial credit.)*

**Question:** Vertex Ltd. is evaluating a project with the following particulars: initial investment ₹5,00,000; life 5 years; annual sales 10,000 units at ₹100 each; variable cost ₹60 per unit; annual fixed cost (excluding depreciation) ₹1,00,000; cost of capital 10% (5-year annuity factor = 3.791). Ignore taxation and depreciation for cash-flow purposes. Determine the sensitivity of the project's NPV to (i) the initial investment, (ii) the annual cash inflow, and (iii) the selling price, and state which variable is the most sensitive.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Annual cash inflow and base NPV**
Contribution = 10,000 × (₹100 − ₹60) = ₹4,00,000. Annual cash inflow = ₹4,00,000 − ₹1,00,000 = ₹3,00,000.
PV of inflows = ₹3,00,000 × 3.791 = ₹11,37,300.
Base NPV = ₹11,37,300 − ₹5,00,000 = **₹6,37,300**.

**WN-2: Sensitivity of initial investment**
NPV becomes zero when initial investment = PV of inflows = ₹11,37,300.
Maximum % increase tolerable = (₹11,37,300 − ₹5,00,000) ÷ ₹5,00,000 = **127.46%**.

**WN-3: Sensitivity of annual cash inflow**
NPV zero when annual inflow = Initial investment ÷ PVIFA = ₹5,00,000 ÷ 3.791 = ₹1,31,896.
Maximum % fall tolerable = (₹3,00,000 − ₹1,31,896) ÷ ₹3,00,000 = **56.03%**.

**WN-4: Sensitivity of selling price**
Required annual cash inflow at NPV = 0 is ₹1,31,896 (from WN-3).
This equals (Sales value − Variable cost − Fixed cost); so Sales value = ₹1,31,896 + (10,000 × ₹60) + ₹1,00,000 = ₹1,31,896 + ₹6,00,000 + ₹1,00,000 = ₹8,31,896.
Break-even selling price = ₹8,31,896 ÷ 10,000 = ₹83.19.
Maximum % fall in selling price tolerable = (₹100 − ₹83.19) ÷ ₹100 = **16.81%**.

**WN-5: Comparison**

| Variable | Adverse change to reach NPV = 0 | Sensitivity |
|---|---|---|
| Initial investment | +127.46% | Least sensitive |
| Annual cash inflow | −56.03% | Moderate |
| Selling price | −16.81% | Most sensitive |

**Answer:** The project can absorb a 127.46% rise in investment or a 56.03% fall in annual inflow, but only a 16.81% fall in selling price before the NPV turns zero. **The selling price is the most sensitive variable** and must be forecast and controlled most carefully.

*(Full-marks tip: Sensitivity is measured by the SMALLEST adverse percentage change that makes NPV zero — the variable needing the smallest change is the most critical. The selling price is more sensitive than the cash inflow because a change in price affects only the contribution portion, so a small % change in price produces a larger % change in cash flow.)*

### Q34. Ch: Capital Budgeting — Payback Period and Accounting Rate of Return (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** Aster Ltd. is considering a project costing ₹5,00,000 with a life of 5 years and an estimated salvage value of ₹50,000. Depreciation is on a straight-line basis. The estimated profit after depreciation (before considering salvage) is given below. Compute the (i) Payback Period and (ii) Accounting Rate of Return (on average investment).

| Year | Profit after depreciation (₹) |
|---|---|
| 1 | 60,000 |
| 2 | 80,000 |
| 3 | 1,00,000 |
| 4 | 90,000 |
| 5 | 70,000 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Depreciation and annual cash flows**
Depreciation = (₹5,00,000 − ₹50,000) ÷ 5 = ₹90,000 p.a.
Cash flow = Profit after depreciation + Depreciation (salvage of ₹50,000 added in Year 5).

| Year | Profit (₹) | Depreciation (₹) | Cash flow (₹) | Cumulative cash flow (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 60,000 | 90,000 | 1,50,000 | 1,50,000 |
| 2 | 80,000 | 90,000 | 1,70,000 | 3,20,000 |
| 3 | 1,00,000 | 90,000 | 1,90,000 | 5,10,000 |
| 4 | 90,000 | 90,000 | 1,80,000 | 6,90,000 |
| 5 | 70,000 + 50,000 (salvage) | 90,000 | 2,10,000 | 9,00,000 |

**WN-2: Payback Period**
By end of Year 2, ₹3,20,000 is recovered; balance to recover = ₹5,00,000 − ₹3,20,000 = ₹1,80,000, out of Year 3 cash flow of ₹1,90,000.
Payback = 2 + (₹1,80,000 ÷ ₹1,90,000) = 2 + 0.95 = **2.95 years**.

**WN-3: Accounting Rate of Return (ARR)**
Average annual profit = (₹60,000 + ₹80,000 + ₹1,00,000 + ₹90,000 + ₹70,000) ÷ 5 = ₹4,00,000 ÷ 5 = ₹80,000.
Average investment = [(Cost − Salvage) ÷ 2] + Salvage = [(₹5,00,000 − ₹50,000) ÷ 2] + ₹50,000 = ₹2,25,000 + ₹50,000 = ₹2,75,000.
ARR = (₹80,000 ÷ ₹2,75,000) × 100 = **29.09%**.

**OR** (on initial investment): ARR = (₹80,000 ÷ ₹5,00,000) × 100 = **16.00%**.

**Answer:** Payback Period = 2.95 years; Accounting Rate of Return = 29.09% (on average investment) / 16.00% (on original investment).

*(Full-marks tip: Payback uses CASH FLOWS (profit + depreciation), whereas ARR uses accounting PROFITS after depreciation — mixing the two is the most common error. Include salvage value in the Year-5 cash flow for payback, and use the correct average-investment formula [(Cost − Salvage)/2 + Salvage] for ARR.)*

### Q35. Ch: Risk Analysis in Capital Budgeting — Risk vs Uncertainty and Measurement Techniques (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between "risk" and "uncertainty" in the context of capital budgeting, and briefly explain the techniques available to a finance manager for incorporating risk into the investment appraisal.

**Answer:**
Risk in capital budgeting refers to a situation where the possible future outcomes of an investment and the probability of their occurrence are *known* or can be reasonably estimated from past data. Uncertainty, on the other hand, refers to a situation where the future outcomes themselves are unknown and no objective probabilities can be assigned to them. In short, risk is measurable and quantifiable whereas uncertainty is not, though in practice the two terms are often used interchangeably.

The distinction may be tabulated as follows:

| Basis | Risk | Uncertainty |
|---|---|---|
| Outcomes | Known/estimable | Not fully known |
| Probabilities | Objectively assignable | Cannot be assigned |
| Measurement | Standard deviation, coefficient of variation | Largely subjective judgement |
| Data source | Historical/statistical data | No reliable past data |

The principal techniques for handling risk in investment appraisal are:
1. **Statistical techniques** — Probability assignment and computation of Expected NPV, Standard Deviation and Coefficient of Variation to measure the dispersion of returns.
2. **Risk-Adjusted Discount Rate (RADR)** — Adding a risk premium to the risk-free rate so that riskier projects are discounted at a higher rate.
3. **Certainty Equivalent (CE)** — Converting risky cash flows into certain cash flows using CE coefficients and discounting at the risk-free rate.
4. **Sensitivity and Scenario Analysis** — Testing how NPV responds to changes in critical variables.
5. **Decision Tree Analysis** — Mapping sequential decisions and their probabilistic outcomes.

**Conclusion:** Risk is a *quantifiable* variability of returns, while uncertainty is *non-quantifiable*; the finance manager uses statistical, discount-rate, certainty-equivalent and simulation techniques to build risk explicitly into the accept/reject decision.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner rewards a clean risk-vs-uncertainty table and naming at least four techniques with a one-line role each; marks are lost for treating the two words as identical without qualification.)*

### Q41. Ch: Risk Analysis in Capital Budgeting — Sensitivity vs Scenario Analysis (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain "Sensitivity Analysis" as a technique of risk analysis in capital budgeting, and distinguish it from "Scenario Analysis".

**Answer:**
Sensitivity analysis is a technique that measures how responsive the NPV (or IRR) of a project is to a change in one input variable — such as selling price, unit sales, variable cost, project life or discount rate — while holding all other variables constant. It answers the "what-if" question and identifies the **critical/sensitive variable**, i.e. the one to which the project's viability is most vulnerable; the smaller the percentage change in a variable that turns the NPV to zero, the more sensitive (risky) the project is to that variable.

Scenario analysis overcomes the chief limitation of sensitivity analysis — that it changes only one variable at a time. It examines the impact on NPV of a *consistent combination* of variables changing together under defined states of the economy, typically an **optimistic (best case)**, a **most-likely (base case)** and a **pessimistic (worst case)** scenario.

| Basis | Sensitivity Analysis | Scenario Analysis |
|---|---|---|
| Variables changed | One at a time | Several together (as a set) |
| Purpose | Identify the critical variable | See combined effect of a state of nature |
| Output | NPV sensitivity to each input | NPV under best/base/worst case |
| Limitation | Ignores interdependence of variables | Limited to a few defined scenarios |

**Conclusion:** Sensitivity analysis isolates the single most critical variable, whereas scenario analysis captures the joint effect of several variables moving together; the two are complementary tools for assessing project risk.

*(Full-marks tip: the distinction hinges on "one variable vs a set of variables"; the examiner rewards the definition of the critical variable and the best/base/worst framing of scenarios.)*

### Q43. Ch: Dividend Decisions — Walter's Model: Assumptions and Formula (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the assumptions underlying Walter's model of dividend policy, give its valuation formula, and explain the optimum dividend payout it prescribes for a growth firm, a normal firm and a declining firm.

**Answer:**
Walter's model supports the view that dividend policy is **relevant** and affects the market value of a share because it links the firm's internal rate of return (r) with its cost of equity capital (ke). The valuation formula is:

**P = [ D + (r ⁄ ke)(E − D) ] ⁄ ke**

where P = market price per share, D = dividend per share, E = earnings per share, r = internal rate of return, ke = cost of equity capital.

The **assumptions** of the model are:
1. All financing is done through **retained earnings**; no external debt or equity is used.
2. The internal rate of return (r) and the cost of capital (ke) remain **constant**.
3. Earnings (E) and dividends (D) do not change; the values assumed to determine a value are the beginning values.
4. The firm has a **perpetual (infinite) life**.

The optimum payout under the model:

| Type of firm | Relationship | Optimum payout | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Growth firm | r > ke | 0% (retain all) | Retention earns more than shareholders' expectation |
| Normal firm | r = ke | Indifferent (any payout) | Price unaffected by payout |
| Declining firm | r < ke | 100% (pay all) | Shareholders reinvest better than the firm |

**Conclusion:** Under Walter's model dividend policy is relevant; a growth firm should retain fully, a declining firm should distribute fully, and for a normal firm the payout ratio is a matter of indifference.

*(Full-marks tip: state all four assumptions, reproduce the formula exactly, and give the r-vs-ke rule for all three firm types; dropping the "financing only by retained earnings" assumption is a frequent omission.)*

### Q46. Ch: Dividend Decisions — MM Dividend Irrelevance Proof with Arbitrage (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** V Ltd. has 1,00,000 equity shares outstanding, currently priced at ₹100 each. The equity capitalisation (ke) is 10%. The company expects net earnings of ₹5,00,000 during the year and plans a new investment of ₹10,00,000. Using the **Modigliani–Miller (MM) dividend irrelevance** approach, show that the value of the firm is the same whether the company (a) pays a dividend of ₹10 per share, or (b) pays no dividend. Also demonstrate the arbitrage/"homemade dividend" logic for a shareholder.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: MM price at end of year**
MM valuation: P₀ = (P₁ + D₁) ⁄ (1 + ke) ⟹ P₁ = P₀(1 + ke) − D₁.
P₀ = ₹100, ke = 10%.

| Particulars | (a) Dividend ₹10 | (b) No dividend |
|---|---|---|
| P₀ (1 + ke) = 100 × 1.10 (₹) | 110 | 110 |
| Less: D₁ (₹) | 10 | 0 |
| **Year-end price P₁ (₹)** | **100** | **110** |

**WN-2: Number of new shares (ΔN) to be issued to finance investment**
Funds to be raised = Investment − (Earnings − Dividends paid).

| Particulars | (a) Dividend ₹10 | (b) No dividend |
|---|---|---|
| Total dividend = 1,00,000 × D (₹) | 10,00,000 | 0 |
| Retained earnings = 5,00,000 − Dividend (₹) | (5,00,000) | 5,00,000 |
| Funds to raise = 10,00,000 − Retained (₹) | 15,00,000 | 5,00,000 |
| ΔN = Funds to raise ÷ P₁ | 15,00,000 ÷ 100 = 15,000 | 5,00,000 ÷ 110 = 4,545.45 |

**WN-3: Value of the firm**
Value nP₀ = [ (N + ΔN) P₁ − (I − E) ] ⁄ (1 + ke), where I = investment, E = earnings.

| Particulars | (a) Dividend ₹10 | (b) No dividend |
|---|---|---|
| (N + ΔN) × P₁ (₹) | 1,15,000 × 100 = 1,15,00,000 | 1,04,545.45 × 110 = 1,15,00,000 |
| Less: (I − E) = 10,00,000 − 5,00,000 (₹) | (5,00,000) | (5,00,000) |
| Net (₹) | 1,10,00,000 | 1,10,00,000 |
| ÷ (1 + ke) = ÷ 1.10 (₹) | **1,00,00,000** | **1,00,00,000** |

**Main Solution — Conclusion of valuation**
The value of the firm is **₹1,00,00,000 under both policies** (= 1,00,000 shares × ₹100). Hence the dividend decision does **not** affect the wealth of the shareholders.

**Arbitrage / homemade dividend:** A shareholder holding 1 share who *wants* ₹10 cash but the firm pays no dividend can simply **sell a fraction of a share** at the higher year-end price of ₹110 to realise the ₹10 — ending with the identical wealth as the dividend-paying case (₹100 share + ₹10 cash = ₹110 either way). **Answer:** Value of firm = **₹1,00,00,000 under both dividend policies** → under MM, **dividend policy is irrelevant** to shareholder wealth.

### Q47. Ch: Dividend Decisions — MM Hypothesis: Assumptions (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the assumptions of the Modigliani–Miller (MM) hypothesis on dividend policy and explain, in brief, the crux of the "arbitrage" argument on which it rests.

**Answer:**
The Modigliani–Miller (MM) hypothesis holds that under a set of ideal conditions the dividend policy of a firm is **irrelevant** — it has no effect on the market value of the share or the wealth of the shareholders, which are determined solely by the firm's earning power and investment policy. The assumptions of the hypothesis are:
1. **Perfect capital markets** — investors are rational, information is freely available, there are no transaction costs, and securities are infinitely divisible.
2. **No taxes**, or no differential tax treatment between dividends and capital gains.
3. **No flotation or transaction costs** on the issue of new securities.
4. The firm has a **fixed investment policy** that is not changed by the dividend decision.
5. **No uncertainty** — investors can forecast future prices and dividends with certainty (a "perfect certainty" assumption later relaxed).

**Crux of the arbitrage argument:** If dividends were to add value, two identical firms differing only in payout would sell at different prices. Investors would then buy the underpriced firm and sell the overpriced one; a firm paying larger dividends has to raise an exactly offsetting amount of fresh capital, so the gain from the dividend is precisely cancelled by the dilution/decline in the terminal share value. This arbitrage process continues until the values are equalised, proving that dividend policy cannot alter the firm's value.

**Conclusion:** Given perfect markets, no taxes, no flotation costs and a fixed investment policy, arbitrage ensures that dividend policy is **irrelevant** to shareholder wealth under the MM hypothesis.

*(Full-marks tip: list at least four assumptions and explain that the dividend paid is exactly offset by external financing — the examiner rewards linking the assumptions to the arbitrage mechanism rather than merely listing them.)*

### Q51. Ch: Dividend Decisions — Determinants of Dividend Policy and Dividend Stability (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** "Even where dividend theory suggests a particular payout, a company's actual dividend decision is shaped by several practical factors." Discuss the principal **determinants of dividend policy**, and explain what is meant by a policy of **dividend stability**.

**Answer:**
Regardless of the payout indicated by Walter's, Gordon's or the MM models, the dividend a company actually declares is governed by several practical considerations. The principal **determinants of dividend policy** are:
1. **Liquidity / cash position** — dividends are paid in cash, so even a profitable firm short of liquidity may restrict the payout.
2. **Financing needs and investment opportunities** — a firm with abundant profitable projects retains more (as Walter/Gordon prescribe for growth firms), while a firm with few opportunities distributes more.
3. **Legal constraints** — dividends can be paid only out of profits, subject to the provisions of the Companies Act, 2013 (e.g. Section 123 and the Companies (Declaration and Payment of Dividend) Rules), which require transfer/compliance conditions.
4. **Access to the capital market** — a firm that can easily raise external funds can afford a more liberal dividend, whereas one with limited access conserves internal funds.
5. **Shareholders' expectations and the tax position** — the composition of the shareholder base (income-seeking vs growth-seeking) and the relative taxation of dividends versus capital gains influence the decision.
6. **Stability of earnings and control considerations** — firms with stable earnings pay steadier dividends, and existing owners may retain profits to avoid dilution of control through fresh equity.

**Dividend stability** means maintaining a **regular, predictable pattern** of dividend payments rather than letting the dividend fluctuate directly with each year's earnings. It commonly takes three forms: (a) a **constant dividend per share** (a fixed rupee amount, often with a "constant plus extra" in good years); (b) a **constant payout ratio** (a fixed percentage of earnings, so the amount varies with profit); and (c) a **stable rupee dividend plus extra dividend** in prosperous years. A stable policy is valued because it signals financial soundness, meets the needs of income-dependent investors, and supports the market price of the share.

**Conclusion:** The actual dividend decision balances the theoretical optimum against liquidity, investment needs, legal limits, market access and shareholder preferences; and most firms favour **dividend stability** because a steady, predictable dividend enhances investor confidence and share value.

The **cost of trade credit foregone (implicit interest cost)** of not availing the cash discount is:
Cost = [ d / (100 − d) ] × [ 365 / (N − t) ]
where d = discount %, N = net credit period, t = discount period.

**WN-1: Supplier A (2/10, net 45)**
Cost = [2 / (100 − 2)] × [365 / (45 − 10)]
= (2 / 98) × (365 / 35)
= 0.020408 × 10.4286 = **21.29% per annum**.

**WN-2: Supplier B (1/15, net 60)**
Cost = [1 / (100 − 1)] × [365 / (60 − 15)]
= (1 / 99) × (365 / 45)
= 0.010101 × 8.1111 = **8.19% per annum**.

**Decision table**

| Supplier | Cost of credit foregone | Bank rate | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 21.29% | 15% | Cost > bank rate → **Avail the discount** (borrow if needed) |
| B | 8.19% | 15% | Cost < bank rate → **Forgo discount, pay on day 60** |

**Answer:** For **Supplier A**, borrow at 15% and take the 2% discount (saving 21.29% − 15% = 6.29%). For **Supplier B**, do not take the discount; the implicit cost (8.19%) is cheaper than bank finance, so stretch payment to the 60th day.

*(Full-marks tip: The decision rule is to avail the discount only when its implicit cost exceeds the firm's borrowing rate. A frequent error is using (N) rather than (N − t) in the denominator — the extra credit is enjoyed only for the days between the discount date and the due date.)*

### Q55. Ch: Types of Financing & Financial Markets — Factoring (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the meaning of **factoring** as a source of working-capital finance. State its principal features and indicate the type of firm for which factoring is most suitable.

**Answer:**
**Factoring** is an arrangement under which a firm (the client) sells its trade receivables (book debts) to a specialised financial intermediary called a **factor**, who takes over the administration, collection and, where agreed, the credit risk of those receivables in return for a commission and interest. It is essentially a mechanism of financing and managing receivables rather than a loan.

The **principal features** of factoring are:
1. **Assignment of debts** — the client assigns its receivables to the factor, who becomes responsible for their collection.
2. **Prepayment / advance** — the factor immediately pays the client a large proportion (usually 75%–90%) of the invoice value, and the **balance (the factor reserve)** is released on realisation, after deducting charges.
3. **Two components of cost** — a **factoring commission/service charge** for administration and collection, and **interest/discount charge** on the amount prepaid.
4. **Recourse or non-recourse** — under **recourse factoring** the credit risk of bad debts remains with the client; under **non-recourse factoring** the factor bears the loss on approved debts.
5. **Sales-ledger administration** — the factor maintains the client's sales ledger, sends statements and follows up collections, relieving the client of that function.

**Suitability:** Factoring suits firms with a **large volume of credit sales spread over many customers**, growing firms that face working-capital strain from rising receivables, and firms that wish to **outsource collection and reduce bad-debt risk** while freeing management time for core operations. It is less suitable where sales are few but large in value, or where customer relationships require the seller's own direct handling of collections.

**Conclusion:** Factoring converts book debts into ready cash, transfers the burden of collection (and, in the non-recourse form, of bad debts) to the factor, and is best suited to firms with numerous credit customers and a need to accelerate cash inflows.

*(Full-marks tip: State both cost components (commission AND interest) and clearly distinguish recourse from non-recourse — examiners look for the risk-bearing distinction and at least one point on suitability.)*

### Q56. Ch: Types of Financing & Financial Markets — Leasing (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between a **finance lease** and an **operating lease**, and briefly state the advantages of leasing as a source of finance to the lessee.

**Answer:**
A **lease** is a contract whereby the owner of an asset (the **lessor**) grants to another party (the **lessee**) the right to use the asset for an agreed period in return for periodic payments called lease rentals. Leases are broadly classified into finance leases and operating leases.

**Distinction between Finance Lease and Operating Lease**

| Basis | Finance Lease | Operating Lease |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Transfers substantially all risks and rewards incidental to ownership to the lessee | Ownership risks and rewards are retained by the lessor |
| Lease period | Covers the major part of the economic life of the asset (long-term, non-cancellable) | Short-term, usually cancellable, much shorter than the asset's life |
| Cost recovery | Rentals recover the full cost of the asset plus the lessor's return | A single lease does not recover full cost; asset is leased repeatedly |
| Maintenance | Usually borne by the lessee | Usually borne by the lessor |
| Purpose | Essentially a financing device (alternative to a loan) | Provision of asset-use with flexibility |
| Example | Plant and machinery, ships, aircraft on long lease | Computers, vehicles, photocopiers on short hire |

**Advantages of leasing to the lessee:**
1. **100% financing** — the asset can be used without a large initial capital outlay, conserving own funds.
2. **Convenience and speed** — documentation is simpler and faster than raising a term loan.
3. **Flexibility** — lease rentals can be tailored to the lessee's cash flows; operating leases allow the asset to be returned when obsolete.
4. **Off the risk of obsolescence** (in an operating lease) rests with the lessor.
5. **Tax treatment** — lease rentals are a fully deductible business expense.

**Conclusion:** A finance lease is in substance a means of financing the acquisition of an asset, whereas an operating lease is a service-oriented, flexible short-term hire; leasing enables the lessee to obtain the use of assets without heavy upfront investment.

*(Full-marks tip: The single most-rewarded point is "transfer of risks and rewards of ownership" — the defining line between the two lease types. Support it with the lease-period and cost-recovery points.)*

### Q57. Ch: Types of Financing & Financial Markets — Commercial Paper (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** What is **Commercial Paper (CP)**? Explain its salient features and state the eligibility conditions and advantages of raising short-term funds through commercial paper.

**Answer:**
**Commercial Paper** is an **unsecured money-market instrument issued in the form of a promissory note** by highly-rated corporates (and, where permitted, primary dealers and financial institutions) to raise short-term funds. It was introduced in India in **1990** and is regulated by the **Reserve Bank of India** through its guidelines.

**Salient features:**
1. **Unsecured and negotiable** — it is issued at a **discount to face value** and redeemed at par; there is no underlying security.
2. **Denomination** — issued in denominations of **₹5 lakh and multiples thereof**.
3. **Maturity** — ranges from a **minimum of 7 days to a maximum of 1 year** from the date of issue.
4. **Credit rating** — the issue must be **rated by an approved credit-rating agency** (e.g., CRISIL, ICRA, CARE), and the rating must be at least the minimum stipulated (P-2 / A-2 grade or better).
5. **Issue mechanics** — issued through an **Issuing and Paying Agent (IPA)**, which is a scheduled bank.

**Eligibility conditions for an issuing company:**
1. Its **tangible net worth** must be not less than **₹4 crore** as per the latest audited balance sheet.
2. It must have been **sanctioned a working-capital limit** by banks/financial institutions and the account must be classified as a **standard asset**.
3. It must obtain the **specified minimum credit rating**.

**Advantages:**
1. It is a **cheaper source** of short-term finance than bank borrowing for highly-rated firms, as the discount rate is usually below the bank's lending rate.
2. It provides the issuer **flexibility** and an alternative to bank credit, diversifying the funding base.
3. Being negotiable, it offers **liquidity** to investors and thus attracts subscribers.

**Conclusion:** Commercial paper is a short-term, unsecured, rated promissory note that enables financially strong companies to raise working-capital funds directly from the money market at a cost generally lower than bank finance.

### Q64. Ch: Types of Financing & Financial Markets — Money-Market Instruments (Marks: 5) [Theory]

### Q66. Ch: Working Capital Management — Optimum Cash Balance (Baumol Model) (Marks: 5) [Problem]

|---|---|---|
| Transaction cost | 20 × ₹125 | 2,500 |
| Opportunity (holding) cost | (₹50,000 ÷ 2) × 10% | 2,500 |
| **Total cost** | | **5,000** |

### Q67. Ch: Types of Financing & Financial Markets — Cost of Commercial Paper (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** Stellar Ltd. proposes to raise short-term funds by issuing **commercial paper of face value ₹5,00,00,000** for a maturity of **91 days**. The paper will be issued at an **effective interest (discount) rate of 10% per annum**. In addition, the company will incur **issue expenses (rating fee, stamp duty and IPA charges) of ₹1,50,000**. Determine the **net amount realised** on the issue and the **effective annual cost of funds** raised through the commercial paper. (Use a 365-day year.)

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Discount on issue**
Discount = Face value × rate × (days ÷ 365)
= ₹5,00,00,000 × 10% × (91 ÷ 365)
= ₹5,00,00,000 × 0.0249315 = **₹12,46,575**.

**WN-2: Net amount realised**

| Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Face value | 5,00,00,000 |
| **Less:** Discount | (12,46,575) |
| Sale proceeds | 4,87,53,425 |
| **Less:** Issue expenses | (1,50,000) |
| **Net amount realised** | **4,86,03,425** |

**WN-3: Effective annual cost of funds**
Total cost for 91 days = Discount + Issue expenses = ₹12,46,575 + ₹1,50,000 = ₹13,96,575.
Effective cost (p.a.) = (Total cost ÷ Net amount realised) × (365 ÷ 91)
= (₹13,96,575 ÷ ₹4,86,03,425) × 4.0110
= 0.028734 × 4.0110 = 0.11525 = **11.53% per annum**.

**Answer:** Net amount realised on the issue = **₹4,86,03,425**; effective annual cost of the commercial paper = **11.53%**. (The effective cost exceeds the 10% headline discount rate because of the issue expenses and because the discount is deducted upfront on the face value.)

### Q68. Ch: Types of Financing & Financial Markets — Primary vs. Secondary Market (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between the **primary market** and the **secondary market**, and briefly explain the **functions of the secondary (stock) market**.

**Answer:**
The **capital market** through which corporates and governments raise long-term funds has two interlinked segments — the primary market and the secondary market.

The **primary market (new-issue market)** is the market in which **securities are issued for the first time** and funds flow directly from investors to the issuing company (e.g., through an IPO, further public offer, rights issue or private placement). The **secondary market (stock exchange)** is the market in which **existing/already-issued securities are bought and sold among investors**; here funds flow from one investor to another, not to the company.

**Distinction between Primary Market and Secondary Market**

| Basis | Primary Market | Secondary Market |
|---|---|---|
| Nature of securities | New securities issued for the first time | Existing securities traded among investors |
| Flow of funds | From investors to the issuing company | From one investor to another investor |
| Parties | Company and investors | Investors (buyers and sellers) |
| Price | Determined/fixed by the issuer (issue price) | Determined by demand and supply on the exchange |
| Frequency | Occasional (only when the company issues securities) | Continuous, day-to-day trading |

**Functions of the secondary market:**
1. **Provides liquidity and marketability** — investors can convert their holdings into cash readily, which in turn encourages fresh investment in the primary market.
2. **Continuous price discovery** — regular trading establishes fair market prices for securities.
3. **Mobilises savings and channels them** into productive investment by making financial assets easily transferable.

**Conclusion:** The primary market creates securities and raises fresh capital for issuers, while the secondary market provides the liquidity and price discovery that make such securities attractive; the two are complementary and together constitute an efficient capital market.

*(Full-marks tip: The load-bearing distinction is the "flow of funds" — to the company (primary) versus between investors (secondary). Support it with the liquidity function, which is why an active secondary market underpins a successful primary market.)*

### Q69. Ch: Introduction to Strategic Management — Stages of the Strategic Management Process & Who is Responsible (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** "Strategic management is not a one-time activity done by the Managing Director alone; it is a continuous process with distinct stages, and each stage draws in a different set of people in the organisation." Explain the stages of the strategic management process and indicate at which stage the involvement of different organisational levels is the greatest.

**Answer:** Strategic management is the process by which an organisation determines its long-term direction and mobilises its resources to achieve it in a dynamic environment. It is best understood as a sequence of four inter-related stages, and it is a myth (as clarified in the study material) that it is done only by the top-most executive — in reality it is a shared responsibility across levels.

1. **Environmental / situation analysis** — The organisation scans its external environment (opportunities and threats) and appraises its internal capabilities (strengths and weaknesses). Because information comes from every function — marketing, operations, finance, HR — this stage draws on **middle and operating managers** the most, as they are closest to customers, competitors and processes.

2. **Strategy formulation (determination of strategy)** — Based on the analysis, the vision, mission, objectives and the strategy (corporate, business and functional) are set. This stage is dominated by **top management / the board**, because it concerns the whole enterprise and long-term commitment of resources.

3. **Strategy implementation** — The chosen strategy is put into action by designing structure, allocating budgets, motivating people and building systems. Implementation is where the **largest number of people participate**, because middle and lower-level managers and employees actually convert plans into operating results — hence involvement is greatest at this stage.

4. **Strategy evaluation and control** — Actual performance is compared with standards, deviations are analysed and corrective action is taken, feeding back into a fresh round of analysis. This is largely a **top-and-middle management** responsibility.

| Stage | Core activity | Level most involved |
|---|---|---|
| Environmental analysis | SWOT / scanning | Middle & operating |
| Strategy formulation | Vision, mission, objectives, strategy | Top management / board |
| Strategy implementation | Structure, resources, execution | **All levels — greatest breadth** |
| Evaluation & control | Feedback and correction | Top & middle |

**Conclusion:** Strategic management is a continuous, cyclical process; formulation is concentrated at the top, but implementation involves the greatest number of people, making strategy everybody's business.

*(Full-marks tip: examiners reward naming all four stages in the correct sequence AND correctly stating that implementation (not formulation) involves the widest participation; a common deduction is treating strategy as a purely top-management, one-time exercise.)*

### Q70. Ch: Introduction to Strategic Management — Hierarchy of Strategic Intent (Vision → Mission → Goals → Objectives) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** "GreenLeaf Organics", a start-up, has drafted the following four statements but has jumbled them up:
(i) "To become India's most trusted name in chemical-free food by 2035."
(ii) "We exist to make safe, affordable organic food available to every Indian household while supporting small farmers."
(iii) "Achieve ₹500 crore revenue and presence in 100 cities within 5 years."
(iv) "Increase organic farmer tie-ups from 400 to 1,000 and reduce packaging cost by 8% in the current financial year."

Identify which level of the hierarchy of strategic intent each statement represents, and briefly explain the hierarchy.

**Answer:** The **hierarchy of strategic intent** translates a broad aspiration into concrete, measurable action. It moves from the most abstract and long-term at the top to the most concrete and short-term at the bottom, so that day-to-day work stays aligned with the founder's ultimate purpose. The four levels are:

- **Vision** — describes what the organisation aspires to *become* in the long run; it is future-oriented, inspirational and broad.
- **Mission** — states the organisation's fundamental *purpose*: what business it is in, whom it serves and why it exists today.
- **Goals** — the intended future states / desired results, broader and stated in fairly general terms, giving direction over the medium term.
- **Objectives** — the specific, measurable, time-bound ends to be achieved in the short run; they operationalise goals.

Classification of GreenLeaf's statements:

| Statement | Level of strategic intent | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| (ii) "…make safe, affordable organic food available… supporting small farmers." | **Mission** | States present purpose, customers served and reason to exist. |
| (i) "…most trusted name in chemical-free food by 2035." | **Vision** | Long-run aspiration of what it wants to *become*. |
| (iii) "₹500 crore revenue, 100 cities within 5 years." | **Goal** | Broad desired future result over medium term. |
| (iv) "Farmer tie-ups 400→1,000 and packaging cost −8% this year." | **Objective** | Specific, measurable, time-bound (current year). |

**Conclusion:** The correct top-down order is Vision (i) → Mission (ii) → Goals (iii) → Objectives (iv); each lower level makes the level above it operational and measurable.

*(Full-marks tip: examiners look for correct matching PLUS the reason (aspiration vs purpose vs broad result vs measurable/time-bound); mixing up vision and mission is the most common error — remember mission = present purpose, vision = future aspiration.)*

### Q71. Ch: External Environment Analysis — Porter's Five Forces Applied to the Indian Ride-Hailing Industry (Marks: 8) [Application]
**Question:** A new investor is evaluating whether to enter the Indian **app-based ride-hailing (cab aggregator)** industry. Using Michael Porter's Five Forces model, analyse the attractiveness of this industry and give a reasoned conclusion on its overall profitability potential.

**Answer:** Porter's Five Forces model holds that the profit potential of an industry is determined by five competitive forces; the stronger the forces, the lower the industry's attractiveness. Applied to Indian ride-hailing:

1. **Threat of new entrants (HIGH):** Although building a two-sided network (drivers and riders) needs capital and technology, the underlying app technology is not proprietary, drivers do not own exclusivity, and well-funded players (including global and local start-ups) can enter. Low switching cost for drivers/riders weakens entry barriers → high threat.

2. **Bargaining power of buyers/riders (HIGH):** Riders face near-zero switching cost, routinely keep multiple apps and compare fares/coupons instantly, making them very price-sensitive → strong buyer power.

3. **Bargaining power of suppliers/drivers (MEDIUM-HIGH):** Drivers are the key "supplier" of capacity; they too multi-home across platforms and can shift to the platform paying higher incentives, and can unionise/protest over commissions → meaningful supplier power.

4. **Threat of substitutes (HIGH):** Public transport (metro, buses), auto-rickshaws, personal two-wheelers/cars, and now bike-taxis and rentals are ready substitutes, capping fares → strong substitute pressure.

5. **Rivalry among existing competitors (VERY HIGH):** A few large aggregators fight for the same riders and drivers with discounts and incentives; high fixed technology cost and undifferentiated service intensify price-based rivalry → very high.

| Force | Intensity | Effect on attractiveness |
|---|---|---|
| New entrants | High | Unfavourable |
| Buyer power | High | Unfavourable |
| Supplier power | Medium-High | Unfavourable |
| Substitutes | High | Unfavourable |
| Competitive rivalry | Very High | Unfavourable |

**Conclusion:** All five forces are strong, so the collective strength of the forces is high and the structural profitability of Indian ride-hailing is **low/thin**; entry is attractive only for a player with deep pockets, a differentiating capability (safety, super-app ecosystem, driver loyalty) or a cost advantage that can blunt these forces.

*(Full-marks tip: examiners reward applying each force to the specific industry with a reason and an intensity, then a synthesised verdict — not a textbook definition of the five forces in the abstract; failing to conclude on overall attractiveness is a common deduction.)*

### Q72. Ch: External Environment Analysis — Strategic Groups & Mobility Barriers (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Within the Indian passenger-car industry, some firms compete on low price and fuel economy for mass buyers, while others compete on luxury, brand and technology for premium buyers. Explain the concepts of a **strategic group** and **mobility barriers**, and how they help a firm analyse competition more sharply than looking at the industry as a whole.

**Answer:** Firms in the same industry do not all follow the same strategy; a **strategic group** is a set of firms within an industry that follow the *same or similar strategy* along the strategic dimensions that matter — for example price positioning, product range, quality/technology level, distribution reach or degree of vertical integration. Grouping competitors this way lets a firm see who its *direct* rivals really are, rather than treating every firm in the industry as an equal competitor.

In the Indian passenger-car industry, for instance, one strategic group is the **mass-market, value-for-money group** (low price, high fuel economy, wide service network) and another is the **premium/luxury group** (high price, advanced technology, aspirational brand). Rivalry is most intense *within* a group; firms in different groups compete less directly because they target different buyers.

**Mobility barriers** are the factors that make it difficult and costly for a firm to *shift from one strategic group to another*. They are, in effect, entry barriers to a particular group. Examples: a mass-market maker wanting to move into the luxury group must build a premium brand image, R&D and technology, and premium dealerships — all expensive and slow. These barriers protect the profitability of firms already inside a group and explain why positions are stable over time.

| Concept | Meaning | Strategic use |
|---|---|---|
| Strategic group | Firms following similar strategy on key dimensions | Identifies direct/closest rivals |
| Mobility barrier | Obstacle to moving between groups | Explains stability and who *can* threaten whom |

**Conclusion:** Mapping strategic groups and the mobility barriers around them gives a far sharper picture of competitive rivalry than an industry-wide view, helping the firm anticipate which rivals can attack its position and how defensible its own group is.

*(Full-marks tip: examiners want the link between the two ideas — mobility barriers guard strategic groups — and a concrete grouping example; merely defining "strategic group" without explaining mobility barriers or their protective role loses marks.)*

### Q73. Ch: Internal Environment Analysis — Three Tests of a Core Competence (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** The management of "Aster Diagnostics" claims that its "fast and accurate pathology testing" is a core competence. Explain the three tests prescribed (by Hamel and Prahalad) that a capability must pass to qualify as a core competence, and apply them to judge Aster's claim.

**Answer:** A **core competence** is a unique, integrated set of skills and technologies — a bundle of capabilities — that results from an organisation's collective learning and enables it to provide a particular benefit to customers. Not every strength is a core competence. According to Hamel and Prahalad, a capability qualifies as a core competence only if it passes **three tests**:

1. **Customer value test** — The competence must make a *significant contribution to the perceived customer benefit* of the end product/service. *Applied to Aster:* accurate and quick diagnosis directly affects a patient's treatment and confidence, so it clearly delivers a benefit customers value. **Pass.**

2. **Competitor differentiation (uniqueness) test** — The competence must be *competitively unique*, i.e., difficult for rivals to imitate; if every laboratory can do it equally, it is a necessary capability, not a core competence. *Applied to Aster:* if Aster's speed and accuracy rest on a distinctive combination of trained specialists, proprietary quality systems and technology that rivals cannot easily copy, it passes; if any lab can buy the same machines and match it, it fails this test.

3. **Extendability (application to new markets) test** — The competence should provide *potential access to a wide variety of markets*, i.e., it can be leveraged into new products/markets. *Applied to Aster:* the underlying capability can be extended to home-collection testing, tele-pathology, preventive health packages and new cities. **Pass.**

| Test | Question it asks | Aster verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Customer value | Does it add benefit customers value? | Pass |
| Competitor differentiation | Is it hard to imitate / unique? | Pass only if genuinely distinctive |
| Extendability | Can it open new markets? | Pass |

**Conclusion:** "Fast and accurate testing" is a genuine core competence for Aster **only if** it is also competitively unique (hard to imitate); it easily satisfies the customer-value and extendability tests, so the decisive test is differentiation.

*(Full-marks tip: examiners reward naming all three tests correctly and applying each to the facts with a pass/fail judgement; the extendability test is the one students most often forget, and simply asserting "it is a core competence" without the uniqueness test is a common deduction.)*

### Q74. Ch: Internal Environment Analysis — Value Chain: Primary vs Support Activities & Linkages (Marks: 8) [Application]
**Question:** "PureBrew", a packaged cold-coffee company, wants to find where it creates value and where it can cut cost. Explain Michael Porter's Value Chain Analysis, distinguishing primary and support activities, and illustrate how **linkages** between activities can be a source of competitive advantage for PureBrew.

**Answer:** Michael Porter's **Value Chain Analysis** is a tool for internal environment analysis that disaggregates a firm into its *strategically relevant activities* to understand the behaviour of costs and the existing and potential sources of differentiation. The value chain consists of value activities and a margin (the difference between total value created and the cost of performing the activities). Value activities are of two broad types:

**Primary activities** — those directly involved in creating and delivering the product:
- **Inbound logistics** — receiving and storing milk, coffee, packaging (for PureBrew, cold-chain storage of dairy inputs).
- **Operations** — brewing, mixing, pasteurising and bottling.
- **Outbound logistics** — chilled distribution to retailers.
- **Marketing and sales** — branding, pricing, trade promotion.
- **Service** — handling returns, retailer support, freshness guarantees.

**Support (secondary) activities** — those that enable and back up the primary activities:
- **Firm infrastructure** — general management, finance, quality systems.
- **Human resource management** — recruiting and training operations and sales staff.
- **Technology development** — recipe R&D, shelf-life technology, plant automation.
- **Procurement** — sourcing of milk, beans, bottles.

| Category | Activities |
|---|---|
| Primary | Inbound logistics, Operations, Outbound logistics, Marketing & sales, Service |
| Support | Firm infrastructure, HRM, Technology development, Procurement |

**Linkages** are the *relationships between the way one value activity is performed and the cost or performance of another*. Managing them well ("optimisation" and "coordination" of linkages) is itself a hard-to-imitate source of competitive advantage. For PureBrew:
- A linkage between **Technology development (a longer-shelf-life recipe)** and **Outbound logistics (fewer chilled-return trips and less spoilage)** lowers total cost.
- A linkage between **Procurement (better-quality beans)** and **Operations & Marketing (a premium taste claim)** supports differentiation.
- A linkage between **Inbound cold-chain and Service (freshness guarantee)** reduces complaints.

Because these linkages cut across departments, rivals find them difficult to copy, converting internal coordination into external advantage.

**Conclusion:** By mapping its primary and support activities and, crucially, managing the *linkages* among them, PureBrew can locate its cost drivers and differentiation sources and build an advantage that is greater than the sum of individual activities.

*(Full-marks tip: examiners reward the full list of primary and support activities in a table AND at least two concrete cross-activity linkages tied to the case; listing the activities but ignoring "linkages / margin" — the heart of the model — is the usual reason for lost marks.)*

### Q76. Ch: Introduction to Strategic Management — Difference between Strategic and Operational Decisions (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between **strategic decisions** and **operational (routine) decisions**, giving suitable examples.

**Answer:** Every organisation takes decisions at different levels; the character of a decision changes as one moves from the top to the operating level. **Strategic decisions** shape the long-term direction and scope of the whole organisation, whereas **operational decisions** deal with the day-to-day running of the business within the framework set by strategy.

| Basis | Strategic decisions | Operational (routine) decisions |
|---|---|---|
| Time horizon | Long-term | Short-term / day-to-day |
| Nature | Complex, one-of-a-kind | Routine, repetitive |
| Level taking them | Top management / board | Middle & lower management |
| Scope | Whole organisation | A department / function |
| Information | Uncertain, external, incomplete | Largely internal, certain |
| Impact of error | Serious, long-lasting | Limited, easily corrected |
| Resource commitment | Major, hard to reverse | Minor, reversible |
| Example | Entering a new country; acquiring a rival | Scheduling a shift; reordering stock |

Strategic decisions provide the *ends and boundaries* within which operational decisions are made; operational decisions ensure the *efficient use of resources* to carry out the strategy.

**Conclusion:** Strategic decisions determine "what business and direction" (effectiveness) and are top-driven and hard to reverse, while operational decisions determine "how to run it efficiently" day to day; both are necessary but differ in horizon, scope, level and impact.

### Q77. Ch: External Environment Analysis — Competitive Landscape & Steps to Understand Competition (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the term "competitive landscape" and state the steps a company should follow to understand its competitive landscape.

**Answer:** The **competitive landscape** is a business-analysis framework that identifies the pattern of competition in an industry — who the competitors are, what they offer, and their strengths and weaknesses — so that the firm can understand the dynamics of competition and craft a strategy to gain and hold an advantage. It is not a static picture; it keeps changing as rivals, technology and customer needs change, so it must be scanned continuously.

To understand the competitive landscape, a company follows these steps:

1. **Identify the competitors** — Determine the industry and the direct and indirect firms competing for the same customers.
2. **Understand the competitors** — Study each competitor's product/service offering, target market and positioning to know how they compete.
3. **Determine the strengths of the competitors** — Analyse what each rival does well (cost, brand, distribution, technology) that draws customers to it.
4. **Determine the weaknesses of the competitors** — Find the gaps and shortcomings in rivals' offerings that the firm could exploit.
5. **Put all the information together** — Combine the above into a coherent picture (for example a strengths-and-weaknesses comparison) to identify opportunities and threats.

Using this understanding, the firm can decide where to position itself, which rivals to attack or avoid, and how to differentiate.

**Conclusion:** Studying the competitive landscape through these five steps enables a company to anticipate competitors' moves and to position its strategy where it can compete most effectively.

*(Full-marks tip: examiners reward the five sequential steps in order plus a correct definition; skipping the "put it all together" step or confusing this with Porter's Five Forces is a common deduction.)*

### Q78. Ch: Internal Environment Analysis — Strengths, Weaknesses & SWOT / TOWS Strategic Options (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** After analysing itself, "Zephyr Apparel" lists Strengths (S1 strong design team, S2 loyal urban customers), Weaknesses (W1 weak online presence, W2 high dependence on a single supplier), and from the environment Opportunities (O1 booming e-commerce, O2 rising demand for sustainable fashion) and Threats (T1 fast-fashion imports, T2 volatile cotton prices). Explain how SWOT/TOWS analysis is used to generate strategic options, and derive one option under each of the four combinations.

**Answer:** **SWOT analysis** examines the fit between the organisation's internal factors — **Strengths** (things it does well) and **Weaknesses** (areas where it is deficient) — and its external factors — **Opportunities** and **Threats**. The purpose is not merely to list items but to *match* internal and external factors to generate strategies. When the matching is done systematically it is called the **TOWS matrix**, which produces four sets of strategic options:

| | Opportunities (O) | Threats (T) |
|---|---|---|
| **Strengths (S)** | **SO (Maxi-Maxi):** use strengths to seize opportunities | **ST (Maxi-Mini):** use strengths to counter threats |
| **Weaknesses (W)** | **WO (Mini-Maxi):** overcome weaknesses by using opportunities | **WT (Mini-Mini):** minimise weaknesses and avoid threats |

Applied to Zephyr Apparel:
- **SO strategy:** Use the strong design team (S1) to launch a sustainable-fashion line (O2) — leveraging a strength to grab an opportunity.
- **ST strategy:** Use loyal urban customers (S2) and design strength (S1) to differentiate against cheap fast-fashion imports (T1).
- **WO strategy:** Overcome the weak online presence (W1) by riding the e-commerce boom (O1) — build a strong D2C website/app.
- **WT strategy:** Reduce single-supplier dependence (W2) and hedge against volatile cotton prices (T2) by diversifying suppliers and materials — a defensive move to survive.

**Conclusion:** SWOT identifies the four factors, and the TOWS matrix converts them into four concrete strategy sets (SO, ST, WO, WT); the value lies in the *matching*, which gives Zephyr an actionable menu rather than a static list.

*(Full-marks tip: examiners reward the 2×2 TOWS matrix with the maxi/mini labels AND one tailored option in each cell; merely listing S, W, O, T without generating the four matched strategies is the most common reason marks are lost.)*

### Q79. Ch: Introduction to Strategic Management — Importance / Benefits and Limitations of Strategic Management (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** "Strategic management is desirable but not a guarantee of success." Discuss the importance (benefits) and the limitations of strategic management.

**Answer:** **Strategic management** gives an organisation a sense of long-term direction and helps it prepare for an uncertain future, but it is not a magic wand. Its benefits and limitations must both be appreciated.

**Importance / benefits of strategic management:**
1. It gives the organisation a clear **sense of direction** and a common purpose, aligning effort towards long-term goals.
2. It helps the firm be **proactive rather than reactive** — shaping its future instead of merely responding to events.
3. It prepares the organisation to **face a turbulent, changing environment** by anticipating opportunities and threats.
4. It provides a **framework for allocating resources** rationally among competing uses.
5. It improves **coordination and control**, since goals and standards are set in advance.

1. The **environment is highly uncertain and turbulent**, so even the best strategy can be overtaken by unforeseen change.
2. It is a **time-consuming and costly** exercise in analysis and planning.
3. It is **difficult to implement**; formulation is easier than execution.
4. There can be a **gap between formulation and reality** — plans made at the top may not fit ground conditions.
5. It does **not guarantee success**; competitive dynamics and luck also decide outcomes.

**Conclusion:** Strategic management substantially improves an organisation's readiness and direction and tilts the odds towards success, but because of environmental uncertainty and implementation difficulties it reduces risk rather than eliminating it — it is desirable, not a guarantee.

### Q80. Ch: External Environment Analysis — Micro (Task) vs Macro (General) Environment (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between the **micro-environment** and the **macro-environment** of a business, giving the key elements of each.

**Answer:** A firm's external environment is conventionally divided into two layers according to how closely and directly the forces touch the firm. The **micro-environment** (also called the task or operating environment) consists of the forces in the firm's *immediate* surroundings that directly affect its ability to serve customers and over which it may have *some* influence. The **macro-environment** (general environment) consists of the *broad societal* forces that affect all firms in the economy, are largely *uncontrollable*, and act on the firm only indirectly.

| Basis | Micro-environment | Macro-environment |
|---|---|---|
| Also called | Task / operating environment | General environment |
| Proximity | Immediate, close to firm | Broad, distant |
| Control | Firm may partly influence it | Beyond firm's control |
| Effect | Direct and specific to the firm | Indirect, affects all firms |
| Key elements | Customers, competitors, suppliers, intermediaries, financiers | Political, Economic, Socio-cultural, Technological, Legal, Environmental (PESTLE) forces |

The micro-environment differs from firm to firm even in the same industry (each has its own suppliers and customers), whereas the macro-environment is common to all firms in a country.

**Conclusion:** The micro-environment is the firm's immediate, partly-influenceable task environment (customers, suppliers, competitors), while the macro-environment is the broad, uncontrollable PESTLE backdrop; a firm must scan both to identify opportunities and threats.

*(Full-marks tip: examiners reward correctly listing the *elements* of each layer (task-environment players vs PESTLE forces) and the control/proximity distinction; putting "competitors" in the macro-environment is a common error.)*

### Q81. Ch: Internal Environment Analysis — Resource-Based View: Distinctive Competence & Sustainable Advantage (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the concept of **competitive advantage** and how it becomes *sustainable*. In this context, distinguish between a **distinctive competence** and a **core competence**, and state what characteristics a resource must have to yield a lasting advantage.

**Answer:** A firm has a **competitive advantage** when it is able to create more economic value — through lower cost or greater differentiation — than its rivals, and can thereby earn superior returns. An advantage is merely **sustainable** when competitors are *unable to imitate or neutralise it* over a long period.

A **distinctive competence** is something an organisation does *especially well in comparison to its competitors* — a competitive edge in a particular activity. A **core competence** is a special type of distinctive competence: a bundle of skills and technologies arising from collective learning that provides a valued customer benefit and access to many markets. Thus every core competence is a distinctive competence, but a distinctive competence becomes "core" only when it is central, hard to imitate and extendable across markets.

For a resource or competence to yield a *sustainable* advantage (the resource-based view), it should generally be:
1. **Valuable** — it must exploit an opportunity or neutralise a threat, i.e., add customer value.
2. **Rare** — not widely held by competitors; if everyone has it, it only achieves parity.
3. **Difficult / costly to imitate** — protected by uniqueness, history, causal ambiguity or complexity so rivals cannot easily copy it.
4. **Non-substitutable / well-organised to exploit** — no equivalent can replace it, and the firm is organised to capture its value.

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Distinctive competence | An activity a firm does better than rivals |
| Core competence | A valued, hard-to-imitate, extendable bundle of skills — a special distinctive competence |
| Sustainable advantage | Edge rivals cannot imitate/neutralise over time |

**Conclusion:** Competitive advantage rests on distinctive/core competences, but it becomes *sustainable* only when the underlying resources are valuable, rare, hard to imitate and non-substitutable; otherwise rivals catch up and the advantage erodes to competitive parity.

*(Full-marks tip: examiners reward linking core competence to sustainability via the imitability/rarity conditions and correctly distinguishing distinctive vs core competence; treating the two terms as identical, or omitting "hard to imitate", is a common deduction.)*

### Q82. Ch: Introduction to Strategic Management — Levels at Which Strategy Operates (Corporate, Business, Functional) (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** "Strategy is not made at a single level." Explain the **three levels of strategy** in a large multi-business organisation, indicating who is primarily responsible and the key question each level answers.

**Answer:** In a large, diversified organisation strategy is formulated at three hierarchical levels, each answering a different question and involving different managers. The levels are interdependent: higher-level strategy sets the frame for the level below it.

1. **Corporate-level strategy** — Formulated by the **board and top management (CEO/MD)**, it deals with the organisation as a whole and answers *"In which businesses / industries should we compete?"* It covers decisions on expansion, diversification, stability, retrenchment and allocation of resources across the various businesses.

2. **Business-level (SBU) strategy** — Formulated by the **head of each Strategic Business Unit (SBU) / division**, it deals with a single line of business and answers *"How should we compete in this particular industry/market?"* — e.g., through cost leadership, differentiation or focus, to build competitive advantage in that business.

3. **Functional-level strategy** — Formulated by **functional managers** (marketing, finance, production, HR), it deals with a single function and answers *"How do we support the business strategy within our function?"* — ensuring maximum resource productivity in day-to-day operations.

| Level | Responsible manager | Key question |
|---|---|---|
| Corporate | Board / CEO / top management | Which businesses to be in? |
| Business (SBU) | SBU / divisional head | How to compete in this business? |
| Functional | Functional managers | How to support the business strategy? |

The three levels must be *consistent and aligned* — functional strategies support business strategy, which in turn serves the corporate strategy.

**Conclusion:** Strategy operates at corporate, business and functional levels, each with its own responsible managers and question; their alignment is essential for the organisation to move coherently towards its objectives.

*(Full-marks tip: examiners reward stating for each level the responsible person AND the distinct question it answers, plus the alignment point; a single-business firm effectively merges the corporate and business levels — worth noting for full marks.)*

### Q83. Ch: External Environment Analysis — Industry Analysis: Key Factors an Organisation Should Consider (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Before entering an industry, a firm undertakes an "industry analysis". Explain what industry analysis involves and the key factors/dimensions a firm should examine to judge whether an industry is attractive.

**Answer:** **Industry analysis** is the study of a specific industry's structure, dynamics and profitability potential so that a firm can decide whether and how to compete in it. An "industry" is a group of firms whose products are close substitutes for one another. The analysis helps identify the opportunities and threats within the industry and its underlying attractiveness. The key factors/dimensions a firm should examine are:

1. **Strategic groups** — Whether the industry contains distinct groups of firms following similar strategies, and how intense rivalry is within and between these groups.
2. **Industry life cycle / market size and growth** — The stage of the industry (introduction, growth, maturity, decline); a growing industry generally offers more opportunity than a declining one.
3. **Nature and intensity of competition** — The number and strength of rivals and the basis of competition (price, quality, innovation), often studied through Porter's Five Forces.
4. **Profitability of the industry** — The structural ability of firms in the industry to earn healthy margins, driven by the collective strength of the competitive forces.
5. **Entry and exit barriers / mobility barriers** — How easy it is to enter (capital, technology, brand) and to exit; high entry barriers protect incumbents' profits.
6. **Cost structure and key success factors** — The main cost drivers and the factors a firm *must* get right to succeed in that industry (e.g., scale, distribution, technology).

By weighing these factors together the firm forms a view on whether the industry is attractive and where it could position itself.

**Conclusion:** Industry analysis examines strategic groups, the industry life cycle, the intensity of competition and profitability, entry/mobility barriers and key success factors; a firm should enter only where the balance of these factors indicates durable opportunity relative to threats.

*(Full-marks tip: examiners reward covering several distinct dimensions — not just Porter's Five Forces — and tying them to "attractiveness"; listing only one factor or repeating the five forces verbatim without the wider factors is a common deduction.)*

### Q84. Ch: Internal Environment Analysis — Strategic Drivers and Business Model / Capability Alignment (Marks: 8) [Application]
**Question:** "Nimbus Logistics" is a road-freight company that wants to reposition itself as a technology-driven, same-day delivery specialist. Using the concepts of **strategic drivers** and **internal capability analysis**, advise the management on (a) the strategic drivers it must get right, and (b) how it should analyse whether its internal environment supports this repositioning. Integrate the ideas of core competence and value chain in your answer.

**Answer:** Internal environment analysis judges whether an organisation has the *capabilities* needed to pursue its intended strategy. Repositioning Nimbus from ordinary road-freight to a *technology-driven same-day delivery specialist* is a strategic change that must be backed by the right strategic drivers and genuine internal capability, otherwise the strategy will fail at implementation.

**(a) Strategic drivers Nimbus must get right:** Strategic drivers are the fundamental factors that shape and propel an organisation's strategy — chiefly its **industries and markets, customers and their needs, products/services, and channels/geographies served**. For Nimbus the critical drivers are:
- **Customers and their changing needs** — e-commerce and quick-commerce customers now demand *speed and reliability*, which is the very basis of the new positioning.
- **Products/services** — a redefined service (guaranteed same-day delivery with real-time tracking) rather than plain point-to-point freight.
- **Channels and markets** — dense urban clusters where same-day delivery is feasible and valued.
- **Technology as an enabler** — routing algorithms, tracking apps and data — the driver that differentiates the new model.
Getting these drivers right defines *where and how* Nimbus intends to win.

**(b) Analysing whether the internal environment supports it:** Nimbus should audit its resources and capabilities to test the fit, using two lenses:

1. **Core competence test** — Does Nimbus possess (or can it build) a competence that is *valuable, hard to imitate and extendable*? A same-day network needs a distinctive bundle of fleet-density, warehouse micro-hubs, routing technology and trained staff. If this bundle passes the three tests (customer value — speed customers pay for; uniqueness — a network rivals cannot quickly replicate; extendability — usable across cities and product categories), it is a genuine core competence on which to base the strategy.

2. **Value chain analysis** — Nimbus should map its primary activities (inbound movement, operations/sorting, outbound last-mile delivery, marketing, service) and support activities (technology development, HRM, infrastructure, procurement) to see where value is created and where gaps lie. Crucially, it must build **linkages** — e.g., between *technology development (route optimisation)* and *outbound logistics (faster last mile)*, and between *operations (micro-hub sorting)* and *service (on-time guarantee)* — because these cross-activity linkages are what make speed both achievable and hard to copy.

| Analytical lens | What Nimbus checks | Fit for same-day strategy |
|---|---|---|
| Strategic drivers | Customers, service, channels, technology | Defines where/how to win |
| Core competence (3 tests) | Valuable? Unique? Extendable? | Is the speed-network a real advantage? |
| Value chain & linkages | Primary/support activities + coordination | Where value & cost lie; source of hard-to-imitate speed |

**Conclusion:** Nimbus should proceed with the repositioning only if internal analysis confirms it can build a same-day delivery *core competence* supported by well-linked value-chain activities; the strategic drivers point to a real market opportunity, but the decisive question is whether the internal environment (capabilities, technology, network) can deliver the speed that the new positioning promises.

*(Full-marks tip: examiners reward integrating three concepts — strategic drivers, core competence tests and value-chain linkages — around the case, with a capability-based go/no-go conclusion; answering with only a generic value chain and ignoring the drivers or the core-competence fit is the common reason for lost marks in such integrated application questions.)*

### Q85. Ch: Strategic Choices — Concentration vs Integration vs Diversification (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** A profitable mid-sized paint manufacturer, currently selling only decorative wall paints in three southern states, wants to grow. Its board is divided: one group wants to "sell more of the same paint in more states", a second wants to "buy the resin and packaging suppliers", and a third wants to "enter the unrelated business of adhesives and construction chemicals". Identify the expansion route each group is proposing and explain the rationale, and the risk, of each so that the board can choose.

**Answer:** All three proposals are variants of an **Expansion / Growth grand strategy**, but each represents a different directional route, and the choice turns on how far the firm moves from its present products and markets.

The **first group is proposing Expansion through Concentration** — intensively growing the *same* product (decorative wall paint) in the *same or new* geographic markets. Concentration means converging resources profitably in a narrow product-market range. Its rationale is that the firm exploits a business it already understands, so managerial risk is low and it deepens distinctive competence and market share. Using the Ansoff logic, "more paint in the same three states" is **market penetration**, while "the same paint in new states" is **market development**; developing a new emulsion for the same states would be **product development**. The risk is that concentration puts "all eggs in one basket" — a single product line, technology or market means that a demand slump, a substitute, or a regional downturn threatens the whole firm, and there is a ceiling to how far one product can grow.

The **second group is proposing Expansion through (Vertical) Integration** — acquiring the resin and packaging suppliers, which is **backward integration** (moving towards the source of raw materials). Rationale: it secures assured supply and quality, captures the supplier's margin, reduces bargaining power of suppliers, and can lower cost. The risk is that it raises the firm's fixed investment and exit barriers, ties it to a possibly sub-scale captive supplier, and forces it to manage a business (chemicals/packaging) with a different cost and technology structure from paint manufacturing.

The **third group is proposing Expansion through Diversification** — entering adhesives and construction chemicals. As these serve construction/repair customers and can share the firm's chemistry, distribution and brand, this is **concentric (related) diversification**; had they proposed a wholly unlinked business (say, insurance), it would be **conglomerate (unrelated) diversification**. Rationale: it spreads risk across businesses, uses a common thread of technology and channels to create synergy, and opens a larger growth pool than one product allows. The risk is that management is stretched into businesses where it has less competence, synergy is often overestimated, and unrelated diversification in particular can destroy value.

| Route | Movement from present product-market | Core rationale | Principal risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Concentration | Same product, same/new markets | Low risk, exploits known competence | "All eggs in one basket" |
| Vertical Integration | Own the supply/distribution stage | Assured supply, capture margin | High fixed cost, exit barriers, new skills |
| Diversification | New products/businesses | Spreads risk, synergy, larger growth pool | Managerial over-stretch, over-stated synergy |

**Conclusion:** The three groups are respectively advocating Concentration, Vertical (backward) Integration and (concentric) Diversification; the board should match the choice to its risk appetite and to where its distinctive competence can be most safely leveraged.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards correctly LABELLING each route AND giving both a rationale and a risk for each; a common deduction is confusing integration (owning a value-chain stage) with diversification (a new business) — state the defining test for each.)*

### Q86. Ch: Strategic Choices — Turnaround Strategy Signals (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** "Sunrise Ceramics Ltd." has reported falling market share for six consecutive quarters, negative cash flow, an eroding contribution margin, and rising employee attrition, and its bankers have flagged covenant breaches. The CEO argues "the market will recover on its own." As a strategy consultant, identify the internal and external signals that indicate a turnaround is required, and outline the action stages of a turnaround strategy.

**Answer:** A **turnaround strategy** is a retrenchment-type grand strategy adopted when an organisation, though worth saving, is bleeding on several fronts and must reverse a negative trend before it becomes terminal. - **Declining market share** quarter after quarter and falling contribution/margins.

- High employee turnover and **low morale**, and rising interest/finance burden.

### Q87. Ch: Strategic Choices — Stability Strategy & Grand Strategy Choice (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between a "Stability strategy" and an "Expansion strategy", and explain the circumstances in which a firm would prefer to adopt a Stability strategy.

**Answer:** A **grand strategy** (master/corporate strategy) indicates the basic direction of a firm's strategic actions. Two of the four grand strategies — Stability and Expansion — sit at opposite ends of the growth spectrum.

A **Stability strategy** is adopted when the firm is satisfied with its present position and chooses to continue pursuing the same or similar objectives, improving performance incrementally by managing the *same* products in the *same* markets with essentially the same functions. It is a "safety-oriented, status-quo" strategy involving little significant change. An **Expansion strategy** (growth), by contrast, involves redefining the business by adding new products/markets, substantially raising investment and the pace of activity, and pursuing higher growth as a deliberate departure from the past.

| Basis | Stability strategy | Expansion strategy |
|---|---|---|
| Attitude to change | Status-quo, incremental | Aggressive, redefines business |
| Products/markets | Same or similar | New products / new markets added |
| Risk & investment | Low | High |
| Managerial effort | Modest, refine existing | Substantial, learn new areas |
| Suitability | Mature/stable environment | Growing/opportunity-rich environment |

**Circumstances favouring Stability:** the firm is doing reasonably well and is satisfied with current performance; the environment is relatively stable with limited threats or opportunities; expansion is perceived as risky; the firm wishes to **consolidate** after a period of rapid growth and let its resources catch up; management prefers a cautious, low-risk course; or external constraints (e.g. anti-trust, resource limits) discourage growth.

**Conclusion:** Stability is a deliberate "hold and consolidate" choice suited to a satisfied firm in a stable environment, whereas expansion is a growth-seeking departure; the two differ fundamentally in risk, investment and attitude to change.

*(Full-marks tip: Stress that Stability is a POSITIVE, chosen strategy — not "no strategy"; the examiner deducts marks when students treat it as mere inaction.)*

### Q88. Ch: Strategic Choices — Divestment vs Liquidation (Retrenchment) (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** A diversified group is exiting two of its businesses: Business A is being sold as a going concern to another company, while Business B is being closed and its assets sold off piecemeal because no buyer is willing to run it. Identify the retrenchment strategy in each case and distinguish between them.

**Answer:** Both moves are variants of a **Retrenchment grand strategy**, under which a firm reduces the scope of its activities by pulling back from businesses that are unattractive or that do not fit its strategic focus.

Selling **Business A as a going concern** to another company is a **Divestment (divestiture) strategy** — the sale or liquidation of a portion of the business, usually a SBU, division or product line, that is then run by the acquirer. Divestment is adopted when a turnaround has been attempted but failed, when a business becomes unattractive or too small to be worth keeping, when it does not fit the firm's core, or when a better investment opportunity needs the freed-up resources. Value is preserved because the unit continues to operate.

Closing **Business B and selling its assets piecemeal** is a **Liquidation strategy** — the most extreme retrenchment, involving closing down the business and selling its assets, so the enterprise ceases to exist as an operating entity. It is the least attractive option because it implies failure, loss of employment and reputational damage, and is a last resort when no buyer will take the unit as a going concern.

| Basis | Divestment | Liquidation |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Sell a part as a going concern | Close down and sell assets piecemeal |
| Continuity | Unit continues under new owner | Unit ceases to exist |
| Value realised | Higher (going-concern value) | Lower (scrap/asset value) |
| Position on scale | Moderate retrenchment | Extreme / last resort |

**Conclusion:** Business A represents Divestment (sold as a going concern) and Business B represents Liquidation (closed and assets sold off); liquidation is the more drastic and least desirable of the two.

*(Full-marks tip: The distinction hinges on "going concern vs cease to exist" — say it explicitly; also note divestment often follows a failed turnaround, which links the retrenchment options.)*

### Q89. Ch: Strategic Choices — Ansoff's Product-Market Growth Matrix (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** "FitLife", a maker of protein bars sold to gyms, is planning four separate initiatives: (i) run a loyalty programme to make existing gym customers buy more bars; (ii) start selling the same bars through online marketplaces to retail consumers; (iii) launch a new range of protein shakes for its existing gym customers; and (iv) enter the pet-nutrition business. Map each initiative onto Ansoff's Product-Market Growth Matrix and comment on the relative risk.

**Answer:** **Ansoff's Product-Market Growth Matrix** classifies growth options along two dimensions — products (existing / new) and markets (existing / new) — giving four strategies of increasing risk. Mapping FitLife's initiatives:

| Initiative | Product | Market | Ansoff quadrant |
|---|---|---|---|
| (i) Loyalty programme so existing gyms buy more bars | Existing | Existing | **Market Penetration** |
| (ii) Same bars sold online to retail consumers | Existing | New | **Market Development** |
| (iii) New protein shakes for existing gym customers | New | Existing | **Product Development** |
| (iv) Enter pet-nutrition business | New | New | **Diversification** |

**Explanation and risk:**
- **Market Penetration** (existing product, existing market) — selling more of the same product to the same customers, e.g. through loyalty schemes, promotion or higher usage. Lowest risk, because both product and market are known.

### Q90. Ch: Strategy Implementation & Evaluation — Matching Structure to Strategy (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** "Structure follows strategy." Explain the relationship between an organisation's strategy and its structure, and describe how the choice of organisational structure changes as a firm grows from a single-product business into a large, geographically spread, multi-product company.

**Answer:** The proposition **"structure follows strategy"** — associated with Alfred Chandler — means that when a firm changes its strategy, it must change the way it is organised so that the structure supports, rather than obstructs, the new strategy. Structure is the framework of task allocation, reporting relationships and coordination through which strategy is executed; a strategy is only as good as the organisation's ability to implement it, and an ill-fitting structure causes coordination failures, delays and poor performance. Hence a change in strategy normally requires a corresponding change in structure, and structure is designed *after* strategy is chosen.

As a firm grows, its strategy becomes more complex and its structure evolves accordingly:

- **Entrepreneurial / simple structure:** In a small single-product firm the owner-manager takes most decisions directly and the structure is informal, centralised and flexible. Suitable when the strategy is a simple, single business.

- **Functional structure:** As the firm grows in one line of business, work is grouped by **function** (production, marketing, finance, HR), each headed by a specialist, coordinated at the top. It gives specialisation and efficiency and suits a firm following a **concentration/single-dominant-business** strategy, but coordination across functions becomes difficult as products multiply.

- **Divisional (product/SBU) structure:** When the firm's strategy expands into several products or businesses, it is organised into **self-contained divisions**, each responsible for a product or product group with its own functions and profit responsibility. This suits a **related-diversification** strategy and improves accountability and focus, at the cost of duplication of resources.

- **Geographic / territorial structure:** When the strategy is **market development across regions**, divisions are formed by **geographic area**, so each region can respond to local conditions.

- **SBU / multi-divisional and matrix structures:** A very large, diversified firm may group many divisions into **Strategic Business Units** for manageability, or adopt a **matrix structure** (dual reporting along function and project/product) when it must simultaneously pursue efficiency and responsiveness — as in project-based or multi-product global firms.

**Conclusion:** Strategy and structure are interdependent, but structure is generally shaped to fit strategy; as a firm moves from a single product to a large geographically spread multi-product enterprise, its structure typically evolves from simple to functional to divisional/geographic and finally to SBU or matrix forms.

*(Full-marks tip: State Chandler's thesis, explain WHY a misfit hurts implementation, and show the growth-linked PROGRESSION of structures; naming structures without linking each to the strategy it serves loses marks.)*

### Q91. Ch: Strategy Implementation & Evaluation — Operational vs Strategic Control (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between "Strategic Control" and "Operational Control", and briefly explain the four types of strategic control an organisation should exercise.

**Answer:** **Strategic control** is a type of organisational control aimed at monitoring and evaluating the strategy management process to ensure that it is working properly — it focuses on the *direction* of the firm over the long term, asking whether the strategy is still valid and being implemented as intended. **Operational control**, by contrast, is concerned with action or performance and focuses on individual operations/tasks over the short term — it checks whether day-to-day activities are being carried out efficiently and whether short-term results conform to plans.

| Basis | Strategic control | Operational control |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | Whole strategy and its direction | Specific operations/tasks |
| Time horizon | Long term | Short term |
| Question asked | "Are we doing the right things?" | "Are we doing things right?" |
| Nature of correction | May change strategy/premises | Adjust action to meet plan |
| Main users | Top / corporate management | Operating / middle management |

**Four types of strategic control:**
1. **Premise control** — systematically checks whether the assumptions (environmental and industry premises) on which the strategy was built still hold; if a key premise fails, the strategy may need revision.
2. **Strategic surveillance** — a broad, unfocused "watch" over a range of events inside and outside the firm that could affect the strategy, so that unanticipated developments are spotted.
3. **Special alert control** — the rapid, thorough reconsideration of the strategy triggered by a sudden, unexpected event (e.g. a crisis, an acquisition by a rival, a natural calamity).
4. **Implementation control** — assesses whether the strategy should be changed in the light of ongoing results, by monitoring **milestones** and **strategic thrusts** as implementation proceeds.

**Conclusion:** Strategic control safeguards the long-term direction of the firm ("doing the right things"), whereas operational control safeguards short-term efficiency ("doing things right"); the four strategic controls — premise, surveillance, special-alert and implementation control — together keep the strategy relevant and on track.

*(Full-marks tip: Nail the "right things vs things right" contrast, then list ALL FOUR strategic controls with a one-line role each — dropping special-alert or implementation control is the usual mark loss.)*

### Q92. Ch: Strategy Implementation & Evaluation — Role of Leadership in Implementation (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the role of strategic leadership in successful strategy implementation. What are the two key leadership approaches available to a strategic leader?

**Answer:** **Strategy implementation** is the action stage that converts a chosen strategy into results, and it is far more difficult than formulation because it requires mobilising people, resources, structure, systems and culture. **Leadership** is the pivotal ingredient: a strategic leader gives the organisation direction, secures commitment to the strategy and steers the changes that implementation demands.

**Role of strategic leadership in implementation:**
- **Providing direction and vision** — the leader articulates a clear vision and strategic intent so that everyone understands where the firm is going.
- **Building and shaping the organisation** — putting the right structure, people and capabilities in place so the strategy can be executed.
- **Allocating resources** to strategy-critical activities and aligning budgets, policies and systems with the strategy.
- **Shaping the culture and climate** — instilling values, motivation and a performance orientation that support the strategy.
- **Championing the change** — overcoming resistance, keeping the organisation responsive, and personally driving and monitoring execution.
- **Building commitment** rather than mere compliance, so that people willingly pursue strategic objectives.

**Two key leadership approaches** available to a strategic leader:
1. **Transformational leadership** — the leader inspires and motivates followers to transcend their self-interest for the good of the organisation, using vision, charisma and intellectual stimulation to bring about major change; it is well suited to shifting environments and radical strategic change.
2. **Transactional leadership** — the leader focuses on designing systems, structures and rewards, using exchanges (rewards for performance) and monitoring to get results within the existing framework; it is suited to stable settings and incremental improvement.

**Conclusion:** Effective strategic leadership supplies direction, resources, culture and the drive for change that make implementation succeed, and the leader can adopt a transformational style to inspire change or a transactional style to run systems and rewards, choosing the mix that fits the situation.

### Q93. Ch: Strategy Implementation & Evaluation — Role of Culture in Implementation (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** After a merger, "NovaTech" (an informal, risk-taking software start-up) and "SteadyCorp" (a hierarchical, rule-driven engineering firm) find that the combined firm's new growth strategy is stalling, with mistrust and turf wars between the two groups. Explain the role of organisational culture in strategy implementation and how the leadership should manage the culture to make the strategy work.

**Answer:** **Organisational culture** is the set of shared values, beliefs, assumptions and norms that shape how members of an organisation behave — "the way we do things here". In strategy implementation, culture is decisive because it either supports or sabotages the strategy: a culture aligned with the strategy energises execution, whereas a culture in conflict with it silently resists, no matter how sound the plan. NovaTech's clash illustrates a **culture-strategy misfit** — two very different cultures (informal/risk-taking vs hierarchical/rule-bound) now sit under one growth strategy, and the resulting mistrust and turf wars are strangling implementation.

**Role of culture in implementation:**
- Culture provides **shared understanding and commitment**, so employees pursue strategic goals willingly rather than merely complying.
- A **strategy-supportive culture** promotes the behaviours the strategy needs (e.g. innovation, speed, quality) and makes execution smoother and faster.
- Culture is a source of **stability and identity**, but it can also breed **resistance to change** when the strategy demands behaviours the existing culture rejects.
- A **strong but ill-fitting** culture is more dangerous than a weak one, because it entrenches the wrong behaviours.

**How leadership should manage culture at NovaTech:**
1. **Diagnose the two cultures** and identify where each conflicts with, and where each supports, the combined growth strategy.
2. **Build a shared vision and superordinate goals** that both groups value, replacing "us vs them" with a common purpose.
3. **Change what needs changing while preserving strengths** — retain NovaTech's innovation and SteadyCorp's discipline, forging a hybrid culture suited to the strategy rather than forcing one side to surrender.
4. **Use symbolic and substantive actions** — leaders visibly modelling the desired behaviour, mixed teams, aligned reward systems, common policies and communication to embed new norms.
5. **Align structure, rewards and HR practices** with the desired culture so that the new values are reinforced, and manage resistance through participation and communication.

**Conclusion:** Culture can make or break strategy implementation; at NovaTech the leadership must consciously integrate the two cultures around a shared vision — keeping the best of each and aligning structure and rewards — so that culture becomes a support for, rather than an obstacle to, the growth strategy.

*(Full-marks tip: Define culture, state the culture-strategy FIT idea, and give concrete integration steps tied to the case — generic "communicate more" answers score low; mentioning that a strong misaligned culture is worse than a weak one shows depth.)*

### Q94. Ch: Strategy Implementation & Evaluation — McKinsey 7-S Framework (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the McKinsey 7-S Framework and its usefulness as a tool for strategy implementation. Which of the seven elements are "hard" and which are "soft"?

**Answer:** The **McKinsey 7-S Framework** is a diagnostic model, developed by consultants at McKinsey, which holds that effective strategy implementation depends on the alignment of **seven interdependent internal elements** of an organisation, all of which begin with the letter "S". The central idea is that a strategy cannot be implemented in isolation — all seven elements must be mutually consistent and support one another, and a change in any one requires realignment of the rest.

The seven elements are:
1. **Strategy** — the plan of action by which the firm intends to achieve competitive advantage and its goals.
2. **Structure** — the way the organisation is arranged: reporting lines, division of work and coordination.
3. **Systems** — the formal and informal procedures and processes (planning, budgeting, information, rewards) by which work gets done.
4. **Shared Values** (superordinate goals) — the core values and beliefs at the centre of the model that guide the organisation and bind the other elements together.
5. **Style** — the leadership and management style, and the pattern of behaviour of top management.
6. **Staff** — the people, their number and categories, how they are recruited, developed and socialised.
7. **Skills** — the distinctive capabilities and competencies of the organisation and its people.

| "Hard" elements (tangible, easier to change) | "Soft" elements (intangible, culture-driven) |
|---|---|
| Strategy | Shared Values |
| Structure | Style |
| Systems | Staff |
| | Skills |

The **hard** S's (Strategy, Structure, Systems) are concrete and identifiable and can be directly influenced by management; the **soft** S's (Shared Values, Style, Staff, Skills) are intangible, culture-driven and harder to manage but are often decisive for success. **Shared Values** sits at the centre because it links and aligns all the others.

**Usefulness for implementation:** The framework helps managers to (i) check whether all seven elements are aligned with the chosen strategy, (ii) diagnose *why* implementation is failing by locating the mis-aligned element, and (iii) plan change comprehensively, recognising that altering one element (say structure) demands adjustments in the others.

**Conclusion:** The 7-S Framework is a holistic implementation tool asserting that success depends on the mutual fit of Strategy, Structure, Systems, Shared Values, Style, Staff and Skills — three "hard" and four "soft" elements — held together by shared values.

*(Full-marks tip: List all seven with a one-line meaning, correctly split hard vs soft, and stress interdependence with Shared Values at the centre — omitting the hard/soft classification or Shared Values loses easy marks.)*

### Q95. Ch: Strategy Implementation & Evaluation — Strategic Change & Kurt Lewin's Model (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Change is often resisted in organisations. Explain the process of managing strategic change using Kurt Lewin's model of change.

**Answer:** **Strategic change** is a proactive alteration of the way an organisation operates that is required when it adopts a new strategy or responds to shifts in its environment. Because people are comfortable with the status quo and fear the unknown, change is commonly resisted, and management must handle it as a deliberate, staged process. **Kurt Lewin's three-stage model** is the classic framework for managing such change:

1. **Unfreezing the situation** — the existing mindset, behaviour and equilibrium are "melted" so that the organisation becomes ready for change. This is done by reducing the forces that maintain the status quo, communicating why change is necessary, creating awareness of the problem or opportunity, and thereby lowering resistance. Unless people first *accept* the need to change, no lasting change is possible.

2. **Changing to the new situation (Moving)** — the actual change is introduced: new behaviours, systems, structures, technology and attitudes are adopted. This is typically achieved through **compliance** (accepting change to gain rewards or avoid punishment), **identification** (adopting the behaviour of a respected role model), and **internalisation** (embracing the new behaviour because it fits one's own values). Support, training, participation and communication are provided so the new ways take hold.

3. **Refreezing** — the new behaviour is stabilised and made the norm so that the organisation does not slip back to the old ways. Reinforcement, rewards, revised policies, and consistent leadership lock the change into the culture, making it the new equilibrium.

**Conclusion:** Managing strategic change effectively means guiding the organisation through Lewin's stages — unfreezing the old equilibrium, moving to the new state, and refreezing it as the new normal — while actively addressing resistance at each step.

*(Full-marks tip: Give all three stages with the *purpose* of each, and mention compliance/identification/internalisation in the moving stage; skipping refreezing — the step that prevents relapse — is the most common omission.)*

### Q96. Ch: Functional & Digital Strategy — Marketing Mix and Strategy (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** A regional dairy is launching a premium probiotic yoghurt aimed at health-conscious urban consumers. Explain the elements of the marketing mix (the 4 Ps) the marketing manager must decide, applying each element to this launch.

**Answer:** **Marketing strategy** is the functional strategy through which a firm decides how to create value for a chosen target market and outperform competitors. Its central tool is the **marketing mix**, popularly the **4 Ps** — Product, Price, Place and Promotion — the controllable variables the firm blends to serve the target market. Applied to the dairy's premium probiotic yoghurt aimed at health-conscious urban consumers:

1. **Product** — decisions on the goods/services offered, including quality, features, brand, packaging and variety. Here: a probiotic yoghurt with clearly stated health benefits (gut health, live cultures), attractive hygienic packaging, a distinct premium brand name and a few flavour variants.

### Q97. Ch: Functional & Digital Strategy — E-Business Models (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Classify and explain the following e-commerce relationships, giving the model name and a suitable example for each: (a) a manufacturer selling components online to another factory; (b) an online store selling shoes directly to individual shoppers; (c) a person selling a used phone to another person through an online marketplace; and (d) a citizen paying property tax on a municipal portal.

**Answer:** **E-business / e-commerce models** are classified by *who* transacts with *whom* over electronic networks. The four principal relationships and their application to the given cases are:

| Case | Parties | Model | Explanation | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| (a) Manufacturer selling components to another factory | Business ↔ Business | **B2B (Business-to-Business)** | One business sells goods/services to another business online; typically bulk, ongoing supply relationships | A parts maker supplying an auto plant via an online portal |
| (b) Online store selling shoes to individual shoppers | Business ↔ Consumer | **B2C (Business-to-Consumer)** | A business sells directly to the final consumer over the internet; the most familiar retail model | An online footwear retailer selling to individual buyers |

### Q99. Ch: Functional & Digital Strategy — Production/Operations & Supply Chain Strategy (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** A furniture maker suffers high inventory-carrying costs, long delivery lead times and frequent stock-outs of popular designs. As its strategist, explain what production/operations strategy and supply chain management (SCM) can contribute, and outline the key decisions it should take to fix these problems.

**Answer:** **Production/operations strategy** is the functional strategy concerned with how the firm produces its goods — decisions on capacity, technology, quality, inventory and processes — so that operations support the overall business strategy. **Supply Chain Management (SCM)** is the management of the entire flow of materials, information and finance from suppliers through production to the final customer, aiming to deliver the right product, at the right place and time, at the lowest cost. The furniture maker's symptoms — high inventory cost, long lead times, stock-outs — are precisely the problems that operations strategy and SCM are designed to solve.

**Contribution of operations strategy and SCM:**
- They **reduce cost** by cutting excess inventory and waste.
- They **improve responsiveness**, shortening lead times so customers are served faster.
- They **balance availability against carrying cost**, reducing both stock-outs and overstocking.
- They build **coordination with suppliers and distributors**, so the whole chain is integrated rather than each link optimising alone.

**Key decisions the strategist should take:**
1. **Inventory strategy** — adopt better inventory control (e.g. **Just-in-Time / lean** for standard inputs, and buffer stock only for the fast-moving popular designs), reducing carrying cost while avoiding stock-outs of best-sellers.
2. **Demand forecasting and planning** — improve forecasting so production matches the demand pattern of popular vs slow designs.
3. **Supplier relationships** — develop reliable, closely-coordinated suppliers and possibly fewer, long-term partners to shorten and stabilise lead times.
4. **Process and capacity decisions** — streamline the production process and align capacity to demand to cut delivery lead times.
5. **Distribution/logistics** — improve warehousing and logistics so finished goods reach customers quickly.
6. **Information integration** — share demand and stock information across the chain so replenishment is timely.

**Conclusion:** Production/operations strategy and SCM can cut the firm's inventory costs, shorten lead times and eliminate stock-outs by integrating and optimising the whole material flow; the strategist should act on inventory control, forecasting, supplier partnerships, process/capacity and logistics to fix the problem.

### Q100. Ch: Functional & Digital Strategy — Strategic Role of HRM & Logistics Strategy (Marks: 8) [Theory]
**Question:** (a) Explain the strategic role of Human Resource Management (HRM) in an organisation and the concept of the "Strategic HR Manager". (b) Distinguish between "Logistics management" and "Supply Chain Management".

**Answer:**

**(a) Strategic role of HRM and the Strategic HR Manager.**
**Human Resource Management (HRM)** is the functional area concerned with acquiring, developing, motivating and retaining the people an organisation needs. Its role has evolved from a purely administrative/personnel function into a **strategic partner** in the management process, because people are the organisation's most critical resource for executing strategy — strategy is ultimately implemented by human beings.

The **strategic role of HRM** includes:
- **Aligning HR practices with strategy** — recruitment, training, appraisal and rewards are designed to support the chosen strategy, so that the firm has the right people with the right skills where the strategy needs them.
- **Building capabilities and competencies** required by current and future strategy through training and development.
- **Managing change and culture** — helping the organisation adapt, shaping a strategy-supportive culture and managing resistance.
- **Motivating and retaining talent** and linking performance to strategic objectives.
- **Workforce planning** — ensuring the right number and mix of people are available for the strategy.

The **Strategic HR Manager** is one who moves beyond routine personnel administration to become a **partner in the strategic management process** — participating in strategy formulation, ensuring HR strategy is integrated with business strategy, and using the human resource as a source of sustained competitive advantage. In this role the HR manager anticipates the people implications of strategy, builds the required competencies in advance, and treats employees as strategic assets rather than costs.

**(b) Logistics management vs Supply Chain Management.**
**Logistics management** deals with the movement and storage of goods **within** the organisation and to/from it — activities such as transportation, warehousing, material handling, inventory and order processing, aimed at getting goods to the right place at the right time and cost. **Supply Chain Management (SCM)** is a **broader** concept: it manages the entire network of organisations, information and processes — from the supplier's supplier to the customer's customer — of which logistics is only one part. SCM includes sourcing, procurement, production coordination and relationships across the whole chain, whereas logistics is essentially the flow/movement component within that chain.

| Basis | Logistics management | Supply Chain Management |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | Narrower — flow and storage of goods | Broader — the whole network of firms and processes |
| Focus | Transport, warehousing, inventory, order flow | Sourcing, production, distribution and relationships across the chain |
| Relationship | A **part** of SCM | The **whole** system that includes logistics |
| Objective | Right goods, right place, right time, least cost | Integrate and optimise the entire chain for the end customer |

**Conclusion:** HRM has become a strategic function and the strategic HR manager is a partner in strategy who turns people into competitive advantage; and while logistics management handles the movement and storage of goods, it is only one component of the wider Supply Chain Management that integrates the entire chain from source to final customer.

*(Full-marks tip: In (a) stress the SHIFT from administrative to strategic partner and the alignment of HR with strategy; in (b) the key line is "logistics is a PART of SCM" — state the scope difference explicitly and use a table.)*
