### Q1. Ch: Ratio Analysis — Reconstruction of Balance Sheet from Ratios (Marks: 10) [Problem]
**Question:** The following particulars relate to **Meghna Textiles Ltd.** for the year ended 31st March, 2026. Using the ratios and figures given, prepare the Balance Sheet of the company in a summarised vertical form.

| Particulars | Value |
|---|---|
| Current Ratio | 2.5 |
| Liquid (Quick) Ratio | 1.5 |
| Proprietary Ratio (Fixed Assets / Proprietary Fund) | 0.75 |
| Working Capital | ₹ 9,00,000 |
| Reserves & Surplus | 40% of Share Capital |
| Bank Overdraft | ₹ 1,00,000 |
| Long-term Debt | Nil |
| Debtors (Debtors form the only "other" current asset besides stock, bank & cash) | ... |

Additional information:
- There are no long-term borrowings; the only current liabilities are trade payables and the bank overdraft.
- Quick assets consist of debtors and cash at bank only (no marketable securities).
- All fixed assets are shown at written-down value.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Current Assets and Current Liabilities from Current Ratio & Working Capital**
Let Current Liabilities = CL. Current Assets = 2.5 CL.
Working Capital = CA − CL = 2.5 CL − CL = 1.5 CL = ₹ 9,00,000
∴ CL = 9,00,000 / 1.5 = **₹ 6,00,000**
CA = 2.5 × 6,00,000 = **₹ 15,00,000**

**WN-2 — Stock (Inventory)**
Quick Ratio = Quick Assets / CL = 1.5 ⇒ Quick Assets = 1.5 × 6,00,000 = ₹ 9,00,000
Stock = Current Assets − Quick Assets = 15,00,000 − 9,00,000 = **₹ 6,00,000**

**WN-3 — Split of Current Liabilities**
Bank Overdraft = ₹ 1,00,000 (given)
Trade Payables = CL − Bank Overdraft = 6,00,000 − 1,00,000 = **₹ 5,00,000**

**WN-4 — Proprietary Fund and Fixed Assets**
Proprietary Ratio (Fixed Assets/Proprietary Fund) = 0.75.
Since Long-term Debt = Nil, the sources of funds are only Proprietary Fund + Current Liabilities.
Total Assets = Fixed Assets + Current Assets = Proprietary Fund + Current Liabilities.
Let Proprietary Fund = P. Fixed Assets = 0.75 P.
∴ 0.75 P + 15,00,000 = P + 6,00,000
15,00,000 − 6,00,000 = P − 0.75 P
9,00,000 = 0.25 P ⇒ **P = ₹ 36,00,000**
Fixed Assets = 0.75 × 36,00,000 = **₹ 27,00,000**

**WN-5 — Share Capital and Reserves**
Proprietary Fund = Share Capital + Reserves = Share Capital (1 + 0.40) = 1.40 × Share Capital
1.40 × Share Capital = 36,00,000 ⇒ Share Capital = **₹ 25,71,429** (rounded)
Reserves & Surplus = 40% × 25,71,429 = **₹ 10,28,571**

*(Check: 25,71,429 + 10,28,571 = 36,00,000 ✓)*

**Balance Sheet of Meghna Textiles Ltd. as at 31st March, 2026**

|---|---:|---:|
| **I. EQUITY & LIABILITIES** | | |
| Share Capital | 25,71,429 | |
| Reserves & Surplus | 10,28,571 | |
| **Proprietary Fund** | | 36,00,000 |
| Current Liabilities: | | |
|  — Trade Payables | 5,00,000 | |
|  — Bank Overdraft | 1,00,000 | 6,00,000 |
| **TOTAL** | | **42,00,000** |
| **II. ASSETS** | | |
| Fixed Assets (WDV) | | 27,00,000 |
| Current Assets: | | |
|  — Stock | 6,00,000 | |
|  — Quick Assets (Debtors + Bank) | 9,00,000 | 15,00,000 |
| **TOTAL** | | **42,00,000** |

**Answer:** Balance Sheet total = **₹ 42,00,000**; Proprietary Fund ₹ 36,00,000, Fixed Assets ₹ 27,00,000, Current Assets ₹ 15,00,000 (Stock ₹ 6,00,000 + Quick ₹ 9,00,000).

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the algebraic elimination in WN-4 that solves Proprietary Fund without assuming Total Assets. Common deductions: treating bank overdraft as a reduction of cash instead of a current liability, and forgetting that working capital = 1.5 × CL.)*

### Q2. Ch: Cost of Capital — WACC using Market-Value Weights (Marks: 10) [Problem]
**Question:** **Aravalli Industries Ltd.** has the following capital structure as on 31st March, 2026. You are required to compute the Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC) using **market-value weights**. Assume a tax rate of 30%.

| Source | Book Value (₹) | Market Value (₹) | Other data |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Equity Shares (₹10 each) | 40,00,000 | 80,00,000 | Expected dividend ₹ 4 per share, growth 6% |
| 12% Preference Shares (₹100 each) | 10,00,000 | 9,60,000 | Face value ₹100, redeemable at par after 8 yrs |
| 11% Debentures (₹100 each) | 30,00,000 | 30,90,000 | Redeemable at par after 5 yrs |
| Retained Earnings | 20,00,000 | — | — |

Current market price of equity share is ₹ 20. Flotation costs may be ignored. Retained earnings carry the same cost as equity.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Cost of Equity (Kₑ) — Dividend Growth Model**
Kₑ = (D₁ / P₀) + g = (4 / 20) + 0.06 = 0.20 + 0.06 = **0.26 i.e. 26%**
Cost of Retained Earnings (Kᵣ) = Kₑ = **26%** (flotation ignored).

**WN-2 — Cost of Preference Shares (Kₚ) — Redeemable**
Annual preference dividend = 12% × 100 = ₹12. Net proceeds (market value per unit) = ₹96 (9,60,000/10,00,000×100). Redemption value = ₹100, n = 8 yrs.
Kₚ = [PD + (RV − NP)/n] / [(RV + NP)/2]
= [12 + (100 − 96)/8] / [(100 + 96)/2]
= [12 + 0.50] / 98 = 12.50 / 98 = 0.1276 = **12.76%**

**WN-3 — Cost of Debentures (Kd) — Redeemable, after tax**
Interest = 11% × 100 = ₹11. Net proceeds = ₹103 (30,90,000/30,00,000×100). RV = ₹100, n = 5 yrs.
Kd = [I(1−t) + (RV − NP)/n] / [(RV + NP)/2]
= [11(1−0.30) + (100 − 103)/5] / [(100 + 103)/2]
= [7.70 + (−0.60)] / 101.50 = 7.10 / 101.50 = 0.0700 = **7.00%**

**WN-4 — Market Value of Retained Earnings**
Retained earnings have no separate market value; they are treated as part of equity. Following ICAI practice, the market value of equity is apportioned between equity share capital and retained earnings in the ratio of their **book values** (40,00,000 : 20,00,000 = 2 : 1).
Total equity market value = ₹ 80,00,000.
Equity Shares = 80,00,000 × 2/3 = **₹ 53,33,333**
Retained Earnings = 80,00,000 × 1/3 = **₹ 26,66,667**

**Statement Showing Weighted Average Cost of Capital (Market-Value Weights)**

| Source | Market Value (₹) | Weight | Cost (%) | Weighted Cost (%) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Equity Shares | 53,33,333 | 0.4267 | 26.00 | 11.094 |
| Retained Earnings | 26,66,667 | 0.2133 | 26.00 | 5.546 |
| Preference Shares | 9,60,000 | 0.0768 | 12.76 | 0.980 |
| Debentures | 30,90,000 | 0.2472 | 7.00 | 1.730 |
| **Total** | **1,25,00,000** | **1.0000** | | **19.35** |

**Answer:** **WACC (market-value weights) = 19.35% (approx.).**

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner specifically checks that retained earnings are given a weight (not merged into equity and dropped). Splitting the equity market value in book-value ratio is the ICAI-approved treatment. Common deduction: ignoring tax on debenture interest, or using ₹100 instead of ₹96 / ₹103 net proceeds.)*

### Q3. Ch: Cost of Capital — Cost of Retained Earnings vs New Equity (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** **Nilgiri Foods Ltd.** intends to raise fresh long-term funds. The following data is available:
- Current market price per equity share = ₹ 150
- Dividend just paid (D₀) = ₹ 12 per share; expected growth in dividends = 8% p.a.
- Flotation cost on a new equity issue = 5% of the issue price
- Shareholders' personal tax rate on dividends = 10%; brokerage on reinvestment = 3%

You are required to compute (i) Cost of Retained Earnings, and (ii) Cost of New Equity, and explain why the two differ.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Expected Dividend (D₁)**
D₁ = D₀ (1 + g) = 12 (1 + 0.08) = **₹ 12.96**

**WN-2 — Cost of New Equity (Kₑ)**
Net proceeds per share = Market Price − Flotation Cost = 150 − (5% × 150) = 150 − 7.50 = **₹ 142.50**
Kₑ = (D₁ / Net Proceeds) + g = (12.96 / 142.50) + 0.08 = 0.0910 + 0.08 = 0.1710 = **17.10%**

**WN-3 — Cost of Retained Earnings (Kᵣ)**
Retained earnings carry no flotation cost, but there is an **opportunity cost** to shareholders: had the earnings been distributed, shareholders would have received cash net of personal tax and could have reinvested net of brokerage.
Effective funds available to shareholder per ₹1 retained = (1 − tax)(1 − brokerage) = (1 − 0.10)(1 − 0.03) = 0.90 × 0.97 = 0.873.
Kᵣ = Kₑ (based on gross MP) × (1 − tp)(1 − b)
First Kₑ on market price basis = (D₁ / MP) + g = (12.96 / 150) + 0.08 = 0.0864 + 0.08 = 0.1664 = 16.64%
Kᵣ = 16.64% × 0.873 = **14.53%**

**Statement Showing Comparative Cost**

| Component | Basis | Cost (%) |
|---|---|---:|
| Cost of New Equity (Kₑ) | Net of 5% flotation | 17.10 |
| Cost of Retained Earnings (Kᵣ) | Net of personal tax & brokerage | 14.53 |

**Reason for difference:** New equity bears flotation cost, reducing net proceeds and raising its cost. Retained earnings avoid flotation cost but their opportunity cost is measured after adjusting for the shareholder's personal tax and reinvestment (brokerage) cost — hence Kᵣ < Kₑ.

**Answer:** Cost of Retained Earnings = **14.53%**; Cost of New Equity = **17.10%**.

*(Full-marks tip: State clearly that retained earnings have an opportunity cost, not a zero cost. Common deduction: forgetting the (1 − tp)(1 − b) adjustment, or applying flotation cost to retained earnings.)*

Comment on the movement between the two years.

| Balance Sheet (₹ in lakh) | 2024-25 | 2025-26 |

| Reserves & Surplus | 100 | 150 |
| 12% Long-term Loan | 150 | 150 |

### Q5. Ch: Cost of Capital — Cost of Redeemable Debenture (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** **Chambal Cements Ltd.** issued 9% Debentures of face value ₹ 100 each, redeemable at a premium of 10% after 6 years. The debentures were issued at a discount of 4%. Flotation cost is 2% of face value. The corporate tax rate is 25%. Compute the cost of debenture (i) using the approximation (short-cut) method, and (ii) verify the direction using the internal rate approach concept.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Net Proceeds (NP)**
Issue price = Face value − Discount = 100 − 4 = ₹ 96
Less: Flotation cost = 2% × 100 = ₹ 2
Net Proceeds = 96 − 2 = **₹ 94**

**WN-2 — Redemption Value (RV)**
RV = Face value + Premium on redemption = 100 + 10% × 100 = **₹ 110**

**WN-3 — Annual Interest (after tax)**
Interest = 9% × 100 = ₹ 9. After-tax interest = 9 (1 − 0.25) = **₹ 6.75**

**Cost of Debenture — Approximation Method**
Kd = [I(1 − t) + (RV − NP)/n] / [(RV + NP)/2]
= [6.75 + (110 − 94)/6] / [(110 + 94)/2]
= [6.75 + 2.6667] / 102
= 9.4167 / 102 = 0.0923 = **9.23%**

**Cross-check (direction):** Since the debenture is redeemed at ₹110 against net proceeds of ₹94, there is a large capital gain of ₹16 amortised over the term, pushing Kd (9.23%) above the after-tax coupon of 6.75%. An IRR-based computation would give a value close to this figure, confirming the direction.

**Answer:** Cost of Redeemable Debenture (after tax) = **9.23% (approx.).**

*(Full-marks tip: Deduct BOTH discount and flotation from face value to get net proceeds, and add the redemption premium to RV. Common deduction: forgetting the after-tax factor on interest, or netting flotation against RV instead of NP.)*

### Q6. Ch: Ratio Analysis — Computing Missing Figures from Ratios (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** The following information is extracted for **Kaveri Traders** for the year ended 31st March, 2026. Compute the values of the items marked as unknowns.

| Particulars | Value |
|---|---|
| Gross Profit Ratio | 25% |
| Stock Turnover Ratio (on COGS, using closing stock) | 6 times |
| Debtors Collection Period | 45 days (year = 360 days) |

| Gross Profit | ₹ 3,00,000 |
| All sales and purchases on credit; no cash purchases. | |

Find: (a) Sales, (b) Cost of Goods Sold, (c) Closing Stock, (d) Debtors, (e) Credit Purchases and Creditors.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Sales**
Gross Profit Ratio = GP / Sales = 25% ⇒ Sales = GP / 0.25 = 3,00,000 / 0.25 = **₹ 12,00,000**

**WN-2 — Cost of Goods Sold**
COGS = Sales − Gross Profit = 12,00,000 − 3,00,000 = **₹ 9,00,000**

**WN-3 — Closing Stock**

COGS = Opening Stock + Purchases − Closing Stock
9,00,000 = 1,30,000 + Purchases − 1,50,000
Purchases = 9,00,000 + 1,50,000 − 1,30,000 = **₹ 9,20,000**

**WN-6 — Creditors**

### Q7. Ch: Cost of Capital — Concept of Marginal & Overall Cost (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between the **Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC)** and the **Marginal Cost of Capital**. When is each relevant in capital-budgeting decisions?

**Answer:**
The **Weighted Average Cost of Capital** is the composite cost of the firm's existing capital structure — it is computed by weighting the specific cost of each source (equity, preference, debt, retained earnings) by that source's proportion in the total capital, using either book-value or market-value weights. It represents the average rate of return the firm must earn on its existing pool of funds to keep the value of the firm unchanged.

The **Marginal Cost of Capital** is the cost of raising **one additional rupee** of capital. It is the weighted average cost of the *new* or *incremental* funds raised, and it changes (usually rises) as the firm exhausts cheaper sources such as retained earnings and moves to costlier new issues.

| Basis | WACC (Overall) | Marginal Cost of Capital |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Composite cost of the entire existing capital | Cost of the next/additional unit of capital |
| Weights used | Proportions of existing structure | Proportions of new financing raised |
| Behaviour | Relatively stable | Rises in steps as cheap sources exhaust |
| Use | Evaluating overall performance / existing projects | Accept-reject decision on new/incremental projects |

**Conclusion:** For evaluating a *new* investment financed by fresh funds, the **marginal cost of capital** is the correct cut-off rate; the overall WACC is used to appraise the firm's existing capital and average performance.

*(Full-marks tip: The distinction table plus the "when relevant" line earns the last mark. Common deduction: defining only WACC and omitting the incremental nature of marginal cost.)*

### Q8. Ch: Ratio Analysis — DuPont Analysis of Return on Equity (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** **Godavari Retail Ltd.** reports the following for FY 2025-26. Using the **three-factor DuPont model**, compute the Return on Equity (ROE) and identify which driver contributes most.

| Particulars | ₹ in lakh |
|---|---:|
| Net Sales | 2,000 |
| Net Profit after Tax | 120 |
| Total Assets | 1,000 |
| Shareholders' Equity | 500 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Net Profit Margin**
= PAT / Net Sales = 120 / 2,000 = 0.06 = **6%**

**WN-2 — Total Asset Turnover**
= Net Sales / Total Assets = 2,000 / 1,000 = **2.00 times**

**WN-3 — Equity Multiplier (Financial Leverage)**
= Total Assets / Shareholders' Equity = 1,000 / 500 = **2.00**

**DuPont Decomposition of ROE**

| Component | Value |
|---|---:|
| Net Profit Margin | 6.00% |
| × Asset Turnover | 2.00 |
| × Equity Multiplier | 2.00 |
| **= Return on Equity** | **24.00%** |

ROE = 0.06 × 2.00 × 2.00 = 0.24 = **24%**

**Cross-check:** ROE = PAT / Equity = 120 / 500 = 24% ✓

**Interpretation:** Both asset turnover (2.0) and the equity multiplier (2.0) act as strong multipliers, while the net margin is thin at 6%. ROE is being driven primarily by **efficient asset utilisation and leverage** rather than by margins; a margin improvement would have the largest incremental effect since it is the weakest link.

**Answer:** **ROE = 24%**, decomposed as 6% margin × 2.0 turnover × 2.0 leverage.

*(Full-marks tip: Show the identity ROE = Margin × Turnover × Multiplier and reconcile with PAT/Equity. Common deduction: stopping at the number without identifying the dominant driver.)*

### Q9. Ch: Cost of Capital — Cost of Preference & Overall Cost (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** **Tapti Chemicals Ltd.** issued 10,000, 10% Preference Shares of ₹ 100 each at a discount of 5%, redeemable at a premium of 8% at the end of 10 years. Flotation cost is ₹ 2 per share. Compute the cost of preference capital.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Net Proceeds per Share**
Issue price = 100 − 5% × 100 = ₹ 95
Less flotation cost = ₹ 2
Net Proceeds (NP) = 95 − 2 = **₹ 93**

**WN-2 — Redemption Value**
RV = 100 + 8% × 100 = **₹ 108**

**WN-3 — Annual Preference Dividend**
= 10% × 100 = **₹ 10** (no tax adjustment — dividend is an appropriation)

**Cost of Preference Capital (Kₚ)**
Kₚ = [PD + (RV − NP)/n] / [(RV + NP)/2]
= [10 + (108 − 93)/10] / [(108 + 93)/2]
= [10 + 1.50] / 100.50
= 11.50 / 100.50 = 0.1144 = **11.44%**

**Answer:** Cost of Preference Capital = **11.44%.**

*(Full-marks tip: Never apply the (1 − t) tax factor to preference dividend — it is paid out of post-tax profits. Common deduction: netting flotation against redemption value instead of proceeds.)*

### Q10. Ch: Ratio Analysis — Interpreting a Deteriorating Position (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** A banker is reviewing **Bhima Auto Ltd.** for a working-capital loan. Two ratios have moved sharply between years:
- Current Ratio: rose from 2.0 to 3.2
- Inventory Turnover: fell from 8 times to 4 times

Sales are flat. As the credit analyst, explain what these two movements together most likely indicate, and what further checks you would perform before sanctioning the loan.

**Answer:**
A **rising current ratio** is normally read as improving liquidity, but read *alongside* a **halving of inventory turnover** with flat sales, the more likely explanation is that current assets have swollen because of a **build-up of slow-moving or obsolete inventory**, not because of genuine liquidity strength. Inventory turnover falling from 8 to 4 means stock is now held roughly twice as long, tying up working capital and inflating the current ratio artificially.

The two ratios together therefore signal **deteriorating operating efficiency masquerading as improved liquidity** — a classic red flag in credit appraisal.

**Further checks before sanction:**
- Compute the **Quick (acid-test) Ratio** — if it has *not* risen in step with the current ratio, the increase is confined to inventory, confirming the diagnosis.
- Prepare an **ageing analysis of inventory** to isolate obsolete/dead stock and assess its net realisable value.
- Examine the **debtors collection period** for a parallel build-up of receivables.
- Review the **cash conversion cycle** and actual cash flows rather than balance-sheet ratios alone.

**Conclusion:** The combined movement points to unproductive inventory accumulation; the banker should verify with the quick ratio and an inventory ageing before extending further credit.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards linking two ratios into one diagnosis rather than commenting on each in isolation. Common deduction: praising the higher current ratio uncritically.)*

### Q11. Ch: Cost of Capital — Book-Value vs Market-Value Weights (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the difference between using **book-value weights** and **market-value weights** in computing the Weighted Average Cost of Capital. Which is theoretically superior and why?

**Answer:**
In computing WACC, each source's specific cost is weighted by its proportion in the total capital. These proportions can be measured in two ways.

**Book-value weights** use the amounts at which the sources appear in the balance sheet (paid-up share capital, reserves, book value of debt). They are **easy to obtain, stable, and consistent with the firm's accounting records**, and are convenient when a target capital structure is expressed in book terms.

**Market-value weights** use the current market prices of equity, preference and debt. They reflect the **investors' actual required returns and the opportunity cost of capital as of today**, and are consistent with the objective of maximising the market value of the firm.

| Basis | Book-Value Weights | Market-Value Weights |
|---|---|---|
| Source of data | Balance sheet figures | Current market prices |
| Reflects | Historical/accounting position | Current investor expectations |
| Stability | Stable, easy to compute | Fluctuates with the market |
| Treatment of reserves | Shown separately at book value | Subsumed in equity market value |

**Conclusion:** **Market-value weights are theoretically superior** because the cost of capital is an opportunity concept and should reflect current market conditions and investors' present required returns; book-value weights are used only for convenience or when market prices are unavailable.

### Q17. Ch: Cost of Capital — Significance and Determinants (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** "The cost of capital is the minimum rate of return a firm must earn." In light of this statement, briefly explain the **significance of the cost of capital** and list the **factors that determine** it.

**Answer:**
The **cost of capital** is the minimum required rate of return that a firm must earn on its investments so that the market value of the firm's equity does not fall. It is the composite rate the firm pays to its suppliers of long-term funds (equity, preference and debt holders) and therefore acts as the firm's **hurdle rate**.

**Significance of the cost of capital:**
- **Capital budgeting** — it is the discount rate for computing NPV and the cut-off rate for the IRR accept-reject decision; a project is accepted only if its return exceeds the cost of capital.
- **Capital structure design** — the firm chooses the debt-equity mix that minimises the overall cost of capital and thereby maximises firm value.
- **Evaluation of financial performance** — actual returns are compared against the cost of capital to judge whether value is being created.
- **Other financial decisions** — dividend policy, working-capital policy and lease-vs-buy decisions all reference the cost of capital.

**Factors determining the cost of capital:**
- General economic conditions (demand and supply of capital, inflation).
- Market conditions and the marketability/risk of the security.
- The firm's operating and financing decisions (business risk and financial risk).
- The amount of financing required — larger issues may raise flotation costs and hence cost.

**Conclusion:** The cost of capital is the linchpin of investment and financing decisions; it is governed by economic, market and firm-specific risk factors.

### Q20. Ch: Capital Structure & Leverage — Financial Break-Even Point (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the meaning of "Financial Break-Even Point" and state its significance in capital structure planning.

**Answer:** The **Financial Break-Even Point (FBEP)** is that level of **Earnings Before Interest and Tax (EBIT)** which is just sufficient to cover the **fixed financial charges** of a company, namely interest on debt and preference dividend (grossed up for tax). At this level of EBIT, the **Earnings Per Share (EPS) available to equity shareholders is exactly zero**, because the entire operating profit is absorbed in meeting the prior fixed obligations.

Where a company has only interest as a fixed financial charge, the financial break-even point is simply equal to the interest amount, i.e. **FBEP = Interest (I)**. Where preference share capital also exists, since preference dividend is paid out of post-tax profits, it must be grossed up, giving:
$$FBEP = Interest + \frac{Preference\ Dividend}{(1 - Tax\ rate)}$$

**Significance:** The FBEP sets the **minimum EBIT a firm must earn before equity shareholders begin to gain**; any EBIT above this point produces positive EPS, and any EBIT below it produces a loss to equity holders. It thus measures the **financial risk** attaching to a capital structure — the higher the fixed financial charges, the higher the financial break-even point and the greater the risk that operating profits may fall short of it.

**Conclusion:** The financial break-even point is the EBIT at which EPS is nil, and it acts as a benchmark of the minimum operating profit required to justify a given level of debt and preference financing.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner looks for the phrase "EPS becomes zero" and the correct grossing-up of preference dividend by (1 − t). Merely writing FBEP = Interest without the preference-dividend treatment loses a mark.)*

### Q23. Ch: Capital Structure & Leverage — Business Risk vs Financial Risk (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between **Business Risk** and **Financial Risk**.

**Answer:** Every firm is exposed to two distinct categories of risk arising respectively from its operating decisions and its financing decisions.

**Business risk** is the risk attached to the firm's **operations** — the variability of its operating profit (EBIT) caused by the composition of fixed and variable operating costs and by uncertainty in sales. It is measured by the **degree of operating leverage** and exists even in an all-equity firm.

**Financial risk** is the **additional risk** placed on equity shareholders as a result of the firm's decision to use **fixed-charge financing** (debt and preference capital). It is measured by the **degree of financial leverage** and arises only when the firm has fixed financial obligations.

| Basis | Business Risk | Financial Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Operating decisions / cost structure | Financing decisions / capital structure |
| Cause | Fixed operating costs & sales variability | Fixed interest & preference dividend |
| Measured by | Degree of Operating Leverage (DOL) | Degree of Financial Leverage (DFL) |
| Affects | Variability of EBIT | Variability of EPS for a given EBIT |
| Exists in unlevered firm? | Yes | No |
| Controllable through | Cost / product-mix decisions | Debt-equity mix |

**Conclusion:** Business risk springs from the asset side and operating structure, whereas financial risk springs from the liability side and the use of leverage; the total risk borne by equity holders is the combination of the two.

### Q25. Ch: Capital Structure & Leverage — Over-capitalisation vs Under-capitalisation (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between **Over-capitalisation** and **Under-capitalisation**, stating one remedy for each.

**Answer:** These two conditions describe a mismatch between a company's **capitalisation (its long-term funds)** and its **earning capacity**.

A company is said to be **over-capitalised** when its **actual capitalisation is higher than the amount warranted by its earning capacity**, so that the rate of return earned on capital employed is persistently lower than the normal rate expected by investors. The book value of shares exceeds their real/market worth.

A company is **under-capitalised** when its **actual capitalisation is lower than that warranted by its earning capacity**, so that it earns an abnormally high rate of return on its capital employed and the real worth of its shares exceeds their book value.

| Basis | Over-capitalisation | Under-capitalisation |
|---|---|---|
| Capital vs earnings | Capital > earnings justify | Capital < earnings justify |
| Rate of return | Below normal | Above normal |
| Value of shares | Real value < book value | Real value > book value |
| Effect | Fall in market price, low dividends | High profits, risk of trade-union/tax scrutiny |
| Remedy | Reduce/redeem capital, write down assets | Issue bonus shares, split, raise more capital |

**Conclusion:** Over-capitalisation reflects idle or over-valued capital dragging down returns, while under-capitalisation reflects intensively used capital yielding super-normal returns; both are undesirable and call for corrective capital restructuring.

*(Full-marks tip: State the earning-capacity test explicitly — examiners deduct marks for the popular misconception that over-capitalisation simply means "excess cash." Give at least one remedy each.)*

### Q28. Ch: Capital Budgeting — NPV-IRR Conflict in Mutually Exclusive Projects (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** Girnar Ltd. must choose between two mutually exclusive projects A and B, each costing ₹10,00,000, with the following after-tax cash inflows. The cost of capital is 10%.

| Year | Project A (₹) | Project B (₹) | PV factor @10% |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 6,00,000 | 2,00,000 | 0.909 |
| 2 | 5,00,000 | 4,00,000 | 0.826 |
| 3 | 3,00,000 | 9,00,000 | 0.751 |

Rank the projects using NPV and IRR, explain the conflict, and recommend which should be accepted.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: NPV at 10%**

| Year | PV@10% | Proj A CFAT | PV_A (₹) | Proj B CFAT | PV_B (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.909 | 6,00,000 | 5,45,400 | 2,00,000 | 1,81,800 |
| 2 | 0.826 | 5,00,000 | 4,13,000 | 4,00,000 | 3,30,400 |
| 3 | 0.751 | 3,00,000 | 2,25,300 | 9,00,000 | 6,75,900 |
| | | PV of inflows | 11,83,700 | | 11,88,100 |

- NPV_A = 11,83,700 − 10,00,000 = **₹1,83,700**
- NPV_B = 11,88,100 − 10,00,000 = **₹1,88,100**

**WN-2: IRR of Project A** (front-loaded, so try 20% and 22%)
- @20% (0.833, 0.694, 0.579): 4,99,800 + 3,47,000 + 1,73,700 = 10,20,500 → NPV +20,500
- @22% (0.820, 0.672, 0.551): 4,92,000 + 3,36,000 + 1,65,300 = 9,93,300 → NPV −6,700
$$IRR_A = 20\% + \frac{20,500}{20,500+6,700}\times 2\% = 20\% + 1.51\% \approx \textbf{21.5\%}$$

**WN-3: IRR of Project B** (back-loaded, so try 18% and 19%)
- @18% (0.847, 0.718, 0.609): 1,69,400 + 2,87,200 + 5,48,100 = 10,04,700 → NPV +4,700
- @19% (0.840, 0.706, 0.593): 1,68,000 + 2,82,400 + 5,33,700 = 9,84,100 → NPV −15,900
$$IRR_B = 18\% + \frac{4,700}{4,700+15,900}\times 1\% = 18\% + 0.23\% \approx \textbf{18.23\%}$$

**WN-4: Ranking**

| Criterion | Project A | Project B | Preferred |
|---|---|---|---|
| NPV @10% | ₹1,83,700 | ₹1,88,100 | **B** |
| IRR | 21.50% | 18.23% | **A** |

**The conflict and its resolution:** The two methods rank the projects differently because of the **difference in the timing of cash flows** — Project A returns cash earlier (giving a higher IRR, which implicitly assumes reinvestment at the high IRR), whereas Project B returns more cash later and hence has a higher NPV at the 10% cost of capital. When mutually exclusive projects conflict, the **NPV method is theoretically superior** because it measures the absolute addition to shareholders' wealth and assumes reinvestment at the more realistic cost of capital. This is confirmed by the **incremental approach (B − A)**: the incremental NPV @10% = 1,88,100 − 1,83,700 = **+₹4,400**, i.e. the extra investment pattern in B earns more than 10%.

**Recommendation:** **Accept Project B**, as it maximises shareholders' wealth (highest NPV).

**Answer:** NPV ranks **B** (₹1,88,100 > ₹1,83,700); IRR ranks **A** (21.5% > 18.23%). Following the NPV rule, **choose Project B**.

*(Full-marks tip: Naming the cause (difference in timing/scale of cash flows and the reinvestment-rate assumption) and confirming with incremental NPV is exactly what the examiner rewards; simply stating "follow NPV" without the reason loses marks.)*

### Q30. Ch: Capital Budgeting — NPV vs IRR Method (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** "The NPV method is generally regarded as superior to the IRR method." Discuss, bringing out the points of distinction.

**Answer:** Both the **Net Present Value (NPV)** and the **Internal Rate of Return (IRR)** are **discounted cash-flow techniques** of capital budgeting, but they differ in approach and, in some situations, in the ranking they give.

The **NPV method** discounts all cash flows at the firm's cost of capital and accepts a project if NPV is positive; it expresses the result in **absolute money terms** (addition to shareholders' wealth). The **IRR method** finds the rate at which the NPV is zero and accepts a project if the IRR exceeds the cost of capital; it expresses the result as a **percentage rate of return**.

| Basis | NPV Method | IRR Method |
|---|---|---|
| Expressed as | Absolute amount (₹) | Percentage rate |
| Discount rate used | Given cost of capital | The IRR itself (solved for) |
| Reinvestment assumption | At cost of capital (realistic) | At the IRR (often unrealistic) |
| Multiple answers | Always one | May give multiple IRRs with non-conventional flows |
| Ranking of mutually exclusive projects | Correct (wealth-maximising) | May give incorrect ranking |
| Wealth maximisation | Consistent with it | Not always consistent |

The NPV method is regarded as **superior** because (i) it assumes reinvestment of intermediate cash flows at the **cost of capital**, which is more realistic than the IRR's assumption of reinvestment at the internal rate; (ii) it always yields a **unique** answer even where cash flows change sign more than once; and (iii) for **mutually exclusive** projects it correctly ranks by the **absolute increase in shareholders' wealth**.

**Conclusion:** Although IRR is intuitively appealing as a rate of return, the NPV method is theoretically sounder and, in cases of conflict, its recommendation should prevail.

*(Full-marks tip: The reinvestment-rate assumption and the multiple-IRR problem are the two points examiners specifically look for; a comparison table secures the presentation marks.)*

### Q33. Ch: Capital Budgeting — Modified Internal Rate of Return (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the concept of **Modified Internal Rate of Return (MIRR)** and state how it overcomes the limitations of the ordinary IRR.

**Answer:** The **Modified Internal Rate of Return (MIRR)** is the discount rate at which the **present value of a project's cost (cash outflows)** equals the **present value of the project's "terminal value" of cash inflows**, where the terminal value is obtained by **compounding each intermediate cash inflow forward to the end of the project's life at the firm's cost of capital (reinvestment rate)**.

In computing the MIRR, the intermediate cash inflows are first reinvested (compounded) up to the terminal year at the cost of capital to arrive at a single **terminal cash flow**, and then the rate that equates the present value of that terminal flow with the initial outlay is found:
$$Initial\ Outlay = \frac{Terminal\ Value\ of\ Inflows}{(1 + MIRR)^n}$$

**How it overcomes the IRR's limitations:**
- The ordinary IRR **assumes intermediate cash flows are reinvested at the IRR itself**, which is often unrealistically high; MIRR assumes reinvestment at the **cost of capital**, which is more realistic.
- The ordinary IRR can produce **multiple values** when cash flows are non-conventional (sign changes more than once); MIRR always gives a **single, unique rate**.
- MIRR therefore gives rankings that are **more consistent with the NPV method** and hence with the goal of wealth maximisation.

**Conclusion:** MIRR retains the intuitive appeal of a percentage rate of return while correcting the unrealistic reinvestment assumption and the multiple-rate problem of the conventional IRR.

*(Full-marks tip: The two curative points — reinvestment at cost of capital and a unique rate — are the heart of the answer; simply defining MIRR without contrasting it against IRR limits the marks.)*

### Q35. Ch: Risk Analysis in Capital Budgeting — Risk-Adjusted Discount Rate vs Certainty-Equivalent on the Same Project (Marks: 10) [Problem]
**Question:** M/s Novena Ltd. is evaluating a project requiring an initial outlay of ₹12,00,000. The project has a life of 4 years. The expected cash inflows and the certainty-equivalent (CE) coefficients assigned by the finance team are given below. The firm's risk-free rate of return is 6% and, considering the business risk of the project, the management applies a risk-adjusted discount rate (RADR) of 14%.

| Year | Expected Cash Inflow (₹) | Certainty-Equivalent Coefficient |
|------|--------------------------|----------------------------------|
| 1 | 4,50,000 | 0.95 |
| 2 | 5,00,000 | 0.85 |
| 3 | 5,50,000 | 0.75 |
| 4 | 6,00,000 | 0.60 |

PV factors: at 6% — 0.943, 0.890, 0.840, 0.792; at 14% — 0.877, 0.769, 0.675, 0.592.

You are required to: (i) compute the NPV under the RADR approach; (ii) compute the NPV under the CE approach; and (iii) comment on the results and the internal consistency of the two risk premia.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: NPV under the Risk-Adjusted Discount Rate (RADR) approach**
Under RADR, the *unadjusted* expected cash flows are discounted at 14% (which already embeds the risk premium of 8% over the risk-free rate).

| Year | Cash Inflow (₹) | PV Factor @14% | Present Value (₹) |
|------|-----------------|----------------|-------------------|
| 1 | 4,50,000 | 0.877 | 3,94,650 |
| 2 | 5,00,000 | 0.769 | 3,84,500 |
| 3 | 5,50,000 | 0.675 | 3,71,250 |
| 4 | 6,00,000 | 0.592 | 3,55,200 |
| **Total PV of inflows** | | | **15,05,600** |

NPV (RADR) = 15,05,600 − 12,00,000 = **₹3,05,600**

**WN-2: NPV under the Certainty-Equivalent (CE) approach**
Under CE, the risk is removed from the numerator by converting risky cash flows into their "certain equivalents" (Expected CF × CE coefficient), and these certain flows are discounted at the *risk-free rate* of 6%.

| Year | Cash Inflow (₹) | CE Coeff. | Certain CF (₹) | PV Factor @6% | Present Value (₹) |
|------|-----------------|-----------|----------------|---------------|-------------------|
| 1 | 4,50,000 | 0.95 | 4,27,500 | 0.943 | 4,03,133 |
| 2 | 5,00,000 | 0.85 | 4,25,000 | 0.890 | 3,78,250 |
| 3 | 5,50,000 | 0.75 | 4,12,500 | 0.840 | 3,46,500 |
| 4 | 6,00,000 | 0.60 | 3,60,000 | 0.792 | 2,85,120 |
| **Total PV of inflows** | | | | | **14,13,003** |

NPV (CE) = 14,13,003 − 12,00,000 = **₹2,13,003**

**Statement Showing Comparison of the Two Approaches**

| Particulars | RADR Approach | CE Approach |
|-------------|---------------|-------------|
| PV of inflows (₹) | 15,05,600 | 14,13,003 |
| Less: Initial outlay (₹) | 12,00,000 | 12,00,000 |
| **NPV (₹)** | **3,05,600** | **2,13,003** |
| Decision | Accept | Accept |

**(iii) Comment:** Under both approaches the NPV is positive, so the project is **acceptable**. However, the CE approach gives a lower NPV, which means the risk implicitly built into the 14% RADR is *lower* than the risk the finance team has expressed through its (fairly aggressive, sharply declining) CE coefficients. Theoretically, the CE approach is **superior** because it adjusts risk directly in the cash flows year-by-year, whereas the RADR compounds a single risk premium over time and therefore assumes risk increases at a constant rate every year — an assumption that is often unrealistic. The declining CE coefficients (0.95 → 0.60) correctly capture the fact that distant cash flows are more uncertain; the RADR forces this pattern to be uniform. The two premia are **not internally consistent**: for them to agree, the RADR-implied discount factors would have to equal (CE coefficient × risk-free discount factor) each year.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards discounting CE flows at the risk-free rate — a very common error is discounting certainty-equivalent cash flows again at the RADR, which double-counts risk. State clearly that RADR adjusts the denominator while CE adjusts the numerator, and that CE is theoretically superior.)*

| Normal | 6,00,000 | 0.50 |

Compute (i) the Expected Monetary Value of cash inflow, (ii) the Expected NPV, (iii) the Standard Deviation of the cash inflow, and (iv) the Coefficient of Variation, and comment on the project's risk.

PV of expected inflow = 6,20,000 × PV factor @10% for 1 year (0.909) = 6,20,000 × 0.909 = ₹5,63,580
Expected NPV = 5,63,580 − 5,00,000 = **₹63,580**

**WN-3: Standard Deviation (σ) of cash inflow**
Deviation d = (x − EMV), where EMV = ₹6,20,000

| x (₹) | d = (x − 6,20,000) | d² | p | p·d² |

### Q37. Ch: Dividend Decisions — Walter's Model Share Valuation (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** The following particulars relate to Harmony Ltd.:

| Particulars | Amount |
|-------------|--------|
| Earnings per share (EPS) | ₹15 |
| Rate of return on investment (r) | 18% |
| Cost of equity capital (Ke) | 12% |

Using **Walter's model**, determine the market price per share when the dividend-payout ratio is (a) 0%, (b) 40%, (c) 75% and (d) 100%. Identify the optimum payout and explain what type of firm this is under Walter's framework.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Walter's valuation formula**
Under Walter's Model, the market price per share is:

P = [D + (r/Ke)(E − D)] ÷ Ke

where D = Dividend per share, E = EPS, r = return on investment, Ke = cost of equity.
Here E = ₹15, r = 0.18, Ke = 0.12, and (r/Ke) = 0.18/0.12 = **1.5**.

**WN-2: Computation of DPS at each payout ratio**

| Payout | DPS = Payout × ₹15 | Retained (E − D) |
|--------|--------------------|--------------------|
| 0% | ₹0 | ₹15 |
| 40% | ₹6 | ₹9 |
| 75% | ₹11.25 | ₹3.75 |
| 100% | ₹15 | ₹0 |

**WN-3: Market price under each payout**

(a) Payout 0%: P = [0 + 1.5(15 − 0)] ÷ 0.12 = [22.50] ÷ 0.12 = **₹187.50**
(b) Payout 40%: P = [6 + 1.5(15 − 6)] ÷ 0.12 = [6 + 13.50] ÷ 0.12 = 19.50 ÷ 0.12 = **₹162.50**
(c) Payout 75%: P = [11.25 + 1.5(15 − 11.25)] ÷ 0.12 = [11.25 + 5.625] ÷ 0.12 = 16.875 ÷ 0.12 = **₹140.625**
(d) Payout 100%: P = [15 + 1.5(15 − 15)] ÷ 0.12 = 15 ÷ 0.12 = **₹125.00**

**Statement Showing Market Price under Walter's Model**

| Payout Ratio | DPS (₹) | Market Price per Share (₹) |
|--------------|---------|-----------------------------|
| 0% | 0 | 187.50 |
| 40% | 6 | 162.50 |
| 75% | 11.25 | 140.63 |
| 100% | 15 | 125.00 |

**Answer:** The market price is **highest (₹187.50) at a payout of 0%** and falls steadily as payout rises. This is because r (18%) > Ke (12%): the firm earns more on retained earnings than shareholders require, so every rupee retained adds more value than a rupee distributed. Harmony Ltd. is therefore a **growth firm**, and under Walter's model its **optimum dividend-payout ratio is 0% (full retention)**.

*(Full-marks tip: State the r vs Ke relationship as the reason — growth firm (r>Ke) → zero payout; declining firm (r<Ke) → 100% payout; normal firm (r=Ke) → payout irrelevant. Merely computing prices without concluding on the firm type and optimum payout loses the conceptual marks.)*

**Question:** Crescent Ltd. has an Earnings per Share of ₹20 and a cost of equity capital of 16%. The company can earn a return of 20% on its investments. Using **Gordon's dividend-growth (dividend-capitalisation) model**, compute the market price per share when the retention ratio (b) is (i) 25%, (ii) 40% and (iii) 60%. Comment on the trend, and cross-check the 40% case against Walter's model.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Gordon's model formula**
Under Gordon's Model, P = E(1 − b) ÷ (Ke − br)
where E = EPS, b = retention ratio, (1 − b) = payout ratio, r = return on investment, Ke = cost of equity, and g = br = growth rate.
Given E = ₹20, r = 0.20, Ke = 0.16.

**WN-2: Growth rate (g = br) and dividend at each retention level**

| Retention b | Payout (1 − b) | DPS = E(1−b) (₹) | g = b × 0.20 | (Ke − g) |
|-------------|----------------|-------------------|--------------|-----------|
| 0.25 | 0.75 | 15 | 0.05 | 0.11 |
| 0.40 | 0.60 | 12 | 0.08 | 0.08 |
| 0.60 | 0.40 | 8 | 0.12 | 0.04 |

**WN-3: Market price under each retention ratio**

(i) b = 25%: P = 15 ÷ 0.11 = **₹136.36**
(ii) b = 40%: P = 12 ÷ 0.08 = **₹150.00**
(iii) b = 60%: P = 8 ÷ 0.04 = **₹200.00**

**Statement Showing Market Price under Gordon's Model**

| Retention (b) | Payout | DPS (₹) | Growth g | Price (₹) |
|---------------|--------|---------|----------|-----------|
| 25% | 75% | 15 | 5% | 136.36 |
| 40% | 60% | 12 | 8% | 150.00 |
| 60% | 40% | 8 | 12% | 200.00 |

**OR — Cross-check the b = 40% case using Walter's model:**
P = [D + (r/Ke)(E − D)] ÷ Ke = [12 + (0.20/0.16)(20 − 12)] ÷ 0.16 = [12 + 1.25 × 8] ÷ 0.16 = [12 + 10] ÷ 0.16 = 22 ÷ 0.16 = **₹137.50**.

### Q39. Ch: Dividend Decisions — Modigliani-Miller Dividend Irrelevance (Marks: 10) [Problem]
**Question:** Vivid Ltd. has 1,00,000 equity shares outstanding, each with a current market price of ₹150. The company's cost of equity (capitalisation rate) is 12%. The Board expects a net income of ₹18,00,000 for the year and plans new investment of ₹30,00,000. The company is contemplating a dividend of ₹9 per share at the year-end.

Using the **Modigliani-Miller (MM) dividend-irrelevance approach** (assume no taxes), show that the value of the firm is the same whether or not the dividend is paid. Also determine the number of new shares to be issued in each case.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Price at the end of year 1 (Po = (P1 + D1)/(1 + Ke))**
Rearranging MM: P1 = Po(1 + Ke) − D1

*Case A — Dividend of ₹9 paid:*
P1 = 150(1 + 0.12) − 9 = 168 − 9 = **₹159**

*Case B — No dividend paid (D1 = 0):*
P1 = 150(1.12) − 0 = **₹168**

**WN-2: Funds required from new share issue**
New shares must finance the part of investment not met by retained earnings.
Amount to be raised = Investment − (Net income − Total dividend)

*Case A (Dividend paid):*
Total dividend = 1,00,000 × ₹9 = ₹9,00,000
Retained earnings = 18,00,000 − 9,00,000 = ₹9,00,000
Amount to raise = 30,00,000 − 9,00,000 = ₹21,00,000
Number of new shares = 21,00,000 ÷ P1 (₹159) = **13,207.55 shares**

*Case B (No dividend):*
Retained earnings = ₹18,00,000
Amount to raise = 30,00,000 − 18,00,000 = ₹12,00,000
Number of new shares = 12,00,000 ÷ P1 (₹168) = **7,142.86 shares**

**WN-3: Value of the firm = [(n + Δn)P1 − I + E] ÷ (1 + Ke)**
where n = existing shares, Δn = new shares, I = investment, E = earnings.

*Case A (Dividend paid):*
(n + Δn)P1 = (1,00,000 + 13,207.55) × 159 = 1,13,207.55 × 159 = ₹1,79,99,999 ≈ ₹1,80,00,000
Value = [1,80,00,000 − 30,00,000 + 18,00,000] ÷ 1.12 = 1,68,00,000 ÷ 1.12 = **₹1,50,00,000**

*Case B (No dividend):*
(n + Δn)P1 = (1,00,000 + 7,142.86) × 168 = 1,07,142.86 × 168 = ₹1,80,00,000
Value = [1,80,00,000 − 30,00,000 + 18,00,000] ÷ 1.12 = 1,68,00,000 ÷ 1.12 = **₹1,50,00,000**

**Statement Showing Value of the Firm under MM Approach**

| Particulars | Case A: Dividend Paid | Case B: No Dividend |
|-------------|-----------------------|----------------------|
| Year-end price P1 (₹) | 159 | 168 |
| New funds to be raised (₹) | 21,00,000 | 12,00,000 |
| No. of new shares | 13,207.55 | 7,142.86 |
| Value of firm (₹) | 1,50,00,000 | 1,50,00,000 |

**Answer:** The value of the firm is **₹1,50,00,000 in both cases**. This proves the **MM dividend-irrelevance hypothesis**: under perfect capital markets, rational investors and no taxes, the dividend decision does not affect the value of the firm — whatever cash is paid out as dividend is exactly offset by the funds raised through fresh share issue (and the consequent dilution of existing shareholders), leaving total wealth unchanged.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the "arbitrage" logic — paying a dividend forces a larger new issue, and the two effects cancel. Show that the numerator (nP1 − I + E) is identical in both cases; a common error is forgetting to add back earnings E or to subtract investment I.)*

### Q40. Ch: Risk Analysis in Capital Budgeting — Sources & Perspectives of Risk (Theory) (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** "Risk in capital budgeting can arise from several sources and can be viewed from more than one perspective." Explain briefly the main sources of risk in a capital-budgeting decision and the three perspectives from which project risk may be analysed.

**Answer:** Risk in capital budgeting refers to the variability of the actual returns of a project from its expected returns, arising because investment decisions are based on future cash flows that are inherently uncertain.

**Main sources of risk** include: (i) **Project-specific risk** — factors peculiar to the project, such as errors in estimating cash flows or the technology chosen; (ii) **Competitive/competition risk** — the possibility that competitors' actions (pricing, new products) alter the project's cash flows; (iii) **Industry-specific risk** — technological, legal or commodity changes affecting the whole industry; (iv) **Market risk** — economy-wide factors such as inflation, interest rates and GDP growth that affect all projects; and (v) **International risk** — exchange-rate and political risk for cross-border projects.

**Three perspectives** from which project risk may be evaluated are set out below:

| Perspective | Basis of Analysis |
|-------------|-------------------|
| **Stand-alone risk** | Risk of the project viewed in isolation, ignoring diversification; measured by σ and CV of the project's own returns. |
| **Firm/Corporate risk** | Risk of the project in the context of the firm's existing portfolio of projects; considers how the project's returns correlate with the firm's other assets. |
| **Market risk** | Risk from the viewpoint of a well-diversified shareholder; measured by the project's beta relative to the market. |

**Conclusion:** Because different stakeholders bear risk differently, a project should ideally be examined from all three perspectives, though for a closely-held or single-project firm the stand-alone perspective is most relevant.

*(Full-marks tip: List distinct *sources* separately from *perspectives* — mixing the two is a common error. Naming the measure attached to each perspective (σ/CV, correlation, beta) earns the application mark.)*

### Q43. Ch: Dividend Decisions — Determinants of Dividend Policy (Theory) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** A newly-appointed CFO argues that a company should simply pay out all its profits as dividends every year. Discuss the major factors (determinants) that a company must consider before finalising its dividend policy, and explain why a 100% payout is rarely optimal.

**Answer:** Dividend policy is the trade-off between distributing current earnings to shareholders and retaining them to finance future growth. The major **determinants of dividend policy** are:

(i) **Availability of funds / Liquidity** — dividends are paid in cash; a profitable company may still lack the liquidity to distribute if profits are tied up in working capital or fixed assets.
(ii) **Financing / Investment opportunities** — where the firm has projects earning a return greater than the shareholders' required return (r > Ke), retention creates more value than distribution (Walter/Gordon logic).
(iii) **Cost of raising external finance** — high floatation costs favour retention, since internally-generated funds are cheaper than fresh issues.
(iv) **Stability of earnings** — firms with stable earnings can afford higher and steadier payouts than those with volatile earnings.
(v) **Shareholders' expectations / Clientele effect** — some investors prefer regular income; abrupt changes in payout disturb this clientele and can depress the share price (signalling).
(vi) **Legal and contractual constraints** — provisions of the Companies Act, 2013 (e.g. transfer to reserves, payment out of free reserves) and loan covenants restrict the amount distributable.
(vii) **Taxation and control considerations** — the tax position of shareholders and the desire to avoid dilution of control through fresh issues.

**Conclusion:** A rigid 100% payout ignores profitable reinvestment opportunities, liquidity needs, floatation costs and the benefits of a stable, sustainable dividend. Hence a **balanced payout** that funds value-adding investment while meeting shareholder expectations is generally optimal, not a full distribution.

*(Full-marks tip: Frame each determinant as a decision factor and tie at least one to the r vs Ke rule (growth firms retain). Simply listing headings without the "why" loses the explanation marks.)*

### Q44. Ch: Dividend Decisions — Graham & Dodd (Traditional) Model / Linter's Model (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Write a short note on (a) the **Traditional (Graham & Dodd) approach** to share valuation, and (b) **Linter's model** of dividend policy.

**Answer:**

**(a) Traditional (Graham & Dodd) approach:** This approach holds that the stock market places considerably **more weight on dividends than on retained earnings**. The value of a share is expressed as:

P = m [D + (E/3)]

where P = market price, D = dividend per share, E = earnings per share, and m = a multiplier. The equation implies that a rupee of dividend has, roughly, **three to four times** the weight in influencing share price as a rupee of retained earnings. The model therefore favours a liberal and stable dividend policy, arguing that investors are "dividend-conscious" and that liberal dividends reduce the perceived risk of a share.

**(b) Linter's model:** Linter observed that firms do not adjust dividends immediately to changes in earnings but move **gradually toward a target payout**, giving a *smoothed* dividend stream. His model is:

D₁ = D₀ + [(EPS × Target payout) − D₀] × Adjustment factor

where D₁ = dividend for the current year, D₀ = previous year's dividend, and the adjustment (speed-of-adjustment) factor lies between 0 and 1. The model captures two behavioural facts: firms are reluctant to cut dividends, and they raise dividends only when they are confident the higher level can be sustained (dividend "stickiness").

**Conclusion:** Both models emphasise, in different ways, that **dividends carry informational and psychological significance** beyond the strict irrelevance argued by MM — Graham & Dodd through a valuation premium on dividends, and Linter through the observed smoothing behaviour of firms.

*(Full-marks tip: Reproduce the exact formulae — P = m[D + E/3] for Graham-Dodd and the partial-adjustment equation for Linter. Stating the "3–4 times weight" and the "adjustment factor between 0 and 1" earns the precision marks.)*

### Q47. Ch: Dividend Decisions — Comparison of Walter, Gordon and MM Models (Theory) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between the **Walter model**, the **Gordon model** and the **Modigliani-Miller (MM) approach** to dividend policy with respect to their assumptions and conclusions on the relevance of dividends.

**Answer:** The three models take fundamentally different positions on whether the dividend decision affects the value of the firm. The **Walter** and **Gordon** models are "dividend-relevance" theories, whereas **MM** is a "dividend-irrelevance" theory. The distinction is set out below.

| Basis | Walter Model | Gordon Model | MM Approach |
|-------|--------------|--------------|-------------|
| **View on dividend** | Relevant | Relevant | Irrelevant |
| **Key assumption on financing** | All financing by retained earnings; no external finance | Same — no external finance; internal financing only | External financing freely available; perfect capital market |
| **Ke and r** | Both constant | Both constant | Ke constant; taxes ignored |
| **Growth treatment** | No explicit growth model; static capitalisation | Explicit growth: g = br; dividends grow | Not applicable to value |
| **Optimum payout** | 0% if r>Ke; 100% if r<Ke; any if r=Ke | Same directional conclusion via retention | No optimum — value independent of payout |
| **Valuation formula** | P = [D + (r/Ke)(E−D)]/Ke | P = E(1−b)/(Ke − br) | P₀ = (P₁+D₁)/(1+Ke) |
| **Core conclusion** | Dividend affects value through r vs Ke | Same, with growth compounding | Dividend & retention are perfect substitutes; value unchanged |

**Conclusion:** Walter and Gordon conclude that, because they assume *no external financing*, the payout ratio directly determines value through the relationship between r and Ke. MM, by allowing frictionless external finance in a perfect market, shows that any dividend paid is exactly offset by fresh issue, so **value depends only on the firm's earning power and investment policy, not on how earnings are split between dividend and retention.**

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards pinning down the single assumption that drives the difference — "no external financing" (Walter/Gordon) vs "perfect market with external financing" (MM). A table comparing assumptions AND conclusions scores higher than prose.)*

### Q49. Ch: Dividend Decisions — Stock Dividend (Bonus) & Stock Split (Theory) (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the concepts of **stock dividend (bonus shares)** and **stock split**, and state their effect on (i) the number of shares, (ii) the face value, and (iii) shareholder wealth.

**Answer:** A **stock dividend (bonus issue)** is a distribution of additional equity shares to existing shareholders in proportion to their holdings, in place of (or in addition to) a cash dividend. It is effected by **capitalising the company's reserves** — reserves are converted into share capital — so it involves no outflow of cash from the company.

A **stock split** is a division of each existing share into a larger number of shares of proportionately **smaller face value** (e.g. one ₹10 share split into two ₹5 shares). It does not touch the reserves; only the face value and number of shares change.

| Effect on | Stock Dividend (Bonus) | Stock Split |
|-----------|------------------------|-------------|
| Number of shares | Increases | Increases |
| Face value per share | Unchanged | Reduces proportionately |
| Reserves | Reduced (capitalised) | Unchanged |
| Paid-up share capital | Increases | Unchanged |
| Shareholder's total wealth | Theoretically unchanged | Theoretically unchanged |

**Conclusion:** In both cases the **shareholder's proportionate ownership and total wealth remain unchanged** — the same value is merely divided among more shares, reducing the market price per share proportionately. Their purpose is to improve marketability/liquidity of the share and to signal management's confidence, not to transfer real value.

*(Full-marks tip: The key distinction is that a bonus issue capitalises reserves and raises paid-up capital, whereas a split only subdivides face value and leaves reserves and capital untouched. Stating "wealth unchanged in both" earns the conceptual mark.)*

### Q51. Ch: Risk Analysis in Capital Budgeting — Concept & Merits/Demerits of RADR and CE Methods (Theory) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the **Risk-Adjusted Discount Rate (RADR)** and the **Certainty-Equivalent (CE)** methods of incorporating risk in capital budgeting. State the merits and demerits of each and indicate which is theoretically superior.

**Answer:**

**Risk-Adjusted Discount Rate (RADR):** Under this method, risk is incorporated in the **denominator** of the NPV equation by adding a **risk premium** to the risk-free rate. Thus RADR = Risk-free rate + Risk premium, and the *unadjusted* expected cash flows are discounted at this higher rate. The greater the perceived risk, the higher the premium and the lower the present value.

**Certainty-Equivalent (CE) method:** Here risk is incorporated in the **numerator**. Each uncertain cash flow is multiplied by a **certainty-equivalent coefficient** (a factor between 0 and 1 reflecting management's confidence: the riskier the flow, the lower the coefficient). These "certain" cash flows are then discounted at the **risk-free rate**.

| Basis | RADR | Certainty-Equivalent |
|-------|------|----------------------|
| Adjusts | Discount rate (denominator) | Cash flows (numerator) |
| Discount rate used | Risk-adjusted rate | Risk-free rate |
| Risk over time | Assumes risk increases at a constant compound rate | Risk adjusted separately for each year |
| Ease of use | Simple, widely used | Requires subjective CE coefficients each year |

**Merits of RADR:** Simple to understand and apply; consistent with the risk-return preference of investors; uses a single, market-observable style of rate.
**Demerits of RADR:** It assumes risk **increases uniformly with time**, which is often unrealistic; the same premium is applied to all cash flows of a year regardless of their individual riskiness; determining the premium is subjective.

**Merits of CE:** Conceptually superior — it separates the **time value of money** (handled by the risk-free rate) from **risk** (handled by the coefficients), and allows risk to be adjusted **year-by-year** to match its actual pattern.
**Demerits of CE:** The certainty-equivalent coefficients are **subjective** and difficult to determine objectively and consistently.

**Conclusion:** The **Certainty-Equivalent method is theoretically superior** because it does not force the unrealistic assumption that risk grows at a constant rate over time; however, RADR remains more popular in practice owing to its simplicity.

*(Full-marks tip: The single most-rewarded point is "RADR adjusts the denominator, CE adjusts the numerator." State that CE discounts at the risk-free rate while RADR uses the risk-adjusted rate, and conclude that CE is theoretically superior because RADR wrongly assumes risk rises uniformly with time.)*

### Q58. Ch: Working Capital Management — Nature and Determinants of Working Capital (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** "The working capital requirement of a firm is not static but is influenced by several factors." Explain any five factors that determine the working capital requirement of a business enterprise.

**Answer:** Working capital represents the funds required to finance the day-to-day operations of a business, i.e., the excess of current assets over current liabilities. Its requirement is not fixed and is governed by the following factors:

**1. Nature of Business:** Trading and financial concerns require a large investment in current assets (inventory and debtors) relative to fixed assets and hence need more working capital, whereas public utilities like electricity and water supply have a small proportion of current assets and require limited working capital.

**2. Scale of Operations / Size of Business:** A firm operating on a larger scale needs to maintain bigger inventories, more debtors and a larger cash balance to sustain higher volumes, thereby increasing the working capital requirement proportionately.

**3. Production Cycle / Length of Operating Cycle:** The longer the time taken to convert raw material into finished goods and finished goods into cash, the greater the funds locked in inventory and debtors, and hence the higher the working capital needed. A short operating cycle reduces the requirement.

**4. Credit Policy:** A liberal credit policy allowing customers a longer collection period blocks more funds in debtors and raises working capital needs; conversely, availing longer credit from suppliers reduces the net working capital required.

**5. Business Cycle and Seasonality:** During a boom, expanded activity requires more working capital, while during a recession the requirement contracts. Seasonal industries need peak working capital during the busy season and much less in the off-season.

**Conclusion:** Since these factors vary from firm to firm and over time, the working capital requirement must be estimated afresh keeping the specific circumstances of the enterprise in view.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner wants each factor stated AND its directional effect on working capital explained in a full sentence — not a bare list. Any five well-explained factors from nature, scale, operating cycle, credit policy, business cycle, seasonality, technology, inflation, growth, or dividend policy earn full marks.)*

### Q60. Ch: Working Capital Management — Cash Management: Baumol vs Miller-Orr (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between the Baumol model and the Miller-Orr model of cash management. State one situation in which each model is more appropriate.

**Answer:** Both models are quantitative techniques used to determine the optimum cash balance a firm should hold, but they rest on different assumptions about the behaviour of cash flows.

| Basis | Baumol Model | Miller-Orr Model |
|---|---|---|
| Nature of cash flows | Assumes cash usage is **certain, steady and uniform** over the period | Assumes cash flows are **uncertain and fluctuate randomly** (stochastic) |
| Inputs required | Total annual cash requirement, fixed transaction cost, opportunity (interest) cost | Variance/standard deviation of daily cash flows, transaction cost, daily interest, lower limit |
| Output | A single optimum lot size (C*) converted each time cash runs out | Two control limits (upper and lower) and a return point |
| Pattern of action | Cash is replenished in equal lots at regular intervals | Action taken only when cash hits the upper or lower limit; otherwise no action |
| Analogy | Similar to the EOQ inventory model | A control-limit / range approach |
| Return point | Cash swings between 0 and C* (average C*/2) | Return point set one-third of the spread above the lower limit |

**Situations:** The **Baumol model** is more appropriate where cash disbursements are predictable and evenly spread, e.g., a firm paying salaries and known bills at regular intervals. The **Miller-Orr model** is more appropriate where daily cash inflows and outflows are irregular and hard to forecast, e.g., a firm with volatile daily sales and collections.

**Conclusion:** Baumol suits deterministic cash flows while Miller-Orr suits stochastic cash flows; the choice depends on the predictability of a firm's cash movements.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner looks for the certainty vs uncertainty distinction as the core difference, plus the "single lot vs control-limit" contrast. Presenting it in a table and giving a concrete situation for each model secures full marks.)*

### Q62. Ch: Types of Financing & Financial Markets — Sources of Long-term Finance (Features & Suitability) (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the meaning and any four features of **debentures** as a source of long-term finance. Also state two situations in which a company would prefer debentures over equity shares.

**Answer:** A debenture is a written instrument acknowledging a debt taken by a company, issued under its common seal, containing a contract for repayment of the principal after a fixed period (or in instalments) and for payment of interest at a fixed rate. It represents **borrowed capital** and the holder is a creditor of the company, not an owner.

**Features of debentures:**

**1. Fixed Return:** Debenture holders receive interest at a fixed, pre-determined rate, which is payable whether or not the company earns profits. Interest is a charge against profit, not an appropriation.

**2. Creditorship, not Ownership:** Debenture holders are creditors of the company. They carry no voting rights and hence do not dilute the control of existing shareholders over the management.

**3. Security / Charge:** Debentures are usually secured by a fixed or floating charge over the assets of the company, giving holders a claim on assets in the event of default or winding up, ahead of shareholders.

**4. Redeemability and Maturity:** Debentures normally carry a fixed maturity date on which the principal is repaid; they may be redeemed in a lump sum or by instalments through a sinking fund.

**5. Tax Advantage (additional):** Interest on debentures is a tax-deductible expense, reducing the effective cost of this source to the company.

**Situations favouring debentures over equity:**
1. When the company has **stable and assured earnings** sufficient to service fixed interest, debentures allow it to enjoy the benefit of **trading on equity (financial leverage)**, raising the return to equity shareholders.
2. When existing shareholders wish to **retain control**, debentures avoid dilution of voting power that a fresh equity issue would cause; the tax-deductibility of interest further lowers cost.

**Conclusion:** Debentures are a suitable long-term source for companies with steady cash flows that seek low-cost, control-preserving finance, though excessive reliance increases financial risk.

### Q64. Ch: Types of Financing & Financial Markets — Money Market Instruments (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Write short notes on any three of the following money-market instruments, bringing out their meaning and one key feature each: (i) Treasury Bills, (ii) Commercial Paper, (iii) Certificate of Deposit, (iv) Call Money.

**Answer:** The money market is the market for short-term funds (maturity up to one year) dealing in highly liquid and safe instruments. The principal instruments are explained below:

**(i) Treasury Bills (T-Bills):** Treasury Bills are short-term promissory instruments issued by the Reserve Bank of India on behalf of the Government of India to meet its short-term borrowing needs. They are issued at a **discount to face value** and redeemed at par, the difference being the investor's return; being government-backed, they carry the lowest (virtually nil) default risk. They are issued in maturities of 91, 182 and 364 days.

**(ii) Commercial Paper (CP):** Commercial Paper is an unsecured short-term promissory note issued by large, creditworthy corporates (and eligible institutions) to raise short-term funds directly from the market. Its key feature is that it is issued at a **discount** and is available only to firms with a high credit rating and a strong net worth, being backed only by the issuer's creditworthiness (unsecured).

**(iii) Certificate of Deposit (CD):** A Certificate of Deposit is a negotiable, unsecured money-market instrument issued by banks and select financial institutions against funds deposited for a fixed period. Its key feature is **negotiability/transferability**, which gives it liquidity, and it typically carries a slightly higher return than an ordinary bank deposit of similar tenure.

**(iv) Call Money:** Call money refers to funds borrowed or lent for a **very short period, generally one day (overnight) to fourteen days**, mainly among banks to meet their reserve (CRR/SLR) requirements. Its key feature is the **highly volatile call rate** determined by the day-to-day demand and supply of funds in the interbank market.

**Conclusion:** These instruments provide safe, liquid avenues for parking or raising short-term funds, differing mainly in issuer, security and maturity.

*(Full-marks tip: For each instrument give both meaning (who issues it, tenor) and a distinguishing feature (discount basis for T-Bills and CP, negotiability for CDs, overnight/volatile rate for call money). Note that T-Bills and CP are "at discount" while CDs and call money are interest/rate-based — this precision is what the examiner rewards.)*

### Q66. Ch: Types of Financing & Financial Markets — Venture Capital and Its Features (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** What is meant by **venture capital financing**? Explain any four features that distinguish venture capital from conventional debt financing.

**Answer:** Venture capital financing refers to the provision of **long-term, equity-oriented risk capital** to new, high-growth-potential businesses (typically start-ups and early-stage enterprises) that are unable to raise funds through conventional sources because of their unproven track record and high risk. The venture capitalist provides not only funds but also managerial and technical support in exchange for a share in the ownership.

**Features distinguishing venture capital from debt:**

**1. Equity Participation:** Venture capital is provided mainly in the form of equity or quasi-equity (convertible instruments) rather than as a loan, so the investor becomes a part-owner and shares in the risks and rewards, unlike a lender who only earns fixed interest.

**2. High Risk–High Return:** It is invested in ventures with a high probability of failure but also a high potential for return; the venture capitalist expects a capital gain on exit rather than periodic interest income.

**3. Long-term Horizon and Illiquidity:** The investment is for a long period (typically 5–10 years) with no immediate return; the venture capitalist realises gains only on exit through a public issue, sale or buy-back, whereas debt carries scheduled repayment.

**4. Active Involvement / "Hands-on" Support:** Beyond funding, the venture capitalist actively participates in the management, strategy and networking of the investee company, adding value — something a conventional lender does not do.

**5. Exit Route (additional):** Venture capital is provided with a planned exit in mind (IPO, trade sale, or promoter buy-back), which is how the investor earns its return.

**Conclusion:** Venture capital is thus patient, risk-bearing, participative equity finance suited to innovative and high-growth start-ups, in contrast to fixed-return, secured, repayable debt finance.

*(Full-marks tip: Stress that venture capital is EQUITY (ownership + risk sharing) with returns via capital gains on exit, contrasted point-by-point with debt (fixed interest, security, repayment). Mentioning the "hands-on" managerial support and planned exit route earns the depth marks.)*

### Q68. Ch: Types of Financing & Financial Markets — Bridge Finance, Seed Capital and Other Terms (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Write short notes on: (i) Bridge Finance, and (ii) Seed Capital assistance, as sources of finance.

**Answer:**

**(i) Bridge Finance:** Bridge finance refers to a **short-term loan** arranged by a company from a commercial bank to "bridge" the gap during the period when a sanctioned term loan (from a financial institution) or a proposed issue of shares/debentures is under process but the funds have not yet been actually disbursed or raised. It is a temporary or interim arrangement that tides the company over until the permanent source of finance materialises. Being interim in nature, it usually carries a **higher rate of interest** than the term loan it bridges, and it is repaid out of the proceeds of the term loan or issue once received. Thus, bridge finance ensures that the company's project or operations do not stall for want of funds during the interim period.

**(ii) Seed Capital Assistance:** Seed capital assistance is a form of financing provided (in India, historically through institutions such as SIDBI/IDBI schemes) to **new entrepreneurs and first-generation promoters** who have a technically feasible and economically viable project but lack the adequate **promoter's contribution / margin money** required to obtain a term loan. It is given on **soft (concessional) terms** — typically as a long-term, interest-free or low-interest "soft loan" — to strengthen the promoter's equity base so that the project becomes eligible for institutional term finance. It thus helps meet the gap in the promoter's own stake in the early (seed) stage of a venture.

**Conclusion:** Bridge finance is a short-term interim facility filling a timing gap in fund flow, whereas seed capital assistance is a soft, long-term facility that supplements a new promoter's contribution at the project's inception.

*(Full-marks tip: For bridge finance, stress "interim/temporary," "bridges the gap until term loan/issue proceeds arrive," and "higher interest." For seed capital, stress "promoter's contribution gap," "soft loan on concessional terms," and "new entrepreneurs." Two crisp, correctly-focused notes secure full marks in a 4-mark question.)*

### Q69. Ch: Introduction to Strategic Management — The Strategic Management Process (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** "Strategic management is not a box of tricks or a bundle of techniques; it is a way of thinking and a continuous, iterative process." In light of this statement, explain the four sequential phases through which the strategic management process moves in an organisation.

**Answer:**
Strategic management is the systematic and continuous process by which an organisation defines its long-term direction and translates that direction into action so as to gain and sustain a competitive advantage. It is not a one-time exercise but an iterative, feedback-driven process; the statement in the question emphasises that it is a disciplined way of thinking rather than a mere toolkit. The process is conventionally described as moving through the following four inter-related phases:

1. **Strategic Intent / Environmental Appraisal (Establishing the direction and scanning):** The process begins with the organisation defining its strategic intent through its **vision, mission, business definition, goals and objectives**, and simultaneously appraising both its external environment (macro-environment and industry) and its internal environment (resources, competencies, strengths and weaknesses). This phase answers the questions "Where do we want to go?" and "Where are we now?".

2. **Strategy Formulation:** On the basis of the appraisal, the organisation **formulates strategies** at the corporate, business and functional levels. It identifies strategic alternatives, evaluates them against the intent and the environment, and selects the best-fit strategy. This phase answers "How do we get there?".

3. **Strategy Implementation:** The chosen strategy is then **put into action** by designing an appropriate organisational structure, allocating resources (budgets), developing supportive policies, systems, leadership and a conducive culture. Formulation places forces before action while implementation manages forces during action; this is the "doing" phase.

4. **Strategic Evaluation and Control:** Finally, the organisation **monitors performance**, measures actual results against pre-set standards, analyses variances and takes corrective action. Because the environment keeps changing, this phase feeds back into the earlier phases, making the whole process **continuous and iterative** rather than linear.

| Phase | Core Question Answered | Nature |
|---|---|---|
| Strategic intent & appraisal | Where are we / where do we want to go? | Direction-setting |
| Formulation | How do we get there? | Thinking / planning |
| Implementation | Putting plan into action | Action / doing |
| Evaluation & control | Are we on track? | Feedback / correction |

**Conclusion:** The four phases together make strategic management a coherent "way of thinking" — intent and appraisal set direction, formulation designs the route, implementation drives action, and evaluation closes the loop by feeding learning back into intent.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards naming all four phases in the correct sequence AND stressing the iterative/feedback loop. A common deduction is treating the process as a one-time linear plan or omitting the appraisal element from the first phase.)*

### Q70. Ch: Introduction to Strategic Management — Levels of Strategy (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** "Sanjeevani Group" is a diversified enterprise operating in three distinct businesses — packaged foods, pharmaceuticals and a chain of quick-service restaurants. The Group Board is deciding how to allocate capital among the three businesses; the head of the pharmaceuticals business is deciding whether to compete on low price or on differentiated speciality drugs; and the marketing manager of that business is deciding the advertising media plan. Identify and explain the three levels of strategy reflected in these three decisions.

**Answer:**
In a multi-business organisation, strategy exists at three hierarchical levels, and the three decisions described map exactly onto these levels:

1. **Corporate-level strategy (the Group Board's capital-allocation decision):** This is the strategy formulated by the top management for the organisation as a whole. It is concerned with the **overall scope and direction of the enterprise** — which businesses the firm should be in, how resources should be allocated among the different businesses, and how the portfolio should be balanced. When the Sanjeevani Board decides how to distribute capital among foods, pharma and restaurants, it is taking a **corporate-level** decision. It answers the question "What set of businesses should we be in?".

2. **Business-level strategy (the pharma head's price-versus-differentiation decision):** This is formulated at the level of a single business unit or Strategic Business Unit (SBU) and is concerned with **how to compete successfully in a particular industry or market**. The pharma head's choice between competing on low cost or on differentiated speciality drugs is a classic business-level (competitive) strategy decision. It answers "How do we compete in this business?".

3. **Functional-level strategy (the marketing manager's media-plan decision):** This operates within a business at the level of individual functions such as marketing, production, finance and HR. It is concerned with **making the best use of resources within each function** to support the business-level strategy. The marketing manager's advertising media plan is a **functional-level** strategy. It answers "How do we support the business strategy within our function?".

| Corporate | Board / top management | Scope & resource allocation across businesses | Capital allocation among 3 businesses |
| Business (SBU) | SBU head | How to compete in one industry | Low price vs. speciality differentiation |
| Functional | Functional managers | Efficient use of resources in a function | Advertising media plan |

**Conclusion:** The three decisions illustrate the corporate, business and functional levels respectively; each lower level must be aligned to and support the level above it so that the whole strategy hierarchy is internally consistent.

### Q71. Ch: Introduction to Strategic Management — Vision and Mission (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between a **Vision statement** and a **Mission statement** of an organisation, and briefly state the essentials of a good mission statement.

**Answer:**
Both vision and mission are components of an organisation's strategic intent, but they answer different questions and operate over different time horizons.

| Basis | Vision Statement | Mission Statement |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Describes what the organisation **aspires to become** in the future — the desired future state | Describes the **fundamental purpose and present business** — why the organisation exists |
| Question answered | "Where do we want to go / what do we want to be?" | "What is our business and whom do we serve?" |

A good mission statement is customer-oriented and defines the business in terms of the needs it satisfies rather than merely the products it makes.

**Conclusion:** Vision inspires by describing the desired future, whereas mission grounds the organisation by defining its present purpose and scope; together they give the organisation both aspiration and direction.

### Q72. Ch: Introduction to Strategic Management — Objectives and Goals (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Objectives are the ends that state specifically how the goals shall be achieved. State the characteristics that well-designed objectives should possess and briefly explain why objectives are needed by an organisation.

**Answer:**
**Meaning:** Objectives are the **specific end-results** that an organisation seeks to achieve in pursuing its mission and goals. While goals are open-ended attributes of a desired future, objectives are the close-ended, time-bound and measurable targets that convert goals into action.

**Characteristics of well-designed objectives:** Sound objectives should be —
1. **Understandable** — expressed clearly so that everyone concerned knows what is expected.
2. **Concrete and specific** — stating exactly what is to be achieved, avoiding vagueness.
3. **Related to a time frame** — having a defined period within which they are to be accomplished.
4. **Measurable and controllable** — capable of being quantified and monitored.
5. **Challenging yet attainable** — set high enough to motivate but realistic enough to be achieved.
6. **Set within constraints** — consistent with the organisation's resources and the external environment.

**Why objectives are needed:** Objectives are required because they —
(i) **define the organisation's relationship with its environment** and help it stand as a legitimate entity; (ii) **help an organisation pursue its vision and mission** by translating them into concrete targets; (iii) provide the **basis for strategic decision-making** by pointing to priorities; and (iv) provide the **standards for performance appraisal and control**, against which actual results are measured.

**Conclusion:** Objectives give the organisation direction, a basis for planning and motivation, and a yardstick for control; they are indispensable for converting broad goals into achievable, measurable results.

### Q75. Ch: External Environment Analysis — Porter's Five Forces (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** The online food-delivery industry in India is dominated by two large aggregators, has thousands of listed restaurants and millions of price-conscious customers, and is now seeing cloud-kitchens and direct restaurant apps emerge. Using **Michael Porter's Five Forces model**, analyse the attractiveness of this industry.

**Answer:**
**Concept:** Michael Porter's Five Forces model holds that the profitability and attractiveness of an industry are determined by five competitive forces. The collective strength of these forces determines the ultimate profit potential of the industry. Applying the model to online food-delivery:

1. **Threat of new entrants:** New entrants bring additional capacity and a desire to gain market share. In food-delivery the **entry barriers are moderate to high** — heavy capital for logistics, deep discounts to acquire customers, strong network effects and brand loyalty favouring incumbents. However, low technological entry cost for niche/regional apps keeps some threat alive. *(Moderate threat.)*

2. **Bargaining power of buyers (customers):** Buyers are powerful when they are price-sensitive, well-informed and face **low switching costs**. Here, customers are numerous but **highly price-sensitive**, can easily switch between the two apps to chase discounts, and pay no switching cost. *(High buyer power.)*

3. **Bargaining power of suppliers (restaurants/delivery partners):** Suppliers are powerful when they are few or provide a differentiated input. Restaurants are **numerous and fragmented**, giving them individually low power, though large/premium restaurant brands and gig delivery-partner unions can exert some pressure. *(Moderate to low supplier power.)*

4. **Threat of substitutes:** Substitutes are alternative ways of meeting the same need. Home cooking, dine-in, direct restaurant apps and cloud-kitchens' own channels are **strong substitutes**, capping the prices aggregators can charge. *(High substitute threat.)*

5. **Rivalry among existing competitors:** Rivalry is intense when competitors are few but equally balanced, growth is slowing, and products are undifferentiated. A **duopoly of two well-matched aggregators** competing through deep discounts and near-identical service produces **intense rivalry** that erodes margins. *(High rivalry.)*

| Force | Assessment | Effect on attractiveness |
|---|---|---|
| Threat of new entrants | Moderate | Neutral to unfavourable |
| Buyer power | High | Unfavourable |
| Supplier power | Moderate–Low | Favourable |
| Threat of substitutes | High | Unfavourable |
| Competitive rivalry | High | Unfavourable |

**Conclusion:** Because four of the five forces (buyers, substitutes, rivalry and, in part, entrants) are strong, the online food-delivery industry is **structurally unattractive with thin profit margins**. To earn above-average returns a firm must reduce these forces — e.g., by building switching costs (loyalty programmes), differentiating service and diversifying revenue beyond price competition.

*(Full-marks tip: Assess each force with a reason drawn from the scenario AND give an overall verdict on attractiveness — merely listing the five forces without judging their strength and net effect loses marks.)*

### Q76. Ch: External Environment Analysis — Barriers to Entry (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Within Porter's model, the "threat of new entrants" is governed by the height of entry barriers. Explain the major sources of barriers to entry that protect established firms in an industry.

**Answer:**
**Concept:** In Porter's Five Forces framework, new entrants add fresh capacity and seek market share, which can drive down prices and profits for incumbents. The seriousness of this threat depends on the **barriers to entry** — the obstacles a new firm must overcome to compete on equal terms. The major sources of entry barriers are:

1. **Economies of scale:** Where existing firms enjoy declining unit costs with volume, a new entrant must either enter at a large scale (risking retaliation) or accept a cost disadvantage. This deters entry.

2. **Product differentiation and brand identity:** Established firms enjoy brand loyalty and customer identification built over time; a new entrant must spend heavily to overcome this loyalty.

3. **Capital requirements:** The need to invest large financial resources up front — in plant, R&D, advertising or working capital — is itself a barrier, especially where such spending is risky or unrecoverable.

4. **Switching costs:** Where buyers face one-time costs of switching from one supplier's product to another's, a new entrant must offer a substantial improvement to induce them to switch.

5. **Access to distribution channels:** If existing firms have tied up the available distribution channels, a newcomer must persuade channels to accept its product, often through price breaks or heavy promotion.

6. **Cost disadvantages independent of scale:** Incumbents may hold advantages such as proprietary technology, favourable locations, access to raw materials, learning-curve/experience effects and government subsidies that a new firm cannot easily replicate.

7. **Government policy:** Licensing requirements, regulations and restrictions on access to raw materials can limit or block entry into an industry.

**Conclusion:** The higher these barriers, the lower the threat of new entrants and the more protected — and profitable — the incumbents; low barriers, by contrast, expose the industry to constant new competition and pressure on margins.

*(Full-marks tip: List the recognised sources (economies of scale, differentiation, capital, switching costs, distribution access, cost disadvantages, government policy). Naming barriers with a one-line explanation each is what earns the marks; omitting "government policy" or "switching costs" is a common gap.)*

### Q77. Ch: External Environment Analysis — Micro vs. Macro Environment (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between the **micro (task) environment** and the **macro (general) environment** of a business, giving examples of the components of each.

**Answer:**
A firm's external environment is conventionally divided into two layers — the micro environment that operates close to the firm and directly affects its operations, and the macro environment that operates more broadly and affects all firms in the economy.

| Basis | Micro (Task) Environment | Macro (General) Environment |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Forces **close to the firm** that directly influence its ability to serve customers | Broad **societal forces** that affect all organisations in the economy |
| Proximity | Immediate / operating environment | Remote / general environment |
| Degree of control | Firm has **some influence** over these actors | Firm has **little or no control**; must adapt |
| Specificity | **Specific** to the particular firm/industry | **Common** to all firms in the economy |
| Components | Customers, suppliers, competitors, market intermediaries, financiers, the public | Political, Economic, Socio-cultural, Technological, Legal, Environmental (PESTLE) forces |

The micro environment consists of the actors in the firm's immediate surroundings — **customers** whose needs it serves, **suppliers** who provide inputs, **competitors** it contends with, **market intermediaries** who help it reach customers, and **financiers/public**. The macro environment consists of the larger **PESTLE** forces which set the backdrop within which all firms and their micro environments operate.

**Conclusion:** The micro environment is firm-specific and partly controllable, whereas the macro environment is economy-wide and largely uncontrollable; a firm must monitor both, but it can influence the former far more than the latter.

*(Full-marks tip: A clear table plus correct examples of each environment's components secures the marks. Mixing PESTLE forces into the micro environment or listing customers/suppliers under macro is a common error.)*

### Q78. Ch: External Environment Analysis — Industry & Competitor Analysis (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** For scanning the competitive environment, a strategist must understand both the industry and the specific competitors within it. Explain the concept of a **strategic group** and outline the key questions a firm should answer while carrying out a **competitor analysis**.

**Answer:**
**Strategic group — concept:** An industry is rarely homogeneous; firms within it pursue different strategies. A **strategic group** is a **set of firms in an industry that follow the same or a similar strategy** along the strategic dimensions that matter — such as price/quality positioning, product line breadth, geographic coverage, distribution channels and degree of vertical integration. Firms within a group compete most directly with one another, and the height of "mobility barriers" between groups explains why some firms in the same industry are consistently more profitable than others. Mapping strategic groups helps a firm identify its closest rivals and locate profitable, less-contested positions.

**Competitor analysis — key questions:** To understand a specific competitor, a firm should analyse four components and answer questions such as:
1. **Future goals:** What are the competitor's objectives and how do its goals compare with our own? What drives it?
2. **Current strategy:** What is the competitor doing now and what can it do? How is it currently competing?
3. **Assumptions:** What assumptions does the competitor hold about itself and about the industry? Are these assumptions realistic, and can they be exploited?
4. **Capabilities:** What are the competitor's strengths and weaknesses — its resources and competencies that determine how it will act and react?

By combining these, a firm can predict the **competitor's likely response profile** — whether it will retaliate, where it is vulnerable, and which of our moves will provoke the strongest reaction.

**Conclusion:** Strategic-group mapping clarifies who a firm's real rivals are, while competitor analysis (goals, strategy, assumptions and capabilities) helps the firm anticipate rivals' moves and reactions, enabling it to choose positions and strategies that avoid direct, damaging confrontation.

*(Full-marks tip: Define "strategic group" precisely and then list the four components of competitor analysis. Examiners reward the linkage — grouping identifies rivals, analysis predicts their behaviour.)*

### Q79. Ch: Internal Environment Analysis — Value Chain Analysis (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** "Rasoi Fresh", a farm-to-fork organic vegetable brand, wants to identify where in its operations it creates value and where costs can be reduced. Using **Porter's Value Chain**, explain the primary and support activities and how Rasoi Fresh can use the analysis to build competitive advantage.

**Answer:**
**Concept:** The **value chain** is a framework developed by Michael Porter that disaggregates a firm into its **strategically relevant activities**, in order to understand the behaviour of costs and the existing and potential sources of differentiation. Every activity a firm performs can be classified as either a **primary activity** (directly involved in creating and delivering the product) or a **support activity** (which underpins the primary activities). Applied to Rasoi Fresh:

**Primary activities:**
1. **Inbound logistics** — sourcing and receiving organic produce from partner farms, quality-grading, storage and cold-chain handling of incoming vegetables.
2. **Operations** — cleaning, sorting, packaging and processing the produce into consumer-ready packs while preserving freshness and organic certification.
3. **Outbound logistics** — warehousing finished packs and running the refrigerated delivery fleet that gets produce to retail points and customers' homes quickly.
4. **Marketing and sales** — building the "organic and fresh" brand, pricing, promotions, the ordering app and subscription plans.
5. **Service** — after-sale support such as freshness guarantees, replacement of spoiled items and customer helplines.

**Support activities:**
1. **Firm infrastructure** — general management, finance, quality-assurance and organic-certification systems.
2. **Human resource management** — recruiting and training farm-liaison staff, packing-line workers and delivery riders.
3. **Technology development** — cold-chain technology, the demand-forecasting and app platform, and farm-yield analytics.
4. **Procurement** — negotiating contracts with farmers, packaging-material vendors and transport providers.

**Using the analysis for competitive advantage:** By examining each link, Rasoi Fresh can — (i) find **cost-reduction** opportunities, e.g. optimising the cold-chain and delivery routes in logistics to cut wastage; and (ii) find **differentiation** opportunities, e.g. strengthening quality-grading in operations and freshness guarantees in service to justify a premium price. It should also examine the **linkages** between activities (better demand forecasting in technology reduces inbound over-ordering and spoilage) since managing linkages is itself a source of advantage.

**Conclusion:** Value-chain analysis lets Rasoi Fresh see the business as a system of value-creating activities; by reducing costs in some links and reinforcing differentiation in others — and by managing the linkages between them — it can build and sustain a competitive advantage in the organic-produce market.

### Q80. Ch: Internal Environment Analysis — Core Competencies (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the concept of **core competence** as a source of competitive advantage, and state the tests/criteria that a resource or capability must satisfy to qualify as a core competence.

**Answer:**
**Concept:** A **core competence** is a unique strength or a bundle of skills, knowledge and technologies that an organisation has developed and that **enables it to provide distinctive value to customers** and to compete effectively. Prahalad and Hamel described core competencies as the "collective learning in the organisation, especially how to coordinate diverse production skills and integrate multiple streams of technologies." Core competencies are the roots from which a firm's products and businesses grow, and they are difficult for rivals to imitate.

**Tests/criteria for a core competence:** Not every strength is a core competence. To qualify, a competence should satisfy the following tests:
1. **Provides access to a wide variety of markets** — a genuine core competence is not tied to a single product but can be leveraged across many products and markets (e.g., a competence in miniaturisation opening many markets).
2. **Makes a significant contribution to the perceived customer benefits** of the end product — it must be something customers value.
3. **Is difficult for competitors to imitate** — it should arise from a complex harmonisation of technologies and skills that rivals cannot easily copy, thereby giving durable advantage.

Related to this, a resource-based view holds that for a resource/capability to yield **sustained** competitive advantage it should be **Valuable, Rare, difficult to Imitate and the firm must be Organised to exploit it (VRIO)** — resources that are valuable and rare give a competitive advantage, and if they are also hard to imitate and well-organised, the advantage is sustainable.

**Conclusion:** A core competence is a distinctive, hard-to-imitate capability that delivers customer value across markets; by identifying, nurturing and leveraging its core competencies, a firm builds a competitive advantage that competitors find difficult to erode.

*(Full-marks tip: State the three Prahalad-Hamel tests explicitly (market access, customer benefit, hard to imitate). Adding the VRIO criteria strengthens the answer, but the three core-competence tests are the marks-bearing part.)*

### Q81. Ch: Internal Environment Analysis — SWOT Analysis (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** "Vachan Publishers", a 40-year-old textbook publisher, has a trusted brand and a large author network but ageing print infrastructure and negligible digital presence. The education market is rapidly shifting to e-learning, new ed-tech start-ups are entering, and government digital-education programmes are expanding. Carry out a **SWOT analysis** of Vachan Publishers and explain how such an analysis helps in strategy formulation.

**Answer:**
**Concept:** **SWOT analysis** is a tool of internal and external appraisal that synthesises a firm's internal **Strengths (S)** and **Weaknesses (W)** with the external **Opportunities (O)** and **Threats (T)**. Strengths and weaknesses are **internal** and largely controllable; opportunities and threats are **external** and largely uncontrollable. Applied to Vachan Publishers:

| Internal | External |
|---|---|
| **Strengths (S):** Trusted 40-year-old brand; large and loyal author network; established relationships with schools and distributors; strong editorial expertise. | **Opportunities (O):** Rapid growth of e-learning; expanding government digital-education programmes; possibility of digital subscription and question-bank products; partnerships with ed-tech firms. |
| **Weaknesses (W):** Ageing print infrastructure; negligible digital presence and capability; possibly an older workforce with limited tech skills; high fixed print costs. | **Threats (T):** New ed-tech start-ups capturing the market; shift of demand away from print; price competition; risk of the brand being seen as outdated. |

**How SWOT helps strategy formulation:** SWOT enables the firm to develop strategies by **matching** the four elements (a TOWS approach):
- **S–O (leverage) strategies:** Use the trusted brand and author network to launch credible digital learning products and win a share of the growing e-learning market.
- **W–O (conversion) strategies:** Overcome the digital weakness by partnering with or acquiring an ed-tech firm, thereby converting the opportunity while covering the capability gap.
- **S–T (defend) strategies:** Use brand trust and school relationships to defend against start-up threats through bundled print-plus-digital offerings.
- **W–T (avoid/minimise) strategies:** Reduce reliance on ageing print infrastructure (the weakness meeting the threat) by gradually shifting investment from print capacity to digital platforms.

**Conclusion:** SWOT reveals that Vachan's brand and author network (strengths) can be matched to the e-learning boom and government digital push (opportunities), provided it urgently fixes its digital weakness before ed-tech threats erode its position. The analysis thus directly guides the firm towards a "digital transformation while leveraging brand trust" strategy.

*(Full-marks tip: Populate all four quadrants with scenario-specific points AND show the S-O/W-O/S-T/W-T matching that turns SWOT into strategy. Merely listing S, W, O, T without linking them to strategy formulation caps the marks.)*

### Q82. Ch: Internal Environment Analysis — Resources, Capabilities & Competitive Advantage (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain how an organisation's **resources and capabilities** combine to create a competitive advantage, and distinguish between **tangible and intangible resources**. What makes a competitive advantage "sustainable"?

**Answer:**
**Concept:** The internal strength of a firm rests on its **resources** (the assets and inputs it owns or controls) and its **capabilities** (the skills to deploy those resources effectively). Neither creates advantage alone: resources are the raw material, and capabilities are the organisational routines that convert resources into value. When valuable resources are combined with the capability to exploit them, the firm develops a **distinctive competence** that becomes the basis of competitive advantage.

**Tangible vs. intangible resources:**

| Basis | Tangible Resources | Intangible Resources |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Physical/financial assets that can be seen and quantified | Non-physical assets built over time |
| Examples | Plant and machinery, land, buildings, cash, inventory | Brand reputation, patents, know-how, culture, customer goodwill, trust |
| Imitability | Relatively **easy** for rivals to acquire/imitate | **Difficult** to imitate; often firm-specific |
| Advantage potential | Usually lower and short-lived | Usually higher and more durable |

Because intangible resources are harder for competitors to identify, buy or copy, they are generally a **stronger and more durable** source of competitive advantage than tangible resources.

**Sustainability of competitive advantage:** A competitive advantage is **sustainable** when it **cannot be readily duplicated or neutralised by competitors**. This durability depends on the resource/capability being **valuable and rare** (to create advantage in the first place) and additionally **costly to imitate and non-substitutable**, with the firm **organised to exploit it** (the VRIO logic). Barriers to imitation such as complexity, causal ambiguity, historical/path dependence and social complexity protect the advantage over time.

**Conclusion:** Competitive advantage arises when a firm combines valuable resources with the capability to exploit them; intangible resources, being hard to imitate, tend to yield the most durable advantage, and the advantage becomes sustainable only when rivals cannot easily copy, substitute for, or neutralise the underlying resources and capabilities.

*(Full-marks tip: Distinguish resources from capabilities, give the tangible/intangible table, and explain "sustainable" via imitability/VRIO. A common deduction is treating "resources" and "competitive advantage" as the same thing without the capability link.)*

### Q85. Ch: Strategic Choices — Evaluation of Grand Strategies (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** "A grand strategy is the master plan that gives the broad direction to the organisation for achieving its objectives." In the light of this statement, explain the four grand strategy alternatives available to a firm and state the circumstances in which each is most appropriate.

**Answer:**
Grand strategies, also called corporate or master strategies, provide the basic long-term direction for the firm and indicate how the firm intends to achieve its objectives. They comprise four broad alternatives — stability, expansion (growth), retrenchment and combination — from which a firm selects the one best suited to its internal capabilities and external environment.

**1. Stability Strategy.** A firm follows a stability strategy when it continues to serve the public in the same product-market sectors, essentially maintaining the current pace of operations without any major change in direction. It is a "safety-oriented, status-quo" strategy in which the firm concentrates its resources where it presently has, and is likely to develop, a meaningful competitive advantage. It is appropriate when the environment is relatively stable, the firm is doing reasonably well, the risks of expansion are high, and the management prefers to consolidate rather than grow. It carries the lowest risk of the four grand strategies.

**2. Expansion / Growth Strategy.** Here the firm seeks a significant increase in its performance objectives by redefining the business — entering new products, new markets, new functions, or increasing the pace of its existing activities. It is a promising and exciting strategy that offers higher rewards but also involves higher risk and greater managerial commitment. It is appropriate when the environment offers growth opportunities, the industry is expanding, the firm has slack resources and distinctive competencies, and stability would mean stagnation. Expansion may be pursued through intensification (market penetration, market development, product development) or diversification.

**3. Retrenchment Strategy.** A firm pursues retrenchment when it retreats from serving certain market sectors, or reduces the scope of some of its activities, in order to arrest decline and restore viability. It involves regrouping through cost and asset reduction, turnaround, divestment or, in the extreme, liquidation. It is appropriate when the firm is performing poorly, faces a hostile environment or a recession, has non-performing units, or when survival itself is at stake and resources must be freed for more productive uses.

**4. Combination Strategy.** A combination strategy is the simultaneous or sequential use of two or more of the above grand strategies across the firm's different businesses or SBUs. A large, multi-business firm may expand a promising division, stabilise a mature one and retrench a declining one at the same time, or apply them in a planned sequence over time. It is appropriate for large, diversified organisations operating in several industries where a single uniform strategy cannot fit all units, and for firms in transition through changing environmental conditions.

**Conclusion:** The choice among stability, expansion, retrenchment and combination is not permanent; management evaluates each against the firm's objectives, resources and environment and selects the grand strategy that best balances risk and reward at a given point in time.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards naming all four AND giving the "when appropriate" condition for each. Marks are lost when students describe only expansion, omit combination, or fail to note that stability is the lowest-risk and expansion the highest-reward alternative.)*

| E | 5 ÷ 30 | 0.17 | Low |

| SBU | Market growth | Relative share | BCG Category |

| A – Herbal soaps | Low | High | **Cash Cow** |

| SBU | Category | Meaning | Recommended Strategy |

| D – Ready-to-cook meals | Star | High growth, market leader; needs heavy investment but is self-sustaining and the future cash cow | **Invest / Build** — protect share, fund growth to keep leadership |

### Q88. Ch: Strategic Choices — BCG vs GE Matrix (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between the BCG Growth-Share Matrix and the GE Nine-Cell Matrix as tools of corporate portfolio analysis.

**Answer:**

### Q89. Ch: Strategic Choices — Expansion through Diversification (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** ShaktiAgro Ltd., a fertiliser manufacturer, is considering four separate growth moves: (i) starting a chain of retail outlets to sell its fertilisers directly to farmers; (ii) manufacturing the ammonia it currently buys from suppliers; (iii) launching branded packaged seeds and pesticides to the same farmer customers; and (iv) acquiring a hotel chain. Identify the type of expansion/diversification represented by each move and briefly justify your classification.

**Answer:**
Expansion through diversification means entering businesses different from the firm's current operations. It may be concentric (related) or conglomerate (unrelated), and where the new business lies along the firm's own value chain it takes the form of vertical integration.

| Move | Type of Expansion | Justification |
|------|-------------------|---------------|
| (i) Own retail outlets to sell fertiliser | **Forward (vertical) integration** | The firm moves downstream toward the customer, taking over the distribution/retail stage that lies ahead in its value chain. |
| (ii) Manufacturing ammonia itself | **Backward (vertical) integration** | The firm moves upstream to produce a raw material (ammonia) it earlier bought, capturing an earlier stage of its own value chain. |
| (iii) Branded seeds and pesticides to the same farmers | **Concentric (related) diversification** | New products that share technology, distribution and the same customer base as the core fertiliser business — there is a common thread of relatedness. |
| (iv) Acquiring a hotel chain | **Conglomerate (unrelated) diversification** | The hotel business has no relationship of technology, product or market with fertilisers; the firm enters a completely unrelated industry. |

**Conclusion:** Moves (i) and (ii) are vertical integration (forward and backward respectively), move (iii) is concentric diversification, and move (iv) is conglomerate diversification — the four illustrate the full range of ShaktiAgro's expansion options and their differing risk profiles, with conglomerate diversification carrying the highest risk owing to the absence of any common strategic thread.

### Q90. Ch: Strategy Implementation & Evaluation — Structure Follows Strategy (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** "Structure follows strategy." Explain this principle and describe the main types of organisational structures a firm may adopt to implement its strategy.

**Answer:**
The proposition "structure follows strategy", associated with Alfred Chandler, holds that a change in a firm's strategy should be followed by a corresponding change in its organisational structure, because the structure is the vehicle through which strategy is executed. Structure refers to the framework of roles, reporting relationships, grouping of activities and allocation of authority. When a firm's strategy changes — for example, from a single product to diversification — the existing structure becomes inadequate, performance declines, and a new structure must be designed to fit the new strategy. Thus strategy is the primary driver and structure the follower; a mismatch between the two leads to inefficiency and strategy failure.

The principal structural forms used to implement strategy are:

**1. Simple / Entrepreneurial Structure.** The owner-manager takes most decisions directly and supervises all activities; staff serve as an extension of the manager. It suits small firms with a single or narrow product line operating in a limited market.

**2. Functional Structure.** Activities are grouped by specialised function — production, marketing, finance, HR, R&D — each headed by a specialist reporting to the chief executive. It suits firms with a single or dominant product line and promotes efficiency through specialisation, but can create coordination problems as the firm grows.

**3. Divisional Structure.** The firm is organised into semi-autonomous divisions based on product, geography or customer, each with its own functional set-up and profit responsibility. It suits large, diversified firms, permits accountability and decentralised decision-making, though it may duplicate resources.

**4. Strategic Business Unit (SBU) Structure.** Related divisions are grouped into a manageable number of SBUs, each a self-contained planning unit with its own competitors and strategy, headed by an executive reporting to top management. It improves planning and control in very large, multi-industry firms.

**5. Matrix Structure.** Functional and product/project lines of authority are superimposed, so an employee reports to both a functional head and a project head. It suits project-driven, complex organisations needing dual focus, but risks confusion from dual command.

**Conclusion:** Because structure is the means of executing strategy, management must redesign the structure whenever strategy changes — choosing the simple, functional, divisional, SBU or matrix form that best fits the firm's chosen strategy, size and diversity.

*(Full-marks tip: Attribute the idea to Chandler and stress the cause-and-effect direction (strategy first, structure second). Listing structures without linking each to the strategy/size it fits is the common mark deduction.)*

### Q91. Ch: Strategy Implementation & Evaluation — Strategic Leadership (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** What is strategic leadership? Explain the two approaches to strategic leadership that a manager may adopt while implementing strategy.

**Answer:**
Strategic leadership is the ability of the top management to anticipate, envision, maintain flexibility, think strategically, and work with others to initiate changes that will create a viable future for the organisation. It provides the vision and direction for the growth and success of the firm and is central to effective strategy implementation, because even a sound strategy fails without leadership able to energise people to execute it.

There are two broad approaches to strategic leadership:

**1. Transformational Leadership.** This style motivates followers to transcend their own self-interest for the good of the organisation. The transformational leader raises the aspirations of followers, articulates and inspires them with a compelling vision, changes their beliefs and values, and empowers them to achieve more than they thought possible. Such leaders systematically challenge the status quo, take the firm into new directions, and generate deep commitment. This approach is best suited to organisations undergoing change or renewal.

**2. Transactional Leadership.** This style focuses on the day-to-day exchange (transaction) between leader and follower — designing systems, structures, rewards and controls to get the routine work done. The transactional leader motivates followers by appealing to their self-interest through a system of rewards for performance and correction for shortfalls, and emphasises efficient, orderly running of current operations rather than transformation. This approach suits stable environments where continuity and control are the priorities.

**Conclusion:** Transformational leadership inspires change and commitment to a vision, whereas transactional leadership secures compliance and efficiency in existing operations; effective strategy implementation usually calls for a blend, with transformational leadership dominant during periods of strategic change.

*(Full-marks tip: Define strategic leadership first, then contrast transformational (change/vision) with transactional (exchange/reward-and-control). The frequent deduction is describing generic leadership styles instead of these two specific strategy-implementation approaches.)*

### Q92. Ch: Strategy Implementation & Evaluation — Strategic Control (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the four types of strategic control used in the process of strategy evaluation.

**Answer:**
Strategic control is the process of monitoring and correcting a firm's strategy and performance as it is implemented, so as to keep the strategy on course in a changing environment. Because strategy is implemented over a long period and under uncertainty, four distinct types of strategic control are exercised:

**1. Premise Control.** Every strategy is based on certain planning premises or assumptions about environmental and industry conditions. Premise control is designed to check systematically and continuously whether the premises on which the strategy was built remain valid. If a key premise no longer holds, the strategy may have to be changed. Responsibility is usually assigned so that the assumptions are monitored by those best able to verify them.

**2. Strategic Surveillance.** Unlike premise control, which is focused on specific assumptions, strategic surveillance is a broad, unfocused monitoring of a wide range of events inside and outside the firm that are likely to affect the strategy. It is a general "watchfulness" — through sources such as trade journals, conferences and observation — intended to uncover information that was not anticipated.

**3. Special Alert Control.** This is the need to thoroughly, and often rapidly, reconsider the firm's strategy because of a sudden, unexpected event — such as a natural calamity, a hostile takeover bid, an accident, or a competitor's surprise move. It triggers an immediate and intense review through crisis teams and contingency plans.

**4. Implementation Control.** Strategy implementation takes place as a series of steps, programmes and resource allocations over time. Implementation control is aimed at assessing whether the overall strategy should be changed in the light of unfolding results from the actions taken to implement it. Its two basic forms are monitoring strategic thrusts (the key phases of the strategy) and milestone reviews (assessment at critical points or intervals).

**Conclusion:** Premise control tests the assumptions, strategic surveillance keeps a broad watch, special alert control responds to sudden shocks, and implementation control reviews step-by-step progress — together they ensure the strategy remains valid and on track.

*(Full-marks tip: Name all four and keep premise control (specific assumptions) clearly separate from strategic surveillance (broad, unfocused). Omitting special alert control or blurring it with surveillance is the usual mark loss.)*

### Q93. Ch: Strategy Implementation & Evaluation — Strategic vs Operational Control (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between strategic control and operational control.

**Answer:**
Both are elements of the overall control system, but they operate at different levels and time horizons of management.

| Basis | Strategic Control | Operational Control |
|-------|-------------------|---------------------|
| Focus | Steering the organisation's future direction and its fit with the environment | Executing and monitoring the ongoing, day-to-day operations |
| Time horizon | Long-term | Short-term (weeks, months, a quarter) |
| Main concern | Whether the strategy is being implemented as planned and whether it is still valid | Whether current performance is in line with the operating plan/budget |
| Environment | Constantly monitors the external environment for change | Largely concerned with the internal environment |
| Corrective action | May require redirecting or even changing the strategy itself | Requires adjusting operations to meet established standards |
| Exercised by | Top management | Middle and lower management |
| Nature | Proactive and continuous | Comparatively routine and periodic |

**Conclusion:** Strategic control asks "are we doing the right things and is the strategy still sound?", whereas operational control asks "are we doing things right within the existing plan?"; both are complementary and necessary for effective strategy evaluation.

*(Full-marks tip: The one-line contrast — "doing the right things" versus "doing things right" — earns the top band. Deductions come from swapping the time horizons or the management levels.)*

### Q95. Ch: Functional & Digital Strategy — Financial Functional Strategy (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** What is a financial functional strategy? State the key decision areas that a financial plan and strategy must address.

**Answer:**
Financial strategy is the functional strategy concerned with the acquisition, allocation and management of funds so as to support the firm's overall business strategy and maximise shareholder value. It examines the financial implications of strategic choices and identifies the best financial course of action. Because finance is the common thread that links all other functions, the financial plan and strategy must address several inter-related decision areas:

**1. Capital structure (financing) decisions** — determining the most appropriate mix of debt and equity to fund the firm's strategy, balancing cost of capital against financial risk.

**2. Sources of funds** — deciding where funds will be raised from (retained earnings, equity issue, borrowings, etc.) and the timing of such raising, in line with the firm's strategic needs.

**3. Capital budgeting (investment) decisions** — allocating funds among competing projects and businesses so that resources flow to the strategically most attractive uses.

**4. Dividend policy** — deciding how much of the earnings to distribute to shareholders and how much to retain for reinvestment, consistent with growth plans.

**5. Working capital management** — ensuring adequate liquidity for day-to-day operations while avoiding idle funds.

**6. Management of cash flows and relationships with financiers** — planning cash flows, and managing relationships with banks, financial institutions and shareholders.

Worth noting is that the financial strategy also involves analysing the worth of the business, acquisitions, mergers and buy-outs, and the mode of financing them, since these are major strategic actions with heavy financial implications.

**Conclusion:** A sound financial functional strategy ensures that funds are raised at least cost and deployed to the strategically most rewarding uses, thereby underpinning the successful implementation of the firm's business strategy.

*(Full-marks tip: Frame finance as the strategy-enabling function and cover BOTH sources and uses of funds. Listing only ratio analysis or budgeting, without capital structure, dividend and worth-of-business decisions, costs marks.)*

### Q97. Ch: Functional & Digital Strategy — E-Business Models & Supply Chain (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the concept of Supply Chain Management (SCM) and state how it differs from logistics management.

**Answer:**
Supply Chain Management (SCM) is the management of the entire flow of goods, services, information and finances as they move from the supplier of raw materials, through manufacturing and distribution, to the final customer. It integrates and coordinates all the activities and organisations involved — suppliers, manufacturers, warehouses, transporters, retailers and customers — so that the right product reaches the right place at the right time and at the least cost, while maximising customer value. SCM treats the supply chain as a single integrated network rather than a set of separate functions, and relies heavily on information technology to link the partners.

Logistics management is often confused with SCM but is in fact a part of it:

| Basis | Supply Chain Management | Logistics Management |
|-------|-------------------------|----------------------|
| Scope | Broad — the entire network from raw-material suppliers to end customers | Narrower — a part of SCM |
| Concern | Coordination and integration of all partners and flows across firms | Movement and storage of goods within and to/from the firm |
| Activities | Sourcing, production, logistics, information flow, customer relations | Transportation, warehousing, inventory handling, order fulfilment |
| Objective | Competitive advantage and customer value across the whole chain | Efficient flow and storage of goods at least cost |
| Relationship | The whole | One component of the whole |

**Conclusion:** SCM is the wider, integrative concept covering all firms and flows from source to consumption, whereas logistics management is one component of SCM dealing specifically with the movement and storage of goods; effective SCM improves cost, speed and customer service and is a key part of a firm's digital and operational strategy.

*(Full-marks tip: The key mark-earner is "logistics is a part of SCM, not a synonym" — state the whole-versus-part relationship explicitly. Treating the two as identical is the usual deduction.)*

### Q98. Ch: Strategy Implementation & Evaluation — McKinsey 7-S Framework (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the McKinsey 7-S Framework and briefly describe each of its seven elements as a tool for effective strategy implementation.

**Answer:**
The McKinsey 7-S Framework, developed by consultants at McKinsey & Company, is a model which holds that effective strategy implementation depends on the alignment of seven interrelated internal elements of an organisation, all of which must reinforce one another. The seven elements are grouped into three "hard" S's (strategy, structure, systems), which are easier to identify and influence, and four "soft" S's (style, staff, skills, shared values), which are harder to manage but equally important. The seven elements are:

**1. Strategy.** The set of actions and plans a firm devises to build and sustain competitive advantage and achieve its objectives.

**2. Structure.** The organisational framework — how activities are grouped, and the pattern of authority and reporting relationships.

**3. Systems.** The formal and informal procedures, processes and routines (such as budgeting, information and reward systems) that govern day-to-day activity.

**4. Style.** The leadership and management style of top managers — how they behave, allocate their time and attention, and the culture they set.

**5. Staff.** The people in the organisation — their number, categories, recruitment, development and socialisation.

**6. Skills.** The distinctive capabilities and competencies of the organisation and its people — what the firm does particularly well.

**7. Shared Values (Superordinate Goals).** Placed at the centre of the framework, these are the core values, guiding beliefs and aspirations that the members share and that hold the other six elements together.

**Conclusion:** The 7-S model stresses that strategy is only one of seven elements; for implementation to succeed, structure, systems, style, staff, skills and shared values must all be aligned and mutually supportive, with shared values at the core binding them together.

*(Full-marks tip: You must name all seven, classify them into hard and soft S's, and place shared values at the centre. Missing "shared values" or confusing "style" with "systems" are the common mark losses.)*

### Q99. Ch: Strategic Choices — Retrenchment / Turnaround Strategy (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** TerraTools Ltd., a mid-sized engineering firm, has recorded losses for three consecutive years due to an over-diversified product range, high fixed costs and a loss-making export division. The board wishes to restore the firm to viability rather than sell or close it. (a) Identify the grand strategy the board should pursue. (b) Explain the concept of turnaround strategy. (c) State the conditions/elements that make a turnaround appropriate and successful for TerraTools.

**Answer:**
**(a) Grand strategy to pursue.** Because TerraTools is performing poorly but the board wishes to restore viability rather than exit, the appropriate grand strategy is **retrenchment**, and specifically a **turnaround strategy** (as opposed to divestment or liquidation).

**(b) Concept of turnaround strategy.** Turnaround is a form of retrenchment strategy adopted when a firm's overall poor performance is not so severe as to be beyond repair; management retreats from and cuts back on some of its activities in order to arrest the decline, stabilise operations, and restore the firm to a position of profitability and strength. It involves regrouping through cost reduction, asset reduction and revenue generation, so that the firm can survive and then grow again. It is essentially a "back-to-health" strategy applied while the firm is a going concern.

**(c) Conditions and elements for TerraTools.** A turnaround is appropriate here because the losses, though serious, are traceable to correctable causes (over-diversification, high fixed costs, one loss-making division) rather than to an irreversible collapse. - **Changing the leadership**, since new management is often needed to drive the turnaround and restore stakeholder confidence.
- **Redefining the firm's strategic focus** — pruning the over-diversified product range to concentrate on the profitable core.
- **Selling off or closing unwanted units** — e.g. exiting or restructuring the loss-making export division (asset reduction).
- **Cost reduction** — cutting high fixed costs and improving operational efficiency.
- **Revenue enhancement** — increasing sales of the retained profitable lines through pricing and promotion.
- **Improving the management of assets and working capital** to release cash.
- **Winning the confidence of stakeholders** — employees, lenders and customers — so that the plan can be carried through.

**Conclusion:** TerraTools should pursue a retrenchment/turnaround strategy — replacing leadership, pruning products, exiting the loss-making export division, cutting costs and lifting revenue — to arrest the decline and return the firm to viability as a going concern.

### Q100. Ch: Functional & Digital Strategy — Integrated Functional Strategy Case (Marks: 8) [Application]

**Answer:**
A functional strategy is the approach a functional area takes to achieve corporate and business objectives by maximising resource productivity; it operationalises the higher-level strategy and gives short-term, action-oriented direction to each function. For UrbanCart's shift to an omni-channel model, the functional strategies must be mutually consistent and jointly support the business strategy, as follows:

*(Full-marks tip: This is an integration question — the marks are for showing how the four functional strategies FIT TOGETHER to serve one business strategy, not for four disconnected definitions. Add a concluding "alignment" paragraph; its absence is the biggest deduction.)*
