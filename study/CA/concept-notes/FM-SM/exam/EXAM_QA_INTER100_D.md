### Q1. Ch: Ratio Analysis — Cash-Flow-Based Coverage Ratios (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** From the following data of Vayu Ltd. for the year ended 31st March, 2026, compute (i) Cash Interest Coverage Ratio and (ii) Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR). Comment briefly on the difference between the two.

| Particulars | ₹ (in lakh) |
|---|---|
| Net Profit after Tax | 84 |
| Tax paid | 36 |
| Interest on long-term debt | 40 |
| Depreciation & amortisation | 30 |
| Non-cash items other than depreciation (provisions written back) | (6) |
| Instalment of principal repayable during the year | 50 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Determination of Earnings before Interest, Tax, Depreciation (cash operating profit)**
Net Profit after Tax = ₹84 lakh
Add: Tax = ₹36 lakh → Profit before Tax = ₹120 lakh
Add: Interest = ₹40 lakh → Profit before Interest & Tax (EBIT) = ₹160 lakh
Add: Depreciation & amortisation = ₹30 lakh
Less: Non-cash write-back (income not involving cash) = ₹6 lakh
**Cash profit available before interest & tax = 160 + 30 − 6 = ₹184 lakh**

**WN-2 — Cash available for debt service**
For DSCR the servicing is met out of cash profit **after tax but before interest** (since interest is what we are covering):
Cash available = Net Profit after Tax + Depreciation + Interest − Non-cash income
= 84 + 30 + 40 − 6 = **₹148 lakh**

**Statement Showing Cash-Flow Coverage Ratios**

| Ratio | Formula | Substitution | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| (i) Cash Interest Coverage | (Cash profit before interest & tax) ÷ Interest | 184 ÷ 40 | **4.60 times** |
| (ii) DSCR | (NPAT + Dep + Interest − non-cash income) ÷ (Interest + Principal instalment) | 148 ÷ (40 + 50) = 148 ÷ 90 | **1.64 times** |

**Comment:** The Cash Interest Coverage Ratio measures only the buffer available to meet **interest** out of cash operating earnings, whereas DSCR measures the ability to service the **entire debt obligation** — interest plus principal repayment — falling due in the year. DSCR (1.64) being materially lower than the interest coverage (4.60) shows that principal repayment is the tighter constraint; a DSCR above 1 nevertheless confirms the company can meet its full debt service from operating cash flows.

**Answer:** Cash Interest Coverage = **4.60 times**; DSCR = **1.64 times**.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards adding back depreciation AND deducting non-cash income, and — for DSCR — putting the principal instalment in the denominator while keeping interest **after** tax add-back logic. Common deduction: forgetting that non-cash income must be subtracted, and mixing EBIT into DSCR numerator instead of cash profit.)*

### Q2. Ch: Ratio Analysis — Market-Value Ratios (P/E, Earnings Yield, Dividend Yield) (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** The equity of Surya Ltd. has the following particulars for FY 2025-26. Compute (i) EPS, (ii) P/E Ratio, (iii) Earnings Yield, (iv) Dividend Yield and (v) Dividend Payout Ratio.

| Particulars | Amount |
|---|---|
| Net Profit after Tax | ₹96,00,000 |
| Preference dividend | ₹6,00,000 |
| No. of equity shares | 15,00,000 |
| Market price per equity share | ₹120 |
| Dividend declared on equity | ₹36,00,000 |
| Face value per share | ₹10 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Earnings available to equity shareholders**
= NPAT − Preference dividend = 96,00,000 − 6,00,000 = **₹90,00,000**

**WN-2 — EPS**
= Earnings available to equity ÷ No. of equity shares = 90,00,000 ÷ 15,00,000 = **₹6 per share**

**WN-3 — Dividend per share (DPS)**
= 36,00,000 ÷ 15,00,000 = **₹2.40 per share**

**Statement Showing Market-Value Ratios**

| Ratio | Formula | Substitution | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| P/E Ratio | MPS ÷ EPS | 120 ÷ 6 | **20 times** |
| Earnings Yield | EPS ÷ MPS × 100 | 6 ÷ 120 × 100 | **5%** |
| Dividend Yield | DPS ÷ MPS × 100 | 2.40 ÷ 120 × 100 | **2%** |
| Dividend Payout | DPS ÷ EPS × 100 | 2.40 ÷ 6 × 100 | **40%** |

**Cross-check (OR):** Earnings Yield (5%) is the reciprocal of P/E (1 ÷ 20 = 0.05). Dividend Yield (2%) = Earnings Yield × Payout = 5% × 40% = 2% ✓.

**Answer:** EPS ₹6; P/E 20 times; Earnings Yield 5%; Dividend Yield 2%; Payout 40%.

*(Full-marks tip: Preference dividend MUST be deducted before EPS. Examiners deduct marks for computing yield on face value instead of market price — dividend yield and earnings yield are always on MPS.)*

### Q3. Ch: Ratio Analysis — Effect of Window-Dressing (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** On 31st March, 2026 Meghna Ltd. reported Current Assets ₹60,00,000 and Current Liabilities ₹30,00,000. On investigation, the auditor found the following window-dressing entries passed just before the year-end:
(a) A creditor of ₹5,00,000 was paid off on 30th March by drawing on cash.
(b) Goods costing ₹4,00,000 already sold were still included in closing inventory.
(c) A short-term bank loan of ₹6,00,000 falling due within 3 months was wrongly grouped under long-term borrowings.

Recompute the corrected Current Ratio and comment on how window-dressing distorted the reported liquidity.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Reported Current Ratio (before correction)**
= Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities = 60,00,000 ÷ 30,00,000 = **2.0 : 1**

**WN-2 — Effect of each adjustment**

| Item | Effect on Current Assets | Effect on Current Liabilities |
|---|---|---|
| (a) Payment of creditor already effected — no reversal needed (both CA & CL already reduced by ₹5L; this is a genuine payment, restores nothing) | Nil (already reflected) | Nil (already reflected) |
| (b) Goods sold wrongly in inventory — remove | − 4,00,000 | Nil |
| (c) Short-term loan wrongly in long-term — reclassify into CL | Nil | + 6,00,000 |

Note on (a): The payment reduces both cash (CA) and creditors (CL) by ₹5L equally. As it is a real transaction it needs no reversal; however, because it was timed to inflate the ratio, we show the pre-payment position for comparison in the comment.

**WN-3 — Corrected figures**
Corrected Current Assets = 60,00,000 − 4,00,000 = **₹56,00,000**
Corrected Current Liabilities = 30,00,000 + 6,00,000 = **₹36,00,000**

**Statement Showing Corrected Current Ratio**

| Particulars | Reported | Corrected |
|---|---|---|
| Current Assets (₹) | 60,00,000 | 56,00,000 |
| Current Liabilities (₹) | 30,00,000 | 36,00,000 |
| Current Ratio | 2.00 : 1 | **1.56 : 1** |

Corrected Current Ratio = 56,00,000 ÷ 36,00,000 = **1.56 : 1**

**Comment:** Window-dressing artificially raised the reported ratio from 1.56 to 2.00. Item (a) — repaying a creditor from cash just before year-end — is the classic device: paying ₹5L reduces both numerator and denominator by the same amount and, since the ratio exceeded 1, mechanically **improves** the ratio. Item (b) overstated inventory (and profit) and (c) understated short-term obligations. Corrected liquidity is materially weaker, showing the reported strength was cosmetic.

**Answer:** Corrected Current Ratio = **1.56 : 1** (against reported 2.00 : 1).

*(Full-marks tip: Marks are earned for recognising that equal payment of a creditor improves a >1 ratio, and for correctly reclassifying the misgrouped short-term loan into current liabilities. Common error: reversing the genuine creditor payment.)*

### Q4. Ch: Ratio Analysis — Interpreting Ratios / Reconstructing the Balance Sheet (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** From the following ratios and information, prepare the Balance Sheet of Ganga Ltd. as on 31st March, 2026.

| Particulars | Value |
|---|---|
| Current Ratio | 2.5 : 1 |
| Liquid (Quick) Ratio | 1.5 : 1 |
| Proprietary Ratio (Shareholders' funds / Total assets) | 0.6 |
| Working Capital | ₹4,50,000 |
| Reserves & Surplus | 40% of Share Capital |
| Bank overdraft (part of current liabilities) | ₹50,000 |

Assume there are no long-term loans other than balancing figure; all current liabilities comprise creditors and bank overdraft only.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Current Assets & Current Liabilities**
Let Current Liabilities = x. Current Ratio 2.5 → CA = 2.5x.
Working Capital = CA − CL = 2.5x − x = 1.5x = ₹4,50,000 → x = **₹3,00,000**
Current Liabilities = **₹3,00,000**; Current Assets = 2.5 × 3,00,000 = **₹7,50,000**

**WN-2 — Inventory (Stock)**
Quick Ratio 1.5 → Quick Assets = 1.5 × 3,00,000 = ₹4,50,000
Inventory = Current Assets − Quick Assets = 7,50,000 − 4,50,000 = **₹3,00,000**

**WN-3 — Current liabilities split**
Bank overdraft = ₹50,000; Creditors = 3,00,000 − 50,000 = **₹2,50,000**

**WN-4 — Shareholders' funds and Total assets (Proprietary Ratio 0.6)**
Proprietary Ratio = Shareholders' funds ÷ Total assets = 0.6.
Total assets = Fixed assets + Current assets. Let Shareholders' funds = SF, Total assets = TA.
Total liabilities side = SF + Long-term debt + Current liabilities = TA.
SF = 0.6 TA → SF + LTD + 3,00,000 = TA and SF = 0.6TA ⇒ LTD = TA − 0.6TA − 3,00,000 = 0.4TA − 3,00,000.

**WN-5 — Split of Shareholders' funds**
SF = Share Capital + Reserves; Reserves = 0.4 × Share Capital → SF = 1.4 × Share Capital.

To fix TA we need one more relation. Using Fixed Assets = TA − CA and the proprietary structure, assume (as ICAI convention here) no long-term debt so LTD = 0:
0.4TA − 3,00,000 = 0 → TA = **₹7,50,000**? That gives Fixed assets = 0, inadmissible. Hence LTD is the balancing figure and TA is fixed by SF.

Take Share Capital such that SF = 0.6 TA. Using Total assets = Fixed Assets + 7,50,000 and treating Fixed Assets from proprietary funds: Set TA = ₹15,00,000 (so SF = 0.6 × 15,00,000 = ₹9,00,000, Fixed Assets = 15,00,000 − 7,50,000 = ₹7,50,000).
Then Share Capital = 9,00,000 ÷ 1.4 = **₹6,42,857** (≈ ₹6,42,857), Reserves = ₹2,57,143.
Long-term debt = TA − SF − CL = 15,00,000 − 9,00,000 − 3,00,000 = **₹3,00,000**.

**Balance Sheet of Ganga Ltd. as on 31st March, 2026**

| Equity & Liabilities | ₹ | Assets | ₹ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Share Capital | 6,42,857 | Fixed Assets | 7,50,000 |
| Reserves & Surplus | 2,57,143 | Inventory | 3,00,000 |
| Long-term Debt | 3,00,000 | Quick Assets (Debtors + Cash) | 4,50,000 |
| Creditors | 2,50,000 | | |
| Bank Overdraft | 50,000 | | |
| **Total** | **15,00,000** | **Total** | **15,00,000** |

**Answer:** Balance Sheet total = **₹15,00,000**, with Fixed Assets ₹7,50,000, Inventory ₹3,00,000, Quick Assets ₹4,50,000; Share Capital ₹6,42,857, Reserves ₹2,57,143, Long-term Debt ₹3,00,000, Creditors ₹2,50,000, Bank OD ₹50,000.

*(Full-marks tip: Show the working chain CR → WC → CL → CA → Quick → Stock, and treat long-term debt as the balancing figure after fixing total assets via the proprietary ratio. Examiners reward internally consistent totals that tie to ₹15,00,000.)*

### Q5. Ch: Ratio Analysis — Du Pont / Return on Equity Decomposition (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** Using the Du Pont framework, compute the Return on Equity (ROE) of Kaveri Ltd. and identify which lever is driving it. Data for FY 2025-26:

| Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Net Profit after Tax | 48,00,000 |
| Sales | 6,00,00,000 |
| Total Assets | 3,00,00,000 |
| Shareholders' Equity | 1,50,00,000 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Three Du Pont components**
Net Profit Margin = NPAT ÷ Sales = 48,00,000 ÷ 6,00,00,000 = **8%**
Total Asset Turnover = Sales ÷ Total Assets = 6,00,00,000 ÷ 3,00,00,000 = **2 times**
Equity Multiplier (Financial Leverage) = Total Assets ÷ Equity = 3,00,00,000 ÷ 1,50,00,000 = **2 times**

**Statement Showing Du Pont Analysis**

| Component | Value |
|---|---|
| Net Profit Margin | 8% |
| Asset Turnover | 2.0 |
| Equity Multiplier | 2.0 |
| **ROE = 8% × 2 × 2** | **32%** |

ROE = Net Profit Margin × Asset Turnover × Equity Multiplier = 0.08 × 2 × 2 = **32%**

**Cross-check (OR):** ROE = NPAT ÷ Equity = 48,00,000 ÷ 1,50,00,000 = **32%** ✓.

**Interpretation:** ROE of 32% is jointly powered by a healthy 8% margin, efficient asset use (turnover 2), and moderate leverage (multiplier 2). Since ROA = margin × turnover = 16%, the leverage of 2 magnifies ROA to 32% — half the ROE comes from financial leverage, so any rise in interest cost would erode returns.

**Answer:** ROE = **32%**, decomposed as 8% × 2 × 2.

*(Full-marks tip: State all three levers separately and show ROA = margin × turnover = 16% before applying leverage. Examiners deduct for skipping the multiplier or using average vs closing equity inconsistently.)*

### Q6. Ch: Ratio Analysis — Defensive-Interval & Cash Ratios (Marks: 4) [Problem]
**Question:** From the following, compute (i) Cash Ratio and (ii) Defensive Interval Ratio (in days) for Tapi Ltd. for FY 2025-26 (assume 360 days).

| Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---|

| Marketable securities | 2,00,000 |
| Trade receivables | 7,00,000 |
| Current liabilities | 10,00,000 |
| Projected daily cash operating expenses | 40,000 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Liquid (near-cash) assets**
Cash + Marketable securities = 3,00,000 + 2,00,000 = **₹5,00,000**
Quick liquid assets (incl. receivables) = 5,00,000 + 7,00,000 = **₹12,00,000**

**Statement Showing Ratios**

### Q7. Ch: Ratio Analysis — Comprehensive Turnover & Operating Cycle (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** Compute the length of the operating cycle (in days, 360-day year) of Narmada Ltd. from the following and comment on the working-capital implication.

| Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Opening raw material inventory | 1,80,000 |
| Closing raw material inventory | 2,20,000 |
| Raw material consumed | 24,00,000 |
| Opening WIP | 90,000 |
| Closing WIP | 1,10,000 |
| Cost of production | 36,00,000 |
| Opening finished goods | 2,40,000 |
| Closing finished goods | 3,60,000 |
| Cost of goods sold | 34,80,000 |
| Credit sales | 48,00,000 |
| Opening debtors | 5,00,000 |
| Closing debtors | 7,00,000 |
| Credit purchases | 25,00,000 |
| Opening creditors | 3,20,000 |
| Closing creditors | 4,80,000 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Raw material holding period**
Avg RM = (1,80,000 + 2,20,000)/2 = 2,00,000 → Period = 2,00,000 ÷ 24,00,000 × 360 = **30 days**

**WN-2 — WIP holding period**
Avg WIP = (90,000 + 1,10,000)/2 = 1,00,000 → Period = 1,00,000 ÷ 36,00,000 × 360 = **10 days**

**WN-3 — Finished goods holding period**
Avg FG = (2,40,000 + 3,60,000)/2 = 3,00,000 → Period = 3,00,000 ÷ 34,80,000 × 360 = **31.03 days**

**WN-4 — Debtors collection period**
Avg Debtors = (5,00,000 + 7,00,000)/2 = 6,00,000 → Period = 6,00,000 ÷ 48,00,000 × 360 = **45 days**

**WN-5 — Creditors payment period**
Avg Creditors = (3,20,000 + 4,80,000)/2 = 4,00,000 → Period = 4,00,000 ÷ 25,00,000 × 360 = **57.6 days**

**Statement Showing Operating Cycle**

| Component | Days |
|---|---|
| Raw material holding | 30.00 |
| WIP holding | 10.00 |
| Finished goods holding | 31.03 |
| Debtors collection | 45.00 |
| **Gross operating cycle** | **116.03** |
| Less: Creditors period | 57.60 |
| **Net operating cycle** | **58.43** |

Net operating cycle ≈ **58 days**.

**Comment:** A net operating cycle of ~58 days means funds are tied up in operations for 58 days after adjusting for supplier credit. Shortening the FG holding (31 days) or the collection period (45 days) would release working capital; the generous creditor period (57.6 days) is already financing more than half the gross cycle.

**Answer:** Gross operating cycle **116 days**; Net operating cycle **≈ 58 days**.

*(Full-marks tip: Use the correct denominator for each stage — RM on consumption, WIP on cost of production, FG on COGS, debtors on sales, creditors on purchases. The frequent error is applying sales throughout.)*

### Q8. Ch: Ratio Analysis — Impact of a Transaction on Ratios (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Yamuna Ltd. currently has Current Ratio 3:1 and Quick Ratio 1.2:1. State, with reasoning, the effect (increase / decrease / no change) of EACH of the following independent transactions on both ratios:
(a) Purchase of raw material on credit ₹2,00,000.
(b) Sale of goods costing ₹1,00,000 for ₹1,50,000 on credit.
(c) Redemption of debentures at par by cheque ₹5,00,000.
(d) Received cash from a debtor ₹80,000.
(e) Wrote off obsolete inventory ₹40,000.

**Answer:**

**Statement Showing Effect on Ratios**

| Txn | Nature | Current Ratio (3:1, >1) | Quick Ratio (1.2:1, >1) |
|---|---|---|---|
| (a) Credit purchase of RM | CA↑ and CL↑ equally | **Decreases** (equal add to both, ratio >1 falls) | **Decreases** (CL↑, quick assets unchanged) |
| (b) Credit sale at profit | CA↑ by ₹1,50,000, inventory ↓ ₹1,00,000 → net CA +₹50,000 | **Increases** | **Increases** (debtors, a quick asset, rise ₹1,50,000) |
| (c) Debenture redemption by cheque | CA↓ ₹5,00,000, CL unchanged (long-term) | **Decreases** | **Decreases** |
| (d) Cash from debtor | Cash↑, debtors↓ (both quick, both current) | **No change** | **No change** |
| (e) Write-off obsolete stock | Inventory (CA) ↓ ₹40,000; quick assets unchanged | **Decreases** | **No change** |

**Reasoning highlights:**
- (a) Adding equal amounts to both numerator and denominator of a ratio greater than 1 pulls it towards 1 → decreases.
- (b) Profit converts ₹1,00,000 inventory into ₹1,50,000 receivable, injecting ₹50,000 into current assets and lifting quick assets.
- (d) A swap within current (and within quick) assets leaves both ratios unchanged.
- (e) Only inventory falls, so quick ratio (which excludes inventory) is unaffected while current ratio dips.

**Answer:** See table above.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner wants the "greater-than-1 ratio moves towards 1 on equal addition" principle explicitly stated, and the distinction that inventory changes never touch the quick ratio.)*

### Q9. Ch: Ratio Analysis — Theory: Limitations of Ratio Analysis & Window-Dressing (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** "Ratios computed from published financial statements can mislead a user." Explain any four limitations of ratio analysis, specifically bringing out how window-dressing undermines their reliability.

**Answer:** Ratio analysis is a widely used tool of financial statement analysis, yet it suffers from inherent limitations that a user must appreciate before drawing conclusions.

First, **ratios are only as reliable as the underlying accounts.** They are computed from figures in the financial statements; if those figures are distorted by **window-dressing** — for example, repaying creditors from cash immediately before the year-end to inflate the current ratio, or timing sales to boost turnover — the resulting ratios present a healthier picture than the true year-round position.

Second, **impact of price-level changes (inflation)** is ignored. Figures of different years are stated at historical cost, so a comparison of ratios over time may reflect merely changing price levels rather than real operating improvement.

Third, **lack of a standard definition** exists — different firms compute the same ratio (e.g., quick ratio, capital employed) differently, so inter-firm comparison can be misleading unless the basis is standardised.

Fourth, **ratios ignore qualitative factors** such as the quality of management, brand strength, and the composition (as opposed to the mere total) of assets and liabilities; a single ratio in isolation may conceal an adverse trend within its components.

**Conclusion:** Because window-dressing and the above factors can distort the numbers, ratios should be read alongside the notes to accounts, the cash flow statement, and trend/industry comparisons, never in isolation.

*(Full-marks tip: Tie at least one limitation explicitly to window-dressing with a concrete example — that specificity is what earns the last mark.)*

### Q10. Ch: Cost of Capital — Weighted Marginal Cost & Break-Point Schedule (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** Chetak Ltd. plans to raise fresh capital and maintains a target capital structure of 50% equity, 10% preference and 40% debt. The costs of each source rise once the following amounts are exceeded within the year:

| Source | Range of new financing | Component cost (after-tax) |
|---|---|---|
| Debt | up to ₹8,00,000 | 7% |
| Debt | above ₹8,00,000 | 9% |
| Preference | any amount | 11% |
| Equity (retained earnings) | up to ₹9,00,000 | 14% |
| Equity (fresh issue) | above ₹9,00,000 | 16% |

Compute the break-points and prepare the Weighted Marginal Cost of Capital (WMCC) schedule.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Break-points**
Break-point = (Amount of source at which cost changes) ÷ (Weight of that source in structure)
- Debt break-point = 8,00,000 ÷ 0.40 = **₹20,00,000**
- Equity break-point = 9,00,000 ÷ 0.50 = **₹18,00,000**

Ordering the break-points on the total-financing scale gives ranges: 0–₹18,00,000; ₹18,00,000–₹20,00,000; above ₹20,00,000.

**WN-2 — Component costs applicable in each range**

| Total financing range | Equity cost | Pref cost | Debt cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 – 18,00,000 | 14% | 11% | 7% |
| 18,00,000 – 20,00,000 | 16% | 11% | 7% |
| above 20,00,000 | 16% | 11% | 9% |

**Statement Showing Weighted Marginal Cost of Capital (WMCC)**

Range 1 (0–18,00,000):
= (0.50 × 14) + (0.10 × 11) + (0.40 × 7) = 7.0 + 1.1 + 2.8 = **10.90%**

Range 2 (18,00,000–20,00,000):
= (0.50 × 16) + (0.10 × 11) + (0.40 × 7) = 8.0 + 1.1 + 2.8 = **11.90%**

Range 3 (above 20,00,000):
= (0.50 × 16) + (0.10 × 11) + (0.40 × 9) = 8.0 + 1.1 + 3.6 = **12.70%**

| Total New Financing (₹) | WMCC |
|---|---|
| 0 – 18,00,000 | 10.90% |
| 18,00,001 – 20,00,000 | 11.90% |
| Above 20,00,000 | 12.70% |

**Answer:** Break-points at **₹18,00,000** (equity) and **₹20,00,000** (debt); WMCC rises **10.90% → 11.90% → 12.70%** across the three financing ranges.

*(Full-marks tip: Break-point = source-limit ÷ its weight — dividing by weight is the step most students miss. The examiner rewards ordering the break-points on the TOTAL financing axis and recomputing WMCC afresh for each slab.)*

### Q11. Ch: Cost of Capital — Cost of Debt Using Realised Yield (YTM) (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** Bijli Ltd. issued 12% debentures of face value ₹100 each, redeemable at a premium of 5% after 6 years. The debentures are currently traded at ₹92 (net of issue costs). The tax rate is 30%. Using the **approximation (short-cut YTM) method**, compute the after-tax cost of debt. Also verify by the **realised-yield / IRR interpolation** method using discount rates of 12% and 14%.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Cash flows**
Net proceeds (current price / issue value) = ₹92
Redemption value = ₹100 + 5% premium = **₹105**
Annual interest = 12% × ₹100 = ₹12 (pre-tax); after-tax interest = 12 × (1 − 0.30) = **₹8.40**

**WN-2 — Approximation method (after-tax Kd)**
Kd = [I(1−t) + (RV − NP)/n] ÷ [(RV + NP)/2]
= [8.40 + (105 − 92)/6] ÷ [(105 + 92)/2]
= [8.40 + 2.1667] ÷ 98.5
= 10.5667 ÷ 98.5 = **10.73%**

**WN-3 — Verification by IRR (present-value interpolation, after-tax)**
Discount the after-tax interest (₹8.40 p.a. for 6 yrs) and redemption (₹105 at yr 6):

At 10%: PVIFA(10%,6)=4.355, PVIF(10%,6)=0.564
PV = 8.40 × 4.355 + 105 × 0.564 = 36.58 + 59.22 = ₹95.80 (> 92)

At 12%: PVIFA(12%,6)=4.111, PVIF(12%,6)=0.507
PV = 8.40 × 4.111 + 105 × 0.507 = 34.53 + 53.24 = ₹87.77 (< 92)

Interpolate between 10% and 12%:
Kd = 10% + (95.80 − 92) ÷ (95.80 − 87.77) × 2%
= 10% + (3.80 ÷ 8.03) × 2% = 10% + 0.9464% = **10.95%**

**Statement Showing Cost of Debt**

| Method | After-tax Kd |
|---|---|
| Approximation (short-cut) | 10.73% |

### Q16. Ch: Cost of Capital — Theory: Concept and Significance of Cost of Capital (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Define "Cost of Capital" and explain its significance in financial decision-making. State the three components it is generally regarded as comprising.

**Answer:** The **Cost of Capital** is the minimum rate of return that a firm must earn on its investments so that the market value of the firm's equity shares does not fall. In other words, it is the rate of return the suppliers of capital — shareholders and lenders — require as compensation for their contribution, and it therefore represents the firm's **required rate of return** or hurdle rate.

Conceptually it comprises three components:
1. **Return at zero risk level (Rf)** — the return expected on a risk-free investment;
2. **Premium for business risk** — the additional return demanded on account of the variability of operating earnings arising from the firm's investment decisions; and
3. **Premium for financial risk** — the further return demanded on account of the firm's capital-structure (use of debt).
Thus, Cost of Capital = Risk-free return + Business-risk premium + Financial-risk premium.

Its **significance** lies in the following:
- **Capital budgeting / investment appraisal** — it is the discount rate used to evaluate NPV and the cut-off (hurdle) rate for IRR; a project is accepted only if its return exceeds the cost of capital.
- **Capital-structure design** — the firm seeks the mix of debt and equity that **minimises** the overall cost of capital, thereby maximising firm value.
- **Evaluation of financial performance** — actual returns are compared against the cost of capital to judge whether value has been created.
- **Other decisions** — dividend policy, lease-vs-buy, and working-capital financing decisions all use it as a benchmark.

**Conclusion:** Cost of capital is the pivotal link between a firm's investment and financing decisions, serving as the benchmark rate that every value-creating decision must clear.

*(Full-marks tip: Give BOTH the definition ("minimum return so market value does not fall") and at least three distinct uses. Listing the three risk components explicitly earns a mark.)*

### Q18. Ch: Capital Structure & Leverage — Net Operating Income (NOI) Approach (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the Net Operating Income (NOI) approach to capital structure. State its key assumptions and describe how the cost of equity, cost of debt and overall cost of capital behave as the degree of leverage in the capital structure changes.

**Answer:**
The Net Operating Income (NOI) approach, propounded by David Durand, holds that the market value of a firm and its overall (weighted average) cost of capital are **independent of the capital structure**. According to this approach, the way a firm is financed — the mix of debt and equity — is irrelevant to the value of the firm; a change in the debt-equity ratio does not affect the total value of the firm or its overall cost of capital.

The essential proposition is that the market capitalises the value of the firm **as a whole**, by discounting its net operating income (EBIT) at an overall capitalisation rate (Ko) which stays constant. The value of the firm is computed as V = EBIT / Ko, and the value of equity is a residual figure (S = V − Value of Debt).

The **key assumptions** of the NOI approach are as follows:
1. The overall capitalisation rate (Ko) remains **constant** for all degrees of leverage.
2. The cost of debt (Kd) also remains **constant**.
3. The market uses Ko to capitalise net operating income, so the split of capitalisation between debt and equity has no bearing on total value.
4. There are no corporate taxes (in the classical version).

**Behaviour of the three costs as leverage rises:**

| Component | Behaviour with increasing leverage | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Cost of debt (Kd) | Remains constant | Assumed constant across all leverage levels |
| Overall cost of capital (Ko) | Remains constant | Market capitalises EBIT at a fixed rate irrespective of financing mix |
| Cost of equity (Ke) | **Increases** | Equity shareholders demand a higher return to compensate for the additional financial risk created by cheaper debt |

The rise in Ke is exactly sufficient to offset the benefit of using more low-cost debt, so the two effects cancel out and Ko stays unchanged. Consequently there is **no optimal capital structure** under the NOI approach — every capital structure is as good as any other.

**Conclusion:** Under the NOI approach the value of the firm and the overall cost of capital are unaffected by the debt-equity mix; the increase in the cost of equity precisely neutralises the advantage of cheaper debt, leaving Ko constant.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner rewards the explicit statement that Ke rises to offset the cheap-debt benefit and the conclusion that there is NO optimum capital structure; marks are commonly lost for confusing NOI with the Net Income approach — under NOI it is Ko, not Ke, that is constant.)*

### Q19. Ch: Capital Structure & Leverage — Traditional View vs NOI Approach (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between the "Traditional View" and the "Net Operating Income (NOI) approach" of capital structure. Does an optimum capital structure exist under each?

**Answer:**
Both are theories that seek to explain the relationship between a firm's financing mix and its value/overall cost of capital, but they arrive at opposite conclusions on whether capital structure matters.

The **Traditional view** is a compromise between the Net Income approach and the Net Operating Income approach. It accepts that a judicious use of debt can lower the overall cost of capital up to a point, because debt is cheaper than equity and the cost of equity does not rise fast enough initially to offset the benefit. Beyond a certain level of leverage, however, both Kd and Ke rise sharply on account of increasing financial risk, and Ko begins to climb. Hence there is a level of leverage at which Ko is minimum and the value of the firm is maximum.

The **NOI approach** holds that Ko and the value of the firm are constant at all levels of leverage, because any benefit from cheap debt is exactly offset by a rise in the cost of equity.

| Basis | Traditional View | NOI Approach |
|---|---|---|
| Effect of leverage on Ko | Ko first falls, reaches a minimum, then rises | Ko remains constant throughout |
| Effect of leverage on value of firm | Value first rises, peaks, then falls | Value is unaffected by leverage |
| Behaviour of Ke | Rises, but slowly at moderate leverage; steeply at high leverage | Rises linearly to offset cheap debt at every level |
| Behaviour of Kd | Constant at low leverage; rises at high leverage | Constant at all levels |
| Optimum capital structure | **Exists** — at the point of minimum Ko | **Does not exist** — every structure is equally good |
| Underlying premise | Market imperfections allow leverage to add value up to a limit | Market capitalises the firm as a whole at a fixed rate |

**Conclusion:** Under the Traditional view an optimum capital structure exists at the point where the overall cost of capital is minimised and firm value is maximised, whereas under the NOI approach no such optimum exists because the overall cost of capital is invariant to the financing mix.

*(Full-marks tip: state clearly "optimum exists" for Traditional and "does not exist" for NOI — that single distinction carries the marks; a comparison table earns more than loose prose.)*

### Q20. Ch: Capital Structure & Leverage — Over-capitalisation vs Under-capitalisation (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the terms "Over-capitalisation" and "Under-capitalisation". State the causes and consequences of each and suggest suitable remedial measures.

**Answer:**
**Over-capitalisation** is a state in which a company's actual capitalisation (the total of its share capital, reserves and long-term borrowings) is **more than** what is warranted by its earning capacity. The company is unable to earn a fair rate of return on its capital employed. Symptomatically, the **actual rate of return is lower than the normal/fair rate of return** prevailing in the industry, and the real value of the company's assets is less than its book capitalisation.

**Under-capitalisation** is the opposite state, in which the actual capitalisation is **less than** what the earning capacity justifies. The company earns an abnormally high rate of return on its comparatively small capital base; the real worth of the assets exceeds their book value. It is a sign of effective and efficient utilisation of resources rather than a shortage of capital.

| Basis | Over-capitalisation | Under-capitalisation |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Capital > justified by earnings | Capital < justified by earnings |
| Rate of return | Actual return < fair return | Actual return > fair return |
| Real value of shares | Below book/face value | Above book/face value |
| Typical causes | Over-issue of capital, acquisition of assets at inflated prices, formation during boom, inadequate depreciation, liberal dividend policy | Under-estimation of earnings, conservative dividend policy building large reserves, promotion during depression at low cost, high efficiency |
| Consequences | Fall in share price, reduced dividend, loss of investor confidence, difficulty in raising fresh capital | Encourages competition, invites labour unrest and Government scrutiny, possibility of over-trading, high share price attracts takeover |

**Remedial measures for over-capitalisation:** reduction of debt by redeeming debentures/repaying loans, redemption of preference share capital, reducing the rate of interest/dividend, buy-back or reduction in the par value of equity shares, and improving operational efficiency to raise earnings.

**Remedial measures for under-capitalisation:** issue of bonus shares by capitalising accumulated reserves, splitting the shares to bring the market price to a reasonable level, and revising (increasing) the capital base to align it with earnings.

**Conclusion:** Both states reflect a mismatch between capitalisation and earning capacity; over-capitalisation signals inefficient use of an excessive capital base and under-capitalisation signals a capital base too small for the earnings, and each is corrected by realigning the capital structure with the true earning capacity.

*(Full-marks tip: the core discriminator the examiner looks for is "actual return vs fair return" — over = below, under = above; do not equate under-capitalisation with shortage of funds, which is a common and costly error.)*

(i) Compute the total value of the firm, the value of equity and the cost of equity (Ke).
(ii) Show what happens to the value of the firm, Ko and Ke if the company increases its debt from ₹20,00,000 to ₹30,00,000 (cost of debt remaining 10%).

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Value of the firm (constant Ko under NOI)**
V = EBIT / Ko = 8,00,000 / 0.125 = **₹64,00,000** (same in both situations under NOI).

**Part (i): Existing debt ₹20,00,000**

| Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Value of firm (V) = EBIT / Ko = 8,00,000 / 0.125 | 64,00,000 |
| Less: Value of debt (D) | 20,00,000 |
| Value of equity (S = V − D) | 44,00,000 |
| Interest = 10% × 20,00,000 | 2,00,000 |
| Earnings available to equity (EBIT − Interest) | 6,00,000 |

Ke = (EBIT − Interest) / S = 6,00,000 / 44,00,000 = **13.64%**.

**Part (ii): Debt increased to ₹30,00,000**

| Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Value of firm (V) — unchanged | 64,00,000 |
| Less: Value of debt (D) | 30,00,000 |
| Value of equity (S = V − D) | 34,00,000 |
| Interest = 10% × 30,00,000 | 3,00,000 |
| Earnings available to equity (EBIT − Interest) | 5,00,000 |

Ke = 5,00,000 / 34,00,000 = **14.71%**.

### Q25. Ch: Capital Structure & Leverage — Business Risk vs Financial Risk (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between "Business Risk" and "Financial Risk" and state how each is linked to the concept of leverage.

**Answer:**
**Business risk** is the risk arising from the firm's operations, i.e., the variability or uncertainty in the firm's operating profit (EBIT) caused by factors such as demand fluctuations, price changes, input costs and the proportion of fixed operating costs. It exists **irrespective of how the firm is financed** and is measured by the **Degree of Operating Leverage (DOL)** — the higher the fixed operating costs, the greater the business risk.

**Financial risk** is the **additional** risk borne by equity shareholders that arises purely from the firm's decision to use fixed-charge sources of finance such as debt and preference capital. Because interest is a fixed obligation, the greater the proportion of debt, the greater the variability in earnings available to equity, and hence the greater the financial risk. It is measured by the **Degree of Financial Leverage (DFL)**.

| Basis | Business Risk | Financial Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Operating activities of the firm | Financing (capital structure) decisions |
| Cause | Fixed operating costs / demand variability | Fixed financial charges (interest) |
| Measured by | Degree of Operating Leverage (DOL) | Degree of Financial Leverage (DFL) |
| Dependence on capital structure | Independent of financing mix | Depends directly on use of debt |
| Borne by | The firm as a whole | Equity shareholders specifically |

The **combined effect** of both risks is captured by the Degree of Combined Leverage (DCL = DOL × DFL), which measures the total variability of EPS to a change in sales.

**Conclusion:** Business risk stems from the operating structure and is measured by operating leverage, whereas financial risk stems from the financing structure and is measured by financial leverage; together they determine the total risk faced by equity shareholders.

*(Full-marks tip: link each risk to its leverage measure — DOL for business risk, DFL for financial risk — and mention DCL as the combined measure; that mapping is the crux of the answer.)*

### Q27. Ch: Capital Budgeting — Mutually Exclusive Projects of Different Sizes: Incremental IRR (Marks: 10) [Problem]
**Question:** Vertex Ltd. must choose between two **mutually exclusive** projects, A and B, of different sizes. The cost of capital is 12%.

| Particulars | Project A | Project B |
|---|---|---|
| Initial outlay (₹) | 10,00,000 | 20,00,000 |
| Annual cash inflow (₹) | 3,20,000 | 6,10,000 |
| Life (years) | 5 | 5 |

PV annuity factors (5 years): 12% = 3.605, 13% = 3.517, 14% = 3.433, 15% = 3.352, 16% = 3.274, 18% = 3.127, 19% = 3.058. Compute the NPV and IRR of each project, and using **incremental (differential) analysis**, advise which project should be selected. Explain the apparent conflict.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Net Present Value of each project (at 12%)**

| Particulars | Project A | Project B |
|---|---|---|
| Annual inflow (₹) | 3,20,000 | 6,10,000 |
| × PVAF (5 yr, 12%) | 3.605 | 3.605 |
| PV of inflows (₹) | 11,53,600 | 21,99,050 |
| Less: Initial outlay (₹) | 10,00,000 | 20,00,000 |
| **NPV (₹)** | **1,53,600** | **1,99,050** |

**WN-2: Internal Rate of Return of each project**
IRR is the rate where PVAF = Outlay / Annual inflow.

*Project A:* Required PVAF = 10,00,000 / 3,20,000 = 3.125. This lies between 18% (3.127) and 19% (3.058).
IRR_A = 18% + [(3.127 − 3.125) / (3.127 − 3.058)] × 1% = 18% + 0.03% ≈ **18.03%**.

*Project B:* Required PVAF = 20,00,000 / 6,10,000 = 3.279. This lies between 15% (3.352) and 16% (3.274).
IRR_B = 15% + [(3.352 − 3.279) / (3.352 − 3.274)] × 1% = 15% + 0.94% ≈ **15.94%**.

**The conflict:** By **IRR**, Project A (18.03%) ranks above Project B (15.94%); but by **NPV**, Project B (₹1,99,050) ranks above Project A (₹1,53,600). Because the projects are mutually exclusive and of **different sizes**, IRR (a percentage measure) is biased in favour of the smaller project and can mislead the ranking.

**WN-3: Incremental (differential) analysis — Project B minus Project A**

| Particulars | Incremental (B − A) |
|---|---|
| Incremental outlay (₹) | 20,00,000 − 10,00,000 = 10,00,000 |
| Incremental annual inflow (₹) | 6,10,000 − 3,20,000 = 2,90,000 |

*Incremental NPV* = 2,90,000 × 3.605 − 10,00,000 = 10,45,450 − 10,00,000 = **+₹45,450** (positive).

*Incremental IRR:* Required PVAF = 10,00,000 / 2,90,000 = 3.448, lying between 13% (3.517) and 14% (3.433).
Incremental IRR = 13% + [(3.517 − 3.448) / (3.517 − 3.433)] × 1% = 13% + 0.82% ≈ **13.82%**.

Since the **incremental IRR (13.82%) exceeds the cost of capital (12%)** — equivalently the incremental NPV is positive (+₹45,450) — the additional ₹10,00,000 invested in the larger project earns more than the required return.

**Answer:** **Project B should be selected.** Its NPV is higher (₹1,99,050 vs ₹1,53,600) and the incremental investment yields an incremental IRR of 13.82% > 12%. The IRR ranking that favoured Project A is misleading because IRR ignores the scale of investment; NPV and incremental analysis, which measure absolute wealth added, correctly favour B.

*(Full-marks tip: examiners award the decisive marks for resolving the NPV–IRR conflict through incremental analysis and comparing incremental IRR (13.82%) with the cost of capital — merely picking the higher-IRR project A is the trap and scores poorly.)*

### Q28. Ch: Capital Budgeting — Why IRR Misleads for Different-Sized Mutually Exclusive Projects (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** In the case of mutually exclusive projects of different sizes, the IRR criterion may rank the projects differently from the NPV criterion. Explain why this conflict arises and how incremental analysis resolves it.

**Answer:**
When two projects are **mutually exclusive** (acceptance of one automatically excludes the other) and are of **different sizes/scale of investment**, the NPV and IRR methods can give **conflicting rankings**. The conflict arises for two principal reasons:

1. **Scale (size) difference:** IRR is a *relative* measure expressed as a percentage of the outlay, whereas NPV is an *absolute* measure of the rupee wealth added. A small project can show a very high percentage return (IRR) on a small base, while a large project with a somewhat lower percentage may nevertheless add far more absolute wealth (NPV). Since the objective of the firm is to **maximise shareholders' wealth in absolute terms**, NPV gives the theoretically correct ranking.

2. **Reinvestment assumption:** IRR implicitly assumes that interim cash flows are reinvested at the project's own IRR, whereas NPV assumes reinvestment at the cost of capital. The NPV assumption is the more realistic, since the cost of capital is the firm's actual opportunity rate.

**Resolution through incremental analysis:** The conflict is resolved by analysing the **incremental (differential) cash flows** — the extra outlay of the larger project over the smaller, and the extra inflows it generates. If the **incremental NPV is positive** (equivalently, the **incremental IRR exceeds the cost of capital**), the additional investment in the larger project is worthwhile and the larger project should be preferred. This aligns the decision with the NPV rule and hence with wealth maximisation.

**Conclusion:** For mutually exclusive projects of different sizes, NPV is the superior criterion; where a ranking conflict with IRR occurs, it is resolved by examining the incremental cash flows and accepting the larger project whenever the incremental IRR is greater than the cost of capital.

*(Full-marks tip: name both causes — scale difference and reinvestment assumption — and state clearly that the incremental-IRR-vs-cost-of-capital test reconciles the conflict in favour of NPV.)*

### Q32. Ch: Capital Budgeting — NPV vs IRR: Reconciliation & Superiority (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** "Where NPV and IRR give conflicting signals for mutually exclusive projects, NPV should be preferred." Discuss the reasons for the conflict between NPV and IRR and explain why NPV is regarded as theoretically superior.

**Answer:**
The Net Present Value (NPV) and Internal Rate of Return (IRR) methods normally give the **same accept/reject decision for a single independent project** (NPV > 0 corresponds to IRR > cost of capital). However, for **mutually exclusive projects** the two methods may **rank the projects differently**. The reasons for the conflict are:

1. **Difference in the scale of investment (size disparity):** IRR is a percentage measure and favours the project earning the higher rate per rupee, whereas NPV measures the absolute rupee addition to wealth. A larger project may add more absolute value yet show a lower percentage return.

2. **Difference in the pattern/timing of cash flows:** Where cash flows are received earlier in one project and later in another, the ranking can reverse around a "crossover" (Fisher's intersection) discount rate.

3. **Difference in project lives.**

4. **Reinvestment rate assumption:** IRR assumes interim cash inflows are reinvested at the project's own IRR, while NPV assumes reinvestment at the firm's cost of capital.

| Basis | NPV | IRR |
|---|---|---|
| Nature of measure | Absolute (₹ of wealth added) | Relative (percentage rate) |
| Reinvestment assumption | At cost of capital (realistic) | At the project's own IRR (often unrealistic) |
| Multiple rates | Not possible | Possible with non-conventional cash flows |
| Consistency with wealth maximisation | Fully consistent | May conflict for mutually exclusive projects |

**Why NPV is superior:** (a) the reinvestment assumption of NPV (cost of capital) is more realistic; (b) NPV directly measures the change in shareholders' wealth in absolute terms, which is the firm's objective; (c) NPV always gives a unique answer, whereas IRR may yield multiple or no real rates for non-conventional cash flows; and (d) NPVs of different projects are additive.

**Conclusion:** Because it rests on a realistic reinvestment assumption and measures absolute wealth creation, **NPV is theoretically the superior criterion**, and where it conflicts with IRR for mutually exclusive projects, the NPV decision should prevail.

*(Full-marks tip: the four conflict-causes plus the four superiority-reasons form the marking grid; the reinvestment-rate assumption and the "absolute wealth vs percentage" contrast are the highest-value points.)*

| Year | Cash inflow (₹) |
|---|---|
| 1 | 5,00,000 |
| 2 | 6,00,000 |
| 3 | 5,00,000 |
| 4 | 4,00,000 |

PV factors at 10%: 0.909, 0.826, 0.751, 0.683. Compute the **Discounted Payback Period** and the **Net Present Value**, and advise.

**Solution:**

| Year | Cash inflow (₹) | PV factor @10% | PV of inflow (₹) | Cumulative PV (₹) |

### Q34. Ch: Capital Budgeting — Treatment of Working Capital & Salvage Value in Cash Flows (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain how **working capital** and **salvage value** are treated while estimating the cash flows of a capital budgeting proposal.

**Answer:**
In capital budgeting, only the **incremental, after-tax cash flows** attributable to a project are relevant. Two items that require careful treatment are the working capital and the salvage value of the asset.

**Treatment of Working Capital:**
1. Any **additional working capital** required to operate the project (extra inventory, receivables net of payables) is treated as a **cash outflow** in the year in which it is committed — usually at the commencement (Year 0), and any further increments in later years are outflows in those years.
2. Working capital does **not** attract depreciation and is **not** a tax-deductible expense; it is merely funds tied up.
3. At the **end of the project's life** the working capital is assumed to be **released/recovered** and is therefore shown as a **cash inflow** in the terminal year (discounted at the terminal-year PV factor). Only the *incremental* working capital is recovered.

**Treatment of Salvage (residual) Value:**
1. The salvage value realised on disposal of the asset at the end of the project is a **cash inflow** in the terminal year.
2. Its **tax effect** must be considered: if the sale price exceeds the asset's book (written-down) value, the excess is a **profit on sale that is taxable**, so only the **net-of-tax salvage** is taken; conversely, if the sale price is below book value, the **loss on sale gives a tax saving**, which increases the effective inflow.
3. Where salvage equals book value, there is no profit or loss and the full salvage is taken without any tax adjustment.

| Item | On commitment | On completion (terminal year) |
|---|---|---|
| Working capital | Cash outflow (not depreciable, no tax effect) | Cash inflow — full recovery |
| Salvage value | — | Cash inflow — net of tax on profit/loss on sale |

**Conclusion:** Additional working capital is an outflow when invested and an inflow (fully recovered) at the end of the project, while salvage value is a terminal inflow taken **net of the tax effect** of any profit or loss on disposal.

*(Full-marks tip: emphasise that working capital is recovered in full (no tax) whereas salvage is taken net of tax on the profit/loss on sale — mixing these two treatments is the frequent error the examiner penalises.)*

### Q40. Ch: Risk Analysis in Capital Budgeting — Risk-Adjusted Discount Rate vs Certainty Equivalent (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between the **Risk-Adjusted Discount Rate (RADR)** approach and the **Certainty-Equivalent (CE)** approach to incorporating risk in capital budgeting.

**Answer:**
Both the Risk-Adjusted Discount Rate approach and the Certainty-Equivalent approach are techniques used in capital budgeting to explicitly incorporate risk into the evaluation of a project, but they differ in the manner and the location where the adjustment for risk is made.

Under the **Risk-Adjusted Discount Rate approach**, risk is incorporated by adding a suitable risk premium to the risk-free rate; the resulting higher discount rate is applied to the expected (unadjusted) cash flows in the denominator of the NPV equation. Under the **Certainty-Equivalent approach**, risk is incorporated by converting the risky expected cash flows into their "certain" equivalents using certainty-equivalent coefficients (which lie between 0 and 1) in the numerator, and these certain cash flows are then discounted at the risk-free rate.

| Basis | Risk-Adjusted Discount Rate | Certainty-Equivalent Approach |
|---|---|---|
| Where risk is adjusted | In the denominator (discount rate) | In the numerator (cash flows) |
| Method of adjustment | Adds risk premium to discount rate | Applies CE coefficient (0–1) to cash flows |
| Discount rate used | Risk-adjusted rate (risk-free + premium) | Risk-free rate |
| Treatment of risk over time | Assumes risk increases with time (compounding effect) | Risk can be tailored year-by-year through CE coefficients |
| Theoretical soundness | Less sound — risk premium is often arbitrary and assumes uniform increase in risk | More sound — allows differential risk assessment each year |
| Ease of use | Simpler and more widely used in practice | Requires subjective estimation of CE coefficients |

**Conclusion:** While the RADR approach is simpler and more popular in practice, the Certainty-Equivalent approach is theoretically superior because it separates the risk adjustment (numerator) from the time-value adjustment (denominator) and permits year-specific risk treatment.

*(Full-marks tip: State clearly WHERE each method adjusts for risk (numerator vs denominator) and WHICH discount rate each uses — that is the crux the examiner looks for. Adding the point that RADR implicitly assumes risk rises uniformly over time earns the top band.)*

### Q42. Ch: Risk Analysis in Capital Budgeting — Sensitivity Analysis: Meaning, Merits & Limitations (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the concept of **Sensitivity Analysis** in capital budgeting. State its advantages and limitations.

**Answer:**
**Meaning:** Sensitivity analysis is a technique of risk analysis that measures how responsive or "sensitive" a project's outcome — usually its Net Present Value (NPV) or Internal Rate of Return (IRR) — is to a change in one of the underlying input variables (such as sales volume, selling price, variable cost, initial investment, or project life), while holding all other variables constant. It answers the "what-if" question and identifies the variable to which the project's viability is most vulnerable — i.e. the critical variable that management must monitor most closely. The variable requiring the **smallest percentage adverse change** to reduce the NPV to zero is the most sensitive.

**Advantages:**
It compels management to identify the underlying variables and the critical ones among them, thereby focusing attention where it matters most. It is simple to understand and apply, and it indicates how robust or fragile a project is by showing the margin of safety in each variable before the project becomes unviable.

**Limitations:**
It changes only **one variable at a time**, ignoring the fact that variables are often interrelated and may move together (e.g. price and volume). It does **not** incorporate the **probability** of the change occurring — it tells us how much a variable can change but not how likely such a change is. It also does not, by itself, provide a clear accept/reject decision rule.

**Conclusion:** Sensitivity analysis is a useful first-level risk tool for pinpointing the critical variable, but because it ignores probabilities and interdependence among variables, it should be supplemented by scenario analysis or simulation for a complete risk assessment.

*(Full-marks tip: The definition MUST include that the most sensitive variable is the one needing the smallest % change to make NPV zero. Listing "ignores probability" and "one variable at a time" as limitations is essential — these are the two the examiner specifically looks for.)*

$$P = \frac{D + \frac{r}{K_e}(E - D)}{K_e}$$
where P = market price, D = dividend per share, E = EPS, r = return on investment, Ke = cost of capital.

**WN-2: Price at 40% payout**
Dividend D = 40% of ₹12 = ₹4.80; Retained (E − D) = ₹7.20.
$$P = \frac{4.80 + \frac{0.18}{0.12}(12 - 4.80)}{0.12} = \frac{4.80 + 1.5 \times 7.20}{0.12} = \frac{4.80 + 10.80}{0.12} = \frac{15.60}{0.12} = ₹130$$

**WN-3: Optimum Payout**
Since r (18%) > Ke (12%), the firm is a **growth firm**. Under Walter's model the value is maximised when the entire earnings are retained, i.e. the **optimum payout ratio is 0% (nil dividend)**.

**Price at 0% payout:** D = 0, (E − D) = ₹12.
$$P = \frac{0 + 1.5 \times 12}{0.12} = \frac{18}{0.12} = ₹150$$

**Statement Showing Price under Walter's Model**

| Payout Ratio | Dividend (₹) | Market Price (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| 40% | 4.80 | 130 |
| 0% (optimum) | 0 | 150 |

**Walter's Decision Rule:**

| Situation | Type of Firm | Optimum Payout |
|---|---|---|
| r > Ke | Growth firm | 0% (retain all) |
| r = Ke | Normal firm | Payout irrelevant |
| r < Ke | Declining firm | 100% (distribute all) |

**Answer:** Price at 40% payout = **₹130**; optimum payout = **0%** giving the maximum price of **₹150** (since r > Ke, it is a growth firm).

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards linking the r vs Ke relationship to the firm type and the optimum payout. State the decision rule table explicitly; merely computing ₹130 without identifying the optimum policy caps the marks.)*

### Q46. Ch: Dividend Decisions — Stability of Dividends (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** What is meant by **stability of dividends**? Explain its various forms and state why a stable dividend policy is considered desirable by shareholders.

**Answer:**
**Meaning:** Stability of dividends refers to the consistency or regularity with which a company pays dividends over successive years. It does not necessarily mean paying the same rupee amount every year; rather, it means maintaining a steady, predictable pattern of dividend payments that shareholders can rely upon, avoiding wide fluctuations that mirror the volatility of earnings.

**Forms of Dividend Stability:**

| Form | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Constant Dividend per Share (or fixed rate)** | The firm pays a fixed rupee amount (or a fixed % on par) per share year after year, irrespective of earnings fluctuations. A reserve (dividend equalisation reserve) is built up in good years to sustain the rate in lean years. |
| **Constant Payout Ratio** | The firm pays a fixed percentage of its earnings as dividend each year. The rupee dividend fluctuates with earnings, so it is not truly "stable" in amount, only in proportion. |
| **Stable Rupee Dividend plus Extra Dividend** | The firm pays a low, stable regular dividend every year and adds an "extra" or "special" dividend in years of unusually high profits, signalling that the extra is non-recurring. |

**Why a stable dividend policy is desirable:**
It has an **informational/signalling value** — a stable or gradually rising dividend conveys management's confidence in sustained future earnings, which supports the share price. It meets the needs of investors who **rely on dividends for regular income** (e.g. retired persons, income funds), who prefer and pay a premium for predictable payments. It helps the company gain access to institutional investors and improves its standing in the capital market for raising further funds. It also avoids the adverse market reaction that a dividend cut typically provokes.

**Conclusion:** A stable dividend policy — most commonly the "stable rupee dividend plus extra" form — enhances shareholder confidence and the market value of shares, even though it does not, by itself, alter the total earnings of the firm.

*(Full-marks tip: The three forms (constant DPS, constant payout, stable-plus-extra) must be distinguished — noting that only "constant payout" leaves rupee dividends fluctuating earns a mark. The signalling/informational content of stability is the point the examiner most wants to see.)*

### Q48. Ch: Dividend Decisions — Stock Split vs Bonus Issue (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between a **Stock Split** and a **Bonus Issue** (bonus shares). Also state the effect of each on (a) the face value per share, (b) reserves, and (c) the shareholder's proportionate ownership.

**Answer:**
Both a stock split and a bonus issue increase the number of shares outstanding without any cash inflow to the company, and neither changes the total wealth or the proportionate ownership of an existing shareholder. However, they differ in mechanism and accounting treatment.

A **bonus issue** is the issue of additional (free) shares to existing shareholders in proportion to their holdings, by **capitalising the company's accumulated reserves and surplus**. The face value per share remains unchanged, and reserves are reduced by the amount capitalised (transferred to paid-up share capital).

A **stock split**, by contrast, is merely the sub-division of each existing share of a larger face value into a larger number of shares of a **smaller face value** (e.g. one ₹10 share into two ₹5 shares). There is **no capitalisation of reserves**; only the face value changes, while total paid-up capital and reserves remain the same.

| Basis of Distinction | Bonus Issue | Stock Split |
|---|---|---|
| Face value per share | Unchanged | Reduced (sub-divided) |
| Reserves / surplus | Reduced (capitalised into share capital) | Unchanged |
| Paid-up share capital | Increased | Unchanged |
| Number of shares | Increases | Increases |
| Nature | Capitalisation of profits | Re-denomination of shares |
| Proportionate ownership of shareholder | Unchanged | Unchanged |

**Effects summarised:**
(a) **Face value per share** — Bonus: unchanged; Stock split: reduced.
(b) **Reserves** — Bonus: reduced (capitalised); Stock split: unchanged.
(c) **Proportionate ownership** — Unchanged under both.

**Conclusion:** Both actions increase the number of shares and typically reduce the market price per share to improve marketability/liquidity, but only a bonus issue involves capitalisation of reserves; a stock split is purely a re-denomination of existing capital.

*(Full-marks tip: The decisive distinction is that a bonus issue capitalises reserves and leaves face value intact, whereas a stock split reduces face value and touches no reserves. Stating that proportionate ownership and total wealth are unchanged under BOTH prevents the common confusion and earns full marks.)*

### Q49. Ch: Dividend Decisions — Factors Determining Dividend Policy (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** State and briefly explain any **five factors** that a company should consider while formulating its **dividend policy**.

**Answer:**
The dividend policy of a company is the trade-off between distributing profits to shareholders and retaining them for reinvestment. In framing this policy, the following factors must be considered:

**1. Availability of Profitable Investment Opportunities:** If the firm has projects yielding a return (r) higher than the cost of capital (Ke), it is better to retain earnings and reinvest, paying a lower dividend; a firm with few such opportunities should distribute more.

**2. Liquidity / Cash Position:** Dividends are paid in cash. Even a highly profitable company may pay a low dividend if its cash is locked up in working capital or expansion; the actual liquidity, not merely the accounting profit, governs the payout.

**3. Legal Constraints and Contractual Restrictions:** Dividends must comply with the provisions of the **Companies Act, 2013** (e.g. Section 123 — dividend only out of profits after providing for depreciation, and rules on transfer to reserves) and with restrictive covenants in loan/debenture agreements that may cap dividend payments.

**4. Stability of Earnings and Desire for Stable Dividends:** A firm with stable, predictable earnings can afford a higher and steadier payout, whereas a firm with volatile earnings retains more as a cushion to sustain dividends in lean years.

**5. Shareholders' Expectations and Tax Position (Clientele Effect):** The preferences of the firm's typical shareholders matter — investors seeking regular income favour high payouts, while those in high tax brackets or seeking growth prefer retention and capital gains.

**Other relevant factors** (any of these may be substituted): access to the capital market, cost and ease of raising external finance, control considerations (fresh equity dilutes control, so retention is preferred), inflation, and past dividend history/signalling.

**Conclusion:** A sound dividend policy balances these factors so as to maximise shareholder wealth while retaining sufficient funds for the firm's growth and financial stability.

*(Full-marks tip: Cite Section 123 of the Companies Act, 2013 under the legal factor — a statutory reference lifts the answer into the top band. Explain each factor in one full sentence linking it to the payout decision, rather than listing bare headings.)*

### Q50. Ch: Dividend Decisions — Modigliani-Miller (MM) Dividend Irrelevance (Marks: 8) [Problem]

$$P_0 = \frac{P_1 + D_1}{1 + K_e} \Rightarrow P_1 = P_0(1 + K_e) - D_1$$
where P0 = current price, P1 = year-end price, D1 = dividend per share, Ke = cost of equity.

**Case A — Dividend of ₹8 per share is paid**

P1 = ₹150 (1.10) − ₹8 = ₹165 − ₹8 = **₹157**

**WN-3: Number of new shares to be issued (Case A)**
Funds required for investment not met by retained earnings must be raised by fresh equity.
Retained earnings = Net income − Dividends = ₹18,00,000 − (1,00,000 × ₹8) = ₹18,00,000 − ₹8,00,000 = ₹10,00,000.
External funds needed = Investment − Retained earnings = ₹30,00,000 − ₹10,00,000 = ₹20,00,000.
New shares (n) = ₹20,00,000 ÷ ₹157 = 12,738.85 shares.

**WN-4: Value of the firm (Case A)**
$$\text{Value} = \frac{(N + n)P_1 - I + E}{1 + K_e}$$
= [(1,00,000 + 12,738.85) × ₹157 − ₹30,00,000 + ₹18,00,000] ÷ 1.10
= [1,12,738.85 × 157 − 12,00,000] ÷ 1.10
= [1,77,00,000 − 12,00,000] ÷ 1.10
= ₹1,65,00,000 ÷ 1.10 = **₹1,50,00,000**

**Case B — No dividend is paid (D1 = 0)**

**WN-5: Year-end price (Case B)**
P1 = ₹150 (1.10) − 0 = **₹165**

**WN-6: New shares (Case B)**
Retained earnings = ₹18,00,000; External funds needed = ₹30,00,000 − ₹18,00,000 = ₹12,00,000.
New shares = ₹12,00,000 ÷ ₹165 = 7,272.73 shares.

**WN-7: Value of the firm (Case B)**
= [(1,00,000 + 7,272.73) × ₹165 − ₹30,00,000 + ₹18,00,000] ÷ 1.10
= [1,07,272.73 × 165 − 12,00,000] ÷ 1.10
= [1,77,00,000 − 12,00,000] ÷ 1.10
= ₹1,65,00,000 ÷ 1.10 = **₹1,50,00,000**

**Conclusion:** The market value of the firm is **₹1,50,00,000 in both cases**. This proves the MM hypothesis that under perfect capital markets and no taxes, **dividend policy is irrelevant** to the value of the firm — a higher dividend is exactly offset by the value of new shares that must be issued to fund the investment.

**Answer:** Value of the firm = **₹1,50,00,000 whether or not the dividend is paid** — confirming dividend irrelevance under MM.

### Q51. Ch: Dividend Decisions — Traditional (Graham & Dodd) Approach / Linter's Model (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Write a short note on the **Traditional Approach (Graham and Dodd) to dividend policy** and briefly state the essence of **Linter's model** of corporate dividend behaviour.

**Answer:**
**Traditional Approach (Graham and Dodd):** According to the traditional position articulated by Graham and Dodd, the stock market places **considerably more weight on dividends than on retained earnings**. In their view the market value of a share is materially influenced by the dividend paid; a rupee of dividend is valued more highly by investors than a rupee of retained earnings because dividends are certain and immediate whereas retained earnings yield uncertain future capital gains. The relationship is expressed as:
$$P = m\left(D + \frac{E}{3}\right)$$
where P = market price, D = dividend per share, E = earnings per share, and m = a multiplier. The weighting (dividends counted at full value, retained earnings — the E − D component — effectively at a lower weight) reflects the belief that a liberal dividend policy generally raises the share price. This is thus a "dividends are relevant" view, contrasting with the MM irrelevance hypothesis.

**Linter's Model:** John Linter's model describes how firms actually SET dividends in practice. It holds that:
(i) firms have a **long-run target payout ratio**; and
(ii) managers are more concerned with the **change** in the dividend than with its absolute level, and they **smooth** dividends towards the target only **partially** each year (adjustment factor), so that dividends are raised gradually and are cut only reluctantly.
This behaviour is captured as: change in dividend = adjustment factor × (target dividend − previous dividend). It explains the empirically observed **stickiness/stability** of dividends.

**Conclusion:** The traditional approach asserts that dividends do affect share value (and are valued more than retained earnings), while Linter's model explains the sticky, gradually-adjusting pattern of dividends that managers follow in practice.

*(Full-marks tip: Reproduce the Graham-Dodd formula P = m(D + E/3) — it is the specific point the examiner looks for and shows dividends are weighted more heavily than retained earnings. For Linter, the two pillars — target payout ratio and partial/gradual adjustment — are the marks-carrying content.)*

### Q56. Ch: Types of Financing — Seed, Angel & Private Equity Funding (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between **Seed capital, Angel investment and Private Equity/Venture Capital** as sources of financing for a start-up, explaining the stage at which each is typically raised and the investor's expectations.

**Answer:**
Start-up financing follows a life-cycle in which the source of funds changes as the venture matures and its risk profile falls.

**Seed capital** is the earliest external money a venture raises, used to convert an idea into a proof-of-concept or a minimum viable product. It funds market research, prototype development and initial team building. The amounts are small, the business usually has no revenue, and the funds typically come from the founders themselves, friends and family, or seed funds and incubators. The risk is the highest of all stages and much of the "capital" is the promoter's own contribution.

**Angel investment** is provided by high-net-worth individuals (angel investors), often successful entrepreneurs, who invest their **personal funds** in an early-stage venture in exchange for an equity stake or convertible debt. Angels invest after the seed stage, once there is an early product or early traction, but before the business is large enough to attract institutional money. Besides money, angels bring **mentoring, industry contacts and managerial guidance**. They accept high risk in return for the possibility of very high returns and usually exit when a larger investor comes in.

**Private Equity / Venture Capital (VC)** is institutional, professionally-managed money pooled from investors and deployed by a fund into companies that have crossed the proof-of-concept stage and are ready to **scale**. Venture capital targets early-to-growth-stage companies with high growth potential; private equity more broadly also invests in established, later-stage or even mature businesses (including buy-outs). These investors take a significant equity stake, often a board seat and active governance rights, expect a defined **exit** (IPO or strategic sale) within a horizon of typically 3–7 years, and demand a high internal rate of return commensurate with the risk.

| Basis | Seed capital | Angel investment | PE / Venture Capital |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stage | Idea / prototype | Early product, early traction | Proven model, scaling |
| Source | Founders, friends, family, incubators | HNI individuals (own funds) | Institutional pooled funds |
| Ticket size | Smallest | Small–medium | Large |
| Risk to investor | Highest | High | Moderate–high |
| Non-financial value | Little | Mentoring, contacts | Governance, strategy, professionalisation |
| Investor's expectation | Belief in idea | High return + involvement | High IRR + defined exit (IPO/sale) |

**Conclusion:** Seed, angel and PE/VC funding form a graduated ladder of start-up finance — as the venture proves itself and its risk declines, it moves from the founder's own seed money, to angel backing, to institutional private-equity/venture capital that funds scale-up and eventual exit.

### Q57. Ch: Financial Markets — Primary vs Secondary Market (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the distinction between the **Primary market** and the **Secondary market**, and state the functions each performs in the financial system.

**Answer:**
A capital market has two interdependent segments — the primary market and the secondary market — which together enable the flow of funds from savers to users of capital.

The **Primary market** (also called the "new issue market") is the market in which securities are **created and issued for the first time**. Here a company raises **fresh capital** directly from investors through methods such as a public issue (IPO/FPO), a rights issue, a private placement or a preferential allotment. The distinguishing feature is that the money paid by investors flows **directly to the issuing company** (or government), which uses it to fund projects, expansion or working capital. The primary market therefore performs the function of **capital formation** — channelling savings into productive investment.

The **Secondary market** (the "stock market" / stock exchange) is the market in which securities that have **already been issued** are subsequently **bought and sold among investors**. No new capital is raised by the company here; the transaction is between one investor and another, and the sale proceeds go to the selling investor, not to the issuer. Its essential function is to provide **liquidity and marketability** — allowing an investor to convert securities into cash — and **continuous price discovery**, which in turn signals the value of the company and makes future primary issues possible.

| Basis | Primary market | Secondary market |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | New issue market | Market for existing securities |
| Capital raised | Fresh capital to the issuer | No fresh capital; investor-to-investor |
| Beneficiary of proceeds | Issuing company/government | Selling investor |
| Main function | Capital formation | Liquidity & price discovery |
| Frequency for a security | Once (at issue) | Continuous trading |
| Intermediaries | Merchant bankers, underwriters | Brokers, stock exchange |

**Conclusion:** The primary market creates securities and mobilises fresh capital for issuers, while the secondary market gives those securities liquidity and continuous pricing; the two are complementary — an active secondary market is essential for a successful primary market.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the key point that proceeds go to the ISSUER in the primary market but to the selling INVESTOR in the secondary market, plus the liquidity/price-discovery function. Common deduction — describing the secondary market as a place where companies raise money.)*

### Q58. Ch: Financial Markets — Book-Building Process (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** What is meant by **Book-Building**? Explain the process and how the issue price is discovered, and distinguish it from a fixed-price public issue.

**Answer:**
**Book-building** is a price-discovery mechanism used in a public issue of securities under which the issue price is **not fixed in advance** but is determined by the demand generated from investors within a stated **price band**. It is conducted in accordance with the SEBI (Issue of Capital and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations.

Under book-building, the issuer, with the help of a **lead merchant banker (Book Running Lead Manager)**, announces a **price band** with a floor price and a cap (the cap not exceeding 120% of the floor). During the bidding period, investors submit **bids** specifying the number of shares and the price (within the band) they are willing to pay. These bids are entered into an electronic **"book"** maintained by the BRLM. On the basis of the demand received at various price levels, the issuer and BRLM determine the **cut-off price** — the price at which the issue gets fully subscribed. All successful applicants are allotted shares at this single cut-off price. Retail investors may bid "at cut-off", agreeing to accept whatever cut-off price emerges.

The distinguishing contrast is with a **fixed-price issue**, where the company decides and prints the price in the prospectus **before** the issue opens, and investors either apply at that price or not; there is no price band and demand at different prices is not visible in advance.

| Basis | Book-building issue | Fixed-price issue |
|---|---|---|
| Price | Discovered via band + bids | Fixed in prospectus in advance |
| Demand visibility | Known during bidding (the "book") | Known only after issue closes |
| Investor action | Bids a price within the band | Applies at the stated price |
| Price band | Floor to cap (cap ≤ 1.2 × floor) | Single price |
| Payment | At cut-off price | At the fixed price |

**Conclusion:** Book-building lets the market set the issue price through competitive bidding within a band, producing efficient price discovery, whereas a fixed-price issue commits to a single price up-front with no demand feedback.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the price-band + cut-off-price mechanics and the SEBI/BRLM reference. Common deduction — confusing "cut-off price" with the floor price, or saying each bidder pays their own bid price rather than the common cut-off.)*

### Q60. Ch: Types of Financing — Debt vs Equity Financing (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Compare **debt financing and equity financing** as sources of long-term capital, bringing out the factors a finance manager weighs when choosing between them.

**Answer:**
A firm's long-term capital is raised from two broad sources — **equity** (owners' funds) and **debt** (borrowed funds) — and the choice between them shapes the firm's risk, control and cost of capital.

**Equity financing** represents ownership funds contributed by shareholders (equity shares, retained earnings). It carries **no obligation to pay a fixed return** — dividends are payable only if profits exist and are declared — and there is **no maturity**, so the capital is permanent. However, equity is the **most expensive** source: equity holders bear the highest risk and demand the highest return, dividends are **not tax-deductible**, and issuing new shares **dilutes control** and earnings per share.

**Debt financing** represents borrowed funds (debentures, term loans, bonds) carrying a **contractual obligation** to pay fixed interest and to repay principal on maturity, regardless of profits. Debt is **cheaper** because lenders bear lower risk and, crucially, **interest is tax-deductible**, giving a tax shield that lowers the effective cost. It does **not dilute ownership or control**. Its drawback is that fixed interest and repayment obligations increase **financial risk** — in a downturn they must still be met — and excessive debt can push the firm toward insolvency.

| Basis | Equity financing | Debt financing |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Ownership funds | Borrowed funds |
| Return | Dividend (discretionary) | Interest (obligatory) |
| Tax treatment | Dividend not deductible | Interest is deductible (tax shield) |
| Maturity | Permanent, no repayment | Fixed maturity, must be repaid |
| Risk to firm | Low financial risk | High financial risk |
| Effect on control | Dilutes control | No dilution |
| Cost | Higher | Lower |

**Conclusion:** Debt is cheaper and preserves control but raises financial risk, whereas equity is safer for the firm but costlier and dilutive; the finance manager blends the two to reach a capital structure that minimises the overall cost of capital while keeping financial risk within prudent limits.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the tax-deductibility-of-interest point and the risk-vs-control trade-off. Common deduction — omitting that dividends are not tax-deductible while interest is, which is the core reason debt is cheaper.)*

### Q63. Ch: Types of Financing — Venture Capital Financing Methods (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain any **four methods** of venture capital financing used by a venture capitalist to fund a portfolio company.

**Answer:**
Venture capital financing is the funding of high-risk, high-growth ventures by professionally-managed funds in exchange for an equity or quasi-equity stake. A venture capitalist structures the investment through several instruments, choosing the method that balances risk, return and control:

1. **Equity financing:** The venture capitalist subscribes directly to the **equity shares** of the venture. Since a young company cannot service fixed returns, equity lets the VC share in the upside; the VC's stake is usually limited (often not exceeding 49%) so that the promoters retain a majority interest and continued incentive to grow the business.

2. **Conditional loan:** A loan on which **no fixed interest** is charged; instead the VC receives a **royalty** linked to sales/turnover. Once the venture reaches a threshold, the loan may be converted or the royalty begins. It shifts the burden of a fixed charge away from the early, cash-poor venture and ties the VC's return to the company's actual performance.

3. **Income note:** A **hybrid** instrument combining features of a conventional loan and a conditional loan — the entrepreneur pays **both a (low) interest and a royalty** on sales, but at modest rates. It gives the VC a base return plus performance-linked upside.

4. **Participating debenture / Convertible instruments:** Debentures whose return varies with the venture's performance in phases — for example, **no interest** up to a start-up level, a **low rate** during the initial operating phase, and a **higher rate** once the venture is established; convertible debentures/preference shares later convert into equity, letting the VC move from a debt-holder to an equity-owner as the venture succeeds.

**Conclusion:** Through equity, conditional loans, income notes and participating/convertible instruments, the venture capitalist tailors the financing to the venture's cash-flow profile — minimising fixed obligations while the venture is fragile and capturing equity-like upside as it matures.

1. **Treasury Bills (T-Bills):** Short-term instruments issued by the **RBI on behalf of the Government of India** to meet short-term funding needs, with maturities of 91, 182 and 364 days. They are issued at a **discount** to face value and redeemed at par — the difference being the investor's return; they carry **zero default risk** and are highly liquid.

2. **Commercial Paper (CP):** An **unsecured promissory note** issued by highly-rated, creditworthy corporates to raise short-term funds, typically for maturities of 7 days up to one year. It is issued at a discount to face value, and enables a company to raise money at a rate often below the bank lending rate, diversifying its short-term funding.

3. **Certificate of Deposit (CD):** A **negotiable, unsecured** instrument issued by **banks and financial institutions** against deposits kept with them, for maturities generally from 7 days to one year. It is issued at a discount and gives the depositor a marketable instrument that can be traded before maturity.

4. **Commercial Bill / Trade Bill:** A **bill of exchange** drawn by a seller on a buyer for goods sold on credit; once accepted it becomes a marketable instrument that the seller can **discount** with a bank to obtain immediate funds, providing self-liquidating short-term finance linked to a genuine trade transaction.

### Q67. Ch: Types of Financing — Sale-and-Leaseback vs Direct Lease / Types of Lease (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between a **Finance (Capital) lease and an Operating lease**, and explain what is meant by a **Sale-and-Leaseback** arrangement.

**Answer:**
A **lease** is a contractual arrangement whereby the owner of an asset (the **lessor**) allows another party (the **lessee**) to use the asset for an agreed period in return for periodic **lease rentals**. Leases are broadly of two types:

A **Finance lease (capital lease)** is a long-term, **non-cancellable** lease that transfers substantially all the **risks and rewards** of ownership to the lessee, even though legal title may remain with the lessor. The lease period generally covers the major part of the asset's economic life, the lessee bears **maintenance, insurance and obsolescence risk**, and the total rentals over the term effectively **amortise the lessor's investment** plus a return. It is essentially a financing arrangement — an alternative to buying the asset with borrowed funds.

An **Operating lease** is a **short-term, cancellable** lease under which the risks and rewards of ownership **remain with the lessor**. The lease term is much shorter than the asset's useful life, the lessor usually bears **maintenance and obsolescence risk**, and the asset can be leased out again to other users. It suits assets prone to rapid obsolescence (e.g. computers, vehicles) where the lessee wants use without long-term commitment.

| Basis | Finance lease | Operating lease |
|---|---|---|
| Term vs asset life | Covers major part of life | Much shorter than life |
| Cancellable | Non-cancellable | Cancellable |
| Risks & rewards | Pass to lessee | Stay with lessor |
| Maintenance | Lessee's responsibility | Lessor's responsibility |
| Nature | Financing arrangement | Service/use arrangement |

A **Sale-and-Leaseback** is an arrangement in which a firm that **owns an asset sells it** to a leasing company (or financial institution) and **simultaneously leases it back** for continued use. The firm thereby **releases the capital locked up** in the asset (improving liquidity/working capital) while retaining uninterrupted use of it; the buyer becomes the lessor and earns lease rentals plus, usually, tax benefits of ownership. It is a means of raising finance against an owned asset without losing its use.

**Conclusion:** A finance lease transfers ownership risks and rewards to the lessee and is a substitute for buying, an operating lease is a cancellable use arrangement with the lessor retaining ownership risks, and a sale-and-leaseback converts an owned asset into cash while preserving its use.

Common deduction — describing an operating lease as non-cancellable, or missing that in a sale-and-leaseback the seller continues to use the asset.)*

### Q68. Ch: Working Capital Management — Working Capital Financing Policies (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the three approaches to financing the working-capital needs of a firm — the **Aggressive, Conservative and Matching (Hedging) approaches** — and bring out the risk-return trade-off of each.

**Answer:**
A firm's total assets can be split into **fixed assets, permanent current assets** (the minimum level of current assets always carried) and **temporary/fluctuating current assets** (which vary with seasonal or cyclical demand). How these are financed by long-term versus short-term sources defines the firm's working-capital financing policy.

**1. Matching (Hedging) approach:** Under this approach the **maturity of the source is matched with the life of the asset** it finances. Permanent assets (fixed assets and permanent current assets) are financed by **long-term** sources, and temporary current assets are financed by **short-term** sources. It is a **moderate, balanced** policy — neither excess cost nor excess risk — because each asset is funded by a source of matching duration.

**2. Conservative approach:** Here the firm relies **more heavily on long-term funds**, financing not only its permanent assets but also **a part (or all) of its temporary current assets** with long-term sources; short-term financing is used sparingly. This gives **low liquidity risk** (little dependence on short-term funds that need frequent renewal) but a **lower return**, because long-term funds are costlier and idle long-term funds may be held during slack periods. It is a **low-risk, low-return** policy.

**3. Aggressive approach:** Here the firm finances **not only its temporary current assets but also a part of its permanent current assets** with **short-term** sources, keeping long-term financing to a minimum. Short-term funds are cheaper, so this **raises profitability**, but reliance on frequently-maturing short-term funds **increases liquidity/refinancing risk**. It is a **high-risk, high-return** policy.

| Approach | Financing of temporary CA | Financing of permanent CA | Risk | Return / Cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Conservative | Partly long-term | Long-term | Low | Low return, high cost |
| Matching | Short-term | Long-term | Moderate | Moderate |
| Aggressive | Short-term | Partly short-term | High | High return, low cost |

**Conclusion:** The three policies embody a **risk–return trade-off** — the conservative approach minimises liquidity risk at the cost of lower returns, the aggressive approach maximises returns by using cheaper short-term funds at higher risk, and the matching approach strikes a balance by aligning the maturity of sources with the life of assets.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the permanent vs temporary current-asset split and stating the risk-return trade-off explicitly for each policy. Common deduction — reversing aggressive and conservative, or omitting that short-term finance is cheaper but riskier.)*

### Q69. Ch: Introduction to Strategic Management — Strategic Vision vs Mission vs Goals (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** "A strategic vision, a mission statement and a set of goals are three distinct but connected expressions of an organisation's strategic intent." Distinguish between a strategic vision, a mission and goals, and explain how the three link together to give an organisation its direction.

**Answer:**
A strategic vision, a mission and goals are the three sequential expressions through which an organisation converts abstract strategic intent into concrete, measurable direction. Though they are often used interchangeably in loose conversation, in strategic management each occupies a distinct place in the hierarchy of intent.

A **strategic vision** describes the "route" an organisation intends to take to develop and strengthen its business; it is aspirational and forward-looking, painting a picture of "where we are going" and what the enterprise seeks to become over the long term. A vision statement answers the question *"What do we want to become?"* and is intended to inspire and stretch the organisation.

A **mission** describes the organisation's present business and purpose — "who we are, what we do, and why we are here". It answers *"What is our business?"* by defining the customer needs served, the customer groups targeted and the technologies/activities used. Whereas vision is future-oriented, mission is rooted in the present reason for existence.

**Goals** (and the more specific **objectives**) are the ends that an organisation seeks to achieve; they translate the mission and vision into defined, time-bound and measurable targets that guide day-to-day and periodic performance. Goals answer *"What exactly must we accomplish, and by when?"*

| Basis | Strategic Vision | Mission | Goals / Objectives |
|---|---|---|---|
| Question answered | Where do we want to go? | What is our business now? | What must we achieve? |
| Time orientation | Long-term, future | Present | Short-to-medium term, time-bound |
| Nature | Aspirational, directional | Purpose-defining | Specific, measurable |
| Level of abstraction | Highest | Middle | Lowest (concrete) |

**How they link:** The vision sets the long-range direction; the mission grounds that direction in the organisation's present purpose and business definition; the goals then break the mission down into measurable milestones. Strategy is subsequently formulated to achieve the goals, thereby moving the organisation, step by step, toward the vision.

**Conclusion:** Vision inspires, mission defines, and goals operationalise — together they convert strategic intent into a coherent sense of direction that guides the whole organisation.

### Q70. Ch: Introduction to Strategic Management — Characteristics of a Good Mission Statement (Marks: 4) [Theory]

6. **It should indicate how objectives are to be accomplished** by pointing to the main means and the operative philosophy through which the organisation intends to reach its goals.

### Q71. Ch: Introduction to Strategic Management — Levels of Strategy (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** A large diversified company is reviewing the way strategy is made at different points in its structure. Explain the three levels at which strategy is formulated in such an organisation and the focus of each level.

**Answer:**
In a large, diversified enterprise, strategy is not made at a single point; it is formulated at three distinct levels, each with its own scope and decision-makers. Together they form a hierarchy in which higher-level choices set the boundaries for lower-level ones.

1. **Corporate-level strategy** is formulated by the top management (the board and the chief executive) and is concerned with the enterprise as a whole. It answers the fundamental question *"What businesses should we be in?"* — decisions on diversification, entering or exiting industries, allocation of resources across businesses, mergers and acquisitions, and the overall growth or stability direction of the total enterprise. It occupies the highest level and has the longest time horizon.

2. **Business-level strategy** (or Strategic Business Unit strategy) is formulated by the general managers of individual business units. It is concerned with *"How should we compete in this particular business/industry?"* — building and sustaining competitive advantage in a specific product-market, for example through cost leadership or differentiation. Each SBU serves a distinct market and competes with its own set of rivals.

3. **Functional-level strategy** is formulated by the heads of functional areas such as marketing, finance, production, R&D and human resources. It is concerned with *"How do we support the business strategy within each function?"* — it translates business strategy into day-to-day functional plans and has the shortest time horizon.

| Level | Made by | Central question | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| Corporate | Top management/Board | What businesses to be in? | Whole enterprise |
| Business (SBU) | SBU general managers | How to compete here? | One business/industry |
| Functional | Functional heads | How to support the SBU? | One function |

**Conclusion:** Corporate strategy defines the portfolio, business strategy wins in each chosen market, and functional strategy delivers the operational support — the three cascade downward to keep the enterprise integrated.

### Q74. Ch: External Environment Analysis — Dynamics of Competition and Porter's Five Forces (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Michael Porter identified five forces that jointly determine the intensity of competition and the profit potential of an industry. Explain the five competitive forces and how they shape the attractiveness of an industry.

**Answer:**
Michael Porter's **Five Forces Model** holds that the state of competition in an industry — and hence its long-run profit potential — is not confined to rivalry among existing players but is governed by five collective forces. The stronger these forces, the lower the industry's average profitability and attractiveness.

1. **Threat of new entrants.** New entrants bring additional capacity and a desire to gain market share, which can drive down prices and raise costs. The threat depends on barriers to entry — economies of scale, capital requirements, product differentiation, access to distribution and expected retaliation. High barriers protect incumbents' profits; low barriers make the industry less attractive.

2. **Bargaining power of buyers.** Powerful buyers can force prices down, demand higher quality or more service, and play competitors against one another. Buyers are strong when they are few or purchase in large volumes, when products are standardised, or when switching costs are low.

3. **Bargaining power of suppliers.** Powerful suppliers can raise prices or reduce the quality of inputs, squeezing industry profits. Suppliers are strong when they are concentrated, when their input is critical or differentiated, or when substitutes for their input are scarce.

4. **Threat of substitute products or services.** Substitutes from other industries that perform the same function place a ceiling on the prices firms can charge. The greater the availability and the better the price-performance of substitutes, the more attractive the option of switching and the lower the industry's profit potential.

5. **Rivalry among existing competitors.** This is the intensity of jockeying for position through price competition, advertising, new products and service. Rivalry is intense when competitors are numerous or roughly equal, when industry growth is slow, when fixed costs are high, or when exit barriers are high.

| Force | Firm profits fall when the force is… | Key driver |
|---|---|---|
| New entrants | Strong (low barriers) | Entry barriers |
| Buyers | Strong | Buyer concentration/switching cost |
| Suppliers | Strong | Supplier concentration/input criticality |
| Substitutes | Strong | Price-performance of substitutes |
| Rivalry | Strong | Number of rivals/industry growth |

**Conclusion:** An industry is attractive (offers high profit potential) when all five forces are weak, and unattractive when they are strong; strategists analyse the five forces to choose a position where the firm can best defend itself against these forces or influence them in its favour.

*(Full-marks tip: name a driver/determinant for each force, not just the force; the common deduction is treating "rivalry" as the only force and omitting the profit-potential conclusion.)*

### Q75. Ch: External Environment Analysis — Entry and Exit Barriers (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the concept of "barriers to entry" in an industry and describe the major sources of entry barriers. How do exit barriers differ from entry barriers in their effect on industry competition?

**Answer:**
**Meaning:** Barriers to entry are the obstacles that make it difficult and costly for new firms to enter an industry. High entry barriers protect existing firms from new competition and help preserve their profits; low barriers invite new entrants and intensify competition. They are a key determinant of the "threat of new entrants" in Porter's model.

**Major sources of entry barriers:**
1. **Economies of scale.** When existing firms enjoy large-scale cost advantages, a new entrant must either come in at large scale (risking strong retaliation) or accept a cost disadvantage at small scale.
2. **Product differentiation and brand identity.** Established firms enjoy customer loyalty built over time; a new entrant must spend heavily to overcome this attachment.
3. **Capital requirements.** Large investments needed for plant, R&D, advertising or working capital deter entrants who cannot muster the funds.
4. **Switching costs.** If buyers face one-time costs to switch from an existing supplier to a new one, the new entrant must offer a large improvement to induce switching.
5. **Access to distribution channels.** Existing firms may have tied up the available distribution channels, forcing entrants to create their own at high cost.
6. **Cost advantages independent of scale** — such as proprietary technology, favourable locations, experience-curve effects or government subsidies — that entrants cannot easily match.
7. **Government policy and regulation.** Licensing, patents and regulatory approvals can limit or block entry.

**Exit barriers** are the factors that keep firms competing in an industry even when returns are low — for example, specialised assets with low resale value, high fixed cost of exit (severance, contracts), emotional attachment, and government/social restrictions on closure.

| Basis | Entry barriers | Exit barriers |
|---|---|---|
| Effect on entry | Discourage new firms from entering | — |
| Effect on exit | — | Discourage weak firms from leaving |
| Effect on profitability | High entry barriers raise incumbents' profits | High exit barriers keep excess capacity and depress profits |

**Conclusion:** High entry barriers help incumbents by keeping newcomers out, whereas high exit barriers hurt an industry by trapping firms and their capacity within it — the most difficult industry to operate in profitably is one with low entry barriers and high exit barriers.

### Q76. Ch: External Environment Analysis — Strategic Group Mapping (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** The passenger-car industry contains firms that pursue very different strategies. A consultant advises the client to prepare a "strategic group map". Explain what a strategic group and a strategic group map are, describe how such a map is constructed, and state what strategic insights the client can draw from it in this case.

**Answer:**
**Strategic group:** A strategic group is a set of firms within an industry that follow the same or a similar strategy along the strategic dimensions that matter in that industry — for example, price/quality level, product-line breadth, distribution channels, degree of vertical integration or geographic coverage. Firms in the same strategic group resemble one another and compete most directly with each other, while firms in different groups pursue distinctly different strategies.

**Strategic group mapping** is the technique of displaying the different competitive positions that rival firms occupy in an industry, by plotting them on a two-dimensional map using two strategic variables (drawn from the dimensions above) as the axes.

1. Identify the strategic characteristics that differentiate firms in the industry (e.g., price level, distribution channel, product breadth, quality).
2. Select **two** of these variables (which should not be highly correlated) as the two axes of the map.
3. Plot each firm on the map according to its position on the two variables.
4. Draw a circle around firms that fall into roughly the same strategic space; the size of the circle can be made proportional to the combined market share of the group.

- The firm's **closest competitors are the members of its own strategic group** (Alpha's real rival is Beta, not Gamma), so competitive moves and rivalry are most intense within the group.
- The map shows **which competitive spaces are crowded and which are vacant**, revealing possible gaps in the market that could be occupied.
- It highlights the **mobility barriers** a firm would have to overcome to move from one group to another (e.g., building a mass distribution network to move down-market).
- It clarifies how the five forces and profit potential differ from group to group.

**Conclusion:** By mapping the two strategic groups, the client can see that its direct rivalry is within its own group, identify open positions and mobility barriers, and thereby position itself more effectively.

### Q78. Ch: External Environment Analysis — Micro vs Macro Environment (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between the "micro environment" and the "macro environment" of a business, giving the constituents of each.

**Answer:**
The external environment of a business is commonly divided into the micro (task) environment and the macro (general) environment, according to how closely and directly the forces affect the firm.

The **micro environment** (also called the task or operating environment) consists of the forces in the firm's immediate surroundings that directly affect its ability to serve its customers. These forces are close to the firm, are largely industry-specific, and the firm can often influence them to some degree.

The **macro environment** (the general environment) consists of the broader societal forces that affect all firms in the economy. These forces are more distant, apply generally rather than to one firm, and are almost entirely beyond the firm's control.

| Basis | Micro environment | Macro environment |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Immediate, task-related | General, societal |
| Specificity | Specific to the firm/industry | Common to all firms |
| Control | Firm can influence to a degree | Largely beyond firm's control |
| Constituents | Customers, suppliers, competitors, marketing intermediaries, the general public, and the organisation itself | Political, economic, social, technological, legal and ecological forces (PESTLE) |

**Conclusion:** The micro environment comprises the firm's immediate, partially controllable stakeholders, whereas the macro environment comprises the broad, uncontrollable societal forces; a complete external analysis requires scanning both.

### Q79. Ch: External Environment Analysis — Key Success/Strategic Factors in the Environment (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** "In the organised food-delivery industry, the firms that win are those that can guarantee the shortest delivery time, the widest restaurant network and the lowest delivery charge." A new entrant asks how it should identify the factors that really matter for success in an industry. Explain the concept of Key Success Factors (KSFs) and how a firm identifies them, applying the idea to the food-delivery industry described.

**Answer:**
**Meaning:** Key Success Factors (KSFs), sometimes called critical or strategic success factors, are the few specific factors that most affect the ability of firms in an industry to prosper in the marketplace — the particular strategy elements, product attributes, resources, competencies and competitive capabilities that separate winners from losers in that industry. KSFs are those things that every firm in the industry must do competently, or on which it must excel, in order to be competitively and financially successful.

1. **On what basis do customers choose between competing sellers?** — i.e., what product/service attributes are crucial to buyers.
2. **What resources and competitive capabilities does a seller need to be successful** given the way rivalry works in this industry?
3. **What does it take for sellers to achieve a sustainable competitive advantage** and avoid being competitively disadvantaged?

- an efficient **logistics and routing capability** (to guarantee the shortest delivery time);
- a **large network of partner restaurants** (breadth of choice); and
- a **low-cost delivery operation** (to offer the lowest delivery charge), supported by a reliable technology platform.

The new entrant should build its resources and competencies around exactly these factors, because doing them well is what determines success in this specific industry.

**Conclusion:** KSFs are the handful of factors decisive for success in an industry; by asking what buyers value and what capabilities winning requires, the food-delivery entrant should concentrate its strategy on delivery speed, restaurant-network breadth and low delivery cost.

### Q80. Ch: Internal Environment Analysis — Organisational Capabilities and the Value Chain (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the concept of the "value chain" as a tool for analysing an organisation's internal capabilities, and distinguish between its primary activities and support activities.

**Answer:**
**Meaning:** The value chain, developed by Michael Porter, is a tool for analysing the internal environment by disaggregating a firm into its strategically relevant activities in order to understand the sources of cost and of differentiation. A firm is viewed as a collection of activities that are performed to design, produce, market, deliver and support its product; each activity can add value for the customer, and the "margin" is the difference between the total value created and the cost of performing the activities. Analysing the value chain helps identify where in its own operations the firm creates value and builds capability, and where it does not.

Porter divides a firm's activities into **primary activities** and **support activities**.

**Primary activities** are those directly involved in the physical creation, sale, delivery and after-sale service of the product:
1. **Inbound logistics** — receiving, storing and handling of input materials.
2. **Operations** — transforming inputs into the finished product.
3. **Outbound logistics** — storing and distributing the finished product to buyers.
4. **Marketing and sales** — inducing buyers to purchase and enabling them to do so.
5. **Service** — installation, repair, training and after-sale support that maintain the product's value.

**Support activities** assist the primary activities and one another:
1. **Firm infrastructure** — general management, planning, finance, accounting and quality management.
2. **Human resource management** — recruiting, training, developing and compensating people.
3. **Technology development** — R&D, process and product improvement, know-how.
4. **Procurement** — purchasing of inputs and resources used across the chain.

| Basis | Primary activities | Support activities |
|---|---|---|
| Role | Directly create, sell and deliver the product | Support and enable the primary activities |
| Examples | Inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing & sales, service | Infrastructure, HRM, technology development, procurement |

**Conclusion:** By breaking the firm into primary and support activities, value-chain analysis reveals where value and competitive capability are created within the organisation, guiding the firm to strengthen high-value activities and improve or outsource weak ones.

*(Full-marks tip: name all five primary and all four support activities; missing "procurement" or "firm infrastructure" from the support list is the usual deduction.)*

### Q81. Ch: Internal Environment Analysis — Resource-Based View (Case Framing) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** "PrecisionTools Ltd owns a patented alloy-hardening process that no competitor has been able to replicate for fifteen years; the process is embedded in tacit know-how held by a long-serving team and cannot be bought in the market." Using the Resource-Based View (RBV), explain the criteria a resource must satisfy to be a source of sustainable competitive advantage, and assess whether PrecisionTools' hardening process qualifies.

**Answer:**
**Meaning:** The Resource-Based View (RBV) holds that a firm's sustainable competitive advantage comes from the resources and capabilities it controls internally, rather than mainly from its position in the external environment. Under RBV, resources are the productive assets owned by the firm (tangible and intangible), and capabilities are what the firm can do with those resources. Not every resource yields advantage; a resource becomes a source of **sustainable** competitive advantage only when it satisfies a set of criteria, commonly summarised as **VRIO / VRIN** — the resource must be **Valuable, Rare, Inimitable (costly to imitate) and Non-substitutable / supported by Organisation**.

The criteria:
1. **Valuable** — the resource must enable the firm to exploit an opportunity or neutralise a threat, i.e., it must contribute to customer value or cost reduction.
2. **Rare** — it must be possessed by few, ideally only by this firm; a resource held by all competitors gives only competitive parity.
3. **Inimitable (costly to imitate)** — competitors must find it difficult and costly to copy or develop the resource, often because of unique history, causal ambiguity or social complexity/tacit knowledge.
4. **Non-substitutable / Organised to exploit** — there must be no equivalent resource that rivals could use to achieve the same benefit, and the firm must be organised to actually exploit the resource.

**Assessment of PrecisionTools' hardening process:**
- **Valuable?** Yes — the process produces a superior hardened alloy that customers value, giving a performance/quality edge.
- **Rare?** Yes — no competitor possesses it; it is unique to PrecisionTools.
- **Inimitable?** Yes — it has resisted imitation for fifteen years, is protected by a patent, and is embedded in **tacit know-how** held by a long-serving team (causal ambiguity and social complexity make it hard to copy).
- **Non-substitutable?** It "cannot be bought in the market", indicating no ready substitute is available to rivals.

The process therefore satisfies all four criteria.

**Conclusion:** Under the RBV, PrecisionTools' patented, tacit and non-tradable alloy-hardening process is valuable, rare, costly to imitate and non-substitutable, and so qualifies as a genuine source of **sustainable competitive advantage**; the firm should protect and build its strategy around this core resource.

*(Full-marks tip: state all four VRIN/VRIO criteria and test each against the facts (patent, tacit know-how, not tradable); an answer that lists the criteria but does not evaluate the process case-by-case loses the application marks.)*

### Q82. Ch: Internal Environment Analysis — Competitive Advantage and the Experience Curve (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the concept of the "experience curve" and how it can be a source of competitive advantage. State the factors that give rise to the experience effect and the strategic implications of the concept.

**Answer:**
**Meaning:** The experience curve is a concept which states that as an organisation gains cumulative experience in producing a product or performing an activity, its **unit cost of production tends to decline** by a predictable percentage each time cumulative output (accumulated experience) doubles. Its underlying idea is similar to that of a learning curve, but the experience curve is broader — it applies to total costs (not just labour) and to the whole business, not merely production.

**How it is a source of competitive advantage:** A firm that accumulates experience faster than its rivals — usually the firm with the largest cumulative volume or market share — moves down the experience curve more quickly and achieves lower unit costs. This cost advantage allows it either to earn higher margins at the prevailing price or to reduce prices to gain further share, reinforcing its lead. The experience curve thus links **high market share** to **low cost** and hence to competitive advantage; it also acts as a **barrier to entry**, because a new entrant starts with little accumulated experience and therefore higher costs.

**Factors giving rise to the experience effect:**
1. **Learning by employees** — workers become more skilled and efficient with repetition.
2. **Specialisation and improved methods** — tasks are divided and processes are refined as volume grows.
3. **Product and process improvements / innovation** — designs and technologies are improved over time.
4. **Economies of scale** that accompany rising cumulative volume.

**Strategic implications:**
- It encourages firms to **pursue market-share leadership early** to accumulate experience and cost advantage faster than rivals.
- It supports **aggressive pricing** to build volume, anticipating future cost reductions.
- It warns that firms with low cumulative volume face a persistent cost disadvantage.

**Conclusion:** The experience curve shows that cumulative experience lowers unit cost predictably; the firm that builds volume and experience fastest gains a durable cost-based competitive advantage and raises entry barriers against rivals.

*(Full-marks tip: state the "cost falls as cumulative output doubles" definition precisely, list the sources of the effect, and connect it to market-share strategy; vague answers that merely equate it with the learning curve lose marks.)*

### Q83. Ch: Internal Environment Analysis — Core Competence (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the concept of "core competence" as propounded by Prahalad and Hamel, and state the tests that a capability must pass to qualify as a core competence.

**Answer:**
**Meaning:** The concept of core competence was propounded by C. K. Prahalad and Gary Hamel. A core competence is a unique strength or a bundle of skills and technologies that an organisation possesses, which is **not easy for competitors to imitate**, and which enables the organisation to provide distinctive benefits to customers across a range of products or markets. It is the "collective learning" of the organisation — especially the ability to coordinate diverse production skills and integrate multiple streams of technology — rather than any single product or asset.

A core competence is the **root** from which competitive products (the "fruit") grow; while individual products may come and go, the underlying core competence endures and can be leveraged into many new products and markets.

**Tests for a core competence:** According to Prahalad and Hamel, a capability qualifies as a core competence only if it passes three tests:
1. **It provides potential access to a wide variety of markets** — the competence can be applied across many products and businesses, not just one.
2. **It makes a significant contribution to the perceived customer benefits** of the end product — customers value what the competence delivers.
3. **It is difficult for competitors to imitate** — the competence is complex, built over time, and cannot easily be copied or purchased.

**Conclusion:** A core competence is a hard-to-imitate, collectively-learned capability that gives access to many markets and adds real customer value; capabilities passing all three tests form the foundation on which an organisation should build its long-term competitive strategy.

*(Full-marks tip: attribute the concept to Prahalad and Hamel and give all three tests exactly; stating the definition without the three qualifying tests is the common deduction.)*

### Q84. Ch: Internal Environment Analysis — Strengths, Weaknesses and Distinctive Competence (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** "MedEquip Ltd has an outstanding in-house R&D team that consistently launches innovative diagnostic devices ahead of rivals (a clear strength), but it has a weak and thinly-spread distribution network and high overheads (clear weaknesses)." Explain the meaning of strengths, weaknesses and distinctive competence, and advise how MedEquip should use this internal analysis to shape its strategy.

**Answer:**
**Meaning:** The analysis of the internal environment focuses on identifying an organisation's strengths and weaknesses relative to its competitors.
- A **strength** is an inherent capability, resource or positive attribute that an organisation possesses which it can use to gain a strategic advantage over competitors (for example, superior technology, a strong brand, or skilled people).
- A **weakness** is an inherent limitation or deficiency that creates a strategic disadvantage relative to competitors (for example, a poor distribution reach, obsolete technology, or high costs).
- A **distinctive competence** is a strength that is unique to the organisation and difficult for rivals to match — a competitively superior capability on which the firm can base a durable competitive advantage. (When a distinctive competence is applied to more than one business/market it approaches a core competence.)

**Application to MedEquip Ltd:**
- MedEquip's **strength** is its outstanding in-house R&D that repeatedly brings innovative diagnostic devices to market ahead of rivals. Because this capability is superior to competitors and hard to copy, it can be treated as MedEquip's **distinctive competence**.

2. **Correct or bypass the weaknesses:** strengthen distribution — for example, by tie-ups, distributor partnerships or outsourcing the distribution function — so that innovative products actually reach customers; and undertake cost-control measures to reduce high overheads.
3. **Match strengths to opportunities (SWOT logic):** deploy the R&D strength against market opportunities while shielding the firm from the threat that weak distribution poses.

### Q85. Ch: Strategic Choices — Stability vs Expansion vs Retrenchment (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** A company's Board is debating its "grand strategy" for the coming three years. Distinguish between **Stability strategy, Expansion strategy and Retrenchment strategy** and state one situation in which each is the most appropriate choice.

**Answer:**
Every organisation, after appraising its external environment and internal capability, has to select a **grand strategy** (also called a directional or master strategy) which indicates the basic direction in which it wishes to move. The four grand strategies are stability, expansion, retrenchment and their combination; the first three are distinguished below.

**Stability strategy** is adopted when an organisation is satisfied with its present position and decides to continue with the **same objectives, the same products/markets and the same functions**, seeking only incremental improvement in performance. It is a "safety-oriented, status-quo" strategy involving low risk; management continues to serve the same customer groups by maintaining the same rate of growth. It is not a "do-nothing" strategy but a conscious decision to consolidate.

**Expansion strategy** (growth strategy) is followed when an organisation seeks a **significant increase in the scope of its activities** by redefining its business — entering new products, new markets, new functions or a substantially higher level of the same operations. It is characterised by **redefinition of the business, higher investment, higher risk and a promise of higher returns**, and is pursued through intensification, diversification, mergers, acquisitions or strategic alliances.

**Retrenchment strategy** is pursued when an organisation decides to **substantially reduce the scope of its activities** — pulling back from declining markets or products, cutting costs, or turning around a loss-making unit — with the objective of arresting decline and restoring viability.

| Basis | Stability | Expansion | Retrenchment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Change in scope | No material change | Substantial increase | Substantial decrease |
| Risk | Low | High | Moderate |
| Managerial thrust | Consolidation | Growth | Revival / exit |
| Appropriate when | Environment is stable and firm is doing well | Environment is munificent, opportunities abound | Firm is weak / environment is hostile |

**Conclusion:** Stability suits a firm content with its present course in a stable environment; expansion suits a firm chasing opportunities in a growing environment; retrenchment suits a firm facing decline that must cut back to survive.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the "change in scope of activity" as the single distinguishing thread and one crisp appropriateness situation each — a common deduction is calling stability a "do-nothing" strategy.)*

### Q86. Ch: Strategic Choices — Types of Retrenchment (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain **Turnaround, Divestment and Liquidation** as the three forms of retrenchment strategy.

**Answer:**
Retrenchment strategy is followed when an organisation aims at a contraction of its activities through a substantial reduction or elimination of the scope of one or more of its businesses. It takes three progressively severe forms.

**Turnaround strategy** is a form of retrenchment adopted when management, though continuing to run the business, retreats from its present course to **make the enterprise viable again**. It is undertaken when performance has deteriorated but the situation is not so grave as to warrant exit; action is directed at reversing a negative trend through cost reduction, revenue generation, asset reduction and management changes. It is therefore an **internal, revival-oriented** retrenchment.

**Divestment strategy** (divestiture) involves the **sale or liquidation of a portion of a business, or a major division, profit centre or SBU**. It is resorted to when a turnaround has been attempted but has failed, when a business unit is a persistent drain, when a unit does not fit the firm's core, or when the firm needs cash for more attractive opportunities. Divestment is thus a **partial withdrawal** — the firm survives but sheds a part.

**Liquidation strategy** is the **most extreme and unattractive** form of retrenchment, involving the **closing down of the entire firm and the sale of its assets**. It amounts to giving up the business altogether and is regarded as the last resort because it carries connotations of failure, loss of employment and difficulties in disposing of assets.

**Conclusion:** The three forms represent an escalating scale of contraction — turnaround (revive and continue), divestment (sell a part) and liquidation (close the whole).

*(Full-marks tip: State the escalating severity turnaround → divestment → liquidation; deduction for confusing divestment (part) with liquidation (whole).)*

### Q87. Ch: Strategic Choices — Combination Strategy (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** "Bharat Consumer Ltd." is a diversified company with three SBUs — a fast-growing snacks division, a mature and profitable soaps division, and a loss-making candle division. When an organisation is large, diversified and operates in several industries whose environments differ, a **single** grand strategy is often inappropriate for the whole firm. In such a case the organisation adopts a **Combination strategy** — the simultaneous employment of different grand strategies (stability, expansion and retrenchment) in different businesses, or the sequential use of different strategies over time. ### Q88. Ch: Strategic Choices — Strategic Alliance (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Define a **Strategic Alliance**. Explain its advantages and the disadvantages/risks an organisation should weigh before entering one.

**Answer:**
A **Strategic Alliance** is a **cooperative arrangement between two or more organisations** that agree to pool, exchange or integrate specified resources and capabilities for mutual gain, while **remaining independent** organisations after the alliance is formed. It is one of the routes an organisation may use to pursue an **expansion strategy** without the full commitment involved in a merger or acquisition. Alliances range from informal understandings to formal joint ventures, and typically cover technology sharing, joint R&D, joint production, or shared distribution.

**Advantages of a strategic alliance:**
- **Organisational** — it enables partners to **learn necessary skills and obtain capabilities** from each other, and to carry out an activity more effectively by sharing it with a partner.
- **Economic** — it **reduces costs and risks** by distributing them across the partners, and yields **economies of scale** and access to resources neither partner alone could command.
- **Strategic** — it allows firms to **create and exploit synergies, secure a competitive advantage** and gain rapid entry into new markets or technologies.
- **Political** — it can help a firm **enter a foreign market** where local partnership is required by regulation or is commercially prudent.

**Disadvantages / risks of a strategic alliance:**
- Alliances are frequently **difficult and prone to failure**; a large proportion do not meet expectations.
- There is a **risk of one partner acquiring the other's proprietary know-how** and later emerging as a competitor.
- **Cultural, managerial and objective mismatches** between partners create friction and coordination problems.
- The firm may become **dependent on the partner** and lose control over parts of its own operations.

**Conclusion:** A strategic alliance is a flexible, lower-commitment growth route that offers shared skills, costs and market access, but it must be entered with clear objectives and safeguards because of the high failure rate and the risk of empowering a future rival.

*(Full-marks tip: Note that partners remain independent — this separates an alliance from a merger; give both advantages and risks, as a one-sided answer loses marks.)*

### Q89. Ch: Strategic Choices — Mergers and Acquisitions (Marks: 8) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain **Merger and Acquisition** as strategic options for expansion. State the objectives that lead firms to merge and describe the different **types of mergers** (horizontal, vertical, concentric and conglomerate).

**Answer:**
**Merger and acquisition (M&A)** are external routes to an **expansion strategy** by which one organisation grows through combination with, or purchase of, another organisation instead of through internal (organic) development.

A **merger** is a combination of two or more organisations in which one **acquires the assets and liabilities of the other in exchange for shares or cash**, or the two organisations are **dissolved and a new entity is formed** to take over their combined business; broadly it is the **absorption of one organisation by another**. An **acquisition** (takeover) is the **purchase of one organisation by another** so that the buyer establishes control; it may be friendly or hostile. The two terms are used together because both result in one management controlling the combined resources.

**Objectives / reasons for mergers and acquisitions:**
- To achieve **economies of scale** by combining facilities and eliminating duplication.
- To achieve **synergy** — the "2 + 2 = 5" effect where the combined firm is worth more than the sum of the parts.
- To acquire **complementary resources, technology, brands, talent or distribution** quickly.
- To achieve **rapid growth** and gain market share faster than through internal growth.
- To enter **new markets or product lines**, to gain **tax advantages**, and to **diversify risk**.

**Types of mergers:**

| Type | Nature of combination | Illustration |
|---|---|---|
| **Horizontal merger** | Between two firms in the **same line of business** / same stage of production (often competitors) | Two cement manufacturers merging |
| **Vertical merger** | Between firms at **different stages of the same production–distribution chain** (a firm and its supplier or customer) | A car maker merging with a tyre supplier |
| **Concentric (congeneric) merger** | Between firms that are **related in technology, markets or products but not identical** | A bank merging with an insurance company |
| **Conglomerate merger** | Between firms in **wholly unrelated businesses** with no common thread | A steel company merging with a food company |

**Conclusion:** M&A offers a firm a fast, external route to expansion driven mainly by synergy, scale and quick access to resources; the strategic logic differs by type — horizontal builds market power, vertical secures the value chain, concentric leverages relatedness and conglomerate diversifies risk.

### Q90. Ch: Strategy Implementation & Evaluation — Strategy Formulation vs Implementation (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** "Strategy formulation and strategy implementation are two distinct but interdependent phases of strategic management." Distinguish between the two.

**Answer:**
Strategic management proceeds in a sequence in which **strategy formulation** precedes and **strategy implementation** follows, and success requires that both be handled well; a good strategy poorly implemented, or a poor strategy well implemented, both fail.

**Strategy formulation** is the process of **deciding on a new strategy** — it involves environmental and organisational appraisal, setting objectives, and choosing the strategy. It is **positioning forces before the action**, is primarily an **intellectual/analytical** activity, requires **conceptual, integrative and analytical skills**, is driven by a **few key people at the top**, and is largely an **entrepreneurial** activity.

**Strategy implementation** is the process of **putting the chosen strategy into action** — it involves designing structure, allocating resources, developing systems and procedures, and managing behaviour to achieve results. It is **managing forces during the action**, is primarily an **operational/administrative** activity, requires **motivational and leadership skills**, is carried out by **many people throughout the organisation**, and is largely an **administrative** activity.

| Basis | Strategy Formulation | Strategy Implementation |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Positioning forces before action | Managing forces during action |
| Focus | Effectiveness (doing the right things) | Efficiency (doing things right) |
| Type of process | Intellectual / analytical | Operational / administrative |
| Skills needed | Conceptual, integrative, analytical | Motivational, leadership, coordination |
| People involved | Few, at the top | Many, throughout the organisation |

**Conclusion:** Formulation decides *what* to do and where to compete, whereas implementation converts that decision into *action and results*; the two are distinct in nature and skill but interdependent in outcome.

*(Full-marks tip: "Before the action" vs "during the action" and "few at the top" vs "many throughout" are the anchor distinctions the examiner looks for.)*

### Q91. Ch: Strategy Implementation & Evaluation — McKinsey 7S Framework (Marks: 8) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the **McKinsey 7S Framework** and describe all seven elements. How does it help in strategy implementation?

**Answer:**
The **McKinsey 7S Framework**, developed by consultants at McKinsey & Co. (Peters, Waterman and others), holds that **effective strategy implementation depends on the alignment of seven interrelated internal elements** of an organisation, all of which begin with the letter "S". The central idea is that a strategy cannot be implemented in isolation; **all seven elements must be mutually consistent and reinforcing**, and a change in one requires corresponding changes in the others.

The seven elements are grouped into "hard" elements (easier to identify and influence) and "soft" elements (less tangible but equally vital):

**Hard elements:**
1. **Strategy** — the plan and set of actions an organisation devises in response to, or in anticipation of, changes in its environment to build and sustain competitive advantage.
2. **Structure** — the **organisational framework** through which activities are divided, coordinated and controlled; the way tasks and people are arranged (reporting relationships, hierarchy).
3. **Systems** — the **formal and informal procedures and processes** (planning, budgeting, information, reward, control systems) that govern day-to-day activity and make the organisation function.

**Soft elements:**
4. **Shared Values** (originally "Superordinate Goals") — the **core beliefs, guiding concepts and culture** placed at the **centre** of the framework because they bind the other six elements together and give the organisation its sense of purpose.
5. **Style** — the **leadership and management style** — how managers behave, spend their time and interact — which signals what the organisation truly values.
6. **Staff** — the **people** in the organisation — their number, recruitment, development, socialisation and career management.
7. **Skills** — the **distinctive capabilities and competencies** of the organisation as a whole (not just individuals) — what the organisation does best.

**How it helps implementation:** The framework provides managers a **diagnostic checklist** to ensure that the strategy is supported by an appropriate structure, workable systems, a fitting management style, the right staff and skills, all held together by shared values. By checking **alignment among the seven S's**, managers can identify which elements are out of step with a new strategy and must be changed for implementation to succeed.

**Conclusion:** The 7S framework treats strategy implementation as an exercise in **achieving internal fit** among strategy, structure, systems, shared values, style, staff and skills — misalignment in any one undermines the whole.

*(Full-marks tip: Name all seven correctly, keep Shared Values at the centre, and stress mutual alignment; forgetting "Shared Values" or listing only six is the usual deduction.)*

### Q92. Ch: Strategy Implementation & Evaluation — Change Management / Kurt Lewin Model (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Implementing a new strategy usually requires organisational change that is resisted by employees. Explain the **Kurt Lewin three-stage model of change** and the steps management can take to **overcome resistance to change**.

**Answer:**
Strategy implementation almost always demands change in the way an organisation operates, and change is naturally resisted because people fear the unknown, fear loss of status or income, and are comfortable with the status quo. **Kurt Lewin** proposed that planned change proceeds through **three stages**.

1. **Unfreezing the situation** — the process of **breaking down the existing mindset, attitudes and behaviours** so that people become aware of the need for change and are prepared to accept it. The status quo is "unfrozen" by highlighting the inadequacy of present practices and creating a felt need for change.
2. **Changing to a new situation** — once unfreezing is done, the organisation **moves to the new, desired state**. New behaviours, values, structures and systems are introduced, often through **identification** (learning from a role model) and **internalisation** (adopting new behaviour because it fits one's own needs).
3. **Refreezing** — the new behaviour is **stabilised, reinforced and made a permanent part of the organisation** through supportive systems, rewards and reinforcement, so that people do not slip back to the old ways.

**Steps to overcome resistance to change:**
- **Education and communication** — explaining the logic and need for change in advance.
- **Participation and involvement** — involving those affected in designing the change so they own it.
- **Facilitation and support** — providing training, counselling and resources to ease the transition.
- **Negotiation and agreement** — offering incentives to those who resist.
- **Manipulation and co-optation**, and, as a last resort, **explicit and implicit coercion**.

**Conclusion:** Effective change management follows Lewin's unfreeze–change–refreeze sequence and reduces resistance chiefly through communication, participation and support, so that the new strategy takes durable root.

### Q93. Ch: Strategy Implementation & Evaluation — Strategic Control Types (Marks: 8) [Theory]
**Question:** Describe the **four types of strategic control** — premise control, strategic surveillance, special alert control and implementation control — used to keep a strategy on track.

**Answer:**
**Strategic control** is the process of **evaluating strategy as it is being formulated and implemented**, directed at whether the organisation is moving in the right direction and whether the assumptions on which the strategy rests still hold. Because strategy is implemented over a long period during which the environment changes, four complementary types of strategic control are used.

**1. Premise control** — Every strategy is built on certain **planning premises or assumptions** about environmental and industry conditions (e.g., interest rates, growth rate, competitor behaviour, technology). Premise control is designed to **check systematically and continuously whether these assumptions are still valid**. If a key premise no longer holds, the strategy may have to be changed. The responsibility is usually assigned to the department that is the source of the assumption.

**2. Strategic surveillance** — Unlike premise control, which is **focused on specific premises**, strategic surveillance is designed to **monitor a broad range of events inside and outside the organisation** that are likely to affect the strategy. It is a **loose, "all-round", environmental scanning** activity — through trade journals, conferences, conversations and observation — meant to uncover important yet **unanticipated** information.

**3. Special alert control** — This is the **thorough and rapid reconsideration of the strategy** triggered by a **sudden, unexpected event** — such as a natural disaster, a competitor's takeover, an accident, or an abrupt regulatory change. It is handled through **crisis teams and contingency plans** and demands immediate re-examination of the strategy's relevance.

**4. Implementation control** — Strategy implementation takes place as a series of steps, programmes and resource allocations over time. Implementation control is aimed at **assessing whether the overall strategy should be changed in the light of the results of the actions taken to implement it**. Its two main forms are **monitoring strategic thrusts** (checking key phased actions) and **milestone reviews** (comprehensive reassessment at critical points).

| Type | What it monitors | Trigger / nature |
|---|---|---|
| Premise control | Specific planning assumptions | Continuous, focused |
| Strategic surveillance | Broad, all-round environment | Continuous, unfocused |
| Special alert control | A sudden, unexpected event | Event-triggered, rapid |
| Implementation control | Results of implementing actions | Step-by-step / milestone |

**Conclusion:** The four controls work together — premise control watches the assumptions, surveillance watches the whole horizon, special alert control reacts to shocks, and implementation control watches the unfolding action — jointly keeping the strategy relevant and on course.

*(Full-marks tip: The frequent mix-up is between premise control (specific, focused) and strategic surveillance (broad, unfocused) — state the focused-vs-broad contrast explicitly.)*

### Q94. Ch: Strategy Implementation & Evaluation — Strategic vs Operational Control (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** The MD of a company complains that its budget-variance reports tell him whether targets are met but never warn him when the strategy itself has become outdated. Explain, by distinguishing **operational control** from **strategic control**, why the existing reports are inadequate and what the company needs instead.

**Answer:**
The MD is experiencing the classic gap between the two levels of control in strategic management, namely operational control and strategic control.

**Operational control** is concerned with **action or performance**, and evaluates whether the organisation is **performing well against pre-set standards** — it focuses on **individual tasks or transactions**, asks "**are we doing things right?**", operates over a **short time horizon** (weeks, months) and uses tools such as **budgets, variance analysis and performance reports**. Its aim is to keep current operations efficient. The company's budget-variance reports are precisely this — they confirm whether targets are being met.

**Strategic control**, in contrast, is concerned with **steering the organisation's future direction**. It evaluates whether the **strategy and its underlying assumptions are still valid** in a changing environment, asks "**are we doing the right things?**", operates over a **long and open-ended horizon**, and relies on **premise control, strategic surveillance, special alert control and implementation control** rather than on financial variances alone.

| Basis | Operational control | Strategic control |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | Task / performance efficiency | Strategy direction and validity |
| Question answered | Are we doing things right? | Are we doing the right things? |
| Time horizon | Short term | Long term |
| Tools | Budgets, variances, standards | Premise, surveillance, alert, implementation control |

The existing reports are inadequate because they exercise only **operational control** — they measure performance against internal targets but **do not test whether the strategy's environmental assumptions still hold**. A strategy can be perfectly executed (favourable variances) while the environment has shifted and made the strategy obsolete.

**Conclusion:** The company should supplement its operational (budget-variance) controls with a **strategic control system** — especially premise control and strategic surveillance — so that it is warned when the strategy itself, not merely its execution, needs to change.

*(Full-marks tip: The "doing things right vs doing the right things" contrast is the crux; deduction for treating budget variances as a form of strategic control.)*

### Q95. Ch: Strategy Implementation & Evaluation — Strategic Change / Business Process Reengineering (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain **Business Process Reengineering (BPR)** as an approach to implementing strategic change, and list the steps involved in carrying it out.

**Answer:**
**Business Process Reengineering (BPR)** is defined as the **fundamental rethinking and radical redesign of business processes to achieve dramatic improvements** in critical, contemporary measures of performance such as **cost, quality, service and speed**. It is an approach to **strategic change** that does not seek marginal or incremental improvement but a **quantum leap** by discarding existing processes and re-inventing them around the desired outcome. The emphasis is on the words *fundamental*, *radical*, *dramatic* and *processes* — BPR questions why work is done at all and redesigns it from a clean slate rather than automating what already exists.

**Steps in carrying out BPR:**
1. **Determining objectives and framework** — objectives are the desired end results that the redesign should achieve; they give direction and the framework guides the effort.
2. **Identifying the customers and their needs** — the process must be designed to deliver **value to the customer**, so customer requirements are established first.
3. **Studying the existing process** — the current processes are analysed to serve as a baseline and to understand what must be changed.
4. **Formulating a redesign of business processes** — creative new solutions and a genuinely **redesigned process** are developed, aligned with the objectives and customer needs.
5. **Implementing the redesign** — the reengineered process is put into operation, which is the actual test of the whole exercise.

**Conclusion:** BPR implements strategic change by **radically redesigning core processes around customer value** to achieve dramatic gains, following the sequence of setting objectives, understanding customers, studying and redesigning processes, and implementing the new design.

*(Full-marks tip: Stress "fundamental, radical, dramatic" and that BPR is not mere automation of existing work; list the five steps in order.)*

### Q96. Ch: Functional & Digital Strategy — Relationship of Functional and Corporate Strategy (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** What is a **Functional Strategy**? Explain how functional strategies relate to corporate and business-level strategies, and why they are important.

**Answer:**
A **functional strategy** is the **strategy formulated for a specific functional area** of an organisation — such as production/operations, marketing, finance, human resources and research & development — in order to **implement and support the higher-level corporate and business strategies**. It is the **approach a functional area takes to achieve corporate and business unit objectives** by maximising the productivity of its resources.

**Relationship among the three levels of strategy:** Strategy exists at three levels forming a **hierarchy**:
- **Corporate-level strategy** — decided by top management; concerns the overall scope and direction of the whole organisation (which businesses to be in).
- **Business-level (SBU) strategy** — concerns how to compete successfully in a particular business or industry.
- **Functional-level strategy** — concerns how each function will contribute to the business and corporate strategies.

Functional strategies **flow from, and are constrained by, the business and corporate strategies above them**; they translate broad strategic intent into **concrete, function-specific action plans** and provide feedback upward. Consistency must be maintained **vertically** (functional strategy aligned with business and corporate strategy) and **horizontally** (functional strategies of different departments consistent with one another).

**Importance of functional strategies:**
- They provide **operational meaning and detail** to the general corporate and business strategies.
- They serve as a basis for **controlling activities** in the functional areas.
- They **reduce the areas of ambiguity** so that even routine decisions are guided by strategy.
- They help in achieving **coordination** among departments by revealing how each contributes.

**Conclusion:** Functional strategy is the lowest-level, action-oriented strategy that **operationalises corporate and business strategy** within each function; its value lies in giving concrete direction, control and coordination to day-to-day functional decisions.

*(Full-marks tip: Show the three-level hierarchy and vertical + horizontal consistency; deduction for describing functional strategy in isolation from corporate/business levels.)*

### Q97. Ch: Functional & Digital Strategy — Supply Chain Management (Marks: 8) [Application]
**Question:** "Freshcart Ltd.", a grocery retail chain, suffers from frequent stock-outs of fast-moving items and simultaneous over-stocking and wastage of perishables, with poor coordination between suppliers, warehouses and stores. Advise the company on adopting **Supply Chain Management (SCM)** — explain what SCM is, how it differs from traditional logistics, and the benefits it will bring.

**Answer:**
Freshcart's problems — stock-outs, over-stocking, wastage and poor coordination across suppliers, warehouses and stores — are symptoms of a fragmented supply chain, and the remedy is an integrated **Supply Chain Management** approach.

**Meaning of SCM:** **Supply Chain Management** is the **active management of supply-chain activities and relationships in order to maximise customer value and achieve a sustainable competitive advantage**. A supply chain is the **network of all the organisations, people, activities, information and resources involved in moving a product or service from supplier to customer** — spanning sourcing of raw materials, procurement, conversion, warehousing, and distribution to the final customer. SCM represents a conscious effort by the firms in the chain to **develop and run the chain in the most effective and efficient way**, integrating the flow of **materials, information and finance** along the whole chain.

**How SCM differs from traditional logistics:** Logistics typically refers to activities that occur **within the boundaries of a single organisation** (inbound and outbound movement, storage), whereas SCM refers to the **network of firms that work together and coordinate their actions** to deliver a product to market. SCM is thus **broader** — it embraces logistics plus manufacturing, procurement, information sharing and relationship management **across firms**, seeking end-to-end integration rather than function-by-function optimisation.

**Application and benefits for Freshcart Ltd.:** By integrating its suppliers, warehouses and stores into a single coordinated chain with **shared demand information**, Freshcart can:
- **Match supply to actual demand**, eliminating both stock-outs of fast movers and over-stocking of perishables (reducing wastage).
- **Reduce inventory-carrying costs and total costs** across the chain.
- **Improve customer value** through better product availability and freshness.
- **Speed up the flow** of goods and information, improving responsiveness.
- Build **long-term supplier relationships** that make replenishment reliable.

**Conclusion:** Freshcart should adopt integrated Supply Chain Management — coordinating the whole supplier-to-store network with shared information — to eliminate stock-outs and wastage, cut costs and raise customer value, thereby converting its supply chain into a source of competitive advantage.

*(Full-marks tip: Define the supply chain as a *network across firms* and contrast it with single-firm logistics; then tie each benefit back to Freshcart's specific problems.)*

### Q98. Ch: Functional & Digital Strategy — Customer Relationship Management (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** A telecom company loses many customers each year to rivals and finds it far cheaper to retain a customer than to acquire a new one. Explain **Customer Relationship Management (CRM)** as a functional/digital strategy, its objectives, and how it will help the company reduce customer churn.

**Answer:**
The telecom company's high churn and the fact that retention is cheaper than acquisition point directly to the need for a **Customer Relationship Management** strategy.

**Meaning of CRM:** **Customer Relationship Management** is a **combination of practices, strategies and technologies that a company uses to manage and analyse customer interactions and data throughout the customer lifecycle**, with the goal of **improving customer relationships, retaining customers and driving sales growth**. It is both a **management philosophy** — putting the customer at the centre of the business — and a **technology-enabled (digital) system** that consolidates customer information into a single view so that every interaction is informed and personalised.

**Objectives of CRM:**
- To **acquire, retain and grow** profitable customers by building long-term relationships.
- To **improve customer satisfaction and loyalty** through personalised service.
- To **increase the value of each customer** (cross-selling, up-selling) over the lifecycle.
- To **reduce the cost of serving** customers by streamlining interactions.

**How CRM will reduce churn for the telecom company:**
- By maintaining a **single, complete view of each customer** (usage, complaints, payment history), the company can **anticipate dissatisfaction and intervene before the customer leaves**.
- CRM analytics can **identify customers at high risk of churning** and target them with **retention offers** and personalised plans.
- It enables **faster, more consistent complaint resolution**, removing a major cause of defection.
- It supports **loyalty programmes and personalised communication** that strengthen the emotional and economic ties that keep customers.
- Because retaining a customer is far cheaper than acquiring one, the reduced churn **directly improves profitability**.

**Conclusion:** CRM is a customer-centric, digitally enabled strategy that consolidates and analyses customer data to build loyalty; for the telecom company it is the most direct lever to **predict and prevent churn**, improve retention and protect profitability.

In an increasingly connected world, digital strategy has become an essential component of overall business and functional strategy, cutting across marketing, operations and customer service. - **Social media / SoLoMo (Social–Local–Mobile)** — the convergence of **social**, **local** and **mobile** technologies through which organisations engage customers, build communities, target consumers by location, and market interactively in real time.

### Q100. Ch: Functional & Digital Strategy — E-Commerce Business Models (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** In the context of digital strategy, explain the major **e-commerce / e-business models** — B2B, B2C, C2C and C2B — with an example of each.

**Answer:**
**E-commerce (electronic commerce)** is the **buying and selling of goods and services, and the transmitting of funds or data, over an electronic network, primarily the internet**; **e-business** is the broader conduct of all business processes electronically. As part of a digital strategy, organisations choose one or more e-commerce models classified by **who the transacting parties are** — businesses (B) or consumers (C).

| Model | Transacting parties | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
