### Q1. Ch: Ratio Analysis — Limitations of Ratio Analysis (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** "Ratios are only symptoms; the physician has to make a proper diagnosis." In the light of this statement, explain the limitations of ratio analysis that a financial analyst must keep in mind before drawing conclusions from computed ratios.

**Answer:**
Ratio analysis is a widely used tool of financial analysis, but it suffers from several inherent limitations which restrict the reliability of the conclusions drawn from it. These limitations are explained below:

1. **Based on historical/past data:** Financial statements are prepared on historical cost and, therefore, the ratios computed from them reflect past performance. They are not necessarily indicative of the future, whereas most financial decisions are forward-looking.
2. **Ignores price-level changes (inflation):** Ratios do not make any adjustment for changes in the purchasing power of money. Comparison of ratios of two different years or two firms becomes misleading when the price level has changed materially, because assets recorded at different dates carry different rupee values.
3. **Window dressing:** Financial statements can be manipulated by management to present a better position than the actual one (e.g., postponing purchases, showing higher year-end liquidity). Ratios based on such window-dressed statements convey a wrong picture.
4. **Absence of standard/uniform definitions:** There is no single universally accepted definition of many terms (e.g., "current assets", "operating profit", "capital employed"). Different firms compute the same ratio differently, impairing inter-firm comparability.
5. **Qualitative factors are ignored:** Ratios measure only quantitative relationships. Important qualitative aspects such as quality of management, employee morale, market reputation and technology are not captured.
6. **Differences in accounting policies:** Different firms adopt different methods of depreciation, inventory valuation, etc. Ratios of such firms are not strictly comparable unless the policies are made uniform.
7. **A single ratio is not conclusive:** One ratio in isolation seldom conveys a meaningful conclusion; a group of related ratios read together with trend and industry data is required.
8. **Ratios are only a means, not an end:** They highlight the symptoms of a problem but do not, by themselves, provide the solution. Managerial judgement is required to interpret them.

**Conclusion:** Ratio analysis is an indispensable but not an infallible tool; ratios only point to the areas requiring investigation and must be interpreted with reference to trends, industry norms and qualitative factors before any diagnosis is made.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards giving at least six distinct limitations, each explained in a full sentence rather than a one-word list. Marks are commonly lost for merely writing "based on past data, window dressing, inflation" without explanation, or for confusing limitations with the "advantages" of ratio analysis.)*

### Q2. Ch: Ratio Analysis — Comprehensive Analysis of Liquidity, Solvency & Efficiency (Marks: 10) [Problem]
**Question:** From the following Balance Sheet and additional information of XYZ Ltd. for the year ended 31st March, 2026, compute the ratios relating to **liquidity, solvency and efficiency** and give your comment on the overall financial position of the company.

| Equity & Liabilities | ₹ | Assets | ₹ |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Equity Share Capital | 10,00,000 | Fixed Assets (net) | 14,00,000 |
| Reserves & Surplus | 5,00,000 | Inventory | 5,00,000 |
| 10% Debentures | 8,00,000 | Trade Receivables | 4,00,000 |
| Trade Payables | 3,00,000 | Cash & Bank | 4,00,000 |
| Other Current Liabilities | 1,00,000 | | |
| **Total** | **27,00,000** | **Total** | **27,00,000** |

**Additional information:** Revenue from operations (all credit) ₹30,00,000; Cost of Goods Sold ₹21,00,000; Earnings before Interest and Tax (EBIT) ₹4,00,000; Interest on debentures ₹80,000; Tax rate 25%. (Assume 365 days in a year.)

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Basic figures**
- Current Assets (CA) = Inventory 5,00,000 + Receivables 4,00,000 + Cash 4,00,000 = **₹13,00,000**
- Current Liabilities (CL) = Payables 3,00,000 + Other CL 1,00,000 = **₹4,00,000**
- Quick Assets = CA − Inventory = 13,00,000 − 5,00,000 = **₹8,00,000**
- Shareholders' Funds (Equity) = Share Capital 10,00,000 + Reserves 5,00,000 = **₹15,00,000**
- PAT = (EBIT − Interest) × (1 − tax) = (4,00,000 − 80,000) × 0.75 = 3,20,000 × 0.75 = **₹2,40,000**

**Statement Showing Computation of Ratios**

| Ratio | Formula | Computation | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Liquidity** | | | |
| Current Ratio | CA ÷ CL | 13,00,000 ÷ 4,00,000 | **3.25 : 1** |
| Quick Ratio | Quick Assets ÷ CL | 8,00,000 ÷ 4,00,000 | **2 : 1** |
| **Solvency** | | | |
| Debt–Equity Ratio | Long-term Debt ÷ Shareholders' Funds | 8,00,000 ÷ 15,00,000 | **0.53 : 1** |
| Interest Coverage Ratio | EBIT ÷ Interest | 4,00,000 ÷ 80,000 | **5 times** |
| Proprietary Ratio | Shareholders' Funds ÷ Total Assets | 15,00,000 ÷ 27,00,000 | **0.56 : 1** |
| **Efficiency** | | | |
| Inventory Turnover Ratio | COGS ÷ Inventory | 21,00,000 ÷ 5,00,000 | **4.2 times** |
| Trade Receivables Turnover | Credit Sales ÷ Receivables | 30,00,000 ÷ 4,00,000 | **7.5 times** |
| Debtors' Collection Period | 365 ÷ Receivables Turnover | 365 ÷ 7.5 | **≈ 49 days** |
| Total Assets Turnover | Sales ÷ Total Assets | 30,00,000 ÷ 27,00,000 | **1.11 times** |
| Net Profit Ratio | PAT ÷ Sales × 100 | 2,40,000 ÷ 30,00,000 | **8%** |

**Comment on overall financial position:**
- **Liquidity:** The current ratio of 3.25 : 1 and quick ratio of 2 : 1 are both well above the conventional norms of 2 : 1 and 1 : 1 respectively. The company therefore has a very comfortable short-term liquidity position; in fact, the excessively high ratios suggest that funds may be lying idle in cash and receivables, indicating **over-liquidity** and possible under-utilisation of resources.
- **Solvency:** With a debt–equity ratio of only 0.53 : 1 and an interest coverage of 5 times, the company is **conservatively financed and highly solvent**. It carries a low degree of financial risk and has ample capacity to service its debt; there is scope to raise further debt to gain from trading on equity.
- **Efficiency:** Inventory turnover of 4.2 times and a collection period of about 49 days are only moderate. Total assets turnover of 1.11 times and a net profit margin of 8% indicate acceptable but not outstanding operating efficiency; the modest inventory turnover coupled with high liquidity confirms that working capital is not being aggressively managed.

**Conclusion:** XYZ Ltd. is financially **sound and low-risk**, but its performance is marred by **over-liquidity and moderate asset utilisation**; deploying the surplus liquid funds into productive assets (or reducing idle balances) would improve overall efficiency and return.

*(Full-marks tip: Group the ratios under the three heads asked (liquidity/solvency/efficiency) and, most importantly, write a comment for each head — the marks for interpretation are as high as the marks for calculation. State the formula and the norm; a bare number without a benchmark comment loses the interpretation marks.)*

### Q3. Ch: Ratio Analysis — Reconstruction of Current Assets from Ratios (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** The following information is available for M/s Sunrise Traders for the year ended 31st March, 2026:

| Particulars | Value |
|---|---|
| Current Ratio | 2.5 : 1 |
| Quick (Liquid) Ratio | 1.5 : 1 |
| Working Capital | ₹3,00,000 |
| Annual Credit Sales | ₹20,00,000 |
| Debtors' Collection Period | 1.5 months |

You are required to compute: (i) Current Liabilities, (ii) Current Assets, (iii) Inventory (Stock), (iv) Trade Receivables (Debtors), and (v) Cash & Bank balance.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Current Liabilities and Current Assets (from working capital)**
Let Current Liabilities = CL. Then Current Assets (CA) = 2.5 CL.
Working Capital = CA − CL = 2.5 CL − CL = 1.5 CL
∴ 1.5 CL = ₹3,00,000 → **CL = ₹2,00,000**
CA = 2.5 × 2,00,000 = **₹5,00,000**

**WN-2: Inventory (Stock)**
Quick Assets = Quick Ratio × CL = 1.5 × 2,00,000 = ₹3,00,000
Inventory = CA − Quick Assets = 5,00,000 − 3,00,000 = **₹2,00,000**

**WN-3: Trade Receivables (Debtors)**
Debtors = Credit Sales × (Collection period ÷ 12) = 20,00,000 × (1.5 ÷ 12) = **₹2,50,000**

**WN-4: Cash & Bank**
Cash & Bank = Quick Assets − Debtors = 3,00,000 − 2,50,000 = **₹50,000**

**Statement Showing the Current Position of M/s Sunrise Traders**

| Current Assets | ₹ | Current Liabilities | ₹ |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Inventory | 2,00,000 | Current Liabilities | 2,00,000 |
| Trade Receivables | 2,50,000 | | |
| Cash & Bank | 50,000 | | |
| **Total Current Assets** | **5,00,000** | **Total** | **2,00,000** |

**Cross-check:** Current Ratio = 5,00,000 ÷ 2,00,000 = 2.5 : 1 ✓; Quick Ratio = (2,50,000 + 50,000) ÷ 2,00,000 = 1.5 : 1 ✓; Working Capital = 5,00,000 − 2,00,000 = 3,00,000 ✓.

**Answer:** Current Liabilities ₹2,00,000; Current Assets ₹5,00,000; Inventory ₹2,00,000; Debtors ₹2,50,000; Cash & Bank ₹50,000.

*(Full-marks tip: Always start from the item you can pin down absolutely — here Working Capital = 1.5 CL. Show the elimination step (CR − QR gives the inventory-to-CL relation) and finish with a cross-check reconciling all given ratios; the reconciliation earns the final mark.)*

### Q4. Ch: Ratio Analysis — Advantages / Uses of Ratio Analysis (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** "Accounting ratios are useful to a wide range of users." Explain the advantages of ratio analysis to the different parties interested in the financial statements of a business.

**Answer:**
Ratio analysis is the process of establishing and interpreting meaningful arithmetical relationships between figures of the financial statements. Its principal advantages are:

1. **Aid to financial forecasting and planning:** Ratios computed over several years reveal trends that help management forecast future performance and prepare budgets and plans.
2. **Useful in judging operating efficiency:** Activity and profitability ratios (e.g., inventory turnover, net profit ratio) enable management to assess how efficiently the resources of the firm are being utilised and to locate weak areas.
3. **Facilitates inter-firm and intra-firm comparison:** Ratios reduce absolute figures to a common base, permitting comparison of a firm's performance with that of competitors, industry averages and its own past record.
4. **Assessment of liquidity and solvency:** Short-term creditors and bankers use liquidity ratios to judge the firm's ability to meet current obligations; long-term lenders use solvency ratios to assess the safety of their funds.
5. **Simplifies and summarises accounting data:** Ratios condense a mass of financial data into a few significant figures, making the information intelligible and readily communicable to those without accounting expertise.
6. **Aid to decision-making and control:** Ratios help management in decisions on pricing, capital structure and investment, and act as instruments of managerial control by measuring actual performance against standards.

**Utility to different parties:** *Shareholders/investors* — profitability and return ratios; *creditors and bankers* — liquidity and coverage ratios; *long-term lenders* — solvency and gearing ratios; *management* — the full range for planning and control; *employees* — profitability (for bonus/wage bargaining); *government/regulators* — for taxation and policy.

**Conclusion:** Ratio analysis serves as a bridge between raw financial data and rational economic decisions, benefiting every stakeholder group provided the ratios are interpreted with due regard to their limitations.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner looks for the advantages linked to the specific user group. Presenting a "who uses what" mapping in addition to the general advantages typically fetches full marks.)*

### Q5. Ch: Ratio Analysis — DuPont Analysis / ROE Decomposition (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** From the following data of ABC Ltd. for the year ended 31st March, 2026, compute the **Return on Equity (ROE)** using the **DuPont model** and explain what each component reveals.

| Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---:|
| Sales | 36,00,000 |
| Profit after Tax (PAT) | 3,60,000 |
| Total Assets | 24,00,000 |
| Shareholders' Equity | 12,00,000 |

**Solution:**

Under the DuPont system, Return on Equity is decomposed into three drivers:
**ROE = Net Profit Margin × Total Assets Turnover × Equity Multiplier**

**WN-1: Net Profit Margin** = PAT ÷ Sales = 3,60,000 ÷ 36,00,000 = **10%**

**WN-2: Total Assets Turnover** = Sales ÷ Total Assets = 36,00,000 ÷ 24,00,000 = **1.5 times**

**WN-3: Equity Multiplier (Financial Leverage)** = Total Assets ÷ Shareholders' Equity = 24,00,000 ÷ 12,00,000 = **2 times**

**Statement Showing DuPont Decomposition of ROE**

| Component | Value | What it measures |
|---|---:|---|
| Net Profit Margin | 10% | Profitability / operating efficiency |
| Total Assets Turnover | 1.5 times | Efficiency of asset utilisation |
| Equity Multiplier | 2 times | Extent of financial leverage |
| **Return on Equity** | **30%** | Return to shareholders |

**ROE = 10% × 1.5 × 2 = 30%**

**Cross-check (direct):** ROE = PAT ÷ Shareholders' Equity = 3,60,000 ÷ 12,00,000 = **30%** ✓

**Answer:** Return on Equity = **30%**. The DuPont breakdown shows that ABC Ltd.'s 30% return is driven by a healthy 10% profit margin, efficient asset usage (1.5 times) and a moderate use of leverage (equity multiplier of 2); management can raise ROE by improving margins, turning over assets faster, or (cautiously) increasing leverage.

*(Full-marks tip: Show the three-factor identity and prove that the product equals the direct ROE. State what each of the three levers represents — the interpretation carries marks in a DuPont question.)*

### Q6. Ch: Ratio Analysis — Solvency & Capital Gearing Ratios (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** From the following capital structure and profit data of Meridian Ltd., compute the (i) Debt–Equity Ratio, (ii) Capital Gearing Ratio, and (iii) Interest Coverage Ratio, and comment on the solvency of the company.

| Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---:|
| Equity Share Capital | 8,00,000 |
| Reserves & Surplus | 4,00,000 |
| 12% Preference Share Capital | 2,00,000 |
| 10% Debentures | 6,00,000 |
| EBIT | 3,60,000 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Classification of funds**
- Equity (Owners') Funds = Equity Capital 8,00,000 + Reserves 4,00,000 = **₹12,00,000**
- Long-term Debt = 10% Debentures = **₹6,00,000**
- Fixed-cost-bearing funds = Preference 2,00,000 + Debentures 6,00,000 = **₹8,00,000**
- Interest on debentures = 10% × 6,00,000 = **₹60,000**

**Statement Showing Solvency Ratios**

| Ratio | Formula | Computation | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Debt–Equity Ratio | Long-term Debt ÷ Equity Funds | 6,00,000 ÷ 12,00,000 | **0.5 : 1** |
| Capital Gearing Ratio | Fixed-cost Funds ÷ Equity Funds | 8,00,000 ÷ 12,00,000 | **0.67 : 1** |
| Interest Coverage Ratio | EBIT ÷ Interest | 3,60,000 ÷ 60,000 | **6 times** |

**Comment:** A debt–equity ratio of 0.5 : 1 is well within the conventional safe limit of 2 : 1, showing that owners' funds substantially exceed borrowed funds. The capital gearing ratio of 0.67 : 1 (fixed-cost funds being less than equity funds) means the company is **low geared**, and hence exposed to low financial risk. An interest coverage of 6 times indicates that EBIT is six times the interest obligation, giving lenders a comfortable margin of safety.

**Conclusion:** Meridian Ltd. is **comfortably solvent and low-geared**; it enjoys sufficient debt-servicing capacity and has the capacity to raise additional fixed-cost capital to benefit from trading on equity.

*(Full-marks tip: Distinguish clearly between debt–equity (only long-term debt) and capital gearing (all fixed-cost funds including preference). Stating whether the firm is "high geared" or "low geared" with the reason earns the comment mark.)*

### Q7. Ch: Ratio Analysis — Effect of Transactions on Current Ratio (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** The current ratio of Zenith Ltd. is **2 : 1**, its Current Assets being ₹4,00,000 and Current Liabilities ₹2,00,000. State, with reasons and revised ratio, the effect (increase / decrease / no change) of **each** of the following independent transactions on the current ratio:
(a) Purchased goods on credit for ₹50,000;
(b) Paid a trade creditor ₹50,000 in cash;
(c) Sold goods costing ₹40,000 for ₹50,000 on credit;
(d) Collected ₹60,000 in cash from a debtor;
(e) Purchased a fixed asset for ₹1,00,000 cash.

**Solution:**

Each transaction is analysed independently starting from CA ₹4,00,000 and CL ₹2,00,000 (ratio 2 : 1).

**Statement Showing Effect on Current Ratio**

| Txn | Revised Current Assets (₹) | Revised Current Liabilities (₹) | New Ratio | Effect |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|
| (a) Credit purchase 50,000 | 4,50,000 | 2,50,000 | 1.8 : 1 | **Decrease** |
| (b) Paid creditor 50,000 | 3,50,000 | 1,50,000 | 2.33 : 1 | **Increase** |
| (c) Credit sale (cost 40,000, price 50,000) | 4,10,000 | 2,00,000 | 2.05 : 1 | **Increase** |
| (d) Cash from debtor 60,000 | 4,00,000 | 2,00,000 | 2 : 1 | **No change** |
| (e) Fixed asset bought for cash 1,00,000 | 3,00,000 | 2,00,000 | 1.5 : 1 | **Decrease** |

**Reasons:**
- **(a)** Both CA (stock ↑) and CL (creditors ↑) rise by ₹50,000. When the existing ratio is more than 1 : 1, an equal addition to numerator and denominator brings the ratio closer to 1 — hence it **falls** to 1.8 : 1.
- **(b)** Both CA (cash ↓) and CL (creditors ↓) fall by ₹50,000. An equal reduction moves a ratio above 1 : 1 further away from 1 — hence it **rises** to 2.33 : 1.
- **(c)** Stock falls ₹40,000 but debtors rise ₹50,000; net CA increases by ₹10,000 (the ₹10,000 profit) while CL is unchanged — ratio **rises** to 2.05 : 1.
- **(d)** Cash increases and debtors decrease by the same amount; total CA and CL are unchanged — **no change**.
- **(e)** Cash (a current asset) falls ₹1,00,000 and is converted into a fixed (non-current) asset; CA falls while CL is unchanged — ratio **falls** to 1.5 : 1.

**Answer:** Effects — (a) Decrease; (b) Increase; (c) Increase; (d) No change; (e) Decrease.

*(Full-marks tip: Treat each transaction independently from the original figures, not cumulatively. The single most common error is item (a)/(b): remember that an equal addition to both sides of a >1 ratio *reduces* it, and an equal subtraction *increases* it.)*

### Q10. Ch: Cost of Capital — Concept and Significance (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Define the term "cost of capital" and explain its significance in financial decision-making.

**Answer:**
**Meaning:** The **cost of capital** is the minimum rate of return that a firm must earn on its investments so as to maintain the market value of its equity shares and to satisfy the expectations of the suppliers of funds. From the investors' angle it is the opportunity cost of funds, and from the firm's angle it is the required rate of return. It comprises three elements — the **return at zero risk level (r₀)**, a **premium for business risk (b)** and a **premium for financial risk (f)**, i.e., Cost of Capital = r₀ + b + f.

**Significance in financial decision-making:**
1. **Capital budgeting / investment decisions:** The cost of capital is used as the *discount rate* (hurdle rate) to evaluate projects under NPV, and as the *cut-off rate* under IRR. A project is accepted only if its return exceeds the cost of capital.
2. **Designing the capital structure:** A firm aims at a capital structure that minimises the overall (weighted average) cost of capital and thereby maximises the value of the firm; the cost of each source guides the financing mix.
3. **Evaluation of financial performance:** Actual returns earned on projects/capital employed are compared with the cost of capital to judge whether management is creating value for the shareholders.
4. **Other decisions:** It also aids decisions on dividend policy, working-capital management, lease-vs-buy and rights issues, wherever a required rate of return is needed.

**Conclusion:** Cost of capital is the **central benchmark rate** in corporate finance; almost every long-term financial decision — investment, financing and performance evaluation — is taken with reference to it.

*(Full-marks tip: Give the three-component build-up (r₀ + b + f) in the definition and then list at least three distinct decision areas. Merely writing "used in capital budgeting" without the other uses limits the marks.)*

### Q11. Ch: Cost of Capital — WACC under Existing vs. Target Capital Structure (Marks: 10) [Problem]
**Question:** Nova Ltd. is reviewing its cost of capital. The following information is available regarding its sources of finance:

| Source | Details |
|---|---|
| Equity shares | Current market price ₹40; expected dividend (D₁) ₹4 per share; expected growth 6% p.a. |
| Preference shares | 12% preference shares of ₹100 each, currently quoted at ₹96 (irredeemable) |
| Debentures | 10% debentures of ₹100 each, issued at par, irredeemable; tax rate 30% |

The company's **existing** capital structure is Equity : Preference : Debt = **50% : 20% : 30%**. It is considering a **target** capital structure of Equity : Preference : Debt = **45% : 15% : 40%**.

You are required to compute the **component costs** and the **Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC)** under (i) the existing structure and (ii) the target structure, and comment on the change.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Cost of Equity (Dividend Growth Model)**
Kₑ = (D₁ ÷ P₀) + g = (4 ÷ 40) + 0.06 = 0.10 + 0.06 = **16%**

**WN-2: Cost of Preference Capital (irredeemable)**
K_p = Preference Dividend ÷ Market Price = 12 ÷ 96 = **12.5%**

**WN-3: Cost of Debt (irredeemable, after tax)**
K_d = Interest × (1 − t) ÷ Net Proceeds = [10 × (1 − 0.30)] ÷ 100 = 7 ÷ 100 = **7%**

**WN-4: WACC — Existing Structure**

| Source | Weight | Cost (%) | Weight × Cost |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Equity | 0.50 | 16.0 | 8.000 |
| Preference | 0.20 | 12.5 | 2.500 |
| Debt | 0.30 | 7.0 | 2.100 |
| **WACC (existing)** | **1.00** | | **12.60%** |

**WN-5: WACC — Target Structure**

| Source | Weight | Cost (%) | Weight × Cost |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Equity | 0.45 | 16.0 | 7.200 |
| Preference | 0.15 | 12.5 | 1.875 |
| Debt | 0.40 | 7.0 | 2.800 |

### Q12. Ch: Cost of Capital — Effect of Change in Tax Rate on WACC (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** Orbit Ltd. has the following capital structure, which it intends to maintain:

| Source | Weight | Cost details |
|---|---:|---|
| Equity | 50% | Cost of equity 15% |
| Preference | 10% | Cost of preference 12% |
| Debt | 40% | 10% debentures issued at par (irredeemable) |

The Finance Minister has proposed to reduce the corporate tax rate from **30% to 25%**. You are required to compute the WACC **before** and **after** the change in the tax rate and explain the effect of the tax change on the company's cost of capital.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: After-tax cost of debt at each tax rate**
K_d = Interest × (1 − t) ÷ Net Proceeds (Net Proceeds = ₹100, issued at par)
- At t = 30%: K_d = 10 × (1 − 0.30) = **7.0%**
- At t = 25%: K_d = 10 × (1 − 0.25) = **7.5%**

(Costs of equity 15% and preference 12% are unaffected, as preference dividend carries no tax shield.)

**WN-2: WACC before change (tax 30%)**

| Source | Weight | Cost (%) | Weight × Cost |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Equity | 0.50 | 15.0 | 7.50 |
| Preference | 0.10 | 12.0 | 1.20 |
| Debt | 0.40 | 7.0 | 2.80 |
| **WACC (t = 30%)** | **1.00** | | **11.50%** |

**WN-3: WACC after change (tax 25%)**

| Source | Weight | Cost (%) | Weight × Cost |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Equity | 0.50 | 15.0 | 7.50 |
| Preference | 0.10 | 12.0 | 1.20 |
| Debt | 0.40 | 7.5 | 3.00 |
| **WACC (t = 25%)** | **1.00** | | **11.70%** |

**Effect of the tax change:** A reduction in the tax rate **reduces the tax shield** available on interest, so the *after-tax* cost of debt rises from 7.0% to 7.5%. Since only the debt component is affected, the WACC increases from **11.50% to 11.70%**, i.e., by **0.20%**. Thus, contrary to intuition, a *cut* in the corporate tax rate *increases* the weighted average cost of capital, because the interest tax benefit that makes debt cheap is diminished. (The cost of preference is unaffected, as preference dividends are paid out of post-tax profits and enjoy no tax shield.)

**Answer:** WACC at 30% tax = **11.50%**; WACC at 25% tax = **11.70%**; the tax cut raises WACC by **0.20%**.

*(Full-marks tip: Isolate the fact that only K_d changes with tax — do not re-adjust K_e or K_p. The key marks come from explaining the counter-intuitive result: a lower tax rate shrinks the interest tax shield and therefore raises the WACC.)*

### Q13. Ch: Cost of Capital — Cost of Equity: Earnings-Yield vs. Dividend-Growth (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** The following data relate to the equity shares of Vertex Ltd.:

| Particulars | Value |
|---|---|
| Earnings per Share (EPS) | ₹12 |
| Market Price per Share | ₹80 |
| Dividend Payout Ratio | 60% |
| Return on Equity (ROE) on retained funds | 20% |

Compute the cost of equity under (i) the **Earnings-Yield (Earnings-Price) method** and (ii) the **Dividend-Growth method**, and explain why the two figures differ.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Growth rate (g)**
Retention ratio (b) = 1 − Payout = 1 − 0.60 = 0.40
g = b × ROE = 0.40 × 20% = **8%**

**WN-2: Dividend figures**
Current dividend D₀ = 60% of EPS = 0.60 × 12 = ₹7.20
Expected dividend D₁ = D₀ (1 + g) = 7.20 × 1.08 = **₹7.776**

**(i) Earnings-Yield Method**
Kₑ = EPS ÷ Market Price = 12 ÷ 80 = **15%**

**(ii) Dividend-Growth Method**
Kₑ = (D₁ ÷ P₀) + g = (7.776 ÷ 80) + 0.08 = 0.0972 + 0.08 = 0.1772 = **17.72%**

**Statement Showing Cost of Equity under the Two Methods**

| Method | Formula | Result |
|---|---|---:|
| Earnings-Yield | EPS ÷ Market Price | **15.00%** |
| Dividend-Growth | (D₁ ÷ P₀) + g | **17.72%** |

**Reason for the difference:** The **earnings-yield method** assumes that EPS remains *constant* and that the *entire* earnings are distributed (i.e., there is no retention and hence no growth); it therefore measures only the current earnings return. The **dividend-growth method**, on the other hand, expressly recognises that a part of earnings (40%) is retained and reinvested at 20%, generating a growth of 8% in future dividends. Because the earnings-yield method ignores this growth, it understates the cost of equity relative to the growth model.

**Answer:** Cost of equity — Earnings-Yield method = **15%**; Dividend-Growth method = **17.72%**.

*(Full-marks tip: Derive g = b × ROE explicitly and grow the dividend to D₁ before applying the growth model. State clearly that the earnings-yield method presumes no growth/full payout — that explanation is where the difference marks are earned.)*

### Q14. Ch: Cost of Capital — Cost of Redeemable Debt (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** Titan Ltd. issued **9% debentures** of the face value of ₹100 each, at a discount, realising net proceeds of ₹96 per debenture. The debentures are **redeemable at a premium of 5%** at the end of **6 years**. The applicable tax rate is **30%**. Compute the cost of debt using the approximation (short-cut) method.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Relevant figures per debenture**
- Annual interest (I) = 9% of ₹100 = ₹9
- After-tax interest = I (1 − t) = 9 × (1 − 0.30) = ₹6.30
- Net Proceeds (NP) = ₹96
- Redemption Value (RV) = 100 + 5% premium = ₹105
- Life (n) = 6 years

**WN-2: Amortisation of discount/premium on redemption**
(RV − NP) ÷ n = (105 − 96) ÷ 6 = 9 ÷ 6 = **₹1.50 per year**

**Computation of Cost of Debt (K_d)**

K_d = [ I(1 − t) + (RV − NP)/n ] ÷ [ (RV + NP) ÷ 2 ]

K_d = [ 6.30 + 1.50 ] ÷ [ (105 + 96) ÷ 2 ]
    = 7.80 ÷ 100.50
    = 0.0776 = **7.76%**

**Answer:** The cost of redeemable debt = **7.76% (approx.)**.

*(Full-marks tip: Apply tax only to the interest (interest is tax-deductible); the redemption premium is *not* adjusted for tax in the standard short-cut formula. Use the *average* of RV and NP in the denominator — using face value or net proceeds alone is the usual error.)*

### Q15. Ch: Cost of Capital — Cost of Redeemable Preference & Retained Earnings (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** (a) Comet Ltd. issued **10% preference shares** of ₹100 each, realising ₹95 per share net of flotation costs, **redeemable at par at the end of 5 years**. Compute the cost of preference capital. (b) The company's cost of equity is **15%**. Assuming that shareholders' marginal tax on dividends is **10%** and brokerage/reinvestment cost is **2%**, compute the cost of retained earnings.

**Solution:**

**Part (a): Cost of Redeemable Preference Capital**

**WN-1: Relevant figures per share**
- Preference Dividend (PD) = 10% of ₹100 = ₹10
- Net Proceeds (NP) = ₹95; Redemption Value (RV) = ₹100; n = 5 years
- (RV − NP) ÷ n = (100 − 95) ÷ 5 = 5 ÷ 5 = ₹1.00

K_p = [ PD + (RV − NP)/n ] ÷ [ (RV + NP) ÷ 2 ]
    = [ 10 + 1.00 ] ÷ [ (100 + 95) ÷ 2 ]
    = 11.00 ÷ 97.50
    = 0.1128 = **11.28%**

(No tax adjustment is made, as preference dividend is not tax-deductible.)

**Part (b): Cost of Retained Earnings**

Retained earnings belong to the equity shareholders; had they received the amount as dividend, they would have paid personal tax and incurred brokerage to reinvest. Hence:

K_r = Kₑ × (1 − tₚ) × (1 − b)
    = 15% × (1 − 0.10) × (1 − 0.02)
    = 15% × 0.90 × 0.98
    = **13.23%**

*(Full-marks tip: For preference, remember there is NO tax adjustment (dividends are post-tax). For retained earnings, apply both the personal-tax factor (1 − tₚ) and the brokerage factor (1 − b) to the cost of equity; when no external factors are given, K_r simply equals Kₑ.)*

### Q17. Ch: Cost of Capital — Marginal vs. Average Cost & Book vs. Market Value Weights (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between (a) the **marginal cost of capital** and the **average cost of capital**, and (b) the use of **book value weights** and **market value weights** in computing the weighted average cost of capital.

**Answer:**

**(a) Marginal cost of capital vs. Average cost of capital**

| Basis | Average Cost of Capital | Marginal Cost of Capital |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | The weighted average cost of *all* sources of finance already employed by the firm | The weighted average cost of raising *one additional/incremental* rupee of new capital |
| Weights used | Based on the *existing* proportion of each source | Based on the proportion in which *fresh* funds are to be raised |
| Costs used | Historical costs of the existing sources | Current/incremental costs of the new funds |
| Use | Evaluating the firm's overall past financing | Investment decisions on new projects; it rises in steps as cheaper sources get exhausted |

**(b) Book value weights vs. Market value weights**

| Basis | Book Value Weights | Market Value Weights |
|---|---|---|
| Derived from | Values shown in the balance sheet (book figures) | Current market prices of the securities |
| Advantage | Simple, readily available and stable; convenient when target structure is set in book terms | Reflect the true economic/opportunity value of each source and the return actually expected by investors |
| Limitation | May be far removed from current reality; ignores market appreciation of equity | Fluctuate frequently and market prices may not always be available (e.g., for closely-held firms) |
| Theoretical preference | Less sound | **Theoretically superior** and generally recommended |

**Conclusion:** For appraising *new* investments the *marginal* cost using *market value* weights is theoretically the most appropriate, while the *average* cost with *book value* weights is used mainly for its simplicity and for evaluating the existing overall financing.

*(Full-marks tip: Answer both distinctions in separate tables. Stating that market-value weights are theoretically superior, and that marginal cost is the relevant rate for new-project decisions, are the specific points the examiner looks for.)*

| Structure | Debt (with rate) | Equity |

Compute the Return on Equity (ROE) under each structure and comment on the effect of financial leverage.

### Q21. Ch: Capital Structure & Leverage — Indifference Point Between Financing Plans (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** Orbit Ltd. needs ₹20,00,000 for a project. It is choosing between (i) Plan I: raising the entire amount through equity shares of ₹100 each, and (ii) Plan II: raising ₹10,00,000 through equity shares of ₹100 each and ₹10,00,000 through 12% debentures. The tax rate is 30%. 
(a) Compute the EBIT indifference point between the two plans. 
(b) State which plan is preferable if the expected EBIT is (i) ₹3,60,000 and (ii) ₹1,80,000.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Number of equity shares and interest under each plan**
- Plan I: 20,00,000 ÷ 100 = 20,000 shares; Interest = Nil.
- Plan II: 10,00,000 ÷ 100 = 10,000 shares; Interest = ₹10,00,000 × 12% = ₹1,20,000.

**WN-2: Indifference point formula**
At the indifference EBIT, EPS under both plans is equal:

(EBIT)(1 − t) ÷ N₁ = (EBIT − I)(1 − t) ÷ N₂

(EBIT)(0.70) ÷ 20,000 = (EBIT − 1,20,000)(0.70) ÷ 10,000

Cancelling (0.70) from both sides:

EBIT ÷ 20,000 = (EBIT − 1,20,000) ÷ 10,000

10,000 × EBIT = 20,000 × (EBIT − 1,20,000)

EBIT = 2EBIT − 2,40,000 ⟹ **EBIT = ₹2,40,000**

**WN-3: EPS at the indifference point (cross-check)**
- Plan I: (2,40,000 × 0.70) ÷ 20,000 = 1,68,000 ÷ 20,000 = ₹8.40
- Plan II: (2,40,000 − 1,20,000) × 0.70 ÷ 10,000 = 84,000 ÷ 10,000 = ₹8.40 ✓ (both equal, confirming the indifference point)

**(b) Decision at different EBIT levels:**
- **At EBIT ₹3,60,000** (above the indifference point of ₹2,40,000): the levered **Plan II** gives a higher EPS and is preferable — favourable leverage operates.
- **At EBIT ₹1,80,000** (below the indifference point): the all-equity **Plan I** gives a higher EPS and is preferable — debt would depress EPS (unfavourable leverage).

**Answer:** Indifference point EBIT = **₹2,40,000** (EPS ₹8.40 under both plans). Above it, prefer Plan II (debt); below it, prefer Plan I (equity).

*(Full-marks tip: cancelling (1 − t) is legitimate ONLY because neither plan has preference capital; if preference shares were present the term (1 − t) would not cancel. Always cross-check by recomputing EPS at the indifference EBIT, and state the decision rule relative to the indifference point.)*

- **DCL 2.22** — a 1% change in sales causes a 2.22% change in EPS; it measures total risk, combining operating and financial risk.

**Answer:** DOL = 1.67, DFL = 1.33, DCL = 2.22 times.

### Q23. Ch: Capital Structure & Leverage — Trading on Equity as a Double-Edged Sword (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the concept of "Trading on Equity" and why financial leverage is described as a "double-edged sword". State the condition under which financial leverage is favourable.

**Answer:** 
"Trading on Equity" refers to the practice of a company using fixed-cost sources of finance — such as debt (carrying interest) and preference share capital (carrying a fixed dividend) — in its capital structure with the objective of increasing the return to the equity shareholders. Because interest is a fixed, tax-deductible charge and preference dividend is a fixed appropriation, any surplus earned on the funds raised over and above their fixed cost accrues entirely to the equity shareholders, thereby magnifying their earnings per share (EPS). The higher the proportion of fixed-cost capital relative to equity, the greater the potential magnification — this magnifying effect is measured by the Degree of Financial Leverage (DFL = EBIT ÷ EBT).

Financial leverage is a **"double-edged sword"** because the magnification it produces works in both directions:
- When EBIT **rises**, EPS rises more than proportionately (favourable leverage), rewarding equity shareholders.
- When EBIT **falls**, EPS falls more than proportionately (unfavourable/adverse leverage), and the fixed interest burden must be met irrespective of profits, increasing the risk of financial distress and even default.

Thus the same lever that amplifies gains in good years amplifies losses in bad years.

**Condition for favourable leverage:** Financial leverage is favourable only when the rate of return on investment (ROI = EBIT ÷ Capital Employed) **exceeds** the fixed rate of cost of the debt (or preference) capital. If ROI equals the cost of debt, leverage is neutral; if ROI falls below the cost of debt, leverage becomes unfavourable and reduces EPS.

**Conclusion:** Trading on equity benefits equity shareholders only so long as the firm earns more on borrowed funds than it pays for them; beyond that, it magnifies losses and financial risk — hence a "double-edged sword".

*(Full-marks tip: examiners reward the explicit favourability condition (ROI > cost of debt) and the two-way magnification. Merely defining trading on equity without the "double-edged" mechanism caps the marks.)*

### Q27. Ch: Capital Budgeting — NPV versus IRR for Mutually Exclusive Projects (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** In the evaluation of mutually exclusive projects, the NPV and IRR methods may give conflicting rankings. Explain the reasons for such conflict and state, with justification, which method should be preferred.

**Answer:** 
The Net Present Value (NPV) method measures the absolute rupee addition to shareholders' wealth by discounting all cash flows at the firm's cost of capital and subtracting the initial outlay, whereas the Internal Rate of Return (IRR) is that discount rate at which the NPV of a project equals zero (i.e. PV of inflows = PV of outflows). For a single independent project both methods lead to the same accept/reject decision. However, for **mutually exclusive projects** (where accepting one means rejecting the other) the two methods can rank projects differently.

**Reasons for the conflict:**
1. **Difference in the scale/size of investment** — a project with a large outlay may have a lower IRR (a percentage measure) yet a higher NPV (an absolute measure), because IRR ignores the size of the investment while NPV captures the total wealth created.
2. **Difference in the timing/pattern of cash flows** — one project may generate larger inflows in the early years and another in the later years; because the two methods assume different reinvestment rates, they may rank such projects differently.
3. **Difference in the reinvestment rate assumption** — NPV implicitly assumes interim cash flows are reinvested at the **cost of capital**, whereas IRR assumes reinvestment at the **IRR itself**. The cost-of-capital assumption is the more realistic, since surplus funds can normally be reinvested only at the market/opportunity rate.
4. **Difference in project lives.**

| Basis | NPV method | IRR method |
|---|---|---|
| Result expressed as | Absolute amount (₹) | Percentage (%) |
| Reinvestment rate assumed | Cost of capital (realistic) | IRR (often unrealistic) |
| Objective served | Direct wealth maximisation | Rate of return |
| Multiple/no solutions | Never | Possible with non-conventional flows |

**Which is preferred:** The **NPV method should be preferred** for mutually exclusive projects. It is consistent with the goal of maximisation of shareholders' wealth (measures wealth in absolute terms), uses the more realistic reinvestment assumption, and always gives a unique answer even where cash flows are non-conventional (IRR may then give multiple or no rates).

**Conclusion:** Where NPV and IRR conflict on mutually exclusive projects, the project with the higher **NPV** should be selected.

*(Full-marks tip: the differentiating point examiners look for is the reinvestment-rate assumption and the wealth-maximisation objective. A comparison table plus a clear preference for NPV secures full marks; simply defining NPV and IRR is insufficient.)*

### Q32. Ch: Capital Structure & Leverage — Factors Determining Capital Structure (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State and briefly explain any six factors that a finance manager should consider while designing the capital structure of a company.

**Answer:** 
Capital structure refers to the composition or mix of long-term sources of finance — equity share capital, preference share capital, retained earnings and long-term debt — used by a company. An optimum capital structure is one that minimises the overall cost of capital and maximises the market value/wealth of the shareholders. The principal factors to be considered are:

1. **Cost of capital** — Debt is the cheapest source because interest is tax-deductible and lenders bear lower risk; a finance manager tilts towards debt to reduce the weighted average cost of capital, subject to risk limits.
2. **Financial risk / cost of financial distress** — Higher debt increases fixed interest obligations and the risk of default and insolvency; the structure must keep financial risk within a level the firm's earnings can service.
3. **Nature and stability of earnings (EBIT)** — A company with stable and predictable operating profits can safely carry more debt, whereas a firm with volatile earnings should rely more on equity.
4. **Control** — Issuing fresh equity dilutes the control of existing shareholders; debt and preference capital allow funds to be raised without diluting voting control, which promoters often prefer.
5. **Flexibility** — The structure should retain the capacity to raise additional funds in future (unused debt capacity) and to substitute one source for another as conditions change.
6. **Trading on equity / return to equity** — Where the return on investment exceeds the cost of debt, greater use of debt magnifies the EPS available to equity shareholders (favourable leverage).

Other relevant factors include the requirements of investors, capital-market conditions, legal/regulatory requirements, floatation costs, the size of the company and the period/purpose of financing.

**Conclusion:** The finance manager must balance these — chiefly the trade-off between the lower cost of debt and the higher financial risk it brings — to arrive at an optimum, wealth-maximising capital structure.

*(Full-marks tip: name the factor AND give a one-line reason for each — a bare list of headings earns only half credit. Anchoring the answer in the objective of "minimising cost of capital / maximising value" frames the six factors correctly.)*

### Q33. Ch: Capital Structure & Leverage — Financial Break-Even Point (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** Explain the meaning of the "Financial Break-Even Point". **Solution:**

**Meaning:** The Financial Break-Even Point is the level of Earnings Before Interest and Tax (EBIT) at which the company's earnings are just sufficient to meet all its fixed financial charges — interest on debt and the (pre-tax equivalent of) preference dividend — leaving **EPS equal to zero**. At this EBIT, the equity shareholders earn nothing but the firm exactly covers its fixed financing obligations.

**WN-1: Formula**
Financial Break-Even Point (EBIT) = Interest + [Preference dividend ÷ (1 − tax rate)]

The preference dividend is grossed up by dividing by (1 − t) because, unlike interest, it is paid out of **after-tax** profits; to state it in pre-tax (EBIT) terms it must be converted to its pre-tax equivalent.

**WN-2: Computation**
- Pre-tax equivalent of preference dividend = 1,40,000 ÷ (1 − 0.30) = 1,40,000 ÷ 0.70 = ₹2,00,000.
- Financial Break-Even EBIT = 3,00,000 + 2,00,000 = **₹5,00,000**

**Verification (EPS = 0 at this EBIT):**

| Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---|
| EBIT | 5,00,000 |
| Less: Interest | 3,00,000 |
| EBT | 2,00,000 |
| Less: Tax @ 30% | 60,000 |
| PAT | 1,40,000 |
| Less: Preference dividend | 1,40,000 |
| Earnings for equity shareholders | Nil ✓ |

**Answer:** Financial Break-Even Point = **₹5,00,000 of EBIT**. 
**Significance:** Any EBIT above ₹5,00,000 leaves a positive residual for equity shareholders (positive EPS); any EBIT below it results in a negative EPS. It thus marks the minimum operating profit the firm must earn to avoid eroding equity shareholders' returns and indicates the degree of financial risk.

*(Full-marks tip: the marks turn on grossing up the preference dividend by dividing by (1 − t) — students who add ₹1,40,000 directly to interest get ₹4,40,000 and lose marks. The verification table (showing EPS = 0) demonstrates understanding and secures full credit.)*

From the table, PVAF of 3.3333 lies between 15% (PVAF 3.3522) and 16% (PVAF 3.2743). Hence the IRR lies between 15% and 16%.

**WN-3: Interpolation**

IRR = LR + [(PVAF at LR − Required PVAF) ÷ (PVAF at LR − PVAF at HR)] × (HR − LR)

where LR = 15%, HR = 16%.

IRR = 15% + [(3.3522 − 3.3333) ÷ (3.3522 − 3.2743)] × (16% − 15%)

IRR = 15% + [0.0189 ÷ 0.0779] × 1% = 15% + (0.2426 × 1%) = 15% + 0.24% = **15.24%**

**WN-4: Cross-check (NPV at IRR ≈ 0)**
At 15%: NPV = (1,50,000 × 3.3522) − 5,00,000 = 5,02,830 − 5,00,000 = +₹2,830.
At 16%: NPV = (1,50,000 × 3.2743) − 5,00,000 = 4,91,145 − 5,00,000 = −₹8,855.
The NPV changes sign between 15% and 16%, confirming the IRR of ≈ 15.24% lies in this range.

**Answer:** IRR of the project = **15.24% (approx.)**. Since the IRR (15.24%) exceeds the cost of capital (12%), the project is financially viable and **should be accepted**.

*(Full-marks tip: for uniform (annuity) cash flows, jump straight to Required PVAF = Outlay ÷ Annual inflow and read the table — no trial NPVs needed to bracket the rate. Interpolate between the two rates whose factors straddle the required factor; getting the interpolation direction right (subtract required factor from the LOWER-rate factor in the numerator) is where marks are commonly lost.)*

### Q35. Ch: Risk Analysis in Capital Budgeting — Risk-Adjusted Discount Rate vs Certainty-Equivalent (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Both the Risk-Adjusted Discount Rate (RADR) approach and the Certainty-Equivalent (CE) approach are used to incorporate risk into capital budgeting appraisal, yet they adjust for risk at different places in the NPV equation. Explain each method, bring out the essential difference between them in a comparative statement, and state which is theoretically superior and why.

**Answer:**
Risk in capital budgeting is the variability of expected cash flows around their most likely value; both the RADR and the CE techniques convert a risky project into an acceptable NPV framework, but they operate on different elements of the discounting equation.

Under the **Risk-Adjusted Discount Rate (RADR)** approach, risk is incorporated in the **denominator** of the NPV equation by adding a risk premium to the risk-free rate. The discount rate used is therefore Risk-adjusted rate = Risk-free rate + Risk premium, and the greater the perceived risk of a project, the higher the premium and the higher the discount rate applied to its expected cash flows. The NPV is computed as the present value of *expected* (unadjusted) cash flows discounted at this inflated rate, less the initial outlay.

Under the **Certainty-Equivalent (CE)** approach, risk is incorporated in the **numerator** of the NPV equation. Each year's risky expected cash flow is multiplied by a certainty-equivalent coefficient (α), which lies between 0 and 1 and falls as risk rises (a certain cash flow carries α = 1). The resulting "certain" cash flows are then discounted at the **risk-free rate**, because the risk has already been removed from the cash flows themselves.

| Basis | Risk-Adjusted Discount Rate (RADR) | Certainty-Equivalent (CE) |
|---|---|---|
| Where risk is adjusted | Denominator (discount rate) | Numerator (cash flows) |
| Adjustment device | Risk premium added to risk-free rate | Certainty-equivalent coefficient (α) |
| Discount rate used | Risk-adjusted (inflated) rate | Risk-free rate |
| Treatment of risk over time | Assumes risk **increases** with time (compounds) | Risk each year specified **independently** by its own α |
| Practical ease | Simpler; single rate for whole project | Difficult; requires a separate α for each year |

The essential difference is that RADR lumps all risk into one discount rate and, by compounding that rate, **implicitly assumes risk increases uniformly with time**, whereas the CE method allows the analyst to specify a distinct risk adjustment for each year and keeps the time-value adjustment (risk-free rate) separate from the risk adjustment.

**Conclusion:** The Certainty-Equivalent approach is theoretically superior because it separates the two adjustments — time value (via the risk-free rate) and risk (via α) — and does not force the unrealistic assumption of ever-increasing risk over time; however, RADR is more widely used in practice owing to its computational simplicity.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the "numerator vs denominator" contrast and the point that RADR compounds risk over time. A common deduction is discounting CE cash flows at the risk-adjusted rate instead of the risk-free rate — that double-counts risk.)*

### Q37. Ch: Risk Analysis in Capital Budgeting — Decision-Tree Analysis of Sequential Decisions (Marks: 10) [Problem]
**Question:** Zenith Ltd. is considering the launch of a new product through a two-stage sequential decision. In **Stage 1 (now)** the company spends ₹5,00,000 on a pilot project. In **Stage 2 (end of Year 1)** the company may either invest ₹30,00,000 in a full-scale plant or abandon the project at no further cost. The full-scale plant will generate the following present values (measured as at the end of Year 1) of operating cash flows:

| Pilot outcome | High demand | Low demand |
|---|---|---|
| Good (prob 0.70) | ₹50,00,000 (prob 0.80) | ₹20,00,000 (prob 0.20) |
| Poor (prob 0.30) | ₹50,00,000 (prob 0.20) | ₹20,00,000 (prob 0.80) |

**Solution:**

The decision tree is solved by the **roll-back (fold-back) technique**: first evaluate the Stage-2 decision nodes at the end of Year 1, choose the better action at each, then discount the resulting expected values to the present and net off the Stage-1 pilot cost.

**WN-1: Expected PV of inflows at each Stage-2 node (as at end of Year 1)**

*Good-pilot node:* (50,00,000 × 0.80) + (20,00,000 × 0.20) = 40,00,000 + 4,00,000 = **₹44,00,000**
*Poor-pilot node:* (50,00,000 × 0.20) + (20,00,000 × 0.80) = 10,00,000 + 16,00,000 = **₹26,00,000**

**WN-2: Stage-2 decision — invest or abandon** (NPV at end of Year 1 = Expected PV of inflows − Plant outlay ₹30,00,000)

| Node | Expected PV inflows (₹) | Less: Outlay (₹) | NPV @ t=1 (₹) | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Good pilot | 44,00,000 | 30,00,000 | **+14,00,000** | Invest |
| Poor pilot | 26,00,000 | 30,00,000 | (4,00,000) | **Abandon → value 0** |

At the *Good* node, investing yields a positive NPV of ₹14,00,000, so the plant is built. At the *Poor* node, investing would give a negative NPV of ₹4,00,000, so the rational choice is to abandon, giving a value of nil.

**WN-3: Expected value at end of Year 1 (roll-back to Stage-1 chance node)**

Expected value @ t=1 = (0.70 × 14,00,000) + (0.30 × 0) = **₹9,80,000**

**WN-4: Present value and net position at t = 0**

PV @ t=0 = 9,80,000 ÷ 1.10 = ₹8,90,909
Less: Pilot cost (incurred now) = (₹5,00,000)
**Net Expected NPV of the whole programme = ₹3,90,909**

**Statement Showing Decision-Tree Valuation**

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Expected value at end of Year 1 (after optimal Stage-2 decisions) | 9,80,000 |
| Present value @ 10% (÷ 1.10) | 8,90,909 |
| Less: Stage-1 pilot cost (t = 0) | (5,00,000) |
| **Net Expected NPV** | **3,90,909** |

**Answer:** The pilot project should be **undertaken**, as the sequential programme yields a positive Net Expected NPV of approximately ₹3,90,909. The value of the pilot lies in the *option* it creates: if the pilot is Good the company invests and earns ₹14,00,000, and if it is Poor the company walks away with no further loss — this flexibility is precisely what the decision tree captures.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards (a) evaluating the Stage-2 nodes first and explicitly choosing "abandon" where NPV is negative, and (b) discounting the rolled-back expected value by only one year before deducting the current pilot cost. Discounting the pilot cost or failing to zero-out the abandoned branch loses marks.)*

### Q42. Ch: Risk Analysis in Capital Budgeting — Sensitivity, Scenario & Simulation Analysis (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between Sensitivity Analysis, Scenario Analysis and Simulation Analysis as techniques for handling risk in capital budgeting.

**Answer:**
These three techniques all seek to reveal how the outcome of a project (typically its NPV) responds to uncertainty in the input variables, but they differ in how many variables they flex at once and in the sophistication of the process.

**Sensitivity Analysis** examines the effect on the project's NPV of a change in **one variable at a time** — such as sales volume, selling price or cost — while holding all other variables constant. It answers the "what-if" question and identifies the variable to which NPV is most sensitive (the one whose small adverse change most damages the project), thereby signalling where forecasting effort should be concentrated. Its limitation is that it ignores the interdependence between variables and does not attach probabilities.

**Scenario Analysis** overcomes the "one-variable" limitation by examining the effect on NPV of a **consistent set of variables changing together** under a few discrete states — typically an optimistic (best-case), a most-likely and a pessimistic (worst-case) scenario. It thus captures the interrelationship among variables, but it considers only a handful of combinations rather than the full range of possibilities.

**Simulation Analysis** (the Monte-Carlo technique) assigns a **probability distribution to each input variable** and, using random sampling by computer, generates a large number of possible outcomes to produce a full probability distribution of NPV, along with its expected value and standard deviation. It is the most comprehensive of the three, handling many variables and their probabilities simultaneously, but it is data-intensive and computationally demanding.

| Basis | Sensitivity | Scenario | Simulation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Variables changed | One at a time | A set together (few discrete states) | All, via probability distributions |
| Probabilities used | No | Implicitly (per scenario) | Yes, explicitly |
| Output | Critical variable | NPV under best/likely/worst case | Full probability distribution of NPV |
| Complexity | Low | Moderate | High (computer-based) |

**Conclusion:** Sensitivity analysis pinpoints the critical variable, scenario analysis evaluates a few internally consistent combinations, and simulation analysis provides the complete probabilistic picture — moving from the simplest to the most rigorous treatment of risk.

*(Full-marks tip: The distinguishing thread is "how many variables flex at once and whether probabilities are attached." Mentioning that simulation = Monte-Carlo and yields a full distribution of NPV earns the top band.)*

### Q43. Ch: Risk Analysis in Capital Budgeting — Probability of Loss using Normal Distribution (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** A project has an Expected NPV of ₹30,000 with a Standard Deviation of ₹20,000, and its NPV is assumed to be normally distributed. Determine (i) the probability that the project will result in a negative NPV, (ii) the probability that the NPV will exceed ₹50,000, and (iii) the probability that the NPV will lie between ₹20,000 and ₹50,000. (Area under the standard normal curve: z = 0.5 → 0.1915; z = 1.0 → 0.3413; z = 1.5 → 0.4332.)

**Solution:**

The standardised value is Z = (X − Expected NPV) ÷ Standard Deviation, where X is the NPV of interest.

**WN-1: Probability of a negative NPV (X < 0)**

Z = (0 − 30,000) ÷ 20,000 = −1.5
Area between the mean and z = 1.5 is 0.4332, so the area in the left tail beyond z = −1.5 is 0.5000 − 0.4332 = **0.0668**.
∴ P(NPV < 0) = **0.0668, i.e. 6.68%.**

**WN-2: Probability that NPV exceeds ₹50,000 (X > 50,000)**

Z = (50,000 − 30,000) ÷ 20,000 = +1.0
Area between the mean and z = 1.0 is 0.3413, so the area in the right tail beyond z = +1.0 is 0.5000 − 0.3413 = **0.1587**.
∴ P(NPV > 50,000) = **0.1587, i.e. 15.87%.**

**WN-3: Probability that NPV lies between ₹20,000 and ₹50,000**

For X = 20,000: Z = (20,000 − 30,000) ÷ 20,000 = −0.5 → area 0.1915
For X = 50,000: Z = (50,000 − 30,000) ÷ 20,000 = +1.0 → area 0.3413
Since the two values lie on opposite sides of the mean, the probability = 0.1915 + 0.3413 = **0.5328, i.e. 53.28%.**

**Statement of Results**

| Event | z-value | Probability |
|---|---|---|
| NPV < 0 (loss) | −1.5 | 6.68% |
| NPV > ₹50,000 | +1.0 | 15.87% |
| ₹20,000 < NPV < ₹50,000 | −0.5 to +1.0 | 53.28% |

### Q46. Ch: Dividend Decisions — Modigliani–Miller (Dividend Irrelevance) Approach (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** Galaxy Ltd. currently has 1,00,000 equity shares selling at ₹100 each. Its cost of equity (Ke) is 10%. The company expects a net income of ₹10,00,000 for the year and plans new investments of ₹20,00,000. Using the Modigliani–Miller (MM) approach, show that the value of the firm remains unchanged whether the company (a) pays a dividend of ₹8 per share, or (b) pays no dividend. Assume no taxes and 100% external financing of the shortfall by fresh equity.

**Solution:**

Under the MM hypothesis, the year-end price is derived from P₀ = (P₁ + D₁) ÷ (1 + Ke), rearranged as **P₁ = P₀(1 + Ke) − D₁**. Any earnings not distributed as dividends reduce the amount of new shares that must be issued to fund the fixed investment; MM shows these two effects exactly offset, leaving firm value unchanged.

**WN-1: Year-end market price (P₁)** [P₀ = ₹100, Ke = 10%]

*Case (a) D = ₹8:* P₁ = 100(1.10) − 8 = 110 − 8 = **₹102**
*Case (b) D = ₹0:* P₁ = 100(1.10) − 0 = **₹110**

**WN-2: New shares to be issued** [Δn = (Investment − Retained earnings) ÷ P₁]

Retained earnings = Net income − Total dividend.

*Case (a):* Dividend paid = 8 × 1,00,000 = ₹8,00,000; Retained = 10,00,000 − 8,00,000 = ₹2,00,000
External finance needed = 20,00,000 − 2,00,000 = ₹18,00,000
Δn = 18,00,000 ÷ 102 = **17,647 shares (approx.)**

*Case (b):* Dividend paid = Nil; Retained = ₹10,00,000
External finance needed = 20,00,000 − 10,00,000 = ₹10,00,000
Δn = 10,00,000 ÷ 110 = **9,091 shares (approx.)**

**WN-3: Value of the firm** using nP₀ = [ (n + Δn)P₁ − I + E ] ÷ (1 + Ke), where I = investment, E = net income

*Case (a):*
(n + Δn)P₁ = (1,00,000 + 17,647) × 102 = 1,17,647 × 102 = ₹1,20,00,000
nP₀ = [1,20,00,000 − 20,00,000 + 10,00,000] ÷ 1.10 = 1,10,00,000 ÷ 1.10 = **₹1,00,00,000**

*Case (b):*
(n + Δn)P₁ = (1,00,000 + 9,091) × 110 = 1,09,091 × 110 = ₹1,20,00,000
nP₀ = [1,20,00,000 − 20,00,000 + 10,00,000] ÷ 1.10 = 1,10,00,000 ÷ 1.10 = **₹1,00,00,000**

**Statement Showing Value of the Firm under MM Approach**

| Particulars | Case (a) D = ₹8 | Case (b) D = 0 |
|---|---|---|
| Year-end price P₁ (₹) | 102 | 110 |
| New shares issued (Δn) | 17,647 | 9,091 |
| Total year-end value (n + Δn)P₁ (₹) | 1,20,00,000 | 1,20,00,000 |
| Less: New investment (₹) | (20,00,000) | (20,00,000) |
| Add: Net income (₹) | 10,00,000 | 10,00,000 |
| Value ÷ 1.10 = nP₀ (₹) | 1,00,00,000 | 1,00,00,000 |

**Answer:** The value of the firm is **₹1,00,00,000 in both cases**, proving the MM proposition that under perfect markets and no taxes, **dividend policy is irrelevant** to the value of the firm. When the firm pays a larger dividend it must issue more new shares, and the dilution of existing shareholders' claim exactly offsets the dividend they receive.

### Q47. Ch: Dividend Decisions — Walter vs Gordon vs MM on the Same Data (Marks: 10) [Application]

Compute the market price per share under (i) Walter's model and (ii) Gordon's model at payout ratios of 0%, 50% and 100%, and (iii) explain what the MM approach would conclude. Show how the **same data lead each model to a different conclusion** about dividend policy.

**Solution:**

The three models rest on different assumptions and therefore reach different conclusions even from identical data. Since r (12%) exceeds Ke (10%), Sirius Ltd. is a **growth firm**.

**WN-1: Walter's model** — P = [D + (r ÷ Ke)(E − D)] ÷ Ke; (r ÷ Ke = 1.20)

*Payout 0% (D = 0):* P = [0 + 1.20(12)] ÷ 0.10 = 14.40 ÷ 0.10 = **₹144.00**

*Payout 100% (D = 12):* P = [12 + 0] ÷ 0.10 = **₹120.00**

**WN-2: Gordon's model** — P = E(1 − b) ÷ (Ke − br); b = retention ratio

*Payout 100% (b = 0):* g = 0; P = 12 ÷ 0.10 = **₹120.00**
*Payout 50% (b = 0.50):* g = 0.50 × 0.12 = 0.06; P = 6 ÷ (0.10 − 0.06) = 6 ÷ 0.04 = **₹150.00**

|---|---|---|---|

| 50% | 132.00 | 150.00 | Unchanged (irrelevant) |
| 100% (full payout) | 120.00 | 120.00 | Unchanged (irrelevant) |

| Model | Conclusion for Sirius Ltd. (r > Ke) |

| Gordon | Price rises with retention but the model becomes invalid at very high retention (br ≥ Ke); it still points to **low payout / high retention** being value-maximising. |

### Q48. Ch: Dividend Decisions — Traditional (Graham–Dodd) View (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the Traditional (Graham–Dodd) approach to the dividend decision. A company has earnings per share of ₹10 and a multiplier (m) of 15. Using the Graham–Dodd formula, compute the market price per share when the dividend per share is (a) ₹6 and (b) ₹4, and comment on the result.

**Answer:**
The **Traditional view**, associated with Benjamin Graham and David Dodd, holds that dividend policy is **relevant** and that the stock market places considerably **more weight on dividends than on retained earnings**. According to this approach the value of a share is expressed as:

**P = m [ D + (E ÷ 3) ]**

where P = market price, m = multiplier, D = dividend per share and E = earnings per share. The formula assigns a weight of "1" to dividends but only "one-third" to earnings, so that ₹1 of dividend is valued about **four times** as heavily as ₹1 of retained earnings. The rationale is that investors prefer the certainty of current dividends to the uncertainty of future capital gains, and that liberal, stable dividends therefore command a higher price-earnings multiple.

**Computation (E = ₹10, m = 15):**

*Case (a) D = ₹6:* P = 15 [6 + (10 ÷ 3)] = 15 [6 + 3.333] = 15 × 9.333 = **₹140.00**
*Case (b) D = ₹4:* P = 15 [4 + (10 ÷ 3)] = 15 [4 + 3.333] = 15 × 7.333 = **₹110.00**

| Dividend per share (₹) | Market Price (₹) |
|---|---|
| 6 | 140.00 |
| 4 | 110.00 |

**Conclusion:** The market price falls from ₹140 to ₹110 when the dividend is cut from ₹6 to ₹4, even though earnings are unchanged at ₹10. This confirms the Traditional (Graham–Dodd) position that **higher dividends increase share value** because the market weights distributed earnings far more heavily than retained earnings — the opposite of the MM irrelevance conclusion.

*(Full-marks tip: Quote the exact formula P = m[D + E/3] and articulate the "dividends weighted four times retained earnings" logic. The examiner wants both the theoretical stance and the numerical demonstration that a higher dividend raises price.)*

### Q49. Ch: Dividend Decisions — Relevance vs Irrelevance Schools & Determinants of Dividend Policy (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** "There is a fundamental controversy in finance on whether dividend policy affects the value of a firm." In this light, distinguish between the dividend-relevance and dividend-irrelevance schools, naming the principal models under each, and briefly list the key determinants of a firm's dividend policy.

**Answer:**
The dividend controversy concerns whether the pattern of distribution of earnings between dividends and retention affects the market value of a firm. Two opposing schools of thought have developed.

**The Relevance school** argues that dividend policy **does** affect the value of the firm. Its principal models are **Walter's model** and **Gordon's model**, both of which show that the market price of a share depends on the payout/retention ratio and on the relationship between the firm's internal rate of return (r) and its cost of equity (Ke). For a growth firm (r > Ke) a low payout maximises value; for a declining firm (r < Ke) a high payout maximises value; for a normal firm (r = Ke) the payout is a matter of indifference. The **Traditional (Graham–Dodd) view** also belongs here, weighting dividends more heavily than retained earnings.

**The Irrelevance school** argues that dividend policy has **no** effect on the value of the firm. Its leading model is the **Modigliani–Miller (MM) hypothesis**, which demonstrates that, under perfect capital markets, rational investors, no taxes and no transaction costs, a shareholder is indifferent between a dividend and an equivalent capital gain, because any dividend paid is exactly offset by the fall in share price (or the dilution from new shares issued to fund investment). Value depends solely on the firm's earning power and investment policy.

| Basis | Relevance School | Irrelevance School |
|---|---|---|
| Effect of payout on value | Value depends on dividend policy | Value independent of dividend policy |
| Principal models | Walter, Gordon, Traditional (Graham–Dodd) | Modigliani–Miller (MM) |
| Key assumption | r and Ke can differ; investors value dividends | Perfect markets, no taxes, arbitrage |

**Key determinants of dividend policy** include: (i) stability and growth of earnings; (ii) availability of profitable investment (reinvestment) opportunities; (iii) liquidity/cash position; (iv) legal and contractual constraints (e.g., provisions of the Companies Act and loan covenants); (v) shareholders' preference and the firm's clientele; (vi) taxation considerations; and (vii) the desire to maintain a stable, predictable dividend record.

**Conclusion:** Walter, Gordon and the Traditional view uphold dividend **relevance**, while MM asserts **irrelevance**; in the real world, imperfections such as taxes, flotation costs and information signalling tend to make dividend policy matter, so firms in practice pursue a stable payout balanced against their reinvestment needs.

*(Full-marks tip: Correctly place each model in its school — a frequent error is calling MM a relevance model. Listing the determinants and closing with the real-world qualification rounds out the answer.)*

### Q54. Ch: Working Capital Management — Operating Cycle Concept (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the concept of the **Operating Cycle (Working Capital Cycle)** and state the factors that lengthen or shorten it.

**Answer:** The operating cycle is the length of time between the outlay of cash on raw materials and the recovery of cash from the sale of finished goods; it measures how long funds remain tied up in current assets before they are converted back into cash. In a manufacturing concern the gross operating cycle comprises four stages — the raw material storage period, the work-in-progress (conversion) period, the finished goods storage period and the debtors' collection period. From this gross cycle the credit period allowed by suppliers (creditors' payment period) is deducted to arrive at the **net operating cycle**, expressed as:

**Net Operating Cycle = (R + W + F + D) − C**, where R = raw material period, W = WIP period, F = finished goods period, D = debtors' period and C = creditors' period.

The longer the net operating cycle, the greater the amount of working capital blocked in the business, since the number of operating cycles per year (360 ÷ net operating cycle days) falls and each cycle must be financed for a longer stretch. The cycle is **lengthened** by holding large raw material and finished goods stocks, slow production processes, and liberal credit to customers; it is **shortened** by faster inventory turnover, quicker conversion, prompt collection from debtors, and by obtaining longer credit from suppliers.

**Conclusion:** The operating cycle is the pivot of working capital estimation — the shorter the net cycle, the lower the working capital a firm must finance, and reducing it is a primary goal of efficient working capital management.

*(Full-marks tip: State the exact formula and identify all five component periods. Common deductions: confusing the gross cycle with the net cycle and omitting the deduction of the creditors' period.)*

### Q56. Ch: Working Capital Management — Strategies for Financing Working Capital (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between **permanent and temporary (fluctuating) working capital**, and explain the three broad strategies a firm may follow to finance its working capital.

**Answer:** Working capital may be classified by its behaviour over time into two parts. **Permanent (fixed) working capital** is the minimum level of current assets that must be maintained continuously to carry on operations at any time of the year; it is a fixed, irreducible core that grows only with the long-term growth of the firm. **Temporary (fluctuating or variable) working capital** is the additional current-asset investment needed to meet seasonal or cyclical peaks in activity and is by nature short-lived.

A firm chooses among three financing strategies based on how it matches the maturity of its sources of finance with the life of the assets financed:

| Strategy | Approach | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Conservative** | Long-term funds finance the whole of fixed assets, all of permanent working capital and part of the temporary working capital; short-term funds are used only for the peak | Low risk of illiquidity but high cost, since costly long-term funds are idle during troughs; lower profitability |
| **Aggressive** | Long-term funds finance only fixed assets and possibly a part of permanent working capital; the rest of permanent and all temporary working capital is financed by cheaper short-term sources | High profitability but high risk of illiquidity, as the firm depends heavily on continuous short-term refinancing |
| **Matching / Hedging** | The maturity of the source is matched with the life of the asset — permanent working capital is financed by long-term funds and temporary working capital by short-term funds | Balances risk and return; each asset is self-liquidating from the funds that financed it |

**Conclusion:** The choice reflects the classic risk–return trade-off: the aggressive plan maximises return at the cost of liquidity, the conservative plan protects liquidity at the cost of return, and the matching approach seeks a moderate middle path.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the risk–return trade-off being tied to each strategy. Common deductions: describing the strategies without linking them to permanent vs temporary working capital.)*

### Q59. Ch: Working Capital Management — Dangers of Excess and Inadequate Working Capital (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** "Both excessive and inadequate working capital are harmful to a business." Discuss the dangers of each.

**Answer:** Working capital must be maintained at an optimum level, because both an excess and a shortage impair the health of a business. **Excessive working capital** means that funds are lying idle in current assets and earning no return; this depresses the return on capital employed and profitability, encourages overtrading and the accumulation of slow-moving or obsolete inventories, breeds inefficiency and laxity in receivables management, and may tempt management into speculative or imprudent investment of surplus funds. **Inadequate (shortage of) working capital** is equally damaging: the firm cannot pay its short-term obligations on time, loses cash discounts and its creditworthiness, is unable to buy raw materials in economical quantities or exploit favourable market opportunities, may be forced to operate below capacity, and in extreme cases faces insolvency despite being profitable on paper.

| Basis | Excess working capital | Inadequate working capital |
|---|---|---|
| Return | Idle funds lower ROI and profitability | Inability to earn on scarce funds; underutilised capacity |
| Operations | Overtrading, obsolete stocks, lax control | Cannot buy economically or meet demand |
| Solvency | May breed speculation | Risk of technical insolvency / loss of goodwill |

**Conclusion:** The objective of working capital management is to strike a balance — enough to sustain smooth operations and liquidity, but not so much that profitability is sacrificed to idle funds.

### Q60. Ch: Types of Financing & Financial Markets — Role of Financial Intermediaries (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the meaning of **financial intermediaries** and discuss the important functions they perform in the financial system.

**Answer:** Financial intermediaries are institutions — such as commercial banks, non-banking finance companies, mutual funds, insurance companies and pension funds — that stand between the ultimate savers (surplus units) and the ultimate borrowers (deficit units), channelling funds from those who have a surplus to those who need them for investment or consumption. Instead of savers and borrowers dealing directly, the intermediary raises funds from savers by issuing its own (indirect) claims and on-lends or invests those funds, thereby performing several economically valuable functions:

- **Maturity transformation / intermediation:** They accept short-term, liquid deposits from savers and convert them into the longer-term finance that borrowers require, reconciling the conflicting maturity preferences of the two sides.
- **Risk pooling and diversification:** By pooling the funds of many savers and lending to many borrowers, they spread and diversify risk, so that no single saver bears the loss of an individual default.
- **Reduction of transaction and search costs:** They bring lenders and borrowers together at low cost, sparing each saver the effort of finding a suitable borrower, appraising credit and drawing up contracts.
- **Provision of liquidity:** They allow savers to withdraw funds at short notice while the underlying loans remain long-term, giving liquidity that direct lending could not.
- **Convenience of denomination and expert management:** They aggregate small savings into large loans and deploy professional expertise in credit appraisal, monitoring and portfolio management, improving the allocation of capital in the economy.

**Conclusion:** Financial intermediaries make the financial system efficient by transforming maturities, pooling risk, cutting transaction costs and supplying liquidity, thereby mobilising savings and directing them to their most productive uses.

*(Full-marks tip: List and explain at least four distinct functions with a one-line rationale each. Common deductions: merely naming the institutions without explaining the transformation and risk-pooling roles.)*

### Q61. Ch: Types of Financing & Financial Markets — Features of Debentures and Bonds (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the essential features of a **debenture** as a source of long-term finance and briefly describe any three types of debentures/bonds.

**Answer:** A debenture is a written instrument acknowledging a debt of the company under its common seal, carrying a fixed rate of interest and a promise to repay the principal on a specified maturity date; the holder is a **creditor** of the company, not an owner. | **Convertible vs Non-convertible** | Convertible debentures may be converted into equity shares after a stated period; non-convertible are redeemed in cash |
| **Redeemable vs Irredeemable (perpetual)** | Redeemable debentures are repaid on maturity; irredeemable debentures have no fixed repayment date |

**Conclusion:** Debentures give a company access to long-term funds at a fixed cost without diluting control, but the fixed interest and repayment obligation increase its financial risk.

### Q62. Ch: Types of Financing & Financial Markets — Features and Types of Preference Shares (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the features of **preference share capital** and distinguish between cumulative and non-cumulative, and participating and non-participating preference shares.

**Answer:** Preference shares are shares that enjoy two preferential rights over equity shares — a **prior right to a fixed rate of dividend** and a **prior right to the return of capital** in the event of winding up. The dividend, though at a fixed rate, is an **appropriation of profit** (unlike debenture interest, which is a charge) and is therefore payable only when the company has distributable profits and the directors declare it. Preference shareholders ordinarily carry **no voting rights**, except on matters that directly affect their interests (such as a variation of their rights or when their dividend is in arrears). Preference capital thus occupies a middle position between debt and equity — it bears a fixed return like debt but represents ownership like equity. The main types are distinguished below:

| Basis | Type | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Right to arrears of dividend | **Cumulative** | Unpaid dividends of past years accumulate and must be paid before any equity dividend |
| | **Non-cumulative** | A dividend not declared in a year lapses and cannot be claimed later |
| Right to surplus profit | **Participating** | Entitled to a further share in surplus profits/assets after a stipulated equity dividend |
| | **Non-participating** | Entitled only to the fixed preference dividend, with no share in surplus |

**Conclusion:** Preference capital lets a company raise funds at a fixed cost without giving away control, while offering investors a safer, prior claim than equity but a lower one than debt.

### Q63. Ch: Types of Financing & Financial Markets — Hybrid Instruments: Convertible Debentures and Warrants (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** What is meant by **hybrid financing instruments**? Explain **convertible debentures** and **warrants**, and state how they combine the features of debt and equity.

**Answer:** Hybrid instruments are securities that blend the characteristics of both debt and equity, giving the investor the safety and fixed return of a creditor together with a potential participation in the ownership and upside of the company. Two important hybrids are convertible debentures and warrants.

A **convertible debenture** is a debenture that carries an option (or obligation) to be converted, wholly or partly, into equity shares of the issuing company after a specified period and at a pre-determined conversion ratio or price. Until conversion it behaves like ordinary debt — it pays a fixed rate of interest and ranks as a creditor's claim — but on conversion the holder becomes an equity shareholder and shares in the company's growth. It may be **fully convertible (FCD)**, where the entire face value is converted into shares, or **partly convertible (PCD)**, where only a portion converts and the balance remains a redeemable debenture. Because of the attached equity upside, a convertible debenture usually carries a **lower coupon** than a comparable non-convertible debenture, reducing the issuer's interest cost.

A **warrant** is a long-dated option issued by a company that entitles its holder to **buy a specified number of equity shares at a fixed exercise price within (or at the end of) a stated period**. It is typically attached as a "sweetener" to a debenture or bond issue to make it more attractive. A warrant carries no interest and no dividend and represents no claim on assets; it merely confers the right — not the obligation — to subscribe to equity later. Unlike a convertible, exercising a warrant requires the holder to **pay fresh cash** (the exercise price) and the debt does not disappear, so the company both retains the borrowing and receives new equity funds when warrants are exercised.

| Instrument | Debt feature | Equity feature |
|---|---|---|
| Convertible debenture | Fixed interest and creditor status until conversion | Converts into shares; holder gains ownership and upside |
| Warrant | Attached to debt as a sweetener; lowers effective cost | Right to buy shares at a fixed price for fresh cash |

**Conclusion:** Convertibles and warrants let a company raise cheaper finance today while offering investors an equity kicker, thereby lowering the current cost of funds at the price of potential future dilution.

*(Full-marks tip: The key distinction rewarded is that a convertible converts the existing debt into equity, whereas a warrant requires fresh cash and leaves the debt outstanding. Common deductions: treating warrants and convertibles as identical.)*

| Participants | Banks, RBI, financial institutions, large corporates | Investors, companies, financial institutions, stock exchanges |

**Conclusion:** The money market serves the short-term liquidity requirements of the economy, whereas the capital market mobilises long-term savings for investment; together they complete the financial system.

Its **straight value of ₹886.44** provides a floor, so the investor's **downside risk is ₹163.56 (18.45%)** — the most that could be lost on the conversion feature if the share does not appreciate. The instrument thus offers equity upside with limited downside.

### Q67. Ch: Types of Financing & Financial Markets — Venture Capital and Seed Financing (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain **venture capital financing** and state its main features. How does **seed capital** differ from later-stage venture funding?

**Answer:** Venture capital is a form of **long-term risk (equity) finance** provided to new, unlisted, high-risk but high-growth-potential enterprises — typically technology-based or innovative start-ups — that are unable to raise funds from conventional sources such as banks because they lack a track record or tangible security. The venture capitalist supplies not merely money but also managerial guidance, technical know-how and business contacts, and expects to earn its reward mainly through **capital gains** on the eventual sale of its stake (through a public issue or trade sale) rather than through regular dividends. Its principal features are: it is essentially **equity or quasi-equity participation** in the venture; it is **long-term** in horizon; it is **high-risk, high-return** finance; the investor takes an **active hands-on role** in the venture's management; and the investment is made in **stages**, with further funding released as the business meets its milestones.

**Seed capital** is the **earliest stage** of venture financing, provided at the idea or concept stage — to develop a prototype, conduct market research or establish the initial feasibility of the business — even before commercial production begins. It is the smallest in amount and the **highest in risk**, since the enterprise is still unproven. **Later-stage** venture funding (start-up, expansion or mezzanine finance) is provided once the concept has been validated and the business is being commercialised or scaled; the amounts are larger and the risk somewhat lower, being supported by demonstrated performance.

**Conclusion:** Venture capital bridges the funding gap for innovative start-ups that cannot access ordinary finance, with seed capital nurturing the idea at its riskiest, earliest stage and later rounds financing proven growth.

### Q68. Ch: Types of Financing & Financial Markets — Valuation of Equity Warrants (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** APEX Ltd. has issued equity **warrants**, each entitling the holder to purchase **3 equity shares** at an exercise price of **₹50 per share** at any time during the next three years. The current market price of the equity share is **₹65** and each warrant is currently trading in the market at **₹60**.
(i) Compute the theoretical (intrinsic) value of one warrant.
(ii) Compute the warrant premium.
(iii) State what the intrinsic value would be if the share price fell to ₹45, and explain why.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Theoretical (intrinsic) value of the warrant**
Intrinsic value = (Current share price − Exercise price) × Number of shares per warrant
= (65 − 50) × 3 = 15 × 3 = **₹45**.

**WN-2: Warrant premium**
Warrant premium = Market price of warrant − Intrinsic value = 60 − 45 = **₹15**.
As a percentage of intrinsic value = 15 ÷ 45 = **33.33%**.
This premium reflects the time value of the warrant — the market's expectation that the share price may rise further during the remaining three-year exercise period.

**WN-3: Intrinsic value if share price falls to ₹45**
Intrinsic value = (45 − 50) × 3 = (−5) × 3 = −₹15. Since a warrant merely confers a **right, not an obligation**, to buy, its holder would simply not exercise it when the exercise price (₹50) exceeds the market price (₹45). The intrinsic value therefore **cannot be negative and is taken as ₹0** — the warrant is "out of the money", though it may still command a positive market price for its remaining time value.

**Statement Summarising the Warrant Valuation**

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Intrinsic value at share price ₹65 (WN-1) | 45.00 |
| Warrant premium (WN-2) | 15.00 (33.33%) |
| Intrinsic value at share price ₹45 (WN-3) | 0.00 (out of the money) |

**Answer:** The theoretical value of one warrant is **₹45** and it trades at a **premium of ₹15 (33.33%)** representing its time value. If the share price falls to ₹45 the warrant's intrinsic value becomes **nil**, because the holder will not exercise a right to pay ₹50 for a share worth ₹45. This illustrates the built-in **gearing (leverage)** of warrants — they magnify gains when the share rises above the exercise price while limiting the loss on the right itself to zero.

### Q69. Ch: Introduction to Strategic Management — Characteristics of Strategic Management (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** "Strategic management is not a box of tricks or a bundle of techniques; it is analytical thinking and commitment of resources to action." In the light of this statement, explain the essential characteristics that distinguish strategic management from routine operational management.

**Answer:**
Strategic management is the process of formulating, implementing and evaluating cross-functional decisions that enable an organisation to achieve its long-term objectives. It integrates the intent of the top management with the environment and the resource position of the firm. Its essential characteristics are as follows:

1. **Top-management orientation** — Strategic management is primarily the responsibility of the top level of management (the board and senior executives), because only they possess the perspective to view the organisation as a whole and to commit resources over the long term.
2. **Future orientation with present action** — It deals with the future course of the organisation, yet it commits present resources to future action. It is anticipatory rather than reactive, requiring the firm to prepare for opportunities and threats before they crystallise.
3. **Organisation-wide (holistic) scope** — Strategic decisions cut across functions and departments; they are not confined to any single function such as marketing or finance. They aim at the total effectiveness of the enterprise rather than the efficiency of a part.
4. **Environment-dependence** — Strategy establishes a fit between the organisation's internal capabilities and the opportunities and threats of its external environment. It is therefore continuously influenced by changes in the environment.
5. **Resource-commitment and irreversibility** — Strategic decisions typically involve the commitment of substantial resources and are difficult and costly to reverse, which is why they demand careful analysis.
6. **Complexity and analytical rigour** — Strategic management is analytical thinking. It does not offer a ready-made prescription; it requires judgement, weighing of alternatives and creativity, since the situations it addresses are novel and unstructured.

**Conclusion:** Strategic management is distinguished by its top-management orientation, future focus, organisation-wide scope, environmental sensitivity and irreversible resource commitments; it is an analytical, thinking discipline rather than a mechanical technique.

### Q70. Ch: Introduction to Strategic Management — Benefits and Limitations of Strategic Management (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** A newly appointed CEO argues that "strategic management guarantees success and removes uncertainty from business." Critically evaluate this statement by discussing both the benefits and the limitations of strategic management.

**Answer:**
The CEO's statement is only partly correct. Strategic management confers important benefits, but it neither guarantees success nor removes uncertainty. Both dimensions must be examined.

**Benefits of strategic management:**
1. It provides the organisation with a clear sense of **direction and vision**, so that effort is not dissipated.
2. It helps the firm to be **proactive rather than reactive** in shaping its own future, allowing it to influence events instead of merely responding to them.
3. It enables the firm to **anticipate and prepare for changes** in the environment, converting threats into opportunities.
4. It provides a **framework for decision-making** at all levels, so that day-to-day decisions are aligned with long-term goals.
5. It improves **coordination and control** by giving a common set of objectives against which performance can be measured.

**Limitations of strategic management:**
1. The **environment is uncertain and turbulent**; strategy is drawn up under conditions of imperfect information, so it can be invalidated by unforeseen change. It does not remove uncertainty.
2. It is a **costly and time-consuming** process that demands managerial effort and resources.
3. It is **difficult to implement**; a good strategy poorly executed yields poor results, so formulation alone does not ensure success.
4. Future events cannot be **predicted with certainty**; strategy is based on forecasts that may prove wrong.
5. There may be **resistance to change** within the organisation, and rigidity in following a plan can prevent the firm from seizing emergent opportunities.

**Conclusion:** Strategic management gives direction and improves preparedness, but because the environment is uncertain and implementation is demanding, it can improve the *probability* of success without ever *guaranteeing* it; the CEO's claim is therefore an overstatement.

### Q71. Ch: Introduction to Strategic Management — Levels of Strategy: Corporate, Business and Functional (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** "Sundara Group" operates three businesses — a dairy business, a packaged-snacks business and a logistics business — under one holding company. The Managing Director wants to know at which level each of the following decisions belongs and how the three levels of strategy connect:
(i) Deciding to exit the loss-making logistics business and invest the proceeds in the dairy business.
(ii) In the snacks business, choosing to compete as the lowest-cost producer of regional snacks.
(iii) In the dairy business, the finance head deciding the mix of debt and equity for a new plant.
Identify the level of strategy for each decision and explain how the three levels are linked.

**Answer:**
Strategy in a multi-business firm is formulated at three hierarchical levels — **corporate, business (SBU) and functional** — each with a distinct scope and decision-maker.

| Level | Who formulates it | Central question it answers | Application to Sundara Group |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Corporate level** | Board / holding-company top management | "In which businesses should we compete?" — scope, portfolio and resource allocation among businesses | Decision (i): exiting logistics and re-allocating funds to dairy is a **corporate-level** portfolio/restructuring decision |
| **Business (SBU) level** | Head of the individual business unit | "How should we compete in this particular business?" — building competitive advantage in one industry | Decision (ii): competing as the lowest-cost producer of regional snacks is a **business-level** (cost-leadership) strategy |
| **Functional level** | Functional heads (finance, marketing, operations, HR) | "How do we support the business strategy through each function?" | Decision (iii): the debt-equity mix for the dairy plant is a **functional-level** (financial) strategy |

Corporate strategy sets the overall direction and decides the portfolio of businesses and the allocation of resources to each. Within that portfolio, each business unit crafts a **business strategy** to win in its own market, consistent with the corporate direction. Each functional strategy is then designed to **support and implement** the business strategy of its unit. Thus objectives cascade downwards (corporate → business → functional) as ends-means chains, while information and capabilities flow upwards to inform corporate choices. Consistency across the levels ensures that functional actions add up to competitive advantage at the business level, which in turn serves the corporate goal.

**Conclusion:** Decision (i) is corporate, decision (ii) is business, and decision (iii) is functional; the levels are linked as a nested hierarchy in which each higher level provides the framework and resources within which the next lower level operates.

### Q72. Ch: Introduction to Strategic Management — Strategic Intent: Vision, Mission, Goals and Objectives (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between "Vision" and "Mission" of an organisation, and explain how goals and objectives translate strategic intent into action.

**Answer:**
Strategic intent gives the organisation a sense of purpose and is expressed through a hierarchy of statements — vision, mission, goals and objectives.

| Basis | Vision | Mission |
|---|---|---|
| **Meaning** | An aspirational description of what the organisation would like to *become* in the future | A statement of the organisation's *present* business — what it does, for whom, and why it exists |
| **Time horizon** | Long-term, forward-looking | Present-oriented (current purpose) |
| **Question answered** | "What do we want to become?" | "What is our business and whom do we serve?" |
| **Nature** | Inspirational, broad and directional | Operational, defines scope and boundaries |

**Role of goals and objectives:** A vision and mission are broad; they must be converted into more concrete milestones to guide action.
- **Goals** state, in general terms, what the organisation intends to achieve to fulfil its mission; they give direction to different units.
- **Objectives** are the specific, measurable and time-bound ends that translate goals into performance targets. They make strategy operational by specifying *how much* is to be achieved and *by when*, and they serve as standards for evaluation and control.

Thus strategic intent flows downward as: **Vision → Mission → Goals → Objectives**, moving from the abstract and aspirational to the concrete and measurable.

**Conclusion:** Vision defines what the firm wants to become while mission defines what it presently is and does; goals and objectives convert this intent into directional and measurable targets, thereby translating purpose into action.

### Q73. Ch: Introduction to Strategic Management — Nature of Strategic Decisions (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** The proprietor of a mid-sized firm remarks: "I take dozens of decisions every day; how is a strategic decision any different from an ordinary operational one?" Explain, with reasons, the characteristics that make a decision strategic, illustrating each with an example.

**Answer:**
Not every decision taken in a firm is a strategic decision. Strategic decisions differ from routine operational decisions in their scope, impact and reversibility. Their distinguishing characteristics are:

1. **They are concerned with the long-term direction of the organisation.** *Example:* Deciding to enter the electric-vehicle segment shapes the firm for a decade, unlike deciding this week's production schedule.
2. **They involve a major commitment of resources.** *Example:* Setting up a new manufacturing plant commits large capital, whereas reordering raw material is minor and routine.
3. **They are complex and taken under uncertainty.** They deal with novel, unstructured situations for which no precedent or standard procedure exists, so they demand judgement rather than rules.
4. **They are organisation-wide in impact.** A strategic decision affects several functions and units simultaneously; an operational decision usually affects only one function.
5. **They are difficult and costly to reverse.** Once resources are committed to a strategic course (e.g., an acquisition), withdrawing is expensive; operational decisions can be corrected quickly and cheaply.
6. **They are taken at the top level of management**, because they require an overall perspective and the authority to commit the organisation.

By contrast, **operational decisions** are short-term, routine, repetitive, confined to a function, based on established procedures, easily reversible, and taken at lower/middle levels.

**Conclusion:** A decision is strategic when it is long-term, resource-intensive, complex, organisation-wide, difficult to reverse and taken by top management; the proprietor's daily choices are largely operational because they lack these features.

### Q74. Ch: Introduction to Strategic Management — Strategy as a Response to a Dynamic Environment (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the meaning of "strategy" and discuss why an organisation cannot afford to keep the same strategy unchanged over a long period.

**Answer:**
**Meaning of strategy:** A strategy is the *game plan* that the management of a firm adopts to achieve its objectives — to stake out a market position, conduct its operations, attract and satisfy customers, compete successfully and achieve organisational goals. It provides an integrated and coordinated set of commitments and actions designed to exploit core competencies and gain a competitive advantage. A strategy is partly **proactive** (deliberate and planned) and partly **reactive** (adaptive responses to fresh developments), so it is a blend of intended and emergent action.

**Why strategy must change over time:**
1. **The environment is dynamic** — technology, customer preferences, competitors, government policy and economic conditions change continuously, so a strategy suited to yesterday's conditions may fail today.
2. **Competitive erosion** — rivals imitate a successful strategy, eroding the advantage it once conferred and forcing the firm to renew it.
3. **Internal changes** — the firm's own resources, capabilities and product portfolio evolve, requiring realignment of strategy.
4. **Life-cycle shifts** — as products and industries move through their life cycle, the appropriate strategy (growth, stability, harvest) changes.

Because of this, strategy is not a one-time statement but a continually evolving response; management must periodically review and reformulate it.

**Conclusion:** Strategy is the firm's game plan for achieving its goals; since the environment and the firm itself are constantly changing, strategy must be reviewed and adapted continually rather than fixed once and for all.

### Q77. Ch: External Environment Analysis — Porter's Five Forces (Marks: 8) [Application]
**Question:** "AquaPure" is evaluating whether to enter the packaged drinking-water industry, which has several large national brands, many small regional bottlers, easily available bottling technology, and buyers (retailers and consumers) who see little difference between brands. Using Porter's Five Forces model, analyse the attractiveness of this industry and advise AquaPure.

**Answer:**
Porter's Five Forces model holds that the profitability (attractiveness) of an industry is determined by five competitive forces. The stronger the forces, the lower the profit potential. Applying the model to the packaged drinking-water industry:

1. **Threat of new entrants — HIGH.** Bottling technology is easily available and capital requirements are modest, so barriers to entry are low. New regional bottlers can enter readily, which limits the profitability of incumbents.
2. **Bargaining power of buyers — HIGH.** Consumers and retailers perceive little difference between brands, and switching costs are negligible, so buyers can easily switch to whichever brand is cheaper. This depresses prices.
3. **Bargaining power of suppliers — LOW to MODERATE.** The main inputs — water, PET bottles and packaging — are widely available from many suppliers, so suppliers have little bargaining power over bottlers.
4. **Threat of substitutes — MODERATE to HIGH.** Boiled/filtered tap water, household water purifiers and other beverages act as substitutes for packaged water, placing a ceiling on prices.
5. **Rivalry among existing competitors — HIGH.** The presence of several large national brands and many small regional bottlers, selling an essentially undifferentiated (commodity) product, produces intense price competition and thin margins.

**Overall assessment:** Four of the five forces are strong (easy entry, powerful buyers, real substitutes and intense rivalry) and only supplier power is weak. On balance, the packaged drinking-water industry is **structurally unattractive** — it offers low profit potential because competition is fierce and products are commoditised.

**Advice to AquaPure:** Entry is risky. If AquaPure still wishes to enter, it should not compete as an undifferentiated commodity player. It should seek to *soften* the adverse forces — for example, by **differentiating** its product (mineral enrichment, premium positioning, distinctive packaging) to reduce buyer power and rivalry, or by pursuing **cost leadership** through scale to withstand price competition, and by building brand loyalty and a strong distribution network to raise entry barriers for later entrants.

**Conclusion:** The five-forces analysis shows a highly competitive, low-margin industry; AquaPure should enter only with a clear differentiation or cost-leadership strategy that mitigates the strong forces, and not as an undifferentiated participant.

*(Full-marks tip: Each force must be *rated* (high/low) *with a reason drawn from the case facts* and the analysis must conclude with an attractiveness verdict plus advice; simply defining the five forces without applying them to the case scores poorly.)*

### Q80. Ch: Internal Environment Analysis — SWOT Synthesis and the TOWS Matrix (Marks: 8) [Application]
**Question:** "Vaayu Appliances", a home-appliances maker, has compiled the following: strong R&D and a loyal dealer network (strengths); high dependence on a single product category and weak online presence (weaknesses); rising demand for energy-efficient appliances and growth of e-commerce (opportunities); aggressive Chinese imports and rising input costs (threats). Explain the purpose of a SWOT analysis and, using the TOWS matrix, generate one strategy under each of the four combinations for Vaayu.

**Answer:**
**Purpose of SWOT analysis:** SWOT analysis is a technique for synthesising the findings of the internal and external environmental appraisals. It identifies the organisation's internal **Strengths (S)** and **Weaknesses (W)** and the external **Opportunities (O)** and **Threats (T)**. Its purpose is to match internal capabilities with external conditions so that the firm can build on its strengths, correct its weaknesses, seize opportunities and defend against threats — thereby providing the logical basis for strategy formulation.

**Limitation of a plain SWOT:** A simple SWOT only *lists* the four elements; it does not by itself generate strategies. The **TOWS matrix** overcomes this by systematically pairing the internal factors with the external factors to yield four combinations of strategies.

**TOWS matrix for Vaayu Appliances:**

| | **Opportunities (O)**: energy-efficient demand; e-commerce growth | **Threats (T)**: Chinese imports; rising input costs |
|---|---|---|
| **Strengths (S)**: strong R&D; loyal dealers | **SO (Maxi-Maxi):** Use strong R&D to develop energy-efficient appliances and ride the rising demand — an aggressive growth strategy | **ST (Maxi-Mini):** Leverage the loyal dealer network and R&D-based product differentiation to defend market share against cheap Chinese imports |
| **Weaknesses (W)**: single-category dependence; weak online presence | **WO (Mini-Maxi):** Build an online sales channel to capture e-commerce growth and diversify the product range, converting weaknesses into opportunity capture | **WT (Mini-Mini):** Reduce single-product dependence and control costs (defensive/retrenchment) to survive import competition and cost pressure |

- **SO strategies** use strengths to exploit opportunities.
- **ST strategies** use strengths to counter threats.
- **WO strategies** overcome weaknesses by taking advantage of opportunities.
- **WT strategies** minimise both weaknesses and threats (defensive posture).

**Conclusion:** SWOT synthesises the environmental appraisal, and the TOWS matrix converts it into action by pairing factors; for Vaayu the priority is the SO strategy of R&D-led energy-efficient products, supported by a WO move into e-commerce, an ST defence through dealer loyalty and differentiation, and a WT plan to diversify and cut costs.

### Q81. Ch: Internal Environment Analysis — Value Chain Analysis (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** The operations head of a garment exporter wants to identify where the firm creates value and where it can cut costs or differentiate. Explain the concept of the value chain and describe its primary and support activities, indicating how the analysis helps in building competitive advantage.

**Answer:**
**Concept:** The value chain, developed by Michael Porter, is the set of interlinked value-creating activities that a firm performs to design, produce, market, deliver and support its product. Each activity adds value (and incurs cost); the firm earns a margin to the extent that the total value it creates exceeds the total cost of performing these activities. Analysing the chain helps a firm locate the sources of cost and of differentiation, and hence of competitive advantage.

The activities are grouped into **primary activities** and **support activities**:

**Primary activities** (directly involved in creating and delivering the product):
1. **Inbound logistics** — receiving, storing and handling input materials (e.g., sourcing and warehousing fabric).
2. **Operations** — transforming inputs into the finished product (e.g., cutting, stitching, finishing of garments).
3. **Outbound logistics** — storing and distributing the finished product to buyers (e.g., packing and shipping export consignments).
4. **Marketing and sales** — activities to inform buyers and induce purchase (e.g., attending trade fairs, building buyer relationships).
5. **Service** — after-sale support that maintains the value of the product (e.g., handling buyer complaints and replacements).

**Support activities** (which underpin the primary activities):
1. **Firm infrastructure** — general management, planning, finance and quality systems.
2. **Human resource management** — recruiting, training and retaining skilled tailors and staff.
3. **Technology development** — improving processes, design (CAD) and machinery.
4. **Procurement** — purchasing of inputs, machinery and services.

**How it builds competitive advantage:** By examining each activity and the *linkages* between them, the firm can (a) identify activities where costs can be reduced to pursue **cost leadership**, and (b) identify activities that can be enhanced to create uniqueness valued by buyers to pursue **differentiation**. It can also decide which activities to perform in-house and which to outsource.

**Conclusion:** The value chain disaggregates the firm into primary and support activities; by analysing the cost and differentiation potential of each activity and their linkages, the garment exporter can pinpoint where to cut cost and where to differentiate, thereby strengthening its competitive advantage.

*(Full-marks tip: Both the five primary and four support activities must be named and briefly explained; full marks also require the closing link to cost-leadership/differentiation, which many students omit.)*

### Q82. Ch: Internal Environment Analysis — Sustainability of Competitive Advantage (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Two restaurants in the same city both earn above-average profits. Restaurant A's advantage rests on a prime location on a long lease and a secret family recipe; Restaurant B's advantage rests only on lower prices achieved by paying below-market wages. As a strategy adviser, explain what makes a competitive advantage *sustainable*, and assess whose advantage is more likely to endure.

**Answer:**
**Meaning:** A competitive advantage is the edge that allows a firm to outperform its rivals — to create more value or offer lower prices. It is **sustainable** when it cannot be easily eroded by competitors' actions over time. A momentary advantage that rivals quickly imitate is not sustainable.

**Conditions for sustainability:** A competitive advantage is durable when the resource or capability underlying it is:
1. **Valuable** — it enables the firm to exploit opportunities or neutralise threats and to deliver value to customers.
2. **Rare** — it is possessed by few, if any, competitors, so it confers distinctiveness.
3. **Difficult (costly) to imitate** — competitors cannot easily copy it, whether because of unique history, causal ambiguity, or social/organisational complexity.
4. **Non-substitutable and durable** — there is no equivalent alternative that rivals can use to achieve the same benefit, and the resource does not quickly depreciate.

An advantage built on such **valuable, rare, inimitable and non-substitutable** resources tends to endure; one based on easily copied or ethically/legally fragile practices does not.

**Assessment of the two restaurants:**
- **Restaurant A** rests on a *prime location secured on a long lease* (rare and not easily replicated by rivals) and a *secret recipe* (valuable, rare and hard to imitate). Both resources are difficult for competitors to duplicate, so A's advantage is **more likely to be sustainable**.
- **Restaurant B** rests only on low prices achieved by paying below-market wages. This is *easily imitated* (any rival can cut prices) and is *fragile and unsustainable* — underpaid staff will leave, wage laws may intervene, and price cuts can be matched, so the advantage will quickly erode.

**Conclusion:** Sustainability depends on resources that are valuable, rare, hard to imitate and non-substitutable; Restaurant A's location-and-recipe advantage meets these tests far better than Restaurant B's imitable and fragile low-wage cost advantage, so A's advantage is the more enduring.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the explicit sustainability criteria (valuable/rare/inimitable/non-substitutable) applied to *both* cases with a reasoned verdict; a generic definition of competitive advantage without the sustainability test is insufficient.)*

### Q83. Ch: Internal Environment Analysis — Core Competence and Distinctive Capabilities (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** The chairman of a diversified group claims, "Our real strength is not our products but our underlying core competence." Explain the concept of core competence, state the tests that identify a genuine core competence, and explain how it differs from a mere strength.

**Answer:**
**Concept of core competence:** A core competence, a concept given by Prahalad and Hamel, is a unique capability or a bundle of skills, knowledge and technologies that a firm possesses which allows it to deliver a distinctive benefit to customers and which is harder for competitors to replicate. It is the collective learning of the organisation — especially the ability to coordinate diverse production skills and integrate multiple technologies — and it underlies a range of the firm's products rather than any single one.

**Tests of a genuine core competence:** According to Prahalad and Hamel, a capability qualifies as a core competence if it satisfies three tests:
1. **Access to a wide variety of markets** — the competence should provide potential access to a broad range of markets, i.e., it can be applied across many products or businesses.
2. **Significant contribution to perceived customer benefits** — it should make a meaningful contribution to the benefits that customers perceive in the end product.
3. **Difficult for competitors to imitate** — it should be hard for rivals to copy, arising as it does from a complex harmonisation of individual technologies and skills.

**Core competence versus an ordinary strength:** Every strength is not a core competence. A strength is any favourable attribute of the firm. A **core competence** is a *special* strength that additionally passes the three tests above — it spans multiple markets, delivers distinctive customer value, and is inimitable. Thus a core competence is the sub-set of the firm's strengths that is the true source of sustainable competitive advantage.

**Conclusion:** A core competence is a distinctive, hard-to-imitate capability that opens up many markets and delivers value to customers; the chairman is right in that the group's durable advantage flows from this underlying competence rather than from any single product, provided it passes the three identifying tests.

### Q84. Ch: Internal Environment Analysis — McKinsey 7-S Framework for Internal Appraisal (Marks: 5) [Application]

**Answer:**
**Concept:** The **McKinsey 7-S framework** is a tool for internal analysis and for diagnosing whether an organisation is properly aligned to implement its strategy. It holds that organisational effectiveness arises from the *fit* among seven interdependent elements, which fall into "hard" and "soft" categories. If a strategy is to succeed, all seven must be mutually consistent; misalignment in any one impedes implementation — which is precisely the problem after the acquisition.

**The seven elements:**

| Element | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Strategy** (hard) | The plan of action by which the firm intends to build and sustain competitive advantage |
| **Structure** (hard) | The way the organisation is divided into units and how authority and reporting relationships are arranged |
| **Systems** (hard) | The formal and informal procedures and processes (e.g., planning, information, budgeting, reward systems) that govern day-to-day activity |

| **Staff** (soft) | The human resources of the organisation — the people, their number and categories |

### Q85. Ch: SM – Strategic Choices — Grand Strategies & the Process of Choosing (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** A diversified consumer-goods company's board is confused about how a firm actually *arrives* at its grand strategy. Explain the four grand strategy alternatives available to a firm and describe the sequential process a firm follows in choosing among them.

**Answer:**
Grand strategies, also called master or corporate strategies, provide the basic direction for strategic actions towards achieving long-term business objectives. They indicate the time period over which the objectives are to be attained and are essentially concerned with the choice of long-term objectives and the broad decision of *how* to reach them.

There are four grand strategy alternatives that a firm may pursue, presented below:

| Grand Strategy | Basic character | When suitable |
|---|---|---|
| **Stability** | Firm stays with its current businesses and product markets; maintains the existing level of effort and is content with incremental growth | Environment is relatively stable, firm is doing reasonably well, risk of change is high |
| **Expansion / Growth** | Firm seeks a significant increase in performance by entering new markets, adding products, or redefining the business — through intensification, diversification, mergers, acquisitions or strategic alliances | Environment is dynamic and offers opportunities; firm wishes to grow, gain market share and economies of scale |
| **Retrenchment** | Firm reduces the scope of its activities through turnaround, divestment or liquidation to arrest decline | Performance is declining, some segments are unprofitable, or survival is threatened |
| **Combination** | Firm adopts a mix of the above three strategies simultaneously or sequentially across its various businesses (SBUs) | Large, diversified, multi-business firms operating in different environments |

**The process of choosing among grand strategies** may be described in the following sequence:

1. **Identifying alternatives** — The firm first recognises the full range of strategic alternatives realistically open to it, rather than fixating on the obvious one.
2. **Evaluating alternatives against objectives and resources** — Each alternative is examined for its consistency with the firm's mission, objectives, resources, competencies and the environmental realities disclosed by SWOT analysis.
3. **Considering the decisional factors** — The choice is influenced by the role of past strategy, the degree of the firm's dependence on external elements, attitude towards risk, internal political considerations, timing, and competitive reaction.
4. **Making the choice** — The alternative that best matches the firm's situation, offers the best fit between internal capability and external opportunity, and carries acceptable risk, is selected.

**Conclusion:** A firm does not pick a grand strategy arbitrarily; it selects from among stability, expansion, retrenchment and combination through a reasoned process that fits its objectives, resources and environment.

### Q86. Ch: SM – Strategic Choices — BCG Matrix vs GE Nine-Cell Matrix (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** "Both the BCG Growth-Share Matrix and the GE Nine-Cell Matrix are portfolio-analysis tools, yet the GE matrix is regarded as more refined." Distinguish between the two techniques and state why the GE matrix is considered an improvement.

**Answer:**
Portfolio analysis is a technique of analysing the mix of businesses (SBUs) held by a diversified firm so that resources can be allocated among them rationally. The two most widely used frameworks are the **BCG Growth-Share Matrix** (developed by the Boston Consulting Group) and the **GE Nine-Cell Matrix** (developed by General Electric with McKinsey).

The **BCG Matrix** classifies SBUs on a two-by-two grid using two axes — *market growth rate* (vertical) and *relative market share* (horizontal) — yielding four cells:
- **Stars** — high growth, high share; require investment to sustain leadership.
- **Cash Cows** — low growth, high share; generate surplus cash to fund others.
- **Question Marks (Problem Children)** — high growth, low share; require heavy cash and a build-or-harvest decision.
- **Dogs** — low growth, low share; candidates for divestment or liquidation.

The **GE Nine-Cell Matrix** plots SBUs on a three-by-three grid using two *composite* axes — *industry (market) attractiveness* and *business strength / competitive position* — each rated as high, medium or low, producing nine cells grouped into three zones: invest/grow (green), selectivity/earn (yellow), and harvest/divest (red).

| Basis | BCG Matrix | GE Nine-Cell Matrix |
|---|---|---|
| Number of cells | Four (2 × 2) | Nine (3 × 3) |
| Axis 1 | Market growth rate (single factor) | Industry attractiveness (composite of many factors) |
| Axis 2 | Relative market share (single factor) | Business strength / competitive position (composite) |
| Number of variables | Two single measures | Many weighted variables on each axis |
| Middle category | Absent — everything is high or low | Present — "medium" allows finer positioning |
| Strategic prescriptions | Four broad prescriptions | Three zones with more graded guidance |

**Why the GE matrix is considered an improvement:**
1. It uses **multiple factors** on each axis instead of a single measure, so market attractiveness reflects size, profitability, competitive intensity, technology, etc., and business strength reflects share, brand, cost position and R&D — a richer picture than growth and share alone.
2. Its **three-by-three form adds a middle band**, avoiding the crude "high/low" split of the BCG and allowing units that are neither clearly strong nor clearly weak to be placed realistically.
3. It therefore gives **more discriminating investment guidance** across nine positions rather than four.

**Conclusion:** While the BCG matrix is simpler and quicker, the GE nine-cell matrix, by using multi-factor composite axes and a nine-cell grid, offers a more comprehensive and realistic basis for portfolio decisions.

*(Full-marks tip: A comparison table earns marks fast; the examiner specifically wants the "why it is an improvement" reasoning — do not merely describe the two matrices without the comparative judgement.)*

### Q87. Ch: SM – Strategy Implementation & Evaluation — Organisational Structures for Strategy (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Structure follows strategy. Explain the functional structure and the divisional structure as means of implementing strategy, and state one situation in which each is most appropriate.

**Answer:**
An organisational structure is the pattern in which the tasks and sub-tasks required to implement a strategy are arranged into positions, departments and relationships. Since strategy is executed through the structure, the choice of structure is a critical strategy-implementation decision — hence the maxim "structure follows strategy."

**Functional Structure:**
A functional structure groups activities and positions on the basis of the major business *functions* to be performed — such as production, marketing, finance, human resources and research. Each function is headed by a specialist, and coordination across functions is achieved at the top by the chief executive. It promotes **specialisation, operating efficiency and clear career paths** within a function, but tends to create a narrow functional outlook and pushes coordination problems up to the top.

*Most appropriate when:* the firm is **single-business or has a narrow, related product line** operating in a relatively stable environment, where deep functional expertise matters more than product-wise autonomy.

**Divisional Structure:**
A divisional structure groups activities on the basis of **products, product lines, geographic territories or customer/market segments**, with each division operating as a semi-autonomous unit having its own set of functions and its own divisional head accountable for results. It permits **product- or market-focused attention, faster decisions and clear profit accountability**, but causes duplication of functional resources across divisions and higher administrative cost.

*Most appropriate when:* the firm is **large and diversified**, handling several products or serving several distinct geographic markets, where each product or region needs dedicated strategic and operating attention.

| Basis | Functional Structure | Divisional Structure |
|---|---|---|
| Basis of grouping | Business functions | Products / regions / customers |
| Focus | Functional specialisation | Product/market performance |
| Accountability | At the top only | At each division |
| Best suited to | Single/related business, stable environment | Large diversified multi-product firm |

**Conclusion:** The functional structure suits a focused single-business firm seeking specialisation, whereas the divisional structure suits a large diversified firm needing product- or region-wise accountability; the correct structure is the one that best supports the chosen strategy.

*(Full-marks tip: Anchor every point to "implementing strategy" and give the suitability condition for each — merely defining the structures without linking them to strategy and situation loses the application marks.)*

### Q90. Ch: SM – Strategy Implementation & Evaluation — Strategic Control Types (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish among the four types of strategic control — premise control, strategic surveillance, special alert control and implementation control — that a firm uses to keep its strategy on track.

**Answer:**
Strategic control is the process of evaluating a strategy as it is being formulated and implemented, so that the firm can take corrective action if events do not unfold as expected. Because strategy is executed over long periods amid uncertainty, four distinct types of strategic control are exercised, as explained below:

1. **Premise Control:** Every strategy is built on certain **assumptions or premises** about environmental and industry conditions (e.g., inflation, competitor behaviour, technology). Premise control systematically **checks whether these assumptions still hold** as implementation proceeds. If a key premise no longer holds, the strategy may need to be revised. It focuses only on the crucial premises.

2. **Strategic Surveillance:** This is a **broad, unfocused, ongoing scanning** of a wide range of events inside and outside the firm that could threaten the course of the strategy. Unlike premise control, it is not aimed at any specific pre-identified factor; it is a general watch through sources such as trade journals, conferences and media to catch anything overlooked.

3. **Special Alert Control:** This is the **rapid reconsideration of the strategy triggered by a sudden, unexpected event** — such as a natural disaster, a competitor's takeover, a major product recall or a regulatory shock. It calls for a quick, thorough review through crisis teams and contingency plans.

4. **Implementation Control:** This is control exercised **as implementation actually takes place**, checking whether the step-by-step actions and intermediate results are moving the firm towards its intended strategy. It operates chiefly through **monitoring strategic thrusts (milestones)** and **milestone reviews**, and may signal that the overall strategy itself needs re-examination.

| Type | Focus | Nature |
|---|---|---|
| Premise control | Specific planning assumptions | Focused, continuous |
| Strategic surveillance | Everything relevant | Broad, unfocused, continuous |
| Special alert control | A single sudden event | Reactive, immediate |
| Implementation control | Ongoing actions and milestones | Step-by-step, continuous |

**Conclusion:** The four controls are complementary — premise control watches assumptions, surveillance keeps a general watch, special alert control reacts to shocks, and implementation control tracks execution — together forming a complete strategic-control system.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner looks for the DISTINCTION among the four, especially the fine line between premise control (focused) and strategic surveillance (unfocused). Blurring these two is the most common deduction.)*

### Q92. Ch: SM – Strategy Implementation & Evaluation — Matrix and Network Structures (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Modern firms increasingly adopt matrix and network (virtual) structures. Explain each of these two structures, and state the chief advantage and chief drawback of each.

**Answer:**
As strategies become more complex, firms move beyond simple functional and divisional forms to **matrix** and **network** structures, which are explained below.

**Matrix Structure:**
A matrix structure **superimposes a project (or product) dimension upon the usual functional dimension**, so that an employee reports simultaneously to two managers — a functional head and a project/product head. It is used where the firm must pursue several projects or products at once while still preserving functional expertise.
- **Chief advantage:** It allows **efficient sharing of specialised functional resources across several projects/products** and improves flexibility and coordination on complex, multi-project work.
- **Chief drawback:** The **dual chain of command violates unity of command**, creating confusion, conflicting demands and power struggles between the two bosses, which can slow decisions.

**Network (Virtual) Structure:**
A network structure, also called a virtual or modular organisation, is one in which the firm **retains only its core activity and outsources most other functions** — manufacturing, distribution, IT, etc. — to a network of specialist partner firms, coordinated by a small central hub, often electronically. The firm is essentially a **small core linked contractually to a web of outside specialists**.
- **Chief advantage:** It gives **great flexibility and low overheads**, letting the firm draw on world-class specialists and scale up or down quickly without owning heavy assets.
- **Chief drawback:** The firm has **reduced direct control over outsourced activities** and depends heavily on partners, so quality lapses, loss of know-how or a partner's failure can hurt it badly.

| Basis | Matrix Structure | Network Structure |
|---|---|---|
| Design principle | Dual (functional + project) authority | Core hub + outsourced partner web |
| Key benefit | Resource sharing across projects | Flexibility, low overhead |
| Key problem | Dual command, conflict | Loss of control over partners |

**Conclusion:** The matrix structure suits multi-project firms needing shared expertise but risks command conflict, while the network structure suits asset-light, flexible firms but risks loss of control; each must be adopted with its drawback consciously managed.

### Q93. Ch: SM – Strategic Choices — Turnaround Strategy (Retrenchment) (Marks: 8) [Application]

Given that Metro Motors' management wants to revive rather than divest, a **turnaround strategy** is the correct recommendation.

2. **Change of leadership / new management drive** — often a turnaround needs fresh, energetic management to signal and lead change and to restore morale.
3. **Cost reduction** — trim bloated costs, eliminate wasteful expenditure and improve operating efficiency.
4. **Asset and product rationalisation** — replace obsolete machines, drop unprofitable products, and focus on the firm's strongest, most profitable segments.
5. **Revenue generation / re-focusing on core** — push profitable product lines, re-price, and improve marketing to rebuild sales.
6. **Improvement of the internal working environment** — restore employee morale through communication, incentives and involvement.
7. **Restructuring finances / working capital** — reschedule debt and tighten working-capital management to ease the cash crunch.
8. **Implementation and follow-up** — set milestones and monitor the recovery closely.

**Conclusion:** Metro Motors should adopt a turnaround strategy: it displays the classic distress signals, and a disciplined plan of leadership renewal, cost reduction, asset and product rationalisation, revenue focus and financial restructuring can nurse it back to profitability without resorting to divestment or liquidation.

### Q94. Ch: SM – Functional & Digital Strategy — Marketing Strategy: The Expanded Marketing Mix (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** For a service business, the traditional 4 Ps of the marketing mix are considered inadequate. Explain the three additional Ps of the expanded (services) marketing mix and why they matter.

**Answer:**
Marketing strategy for a business is put into effect through the **marketing mix** — the controllable variables that the firm blends to satisfy its target market. The traditional mix comprises the **4 Ps — Product, Price, Place and Promotion**. For services, because the product is intangible, inseparable from its provider and variable in quality, the mix is expanded by **three additional Ps — People, Process and Physical evidence**, explained below:

1. **People:** Services are delivered *by* people, so **all human actors — front-line staff, service providers and even other customers — form part of the offering**. Their appearance, attitude, competence and behaviour directly shape the customer's experience, making recruitment, training and motivation a marketing concern, not just an HR one.

2. **Process:** This refers to the **procedures, mechanisms and flow of activities by which the service is actually delivered** — the systems, queuing, steps and speed the customer passes through. A smooth, well-designed process makes the service reliable and pleasant; a clumsy process frustrates customers even when the core service is good.

3. **Physical evidence:** Because a service is intangible, customers look for **tangible cues that signal quality** — the ambience, décor, cleanliness, uniforms, brochures, layout and equipment. This physical evidence helps the customer form expectations and judge the otherwise invisible service.

| Additional P | What it covers | Why it matters for services |
|---|---|---|
| People | Staff and others delivering/consuming the service | They *are* the service; behaviour drives satisfaction |
| Process | Delivery procedures and flow | Determines reliability, speed and convenience |
| Physical evidence | Tangible surroundings and cues | Makes the intangible service credible and judgeable |

**Conclusion:** For services, the 4 Ps are extended to 7 Ps by adding People, Process and Physical evidence — each addressing the intangibility, inseparability and variability of services and thereby completing an effective services-marketing strategy.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner wants all THREE extra Ps explained with the "why services need them" logic. Merely listing "People, Process, Physical evidence" without linking them to the special nature of services loses marks.)*

### Q95. Ch: SM – Strategic Choices — Process of Choosing: Factors Affecting Strategic Choice (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Even after alternatives are evaluated, several subjective and objective factors shape the final strategic choice. Explain the major factors that influence a firm's choice of strategy.

**Answer:**
After a firm generates and evaluates its strategic alternatives, the actual *selection* of a grand strategy is influenced by a set of factors that go beyond pure analysis. The major factors affecting strategic choice are explained below:

1. **Role of past (present) strategy:** Strategists are strongly influenced by the strategies the firm has followed before; there is a tendency to continue with or build upon existing strategy rather than depart radically from it, because past strategy shapes perception and commitment.

2. **Degree of the firm's external dependence:** Where the firm depends heavily on **owners, competitors, suppliers, customers, government or other external elements**, this dependence narrows the range of acceptable choices, since the strategy must keep these powerful parties satisfied.

3. **Attitude towards risk:** The strategists' **risk temperament** matters greatly — risk-averse managers favour stability and safe, familiar options, while risk-taking managers are willing to choose bold expansion or diversification.

4. **Internal political considerations / power relationships:** Strategic choice is affected by the **coalitions and power structures within the firm**; the preferences of dominant individuals or groups can tilt the choice, so the "best" analytical option may yield to the politically acceptable one.

5. **Timing:** The **right moment** to act is critical; a sound strategy chosen at the wrong time may fail, so considerations of when to move influence which option is selected.

6. **Competitive reaction:** Anticipated **responses of competitors** to each alternative influence the choice, since strategists prefer options less likely to provoke a damaging retaliation.

**Conclusion:** Strategic choice is not a purely rational, mechanical outcome; it is shaped by past strategy, external dependence, risk attitude, internal politics, timing and expected competitive reaction, and the strategist must weigh all of these before finalising the grand strategy.

*(Full-marks tip: Name and briefly explain each factor as a separate point. Listing only two or three factors, or confusing these choice-influencers with the SWOT evaluation criteria, causes deductions.)*

### Q97. Ch: SM – Strategy Implementation & Evaluation — Strategic Evaluation and Control Process (Marks: 8) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the meaning and importance of strategic evaluation, and describe the main steps involved in the process of strategic evaluation and control.

**Answer:**
**Meaning:** Strategic evaluation and control is the final phase of the strategic-management process. It is the process of **determining the effectiveness of a given strategy in achieving the organisational objectives and taking corrective action wherever required.** It ensures that what was planned is actually being achieved and, if not, that suitable modifications are made either in the strategy or in its implementation.

**Importance of strategic evaluation:**
1. It provides a **link between planning and results**, checking whether the chosen strategy is producing the intended outcomes.
2. It serves as a **feedback mechanism**, feeding lessons from implementation back into future strategy formulation.
3. It **keeps the firm on course** in a changing environment by catching deviations early and enabling timely correction.
4. It **coordinates tasks and creates discipline**, since managers know their performance against the strategy will be reviewed.

**Steps in the process of strategic evaluation and control:**
1. **Setting standards / performance benchmarks:** Determine *what* is to be measured and *against what standard*. Standards may be quantitative (sales, market share, cost, profit) or qualitative (goodwill, capability), and should relate to the strategic objectives.
2. **Measurement of performance:** Measure the **actual performance** of the firm and its units using reports, budgets and other records, at appropriate intervals. Reliable, timely measurement is essential.
3. **Comparison / analysing variances:** Compare the actual performance against the pre-set standards and **identify the deviations** and the extent of the gaps, distinguishing acceptable tolerances from significant variances.
4. **Taking corrective action:** Where deviations are significant, decide and take **corrective action** — this may involve revising the standards, improving the implementation, reallocating resources, or, in the extreme, reformulating the strategy itself. Where performance meets standards, the strategy is allowed to continue.

**Conclusion:** Strategic evaluation and control completes the strategic-management cycle by measuring performance against standards, analysing deviations and taking corrective action, thereby ensuring that the firm's strategy stays effective and aligned with its objectives.

*(Full-marks tip: Present the four steps in the correct sequence — set standards, measure, compare, correct — and include the "importance/why" part the question asks for. Jumping straight to steps while omitting meaning and importance loses marks.)*

### Q98. Ch: SM – Strategic Choices — BCG Matrix Application (Marks: 8) [Application]
**Question:** "NovaTech Appliances" has four SBUs. From the data below, plot each SBU on the BCG matrix, classify it, and recommend an appropriate strategy for each.

| SBU | Market growth rate | Relative market share | Cash characteristic |
|---|---|---|---|
| A – Smart TVs | High (18%) | High (1.6x) | Uses & generates much cash |
| B – Washing machines | Low (4%) | High (2.0x) | Generates large surplus cash |
| C – Wearables | High (22%) | Low (0.4x) | Uses much cash, low return |
| D – Music systems | Low (3%) | Low (0.5x) | Neither uses nor gives much |

**Answer:**
The BCG (Growth-Share) matrix classifies each SBU on two axes — **market growth rate** (high/low) and **relative market share** (high/low) — into four cells: **Stars** (high growth, high share), **Cash Cows** (low growth, high share), **Question Marks** (high growth, low share) and **Dogs** (low growth, low share). Applying this to NovaTech's four SBUs:

**Working Note — classifying each SBU:**
- SBU A: high growth + high share → top-left cell.
- SBU B: low growth + high share → bottom-left cell.
- SBU C: high growth + low share → top-right cell.

| SBU | Growth | Rel. share | BCG category | Recommended strategy |

### Q99. Ch: SM – Strategy Implementation & Evaluation — Strategic Change and the Role of Leadership (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Implementing a new strategy almost always requires managing change and strong strategic leadership. Explain the process of managing strategic change and the two important leadership roles in strategy implementation.

**Answer:**
When a firm adopts a new strategy, its existing structure, systems, processes and mindset usually have to change; **managing this strategic change** is therefore central to successful implementation. Strategic change is the movement of a company from its present state towards a desired future state to increase its competitiveness.

**Process of managing strategic change** — a commonly cited approach involves the following steps:
1. **Recognise the need for change** — identify and accept that the environment or strategy demands a change from the status quo.
2. **Create a shared vision to manage change** — develop and communicate a clear picture of the desired future so that members understand and accept the direction.
3. **Institutionalise the change** — put in place the structures, systems, resources and reinforcement needed to embed the change and make it a permanent part of the organisation, overcoming resistance.

A widely used underlying model is **Kurt Lewin's three-stage model** — **Unfreezing** (reducing forces that maintain the status quo and creating awareness of the need for change), **Changing/Moving** (developing new attitudes, behaviours and systems), and **Refreezing** (stabilising and reinforcing the change so that it becomes the new normal).

**The two important leadership roles in strategy implementation:**
1. **Transformational leadership:** A leader who **inspires and motivates followers to transcend their own interests** for the good of the organisation, championing change, creating vision and energising people to embrace the new strategy. This role is vital where deep, discontinuous change is required.
2. **Transactional leadership:** A leader who **works through the existing structure using exchanges, rewards and controls** — setting targets, monitoring performance and rewarding compliance. This role keeps day-to-day implementation on track and stable.

**Conclusion:** Successful strategy implementation requires managing change through recognising the need, building a shared vision and institutionalising it (à la Lewin's unfreeze-change-refreeze), supported by transformational leadership to inspire the change and transactional leadership to keep execution disciplined.

### Q100. Ch: SM – Functional & Digital Strategy — Functional Strategy Integration: Production/Operations Strategy (Marks: 8) [Application]
**Question:** "Precision Tools Ltd." wants each of its functional areas to pull in the same direction as its business strategy of "reliable, low-cost, quick-delivery industrial tools." (a) Explain what a functional strategy is and why functional strategies must be developed. (b) For Precision Tools, outline the thrust of its production/operations strategy and its human-resource strategy so that both support the business strategy.

**Answer:**
**(a) Meaning and need for functional strategy:**
A functional strategy is the **short-range, relatively narrow game plan for a key functional area** — such as production, marketing, finance, human resources or R&D — through which the firm's broader business (competitive) strategy is actually translated into action. It gives operational meaning and detail to the business strategy within that function.

Functional strategies must be developed for the following reasons:
1. They **operationalise the business strategy** — the grand or competitive strategy remains a mere intention unless each function has a plan to carry out its part.
2. They **provide direction to functional managers**, telling each department what it must do and enabling day-to-day decisions consistent with the overall strategy.
3. They **ensure coordination and consistency** among the various functions, so that production, marketing, finance and HR reinforce rather than contradict one another.
4. They help in the **effective allocation and use of resources** within each function towards the strategic objective.

**(b) Functional strategies for Precision Tools Ltd.** — the business strategy is "reliable, low-cost, quick-delivery industrial tools," so the functions must be tuned to **quality/reliability, cost and speed** as follows:

**Production / Operations Strategy — thrust:**
1. **Cost efficiency:** Achieve low cost through economies of scale, high capacity utilisation, waste reduction and lean/standardised processes — supporting the "low-cost" goal.
2. **Quality and reliability:** Institute strong quality-control systems, preventive maintenance and standardisation so that tools are dependable — supporting "reliable."
3. **Speed and flexibility of delivery:** Adopt shorter production cycles, efficient scheduling and adequate finished-goods stock or agile capacity so orders are met quickly — supporting "quick delivery."
4. **Supply and inventory management:** Ensure timely material supply and optimal inventory so that neither shortages delay delivery nor excess inflates cost.

**Human-Resource Strategy — thrust:**
1. **Recruitment and training** of a skilled, quality-conscious workforce capable of maintaining precision and reliability.
2. **Productivity-linked incentives and cost consciousness** so employees actively support low-cost operations.
3. **Motivation, morale and retention** measures so that trained talent stays and delivery commitments are consistently met.
4. **Culture of quality and discipline**, aligning employee behaviour with the reliability and speed the strategy demands.

**Conclusion:** Functional strategies convert Precision Tools' business strategy into action; a production strategy centred on cost efficiency, quality and delivery speed, working hand-in-hand with an HR strategy that builds a skilled, motivated, cost-conscious workforce, ensures every function pulls in the same direction as the firm's "reliable, low-cost, quick-delivery" competitive positioning.
