### Q1. Ch: Basic Concepts — Surcharge & Marginal Relief (Individual crossing ₹50 lakh) (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** Mr. Aravind, a resident individual aged 45 years, has a total income of ₹51,00,000 for A.Y. 2025-26, taxable under the **optional old regime**. Compute his tax liability, clearly showing the working of **marginal relief** on surcharge.

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Total Income (all under normal rates) | 51,00,000 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Income-tax on total income of ₹51,00,000 (old regime slabs):**

| Slab | Rate | Tax (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| Up to ₹2,50,000 | Nil | 0 |
| ₹2,50,001 – ₹5,00,000 | 5% | 12,500 |
| ₹5,00,001 – ₹10,00,000 | 20% | 1,00,000 |
| ₹10,00,001 – ₹51,00,000 (₹41,00,000) | 30% | 12,30,000 |
| **Income-tax** | | **13,42,500** |

Since total income exceeds ₹50,00,000 but not ₹1,00,00,000, **surcharge @ 10%** [First Schedule, Finance Act] applies = ₹1,34,250.
Income-tax **plus** surcharge = 13,42,500 + 1,34,250 = **₹14,76,750**.

**WN-2 — Marginal Relief u/s 2 (surcharge provisions):**
Surcharge is subject to marginal relief so that the additional tax (tax + surcharge) on the income exceeding ₹50 lakh does not exceed the amount by which income exceeds ₹50 lakh.

| Step | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| (a) Income-tax on ₹50,00,000 (no surcharge) = 12,500 + 1,00,000 + 30% × 40,00,000 | 13,12,500 |
| (b) Excess income over ₹50,00,000 (51,00,000 − 50,00,000) | 1,00,000 |
| (c) Maximum permissible (tax + surcharge) = (a) + (b) | 14,12,500 |
| (d) Actual (tax + surcharge) [WN-1] | 14,76,750 |
| (e) **Marginal Relief** = (d) − (c) | **64,250** |

Surcharge after marginal relief = 1,34,250 − 64,250 = **₹70,000**.

**Computation of Tax Liability:**

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Income-tax | 13,42,500 |
| Add: Surcharge (after marginal relief) | 70,000 |
| Tax + Surcharge | 14,12,500 |
| Add: Health & Education Cess @ 4% | 56,500 |
| **Tax Liability (rounded off u/s 288B)** | **14,69,000** |

**Answer:** Tax liability of Mr. Aravind = **₹14,69,000**; marginal relief allowed on surcharge = **₹64,250**.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards showing surcharge BEFORE relief, then the relief working separately; common deduction is forgetting to compute cess AFTER giving marginal relief, or applying marginal relief to cess as well — cess is never subject to marginal relief.)*

### Q2. Ch: Basic Concepts — Surcharge & Marginal Relief (Firm crossing ₹1 crore) (Marks: 4) [Problem]
**Question:** M/s Bhaskar & Associates, a partnership firm, has a total income of ₹1,01,00,000 for A.Y. 2025-26. Compute its tax liability with marginal relief.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Income-tax and surcharge:**
A firm is taxed at a flat 30%; surcharge @ **12%** applies where total income exceeds ₹1,00,00,000.
Income-tax = 30% × 1,01,00,000 = ₹30,30,000. Surcharge @ 12% = ₹3,63,600. Tax + surcharge = **₹33,93,600**.

**WN-2 — Marginal Relief:**

| Step | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| (a) Income-tax on ₹1,00,00,000 (no surcharge) = 30% × 1,00,00,000 | 30,00,000 |
| (b) Excess income over ₹1 crore | 1,00,000 |
| (c) Maximum permissible (tax + surcharge) = (a) + (b) | 31,00,000 |
| (d) Actual (tax + surcharge) [WN-1] | 33,93,600 |
| (e) **Marginal Relief** = (d) − (c) | **2,93,600** |

Surcharge after marginal relief = 3,63,600 − 2,93,600 = **₹70,000**.

**Computation of Tax Liability:**

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Income-tax | 30,30,000 |
| Add: Surcharge (net of marginal relief) | 70,000 |
| Tax + Surcharge | 31,00,000 |
| Add: HEC @ 4% | 1,24,000 |
| **Tax Liability** | **32,24,000** |

**Answer:** Tax liability = **₹32,24,000**; marginal relief = **₹2,93,600**.

*(Full-marks tip: State the firm's surcharge threshold (only >₹1 crore, single slab of 12%) — students often wrongly import the individual's 10%/15%/25%/37% ladder into a firm.)*

### Q3. Ch: Basic Concepts — Surcharge cap under default regime u/s 115BAC & Marginal Relief at ₹2 crore (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** Ms. Charulatha, a resident individual aged 50, has total income of ₹2,01,00,000 for A.Y. 2025-26, all taxable at normal rates. She has **not opted out** of the default tax regime u/s 115BAC. Compute her tax liability with marginal relief.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Income-tax under default regime u/s 115BAC(1A):**

| Slab | Rate | Tax (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| Up to ₹3,00,000 | Nil | 0 |
| ₹3,00,001 – ₹7,00,000 | 5% | 20,000 |
| ₹7,00,001 – ₹10,00,000 | 10% | 30,000 |
| ₹10,00,001 – ₹12,00,000 | 15% | 30,000 |
| ₹12,00,001 – ₹15,00,000 | 20% | 60,000 |
| Above ₹15,00,000 (₹1,86,00,000) | 30% | 55,80,000 |
| **Income-tax** | | **57,20,000** |

**WN-2 — Surcharge (note the 115BAC cap):** Under the default regime, the highest surcharge rate is **capped at 25%** — the 37% rate is **not** applicable [proviso to Para inserted for 115BAC]. As income exceeds ₹2 crore, surcharge @ **25%** = 25% × 57,20,000 = **₹14,30,000**. Tax + surcharge = ₹71,50,000.

**WN-3 — Marginal Relief (threshold ₹2,00,00,000, where surcharge steps from 15% to 25%):**

| Step | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| (a) Income-tax on ₹2,00,00,000 = 1,40,000 + 30% × 1,85,00,000 | 56,90,000 |
| (b) Surcharge @ 15% on (a) | 8,53,500 |
| (c) Tax + surcharge at ₹2 crore = (a) + (b) | 65,43,500 |
| (d) Excess income over ₹2 crore | 1,00,000 |
| (e) Maximum permissible (tax + surcharge) = (c) + (d) | 66,43,500 |
| (f) Actual tax + surcharge [WN-1+WN-2] | 71,50,000 |
| (g) **Marginal Relief** = (f) − (e) | **5,06,500** |

Surcharge after marginal relief = 14,30,000 − 5,06,500 = **₹9,23,500**.

**Computation of Tax Liability:**

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Income-tax | 57,20,000 |
| Add: Surcharge (net of marginal relief) | 9,23,500 |
| Tax + Surcharge | 66,43,500 |
| Add: HEC @ 4% | 2,65,740 |
| **Tax Liability** | **69,09,240** |

**Answer:** Tax liability = **₹69,09,240**; marginal relief = **₹5,06,500**.

*(Full-marks tip: The award-winning point is stating the 25% cap under 115BAC and that marginal relief at ₹2 crore is measured against 15% surcharge, not against nil surcharge — mixing up the reference threshold is the most common error.)*

### Q4. Ch: Basic Concepts — Rates of Surcharge & the 15% Cap on Special-Rate Income (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** "Even where an individual's total income exceeds ₹5 crore, the surcharge on certain components of income can never exceed 15%." Explain the rates of surcharge applicable to a resident individual for A.Y. 2025-26 and the special restriction referred to above.

**Answer:**
Surcharge is an additional charge levied on the amount of income-tax, as prescribed by the Finance Act. For a **resident individual** for A.Y. 2025-26, under the **optional old regime**, surcharge is levied on income-tax at the following rates depending on total income:

| Total Income | Rate of Surcharge |
|---|---|
| Exceeds ₹50 lakh but ≤ ₹1 crore | 10% |
| Exceeds ₹1 crore but ≤ ₹2 crore | 15% |
| Exceeds ₹2 crore but ≤ ₹5 crore | 25% |
| Exceeds ₹5 crore | 37% |

The statement is correct because of a special restriction: the surcharge rate of **25% and 37%** shall **not** apply to income by way of **dividend** and to capital gains chargeable under **section 111A (STCG on STT-paid equity), section 112 and section 112A (LTCG)**. On such income, the **maximum surcharge is restricted to 15%**, irrespective of the quantum of total income. Consequently, where total income includes such special-rate income, surcharge is computed in two parts — 15% (capped) on the special-rate portion and the applicable higher rate on the balance.

Further, under the **default regime u/s 115BAC**, the highest surcharge rate is itself **capped at 25%** (the 37% slab does not apply), and the 15% ceiling on the above-mentioned incomes continues to operate.

**Conclusion:** Surcharge on dividend and on capital gains u/s 111A/112/112A is capped at 15% even for the highest income brackets; hence the statement is valid.

*(Full-marks tip: List the exact sections (111A, 112, 112A) and mention dividend — merely saying "capital gains" loses marks; also note the separate 115BAC 25% cap.)*

### Q5. Ch: Basic Concepts — Rounding off (Sec 288A/288B) & "Person" (Sec 2(31)) (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** (a) Explain the rules for rounding off of total income and tax payable under sections 288A and 288B. (b) A group of persons comes together for a common purpose but with **no intention to earn income**. Can it still be a "person" under the Income-tax Act? Explain with reference to section 2(31).

**Answer:**
**(a) Rounding off:**
- **Section 288A** provides that the amount of **total income** computed shall be **rounded off to the nearest multiple of ₹10**. For this, any part of a rupee consisting of paise is ignored, and thereafter if the last figure is ₹5 or more it is increased to the next higher ₹10 and if less than ₹5 it is reduced to the lower ₹10.
- **Section 288B** provides identical rounding — to the **nearest multiple of ₹10** — for the amount of **tax, interest, penalty, fine or any other sum** payable or refundable.

*Illustration:* Total income of ₹5,12,344 is rounded to ₹5,12,340; tax of ₹47,326 is rounded to ₹47,330.

**(b) "Person" under Sec 2(31):** The definition of "person" is inclusive and covers, inter alia, an **Association of Persons (AOP)** or **Body of Individuals (BOI)**, "whether incorporated or not." The **Explanation to section 2(31)** clarifies that an entity referred to in the definition shall be deemed to be a person "whether or not such person was formed or established or incorporated with the object of deriving income, profits or gains." Therefore, absence of a profit motive does **not** take a body outside the definition.

**Conclusion:** A group with no profit intention is still a "person" (AOP/BOI) u/s 2(31); total income and tax are to be rounded to the nearest ₹10 under sections 288A and 288B respectively.

*(Full-marks tip: Quote the Explanation to 2(31) verbatim — "whether or not formed with the object of deriving income" — this is the exact line examiners look for.)*

### Q6. Ch: Residential Status — Income Deemed to Accrue or Arise in India (Sec 9) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Determine, with reasons, whether the following incomes are **deemed to accrue or arise in India** under section 9 for A.Y. 2025-26:

| # | Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| (i) | Salary paid by an Indian company to Mr. Roberto (a non-resident) for services rendered **in India** for 2 months; salary credited to his bank account in Italy | 4,00,000 |
| (ii) | Salary paid by the Central Government to Mr. Nandan, an Indian citizen, for services rendered as a diplomat **in France** | 12,00,000 |
| (iii) | Interest on money borrowed by a non-resident and used for a business carried on **outside India** | 3,00,000 |
| (iv) | Fees for technical services paid by a resident to a non-resident, the services being **utilised for a business carried on outside India** | 5,00,000 |
| (v) | Dividend paid by an Indian company to a non-resident shareholder outside India | 2,00,000 |

**Answer:**
Section 9 enumerates incomes that are deemed to accrue or arise in India irrespective of the place of receipt.

| # | Deemed to accrue in India? | Reason (Section) |
|---|---|---|
| (i) | **Yes** | Salary is deemed to accrue in India if it is earned in India, i.e. for services **rendered in India** [Sec 9(1)(ii)]. Place of receipt (Italy) is irrelevant. Fully taxable — ₹4,00,000. |
| (ii) | **Yes** | Salary payable by the **Government** to a **citizen of India** for services **outside India** is deemed to accrue in India [Sec 9(1)(iii)]. (Allowances/perquisites outside India would be exempt u/s 10(7), but the salary itself is deemed to accrue here.) — ₹12,00,000. |
| (iii) | **No** | Interest payable by a non-resident is deemed to accrue in India **only** if the money is borrowed and used for a business/profession carried on **in India** [Sec 9(1)(v)(c)]. Here it is used outside India, so **not** deemed to accrue in India. |
| (iv) | **No** | FTS payable by a resident is deemed to accrue in India **except** where the services are utilised in a business carried on **outside India** or for earning income from a source outside India [exception in Sec 9(1)(vii)(b)]. The exception applies — **not** deemed to accrue in India. |
| (v) | **Yes** | Dividend paid by an **Indian company** is deemed to accrue in India, wherever paid [Sec 9(1)(iv)]. — ₹2,00,000. |

**Conclusion:** Items (i), (ii) and (v) are deemed to accrue or arise in India; items (iii) and (iv) are not.

*(Full-marks tip: For each item cite the exact clause of Sec 9(1) and, for (iii)/(iv), state the "used/utilised outside India" exception — that exception is the crux, and a bare "yes/no" without the clause earns half marks.)*

### Q7. Ch: Residential Status — Residence of a HUF (Sec 6(2) & 6(6)) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** The HUF of Mr. Dhruv, the *karta*, has its **control and management situated partly in India and partly in Sri Lanka** during P.Y. 2024-25. The karta, Mr. Dhruv, was in India for only 90 days during the previous year, and during the 7 previous years preceding this year he was in India for a total of 640 days; he was resident in India in 5 of the 10 previous years preceding this year. Determine the residential status of the HUF for A.Y. 2025-26.

**Answer:**
Under **section 6(2)**, a **HUF** is said to be **resident** in India in any previous year **unless** the control and management of its affairs is situated **wholly outside India** during that year. Since the control and management of Mr. Dhruv's HUF is situated **partly in India** (and partly in Sri Lanka), it is **not** wholly outside India; therefore the **HUF is a resident** for A.Y. 2025-26.

Whether the resident HUF is **Ordinarily Resident** or **Not Ordinarily Resident** depends on the status of its **karta** (or manager). Under **section 6(6)(b)**, a resident HUF is *not ordinarily resident* if its manager (karta) satisfies **either** of these conditions:
- (a) He has been **non-resident in India in 9 out of the 10 previous years** preceding the relevant year; **or**
- (b) He has been in India for **729 days or less** during the **7 previous years** preceding the relevant year.

Applying to the karta:
- Condition (a): Karta was **resident in 5 of the last 10 years** → resident (not non-resident) in 5 years, i.e. non-resident in only 5 years, not 9. Condition (a) **not** satisfied.
- Condition (b): Karta was in India for **640 days** (≤ 729 days) in the 7 preceding previous years. Condition (b) **is satisfied**.

Since **at least one** condition of section 6(6)(b) is satisfied, the HUF is **Not Ordinarily Resident**.

**Conclusion:** The HUF is **Resident but Not Ordinarily Resident (RNOR)** in India for A.Y. 2025-26.

*(Full-marks tip: Keep the two-stage logic distinct — first residence via control & management (Sec 6(2)), then ROR/RNOR via the karta's two tests (Sec 6(6)(b)). Applying an individual's 182-day test to the HUF itself is a fatal error.)*

### Q8. Ch: Residential Status — Residence of a Company & POEM (Sec 6(3)) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain when a **company** is said to be resident in India for a previous year. What is meant by "**Place of Effective Management (POEM)**"?

**Answer:**
Under **section 6(3)**, a **company** is said to be **resident** in India in any previous year if:
1. it is an **Indian company**; **or**
2. its **place of effective management (POEM)**, in that year, is **in India**.

An **Indian company** (as defined in section 2(26)) is **always resident** in India, regardless of where its management is located. A **foreign company** is resident in India **only if** its POEM for that year is in India.

**Meaning of POEM [Explanation to Sec 6(3)]:** "Place of effective management" means a **place where key management and commercial decisions that are necessary for the conduct of the business of an entity as a whole are, in substance, made**. It looks to the substance of where the real, top-level decisions are taken, not merely the place where board meetings are formally held or where day-to-day operations are carried on.

The POEM test applies to a foreign company having **turnover/gross receipts exceeding ₹50 crore** in a financial year (companies below this threshold are examined under CBDT guidelines but the ₹50 crore filter governs its practical application). Unlike an individual or HUF, a company **cannot** be "not ordinarily resident" — a company is either **resident** or **non-resident**.

**Conclusion:** An Indian company is always resident; a foreign company is resident only if its POEM is in India during the previous year.

*(Full-marks tip: Reproduce the statutory POEM definition ("key management and commercial decisions … in substance … made") and state that a company has no RNOR category — both are frequently tested one-liners.)*

### Q9. Ch: Residential Status — Residence of Firm / AOP (Sec 6(2) & 6(4)) (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain how the residential status of a **partnership firm / AOP** and of "**every other person**" is determined under the Income-tax Act, 1961. Can a firm be "not ordinarily resident"?

**Answer:**
Under **section 6(2)**, a **firm** and an **association of persons (AOP)** are said to be **resident** in India in any previous year **except** where the **control and management of their affairs is situated wholly outside India** during that year. Thus:
- If control and management is **wholly outside India** → **Non-resident**.
- If control and management is **wholly or partly in India** → **Resident**.

Under **section 6(4)**, **every other person** (e.g. a local authority or an artificial juridical person) is resident in India in a previous year in **all cases except** where the control and management of its affairs is situated **wholly outside India** during that year — the same "wholly outside India" test.

A firm or AOP has **only two possible statuses — resident or non-resident**. The category of "**not ordinarily resident**" is available **only to individuals and HUFs** (section 6(6)); it does **not** apply to a firm, AOP, company or any other person.

**Conclusion:** A firm/AOP is resident unless control and management is wholly outside India; it **cannot** be not-ordinarily-resident.

*(Full-marks tip: The distinguishing line "wholly outside India → non-resident; wholly or partly in India → resident" plus "RNOR is only for individuals & HUFs" secures full marks.)*

### Q10. Ch: Residential Status — Individual: Visiting PIO & 120-day rule (Sec 6(1)/6(6)) (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** Mr. Farhan, a **person of Indian origin** settled in Dubai, comes to India on a visit during P.Y. 2024-25. His **total income (other than income from foreign sources)** for the year is **₹18,00,000**. His stay in India is as follows:

| Period | Days in India |
|---|---|
| P.Y. 2024-25 | 130 days |
| 4 previous years immediately preceding (aggregate) | 400 days |
| 7 previous years immediately preceding (aggregate) | 600 days |
| No. of years (out of 10 preceding) he was resident | 3 years |

Determine his residential status for A.Y. 2025-26. Would your answer change if his total income (other than foreign-source income) were only ₹12,00,000?

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Basic conditions u/s 6(1):** An individual is resident if he satisfies **either**:
(a) in India for **≥ 182 days** in the previous year; **or**
(b) in India for **≥ 60 days** in the previous year **and ≥ 365 days** in the 4 preceding previous years.

**WN-2 — Modified 60-day period for a visiting citizen/PIO [Explanation 1(b) to Sec 6(1)]:** For an Indian citizen or PIO who, being outside India, **comes on a visit** to India, condition (b) is applied by **substituting 60 days with 182 days**. **However**, where such person's **total income (other than income from foreign sources) exceeds ₹15 lakh**, the substitution is **120 days** (not 182).

**Case 1 — Total income (other than foreign source) = ₹18,00,000:**
Since income exceeds ₹15 lakh, the **120-day** threshold applies to condition (b).
- Days in P.Y. = 130 (not ≥ 182 → condition (a) fails).
- Condition (b): 130 days ≥ **120 days** ✔ and 400 days ≥ 365 days in the 4 preceding years ✔ → **condition (b) satisfied** → **Resident**.

Now test **section 6(6)(a)** for ROR/RNOR. However, note **section 6(6)(c)**: an individual who is a citizen/PIO having total income (other than foreign source) **> ₹15 lakh**, and who is in India for **120 days or more but less than 182 days**, is **deemed to be "Not Ordinarily Resident"** automatically. Mr. Farhan's stay is 130 days (≥120 but <182) → he is **RNOR**.

**→ Case 1 status: Resident but Not Ordinarily Resident (RNOR).**

**Case 2 — Total income (other than foreign source) = ₹12,00,000 (≤ ₹15 lakh):**
The concession restores the **182-day** substitution for condition (b).
- Condition (a): 130 days < 182 → fails.
- Condition (b): 60 days is substituted by **182 days**; his 130 days < 182 → **fails**.

Neither basic condition is satisfied → **Non-Resident**.

**Conclusion:** With income of ₹18,00,000 he is **RNOR**; if income were ₹12,00,000 he would be a **Non-Resident** for A.Y. 2025-26.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards linking the ₹15 lakh income filter to BOTH the 120-day residence test AND the automatic RNOR under Sec 6(6)(c). Merely concluding "Resident" without spotting the deemed-RNOR rule loses a mark.)*

### Q11. Ch: Residential Status — Deemed Accrual of Interest, Royalty & FTS (Sec 9(1)(v),(vi),(vii)) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the "**source rule**" governing when **interest, royalty and fees for technical services (FTS)** are deemed to accrue or arise in India under sections 9(1)(v), 9(1)(vi) and 9(1)(vii), and the common exception applicable to all three.

**Answer:**
Sections 9(1)(v), (vi) and (vii) apply a **source rule**: the income is deemed to accrue in India based on the residence of the payer and the place of use/utilisation, not on where it is paid.

**Interest — Sec 9(1)(v):** Interest is deemed to accrue in India if it is payable by:
(a) the **Government**; or
(b) a **resident**, except where the borrowing is used for a business/profession carried on **outside India** or for earning income from any source outside India; or
(c) a **non-resident**, only where the borrowing is used for a business/profession carried on **in India**.

**Royalty — Sec 9(1)(vi):** Royalty is deemed to accrue in India if payable by:
(a) the **Government**; or
(b) a **resident**, except where the royalty relates to a right/property/information used for a business carried on outside India or for earning income from a source outside India; or
(c) a **non-resident**, where it relates to a right/property used in a business carried on **in India** or for earning income from a source in India.

**FTS — Sec 9(1)(vii):** Fees for technical services follow the **identical pattern** — deemed to accrue in India if payable by Government, or by a resident (subject to the "utilised outside India" exception), or by a non-resident where the services are utilised in a business carried on in India.

**Common exception:** In each case, where the payer is a **resident**, the income is **not** deemed to accrue in India if the borrowing/right/service is used **for a business carried on outside India or for earning income from a source outside India**. Correspondingly, where the payer is a **non-resident**, the income is deemed to accrue in India **only** where the use/utilisation is **in India**.

**Conclusion:** The situs of interest, royalty and FTS is fixed by the payer's status and the place of utilisation, with a uniform carve-out for uses connected to a business/source outside India.

*(Full-marks tip: Present the three-way payer classification (Government / resident / non-resident) for each head and highlight the common "used/utilised outside India" exception — that structure is exactly how ICAI marks this answer.)*

### Q12. Ch: Income under Salaries — Profit in Lieu of Salary (Sec 17(3)) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** State what constitutes "**profit in lieu of salary**" under section 17(3). Mr. Gaurav received the following from his employer during P.Y. 2024-25. Examine the taxability of each under this head.

| # | Receipt | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| (i) | Compensation received on **termination** of his employment | 6,00,000 |
| (ii) | Amount received under a **Keyman insurance policy** taken by the employer | 3,00,000 |
| (iii) | Lump sum received from an **unrecognised provident fund** — employer's contribution & interest thereon | 2,50,000 |
| (iv) | Ex-gratia received **before joining** the employment | 1,00,000 |

**Answer:**
**Section 17(3)** defines "**profit in lieu of salary**" to include:
(i) any **compensation** due to or received by an assessee from his employer or former employer in connection with the **termination** of his employment or the **modification** of its terms and conditions;
(ii) any **payment** (other than specified exempt payments) due to or received from an employer/former employer or from a provident or other fund, to the extent it does not consist of contributions by the assessee or interest thereon;
(iia) any sum received under a **Keyman insurance policy** including bonus thereon;
(iii) any amount received, whether in lump sum or otherwise, **before joining** or **after cessation** of employment.

Amounts received from a **recognised PF** (to the extent exempt), and payments exempt u/s 10(10) (gratuity), 10(10A) (commuted pension), 10(10B) (retrenchment compensation), 10(11)/(12)/(13)/(13A) are **excluded** to that extent.

### Q17. Ch: Residential Status — Scope of Total Income & Incidence of Tax (Sec 5) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the **scope of total income** under section 5 for a **Resident and Ordinarily Resident (ROR)**, a **Resident but Not Ordinarily Resident (RNOR)** and a **Non-Resident (NR)**. Present the incidence of tax on the various categories of income in a table.

**Answer:**
Section 5 defines the **scope of total income** with reference to the residential status of the assessee. The type of income taxable depends on **where the income accrues/arises and where it is received**.

- For a **Resident and Ordinarily Resident**, total income includes **all income** — income received or deemed to be received in India, income accruing or arising or deemed to accrue or arise in India, **and** income accruing or arising **outside India** (global income) [Sec 5(1)].
- For a **Resident but Not Ordinarily Resident**, income accruing or arising **outside India is included only if** it is **derived from a business controlled from or a profession set up in India**; other foreign income is **not** taxable [proviso to Sec 5(1)].
- For a **Non-Resident**, total income includes only income **received or deemed to be received in India**, and income **accruing or arising or deemed to accrue or arise in India** [Sec 5(2)]. Foreign income is wholly outside the charge.

**Incidence of tax — summary table:**

| Nature of income | ROR | RNOR | NR |
|---|---|---|---|
| Income received / deemed received **in India** | Taxable | Taxable | Taxable |
| Income accruing/arising or deemed to accrue/arise **in India** | Taxable | Taxable | Taxable |
| Foreign income from a **business controlled from / profession set up in India** | Taxable | Taxable | **Not taxable** |
| Any **other foreign income** (no business connection with India) | Taxable | **Not taxable** | **Not taxable** |

**Conclusion:** An ROR is taxed on global income; an RNOR is taxed on Indian income plus foreign income from a business controlled from / profession set up in India; an NR is taxed only on Indian-source and India-received income.

*(Full-marks tip: The RNOR row is the discriminator — state the exact test "business controlled from / profession set up in India." Presenting the four-row incidence table cleanly is what earns full marks in this recurring theory question.)*

|---|---|

| Completion certificate obtained | 15.06.2022 (F.Y. 2022-23) |
| Two-year exemption window under Sec 23(5) | Up to 31.03.2025 |
| Whether let out during P.Y. 2024-25 | No |
| Annual value for A.Y. 2025-26 | NIL |

**Conclusion:** The firm's claim is **correct** for A.Y. 2025-26 — the annual value of the 12 unsold flats is NIL under Section 23(5), as the previous year falls within the two-year period from the end of the year of completion. (Had the flats remained unsold in A.Y. 2026-27, the exemption would lapse and notional annual value would become chargeable.)

### Q20. Ch: Income from House Property — Deemed Ownership [Sec 27] (Marks: 4) [Theory]

**Question:** "Income from house property is chargeable in the hands of the owner, but in certain situations a person who is not the legal owner is treated as the owner." Enumerate any **four** situations in which a person is treated as the **deemed owner** of house property under Section 27 of the Income-tax Act, 1961.

**Answer:**
Under **Section 22**, income from house property is chargeable in the hands of the **owner**. However, **Section 27** creates a legal fiction by which certain persons, though not the registered legal owners, are **deemed to be the owner** and are charged to tax on the income from such property. The situations are as follows:

| S.No. | Deemed owner under Section 27 |
|---|---|
| (i) | **Transfer to spouse or minor child [Sec 27(i)]:** An individual who transfers any house property to his/her **spouse** (otherwise than for adequate consideration or in connection with an agreement to live apart) or to a **minor child** (not being a married daughter) is deemed to be the owner of the property so transferred. |
| (ii) | **Holder of an impartible estate [Sec 27(ii)]:** The holder of an impartible estate is deemed to be the individual owner of all the properties comprised in the estate. |
| (iii) | **Member of a co-operative society/company/AOP [Sec 27(iii)]:** A member of a co-operative society, company or other association of persons to whom a building (or part thereof) is allotted or leased under a **house building scheme** is deemed to be the owner of that building/part. |
| (iv) | **Possession under Section 53A of the Transfer of Property Act [Sec 27(iiia)]:** A person who has acquired possession of a building in **part performance** of a contract of the nature referred to in Section 53A of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882, is deemed to be the owner. |
| (v) | **Long-term lessee [Sec 27(iiib)]:** A person who acquires any right in a building by way of a lease for a term of **not less than 12 years** (excluding a month-to-month or up-to-one-year lease) is deemed to be the owner. |

**Conclusion:** In each of the above situations, the deemed owner — and not the legal/registered owner — is liable to tax on the income from the house property under the head "Income from House Property".

### Q26. Ch: Profits & Gains of Business or Profession — Actual Cost [Sec 43(1) and Explanations] (Marks: 5) [Theory]

**Question:** Explain how the following items affect the determination of the **"actual cost"** of an asset for the purpose of depreciation under **Section 43(1)** of the Income-tax Act, 1961:
(a) Subsidy or grant received from the Government to meet the cost of the asset;
(b) Portion of the cost of the asset paid in cash exceeding ₹10,000 otherwise than by account-payee cheque/bank draft/electronic mode;
(c) Interest on borrowed capital relatable to the period before the asset is first put to use.

**Answer:**
Under **Section 43(1)**, "actual cost" means the actual cost of the asset to the assessee, **reduced by** that portion of the cost, if any, as has been met **directly or indirectly by any other person or authority**. Several Explanations to Section 43(1) modify the actual cost as follows:

| Item | Treatment under Section 43(1) |
|---|---|
| **(a) Government subsidy/grant [Explanation 10]** | Where a portion of the cost of an asset has been met directly or indirectly by way of **subsidy, grant or reimbursement** from the Central/State Government or any authority, **such subsidy amount is deducted** from the actual cost. Where the subsidy is not directly relatable to a specific asset, it is apportioned pro-rata over the assets. Thus actual cost = cost − subsidy attributable to the asset. |
| **(b) Cash payment exceeding ₹10,000 [Proviso to Sec 43(1)]** | Where the assessee incurs **any expenditure for acquisition of an asset** and makes a payment (or aggregate of payments to a person in a day) exceeding **₹10,000** otherwise than by an **account-payee cheque/draft or electronic clearing system** (or other prescribed electronic mode), such payment shall **not** be included in the actual cost of the asset. The disallowed amount is therefore excluded from the cost eligible for depreciation. |
| **(c) Interest before asset is put to use [Explanation 8]** | Interest paid on capital borrowed for acquisition of an asset **for any period after the asset is first put to use is NOT to be capitalised** — it is deductible as revenue expenditure. By implication, interest for the period **up to the date the asset is first put to use** forms **part of the actual cost** of the asset (and is eligible for depreciation). |

**Conclusion:** Actual cost under Section 43(1) is reduced by Government subsidy attributable to the asset and by cash payments above ₹10,000 for its acquisition, while pre-put-to-use interest on borrowings is capitalised as part of the actual cost.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards citing the exact Explanation number (10 for subsidy, 8 for interest) and the proviso for cash payments, plus the "first put to use" cut-off for interest capitalisation.)*

### Q27. Ch: Profits & Gains of Business or Profession — Specific Deductions [Sec 36] (Marks: 5) [Theory]

**Question:** Discuss the deductibility of the following under **Section 36** of the Income-tax Act, 1961, in computing the income under the head "Profits and Gains of Business or Profession":
(a) Bad debts written off;
(b) Employer's contribution to a recognised provident fund;
(c) Employees' contribution to a provident fund recovered from employees' salaries;
(d) Bonus or commission paid to employees.

**Answer:**
Section 36 lists specific deductions allowable in computing business income. The treatment of the given items is as follows:

| Item | Provision | Deductibility |
|---|---|---|
| **(a) Bad debts** | **Sec 36(1)(vii)** | Any debt (or part) which has become bad and is **written off as irrecoverable in the books** is deductible, provided the debt was **taken into account in computing income** of the assessee in that or an earlier year (or represents money lent in the ordinary course of a banking/money-lending business). A **mere provision** for doubtful debts is **not** deductible (except as specifically allowed to banks under Sec 36(1)(viia)). |
| **(b) Employer's contribution to RPF** | **Sec 36(1)(iv)** | Employer's contribution to a **recognised provident fund** or an approved superannuation fund is deductible, subject to prescribed limits, provided it is actually **paid** (also subject to Section 43B — allowed only on actual payment on or before the due date of filing the return). |
| **(c) Employees' contribution** | **Sec 36(1)(va)** | Sums received by the employer from employees as their contribution to any PF/ESI etc. are first treated as **income** [Sec 2(24)(x)], and are deductible **only if credited to the employee's account in the relevant fund on or before the due date under the relevant Act (PF/ESI Act)**. If deposited even one day late, the deduction is **permanently lost** (the Section 43B relaxation does **not** apply to employees' contribution). |
| **(d) Bonus or commission to employees** | **Sec 36(1)(ii)** | Bonus or commission paid to an employee for services rendered is deductible, **provided** the sum would **not** otherwise have been payable to him as **profits or dividend** (i.e., it is genuine remuneration and not a distribution of profits). It is also subject to Section 43B (allowed on actual payment by the due date of filing the return). |

**Conclusion:** Bad debts (actual write-off), employer's PF contribution (on payment), and bonus/commission (genuine, on payment) are deductible; employees' contribution is deductible only if deposited by the due date under the relevant welfare Act.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards clearly distinguishing employer's contribution [Sec 43B relief available] from employees' contribution [Sec 36(1)(va), strict due date, no 43B relief — Checkmate Services SC ruling], and rejecting a mere provision for bad debts.)*

### Q28. Ch: Profits & Gains of Business or Profession — Maintenance of Books of Account [Sec 44AA] (Marks: 4) [Theory]

**Question:** State the provisions of **Section 44AA** of the Income-tax Act, 1961 regarding the compulsory maintenance of books of account by persons carrying on **business** (other than specified professions), including the monetary limits applicable to an **individual/HUF**.

**Answer:**
**Section 44AA** and Rule 6F prescribe the requirement to maintain books of account.

**(a) Specified professions [Sec 44AA(1)]:** Persons carrying on **legal, medical, engineering, architectural, accountancy, technical consultancy, interior decoration, film artist, company secretary, authorised representative** and other notified professions are required to maintain such books of account and documents as prescribed under **Rule 6F** (cash book, journal, ledger, bills/vouchers, etc.), provided their **gross receipts exceed ₹1,50,000** in all three years preceding the relevant previous year (or, for a new profession, if receipts are likely to exceed ₹1,50,000).

**(b) Business and non-specified professions [Sec 44AA(2)]:** A person carrying on **business** (or a profession other than those specified above) must maintain such books of account as would enable the Assessing Officer to compute total income, if **either** of the following limits is exceeded:

| Category of assessee | Income limit | Turnover / Gross receipts limit |
|---|---|---|
| **Individual / HUF** | Income from business/profession **exceeds ₹2,50,000** in any one of the three preceding years | Total sales/turnover/gross receipts **exceed ₹25,00,000** in any one of the three preceding years |
| **Persons other than Individual/HUF** | Income exceeds **₹1,20,000** | Turnover/gross receipts exceed **₹10,00,000** |

For a **newly set-up** business/profession, the test is whether income/turnover is **likely to exceed** the limits in that first year. Additionally, a person covered by a **presumptive scheme** (e.g., Sec 44AD/44ADA/44AE) who claims **lower** profits than the presumptive rate **and** whose income exceeds the basic exemption limit must maintain books under Section 44AA.

**Conclusion:** For an individual/HUF carrying on business, books must be maintained if income exceeds **₹2,50,000** or turnover exceeds **₹25,00,000** in any of the three preceding previous years.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the higher ₹2,50,000 / ₹25,00,000 limits applicable specifically to Individuals/HUF (versus ₹1,20,000 / ₹10,00,000 for others) and the additional presumptive-scheme trigger.)*

### Q31. Ch: Profits & Gains of Business or Profession — Certain Deductions on Actual Payment [Sec 43B] (Marks: 4) [Application]

Under **Section 40A(3)**, where an assessee incurs any **revenue expenditure** in respect of which a payment (or aggregate of payments made to **a person in a day**) exceeds **₹10,000** otherwise than by an account-payee cheque/draft or prescribed electronic mode, **100% of such payment is disallowed**. **Rule 6DD** provides exceptions; a special **enhanced limit of ₹35,000** applies to payments made for **plying, hiring or leasing goods carriages** (transporters).

| Item | Analysis | Amount disallowed (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| (i) Raw material ₹24,000 in cash | Single cash payment exceeds ₹10,000 → fully disallowed under Sec 40A(3) | 24,000 |
| (ii) Freight to transporter ₹45,000 | Payment to a **transporter for goods carriage**; enhanced limit ₹35,000 applies. ₹45,000 > ₹35,000 → fully disallowed | 45,000 |
| (iii) ₹9,000 + ₹8,000 = ₹17,000 to same person, same day | **Aggregate** payments to one person in a day exceed ₹10,000 → fully disallowed (splitting does not help) | 17,000 |
| (iv) Machinery ₹60,000 in cash | **Capital expenditure**; Sec 40A(3) applies only to revenue expenditure. No disallowance under 40A(3) — but under the proviso to **Sec 43(1)** it is **excluded from the actual cost** for depreciation | NIL (under 40A(3)) |

**Total amount disallowed under Section 40A(3)/(3A)** = ₹24,000 + ₹45,000 + ₹17,000 = **₹86,000**.

**Conclusion:** ₹**86,000** is disallowed under Section 40A(3). The machinery payment of ₹60,000 is not hit by Section 40A(3) (being capital), but ₹60,000 is excluded from the actual cost of the machinery under the proviso to Section 43(1), so no depreciation is available on it.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the ₹35,000 transporter limit, the "aggregate per person per day" rule that defeats splitting, and correctly routing the capital (machinery) payment through Section 43(1) rather than Section 40A(3).)*

### Q35. Ch: Capital Gains — Transfer of Depreciable Asset (Sec 50) (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** M/s Prakash Industries, a partnership firm, furnishes the following particulars in respect of its block of "Plant & Machinery" (rate of depreciation 15%) for the previous year 2024-25. All assets are used wholly for business. Compute the capital gain/loss, if any, and state the closing WDV eligible for depreciation.

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Written Down Value of the block on 01.04.2024 | 12,00,000 |
| Add: Machine purchased and put to use on 05.09.2024 | 4,00,000 |
| Less: Sale consideration of some machines sold on 15.02.2025 | 20,00,000 |
| Number of machines remaining in the block on 31.03.2025 | 2 (in use) |

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Applicability of Sec 50:** Where a capital asset forming part of a block of assets on which depreciation has been allowed under the Act is transferred, the computation of capital gain is governed by **Section 50** (special provision), overriding Sections 48 and 49. Under **Section 50(1)**, where the full value of consideration on transfer of assets in a block *exceeds* the aggregate of (i) opening WDV, (ii) actual cost of assets acquired during the year, and (iii) expenditure on transfer, the excess is deemed to be **Short-Term Capital Gain (STCG)** — irrespective of the period of holding.

**WN-2 — Aggregate deductible value (Sec 50):**

| Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---|
| WDV as on 01.04.2024 | 12,00,000 |
| Add: Actual cost of machine acquired during the year | 4,00,000 |
| Aggregate value (a) | **16,00,000** |
| Full value of consideration on transfer (b) | 20,00,000 |

Since (b) ₹20,00,000 > (a) ₹16,00,000, the excess is deemed STCG.

**Statement Showing Capital Gain u/s 50 — PY 2024-25 (AY 2025-26)**

| Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Full value of consideration | 20,00,000 |
| Less: Opening WDV | (12,00,000) |
| Less: Cost of asset acquired during the year | (4,00,000) |
| **Short-Term Capital Gain u/s 50(1)** | **4,00,000** |

Formula: STCG = Consideration − (Opening WDV + Additions) = 20,00,000 − 16,00,000 = **₹4,00,000**.

Consequently, the block ceases to have any WDV; **closing WDV = Nil** and **no depreciation** is allowable for PY 2024-25, even though two machines physically remain in the block.

**OR (cross-check for the alternative fact):** Had the sale consideration been, say, ₹14,00,000 (i.e., *less* than the aggregate value of ₹16,00,000), there would be **no capital gain**; the block would continue with a closing WDV of ₹2,00,000 (16,00,000 − 14,00,000) on which depreciation @15% would be allowable, since neither condition of Sec 50(1)/(2) is satisfied.

**Answer:** Short-Term Capital Gain u/s 50 = **₹4,00,000**; closing WDV = Nil; no depreciation for the year.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the categorical statement that the gain is **STCG regardless of holding period** and that depreciation drops to Nil once WDV is exhausted. Common deduction: treating it as LTCG because the machines were held for years, or forgetting that additions during the year are also deducted.)*

### Q39. Ch: Capital Gains — Determination of Period of Holding in Special Cases (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain, with reference to the Explanation to Section 2(42A), how the **period of holding** is determined in the following situations: (i) bonus shares; (ii) right shares subscribed by the shareholder; (iii) rights entitlement (renouncement) purchased from the market; (iv) a capital asset acquired by gift or inheritance; and (v) shares held in an amalgamated company received against shares in the amalgamating company.

**Answer:** The period of holding decides whether a capital asset is short-term or long-term. The **Explanation 1 to Section 2(42A)** lays down specific rules for computing the period of holding in certain cases, as tabulated below:

| Situation | Reckoning of period of holding |
|---|---|
| (i) **Bonus shares** | From the **date of allotment** of the bonus shares (the earlier holding of original shares is *not* counted). Cost of acquisition u/s 55 is Nil (if allotted after 01.04.2001). |
| (ii) **Right shares** subscribed | From the **date of allotment** of the right shares. |
| (iii) **Rights entitlement purchased** from the market and then subscribed | The right *entitlement/renouncement* is held from the date of the offer to the date of renunciation; the shares subsequently allotted are held from the **date of allotment**. |
| (iv) **Asset acquired by gift, will, inheritance, or succession** [Sec 49(1)] | The period for which the asset was held by the **previous owner is included** in the assessee's period of holding. |
| (v) **Shares in amalgamated company** (Indian) received for shares in amalgamating company | The period for which the shares in the **amalgamating company** were held is **included**. |

**Conclusion:** The general rule is the date of acquisition by the assessee, but for assets received under Sec 49(1) modes (gift/inheritance) and in amalgamation/demerger, the **previous owner's holding period is tacked on**, whereas for bonus and right shares the period runs only from the date of allotment.

### Q43. Ch: Income from Other Sources — Deemed Dividend u/s 2(22)(e) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** M/s Sunrise Traders Pvt. Ltd. is a company in which the public are not substantially interested (a closely-held company). Its accumulated profits (excluding capitalised profits) as on the date of the loan stand at ₹8,00,000. During PY 2024-25 the company granted a loan of ₹12,00,000 to Mr. Praveen, a shareholder holding 15% of the equity shares carrying voting power. Examine the taxability of the loan under Section 2(22)(e) in the hands of Mr. Praveen for AY 2025-26. Would your answer change if the loan had been advanced in the ordinary course of a money-lending business of the company?

**Answer:** **Section 2(22)(e)** treats as **"dividend"** any payment by a **closely-held company** (a company in which the public are not substantially interested) by way of **loan or advance** to: (a) a shareholder who is the **beneficial owner of shares holding not less than 10% of the voting power**, or (b) a concern in which such shareholder has a substantial interest — **to the extent the company possesses accumulated profits**. Such deemed dividend is taxable in the hands of the recipient shareholder under **"Income from Other Sources" u/s 56(2)(i)**, and since Finance Act 2020, dividend (including deemed dividend) is **taxable in the hands of the shareholder at applicable slab rates**.

**Application to facts:**
- Sunrise Traders Pvt. Ltd. is a **closely-held company** — condition satisfied.
- Mr. Praveen holds **15% voting power** (≥ 10%) — condition satisfied.
- Loan = ₹12,00,000; Accumulated profits = ₹8,00,000.
- Deemed dividend = **loan or accumulated profits, whichever is lower** = **₹8,00,000**.

**Statement Showing Deemed Dividend u/s 2(22)(e)**

| Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Loan granted to shareholder | 12,00,000 |
| Accumulated profits on date of loan | 8,00,000 |
| **Deemed dividend (lower of the two)** | **8,00,000** |
| Balance loan of ₹4,00,000 — genuine loan, not dividend | Not taxable |

Hence, if Sunrise Traders were a money-lending company and the loan was granted in the ordinary course of that business, the ₹8,00,000 would **not** be treated as deemed dividend.

**Conclusion:** ₹8,00,000 is taxable as deemed dividend under IOS in Mr. Praveen's hands (slab rates); the balance ₹4,00,000 is a genuine loan. If it were a bona fide money-lending advance, nothing would be taxable u/s 2(22)(e).

### Q44. Ch: Income from Other Sources — Chargeability & Gift Taxation Framework (Sec 56(2)) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** State the framework under which **gifts** received by an individual are taxable under Section 56(2)(x). Explain the monetary threshold, the treatment of money, immovable property and movable property, and list any four categories of receipts that are **exempt** from this charge.

**Answer:** **Section 56(2)(x)** provides that where any person receives, in any previous year, from any person(s), the following without or for inadequate consideration, the specified amount is taxable under **"Income from Other Sources"**:

| Nature of receipt | Taxable amount |
|---|---|
| **(a) Sum of money** (without consideration) | If the **aggregate** exceeds **₹50,000**, the **whole** aggregate is taxable. |
| **(b) Immovable property — without consideration** | If **Stamp Duty Value (SDV) > ₹50,000**, the whole SDV is taxable. |
| **(c) Immovable property — for inadequate consideration** | If (SDV − consideration) exceeds the **higher of ₹50,000 or 10% of consideration**, the **difference** is taxable. |
| **(d) Movable property** (shares, jewellery, drawings, etc.) — without consideration | If **aggregate FMV > ₹50,000**, the whole FMV is taxable. |
| **(e) Movable property — for inadequate consideration** | If (FMV − consideration) > ₹50,000, the **difference** is taxable. |

**Exempt categories** — the charge does **not** apply to sums/property received: (i) from a **relative** (as defined — spouse, siblings, siblings of spouse/parents, lineal ascendants/descendants, etc.); (ii) on the occasion of the **marriage** of the individual; (iii) under a **will or by way of inheritance**; (iv) in **contemplation of death** of the payer/donor; (v) from a **local authority**, or from any fund/trust/institution registered u/s 12A/12AA/10(23C).

**Conclusion:** Gifts are taxed under Sec 56(2)(x) subject to the ₹50,000 threshold applied separately to money, immovable property and movable property, with the notable exemptions for relatives, marriage, will/inheritance and death-bed transfers.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner wants the **three-bucket ₹50,000 threshold** and the "higher of ₹50,000 or 10%" tolerance for inadequate-consideration immovable property, plus the exempt list. Common deduction: applying a single ₹50,000 limit across all buckets, or missing the 10% tolerance.)*

### Q48. Ch: Clubbing & Set-off — Transfer of Asset to Spouse without Adequate Consideration (Sec 64(1)(iv)) (Marks: 4) [Application]

She reinvested that ₹40,000 interest in shares, which yielded a dividend of ₹3,000. Examine the clubbing implications in the hands of Mr. Tarun. Would the position differ if the transfer had been made in connection with an agreement to live apart?

**Answer:** **Section 64(1)(iv)** provides that where an individual transfers, directly or indirectly, an asset (other than house property) to his/her **spouse** otherwise than for adequate consideration or in connection with an agreement to live apart, any income arising from such asset is **clubbed in the hands of the transferor**.

- The ₹5,00,000 was transferred to the wife without consideration; therefore the **interest of ₹40,000** arising on the fixed deposit (income from the transferred asset) is **clubbed in Mr. Tarun's total income**.
- However, income arising from the **accretion** to the transferred asset is **not clubbed**. The ₹40,000 interest, once reinvested by the wife in shares, becomes her own asset; the **dividend of ₹3,000** earned on that reinvestment is **income on income** and is **taxable in Mrs. Tarun's hands** (not clubbed) — following the principle in *CIT v. Prem Bhai Parekh* / the accretion rule.

**Statement of Clubbing**

| Particulars | ₹ | Taxable in the hands of |
|---|---|---|
| Interest on FD from transferred ₹5,00,000 | 40,000 | Mr. Tarun (clubbed) |
| Dividend on shares bought out of the ₹40,000 interest | 3,000 | Mrs. Tarun (accretion — not clubbed) |

**Conclusion:** ₹40,000 is clubbed with Mr. Tarun; ₹3,000 (income on income) is taxed in the wife's hands; had it been an agreement to live apart, nothing would be clubbed.

### Q60. Ch: Advance Tax / TDS / TCS — Certificate for Lower/Nil Deduction (Sec 197 & 197A) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between a **certificate for deduction at a lower rate under section 197** and a **self-declaration for non-deduction under section 197A**. State who can apply and the effect of each.

**Answer:**
Both sections provide relief from the normal rigour of TDS where the recipient's tax liability is nil or lower than the tax otherwise deductible, but they operate differently.

**Section 197 — Certificate from the Assessing Officer:** Where the total income of the **recipient** justifies deduction of tax at a **lower rate or no deduction at all**, the recipient may make an **application to the Assessing Officer** (in Form 13). If the AO is satisfied, he issues a **certificate** authorising the payer to deduct tax at the lower rate (or nil) specified therein. The payer must then deduct at the certified rate until the certificate is cancelled/expires. Section 197 covers most TDS sections such as 192, 193, 194, 194A, 194C, 194H, 194-I, 194J, 194LA, 194Q, etc.

**Section 197A — Self-declaration by the recipient:** In specified cases the **recipient himself** furnishes a **self-declaration** (Form 15G for persons other than senior citizens / Form 15H for resident senior citizens) to the **payer**, declaring that his estimated total income is below the taxable limit and hence his tax is nil. On receipt of a valid declaration, the payer does **not deduct** tax. This facility applies to specified payments (e.g. section 193 interest on securities, 194A interest, 194 dividend, 194DA, 194-I rent, 194K, etc.). Form 15G cannot be filed if the aggregate income exceeds the basic exemption limit (this ceiling does not apply to Form 15H for senior citizens).

**Distinction**

| Basis | Section 197 | Section 197A |
|---|---|---|
| Relief given by | Certificate issued by the **Assessing Officer** | **Self-declaration** by the recipient to the payer |
| Form used | Form 13 (application) → AO's certificate | Form 15G / Form 15H |
| Nature of relief | **Lower rate or nil** deduction | **No deduction** (nil) |
| Who is eligible | Any recipient whose income justifies lower/nil TDS | Only specified recipients whose total tax is nil |
| Payments covered | Wide range of TDS sections including salary (192) | Only specified payments (not salary) |

**Conclusion:** Under section 197 the assessee obtains an **AO's certificate** for lower/nil TDS, whereas under section 197A eligible recipients file a **self-declaration (15G/15H)** to stop deduction where their tax liability is nil.

### Q63. Ch: Return Filing & Assessment — Defective Return u/s 139(9) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the provisions relating to a **defective return** under section 139(9). State the time allowed to rectify the defect, the consequence of non-rectification, and give any three instances treated as defects.

**Answer:**
Section 139(9) empowers the **Assessing Officer**, where he considers that a **return of income is defective**, to **intimate the defect** to the assessee and give him an opportunity to rectify it.

**Time allowed to rectify:** The assessee must rectify the defect within a period of **15 days** from the date of such intimation, or within such **further period** as the AO may, on an application, allow. The AO has discretion to condone the delay and treat a belatedly rectified return as valid even if rectified after the expiry of 15 days (but before assessment).

**Consequence of non-rectification:** If the defect is **not rectified** within the 15 days (or extended time), the **return shall be treated as an invalid return**, and the provisions of the Act shall apply **as if the assessee had failed to furnish the return** — attracting consequences such as interest, fee and possible best-judgment assessment.

**Instances treated as defects (any three):**

### Q64. Ch: Return Filing & Assessment — PAN–Aadhaar Linkage u/s 139AA & Fee u/s 234H (Marks: 4) [Theory]

### Q66. Ch: Return Filing & Assessment — Types of Assessment (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Briefly explain the following types of assessment under the Income-tax Act, 1961: (a) Summary assessment u/s 143(1); (b) Scrutiny assessment u/s 143(3); (c) Best judgment assessment u/s 144.

**Answer:**

**(a) Summary assessment — Section 143(1):** Every return furnished is processed and an **intimation** generated in which the total income/loss is computed after making specified adjustments — (i) any **arithmetical error** in the return; (ii) an **incorrect claim** apparent from the information in the return; (iii) disallowance of loss claimed if the return is belated; (iv) disallowance of expenditure/deduction indicated in the audit report but not in the return, etc. The tax, interest and fee are computed accordingly and any **refund due is granted or demand raised**. This processing must be completed within **nine months from the end of the financial year** in which the return is furnished. It is a **preliminary, prima facie** adjustment and involves no detailed enquiry.

**(b) Scrutiny assessment — Section 143(3):** Where the AO considers it necessary to ensure that the assessee has **not understated income, computed excessive loss, or underpaid tax**, a **notice u/s 143(2)** is served (within **3 months from the end of the financial year** in which the return is furnished). After hearing evidence and making enquiries, the AO passes a **detailed order** assessing the total income/loss and determining the tax payable/refundable. Such assessments are now generally made under the **faceless assessment scheme (Sec 144B)**.

**(c) Best judgment assessment — Section 144:** Where the assessee **fails to file the return** (u/s 139(1)/(4)/(5)), or **fails to comply** with a notice u/s 142(1)/143(2) or a direction for audit u/s 142(2A), the AO makes an assessment **to the best of his judgment** after taking into account all relevant material he has gathered and after giving the assessee an **opportunity of being heard**. The tax is determined on the income so estimated.

**Distinction (summary)**

| Basis | 143(1) | 143(3) | 144 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nature | Automated, prima facie | Detailed scrutiny | Estimated (default) |
| Trigger | Every return | Notice u/s 143(2) | Non-filing/non-compliance |
| Enquiry | Minimal adjustments | Full enquiry | Best judgment |

**Conclusion:** Section 143(1) is a computerised prima-facie processing; section 143(3) is a detailed scrutiny after a 143(2) notice; and section 144 is a best-judgment assessment triggered by the assessee's default, made after a hearing.

*(Full-marks tip: For each type state the trigger, the notice/time limit and the nature of enquiry. Mentioning the 143(2) notice time-limit and the "opportunity of being heard" under 144 are specific marks the examiner looks for.)*

### Q69. Ch: GST – Supply — Schedule II (Works Contract & Restaurant Service) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Section 7(1A) read with Schedule II to the CGST Act, 2017 classifies certain activities as "supply of goods" or "supply of services". Explain, with the specific Schedule II entries, why (i) a works contract relating to an immovable property and (ii) supply of food/drink by a restaurant are each treated as a **supply of services**, even though goods pass to the customer. Also state the entry that treats transfer of title in goods as supply of goods.

**Answer:**
Section 7(1A) provides that where certain activities constitute a supply in accordance with Section 7(1), they shall be treated **either as supply of goods or as supply of services** as referred to in Schedule II. Schedule II thus resolves the goods-versus-services classification for composite/ambiguous transactions.

**(i) Works contract [Para 6(a) of Schedule II].** A works contract as defined in Section 2(119) — a contract for building, construction, fabrication, erection, installation, fitting-out, improvement, modification, repair, maintenance, renovation, alteration or commissioning of any **immovable property** wherein transfer of property in goods (whether as goods or in some other form) is involved — is treated as a **composite supply** and, by Para 6(a) of Schedule II, is declared to be a **supply of services**. Consequently, the entire consideration (both the material and labour components) is taxed as a service; there is no artificial splitting of the goods portion. This overcomes the pre-GST controversy where States taxed the goods leg (VAT) and the Centre taxed the service leg.

**(ii) Restaurant / supply of food [Para 6(b) of Schedule II].** Supply, by way of or as part of any service, of goods being food or any other article for human consumption or drink (other than alcoholic liquor for human consumption), for cash, deferred payment or other valuable consideration, is by Para 6(b) declared to be a **supply of services**. Thus a restaurant, eating joint, mess or canteen supplies a service even though physical food (goods) changes hands, and the transaction is taxed under the restaurant-service rate rather than as a goods supply.

**Transfer of title as supply of goods [Para 1(a) of Schedule II].** Any transfer of the **title in goods** is a supply of goods. (By contrast, transfer of right in goods or of undivided share without transfer of title, and transfer of title under an agreement that property shall pass at a future date upon payment of full consideration, are supply of services / goods respectively under Para 1(b) and Para 1(c).)

| Activity | Schedule II entry | Treated as |
|---|---|---|
| Works contract (immovable property) | Para 6(a) | Supply of **services** |
| Food/drink by restaurant | Para 6(b) | Supply of **services** |
| Transfer of title in goods | Para 1(a) | Supply of **goods** |

**Conclusion:** Both works contract and restaurant supply, though involving movement of goods, are statutorily deemed supplies of **services** under Para 6 of Schedule II, whereas transfer of title in goods is a supply of goods under Para 1(a).

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards quoting the exact paragraph numbers of Schedule II — Para 6(a), 6(b), 1(a) — and the Section 2(119) definition of works contract restricted to immovable property. Marks are lost for merely writing "it is a service" without the entry, or for saying works contract on movable property is also a service.)*

### Q70. Ch: GST – Supply — Schedule III (Activities as Neither Supply of Goods nor Services) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** State whether GST is attracted on each of the following, citing the relevant entry of Schedule III (or the relevant proviso) to the CGST Act, 2017. Give a one-line reason for each.

| Sale of land/plot | Para 5 | Not leviable |
| Services by MP | Para 3 | Not leviable |
| Warehoused goods before home consumption | Para 8(a) | Not leviable |

### Q71. Ch: GST – Supply — Continuous Supply & Composite/Mixed Supply (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** (a) Define "continuous supply of goods" and "continuous supply of services" under Section 2(32) and 2(33) of the CGST Act, 2017, with one example each. (b) Determine whether each of the following is a **composite supply** or a **mixed supply** under Section 2(30)/2(74), identify the **principal supply** where relevant, and state the tax rate applicable.

| Case | Facts (rates given) |
|---|---|
| A | A dealer supplies a laptop (18%) with a pre-loaded laptop bag (28%) and a mouse (18%) for a single price; the bag and mouse are supplied along with the laptop as a package. |
| B | For ₹1,000 a shopkeeper supplies a gift hamper of chocolates (18%), aerated drinks (28%) and dry fruits (12%), each capable of separate supply, for a single price. |
| C | Annual maintenance contract for a lift: periodic servicing (18%) with incidental replacement of spare parts (28%), billed as one AMC. |

**Answer:**
**(a) Continuous supply — Section 2(32) and 2(33).**
"**Continuous supply of goods**" [Section 2(32)] means a supply of goods which is provided, or agreed to be provided, **continuously or on recurrent basis, under a contract**, whether or not by means of a wire, cable, pipeline or other conduit, and for which the supplier invoices the recipient on a **regular or periodic basis** and includes supply of such goods as the Government may notify. *Example:* supply of gases through a dedicated pipeline with monthly invoicing.

"**Continuous supply of services**" [Section 2(33)] means a supply of services which is provided, or agreed to be provided, continuously or on recurrent basis, under a contract, for a **period exceeding three months** with periodic payment obligations, and includes supply of such services as the Government may notify. *Example:* an annual works-contract / annual maintenance service with monthly billing.

**(b) Composite vs Mixed supply.**
A **composite supply** [Section 2(30)] is two or more taxable supplies **naturally bundled** and supplied in conjunction in the ordinary course of business, one of which is a **principal supply**; it is taxed at the rate of the **principal supply** [Section 8(a)]. A **mixed supply** [Section 2(74)] is two or more individual supplies made together for a single price which are **not naturally bundled**; it is taxed at the rate of that particular supply which attracts the **highest rate of tax** [Section 8(b)].

| Case | Nature | Principal supply | Rate applicable |
|---|---|---|---|
| A – laptop + bag + mouse | **Composite** (bag & mouse naturally supplied with laptop) | Laptop | **18%** (rate of principal supply) |
| B – gift hamper | **Mixed** (items independently saleable, not naturally bundled, single price) | Not applicable | **28%** (highest rate — aerated drinks) |
| C – lift AMC with spares | **Composite** (service of maintenance is principal; spares incidental) | Maintenance service | **18%** (rate of principal supply) |

**Conclusion:** Case A and Case C are composite supplies taxed at 18% (principal supply); Case B is a mixed supply taxed at the highest rate of 28%.

*(Full-marks tip: State the "single price" and "not naturally bundled" test for a mixed supply and the "naturally bundled + principal supply" test for composite. Marks are lost if a student taxes a composite supply at the highest rate — that rule belongs only to mixed supply under Section 8(b).)*

| S.No | Transaction |
|---|---|
| (i) | Permanent transfer of a laptop (on which ITC was availed) by a company to its director free of charge |
| (ii) | Diwali gift of ₹40,000 by an employer to one employee during the financial year |
| (iii) | Diwali gift of ₹1,20,000 (aggregate) by an employer to another employee during the financial year |
| (iv) | Stock transfer of goods by the Mumbai (Maharashtra) branch to the Delhi branch of the same company, both registered |
| (v) | Supply of goods by a principal to his agent who undertakes to supply such goods on behalf of the principal |

**Answer:**
Schedule I lists activities treated as supply **even without consideration**.

**(i) Free transfer of business asset with ITC — Supply.** Para 1 of Schedule I covers "permanent transfer or disposal of **business assets** where **input tax credit has been availed** on such assets." Since ITC was availed on the laptop and it is permanently transferred free of charge, it is a supply liable to GST (value under Section 15/valuation rules).

**(ii) Gift of ₹40,000 to employee — Not a supply.** By the proviso to Para 2 of Schedule I, gifts **not exceeding ₹50,000 in value in a financial year** by an employer to an employee are **not** treated as a supply. ₹40,000 is within the limit — no GST.

**(iii) Gift of ₹1,20,000 to employee — Supply (excess).** As the aggregate gift exceeds ₹50,000 in the financial year, the proviso does not apply and it is treated as a supply between related persons (employer and employee being related). GST is attracted (on the full value, the ₹50,000 relief being only a threshold to exclude small gifts, not an exemption slab).

**(iv) Inter-State stock transfer between branches — Supply.** Para 2 of Schedule I treats supply of goods/services between **related persons or distinct persons** (Section 25) made in the course of business as a supply even without consideration. Two registrations in different States are **distinct persons** [Section 25(4)], so the Mumbai-to-Delhi stock transfer is a supply liable to IGST.

**(v) Principal to agent — Supply.** Para 3 of Schedule I treats supply of goods by a **principal to his agent** where the agent undertakes to supply such goods on behalf of the principal as a supply even without consideration. Hence liable to GST.

| Transaction | Schedule I entry | Supply? |
|---|---|---|
| Free laptop (ITC availed) to director | Para 1 | Yes |
| Gift ₹40,000 to employee | Proviso to Para 2 | No |
| Gift ₹1,20,000 to employee | Para 2 | Yes |
| Inter-State branch stock transfer | Para 2 (distinct persons) | Yes |
| Principal → agent | Para 3 | Yes |

**Conclusion:** Items (i), (iii), (iv) and (v) are supplies liable to GST; item (ii) is not, being within the ₹50,000 employer-gift threshold.

*(Full-marks tip: The ₹50,000 test is per financial year and, once breached, the whole gift (not just the excess) is taxable. Note that Para 1 requires ITC to have been availed on the asset — a free supply of an asset on which no ITC was taken is not a Para 1 supply.)*

### Q73. Ch: GST – Supply — Scope of Supply & Business Test (Marks: 4) [Application]
**Question:** With reference to Section 7(1)(a) and the definition of "business" in Section 2(17), examine whether GST is attracted in the following independent cases:
(i) Mr. A, a salaried individual, sells his old personal car to a friend for ₹3,00,000. He is not registered under GST.
(ii) A charitable trust registered under Section 12AA of the Income-tax Act sells advertisement space in its souvenir for ₹5,00,000.
(iii) A registered dealer disposes of old office furniture (business asset, ITC availed) for ₹40,000.

**Answer:**
Section 7(1)(a) provides that "supply" includes all forms of supply of goods or services such as sale, transfer, barter, exchange, etc., made or agreed to be made **for a consideration** by a person **in the course or furtherance of business**. Both a consideration and a business nexus are ordinarily required.

**(i) Sale of personal car by a salaried person — Not a supply.** Although there is a consideration (₹3,00,000), the sale is **not in the course or furtherance of business** — the car is a personal asset and Mr. A is not carrying on business in cars. Hence not a supply; no GST. (He is also below threshold and unregistered.)

**(ii) Sale of advertisement space by charitable trust — Supply.** "Business" under Section 2(17) includes any trade, commerce, or **any activity or transaction in the nature of trade/commerce whether or not for pecuniary benefit**, and includes services in connection therewith. Sale of advertisement space is a commercial activity for consideration and is taxable; charitable status does not exempt this activity. GST is attracted (subject to registration threshold).

**(iii) Disposal of business furniture with ITC — Supply.** Section 2(17) treats disposal of **business assets** as business, and Para 4(a) of Schedule II treats transfer/disposal of business assets as supply of goods. As it is made in the course of business for consideration, GST is payable on ₹40,000.

**Conclusion:** Cases (ii) and (iii) are supplies liable to GST; case (i), lacking a business nexus, is not a supply.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner looks for both limbs — consideration AND course/furtherance of business. A private, one-off sale of a personal asset fails the business test; disposal of business assets passes it under Section 2(17) and Schedule II Para 4.)*

### Q74. Ch: GST – Charge & RCM — Reverse Charge on Import of Service (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Examine the reverse-charge liability, if any, in the following independent cases under Section 5(3) of the IGST Act, 2017 and Notification No. 10/2017-IGST (RCM). State who is liable to pay and give reasons.

| Case | Facts |
|---|---|
| A | XYZ Ltd. (registered, Mumbai) imports management-consultancy service worth ₹8,00,000 from a firm located in Singapore, for use in its business. |
| B | Mr. P, an unregistered individual in India, downloads and pays USD 20 for an OIDAR (cloud storage) service from a US supplier for **personal** use. |
| C | An Indian company receives legal advisory service from a foreign law firm; consideration ₹2,00,000. |

**Answer:**
Under Section 5(3) of the IGST Act, the Government may notify categories of supply on which **IGST is payable on reverse charge by the recipient**. Import of service is defined in Section 2(11) of the IGST Act (supplier outside India, recipient in India, place of supply in India). Notification No. 10/2017-IGST specifies that on **"any service supplied by a person located in a non-taxable territory to any person located in the taxable territory"** (other than non-taxable online recipient), the recipient located in the taxable territory is liable to pay IGST under RCM.

**Case A — XYZ Ltd. liable under RCM.** The supplier (Singapore) is in a non-taxable territory and the recipient (XYZ Ltd., Mumbai) is in the taxable territory and is a registered business recipient. Therefore XYZ Ltd. must pay **IGST on ₹8,00,000 under reverse charge** and can, subject to Section 16/17, avail ITC of the same as it is used in business.

**Case B — Special rule for OIDAR to unregistered person.** Where an OIDAR service is supplied by a person in a non-taxable territory to a **"non-taxable online recipient"** (an unregistered person receiving it for purposes other than business), the reverse charge does **not** apply; instead the **overseas supplier** (or its intermediary) is liable to take registration and pay IGST. Hence Mr. P is not liable under RCM; the US OIDAR supplier must discharge the tax.

**Case C — Indian company liable under RCM.** Legal advisory service imported from a foreign law firm is a service supplied from a non-taxable territory to a taxable-territory recipient; the Indian company pays **IGST ₹2,00,000 under reverse charge** and may avail ITC subject to eligibility.

| Case | Import of service? | Person liable |
|---|---|---|
| A – consultancy from Singapore | Yes | Recipient (XYZ Ltd.) under RCM |
| B – OIDAR to unregistered individual | Yes | Overseas supplier (not RCM) |
| C – foreign legal service to company | Yes | Recipient (Indian company) under RCM |

**Conclusion:** In A and C the Indian recipient pays IGST under reverse charge; in B, being OIDAR to a non-taxable online recipient, the **foreign supplier** must register and pay — reverse charge does not apply.

*(Full-marks tip: Distinguish the general import-of-service RCM (recipient pays) from the OIDAR-to-unregistered-consumer rule (supplier pays). Marks are lost for putting RCM on Mr. P — an unregistered personal consumer cannot discharge RCM, so the law shifts liability to the overseas OIDAR supplier.)*

**Question:** For each service below, state under Notification No. 13/2017-CTR whether tax is payable under **forward charge or reverse charge**, and who is liable.

### Q76. Ch: GST – Charge & RCM — Composition Scheme Conditions (Section 10) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** State the eligibility conditions and the tax rates for the composition levy under Section 10 of the CGST Act, 2017 for a manufacturer/trader. List any four categories of persons who are **not eligible** to opt for the scheme, and state the restrictions imposed on a composition dealer.

**Answer:**
Section 10(1) permits a registered person whose **aggregate turnover in the preceding financial year did not exceed ₹1.5 crore** (₹75 lakh for specified special-category States) to opt to pay tax at a composition rate, in lieu of the normal tax.

**Composition rates [Section 10(1) read with Rule 7]:**

| Category | CGST + SGST (effective) |
|---|---|
| Manufacturer (other than notified goods) | 0.5% + 0.5% = **1%** of turnover in State/UT |
| Trader (supplier of goods) | 0.5% + 0.5% = **1%** of taxable turnover of goods |
| Restaurant / food service (not serving alcohol) | 2.5% + 2.5% = **5%** of turnover |

Under **Section 10(2A)**, a supplier of services (or mixed supplier) not eligible under 10(1) may opt for the composition levy if turnover in the preceding year did not exceed **₹50 lakh**, paying **3% + 3% = 6%**.

**Persons NOT eligible [Section 10(2)] (any four):**
1. A person engaged in supply of services other than restaurant service (except the permitted supply of services up to the higher of 10% of turnover or ₹5 lakh under the proviso to Section 10(1)).
2. A person making any supply of goods/services which are **not leviable** to tax under the Act.
3. A person making any **inter-State outward supply** of goods.
4. A person making supply through an **electronic commerce operator** required to collect TCS under Section 52.
5. A manufacturer of **notified goods** (e.g., ice cream, pan masala, tobacco, aerated water).
6. A casual taxable person or a non-resident taxable person.

**Restrictions on a composition dealer:**
1. He **cannot collect tax** from recipients (tax is out of his own pocket) [Section 10(4)].
2. He is **not entitled to input tax credit**.
3. He must issue a **bill of supply**, not a tax invoice, mentioning "composition taxable person, not eligible to collect tax on supplies".
4. He must display "composition taxable person" on his signboard and business premises.
5. All registrations under the same PAN must opt for composition together.

**Conclusion:** A person within the turnover limits and not falling in the excluded categories may pay tax at 1%/5% (goods) or 6% (services), but forgoes ITC and the right to collect tax and cannot make inter-State outward supplies.

*(Full-marks tip: Quote the ₹1.5 crore / ₹75 lakh (goods) and ₹50 lakh (services, 10(2A)) limits and the 1%/5%/6% rates precisely, and mention the proviso allowing incidental services up to 10% of turnover or ₹5 lakh. The most common deduction is forgetting the "no inter-State outward supply" and "no ITC / no tax collection" bars.)*

### Q78. Ch: GST – Charge & RCM — Persons Liable to Register & RCM Interaction (Marks: 4) [Application]
**Question:** State, with reasons, whether registration under GST is compulsory in the following cases:
(i) Mr. B makes only outward supplies that are entirely taxable under **reverse charge** in the hands of the recipient; his turnover is ₹60 lakh.
(ii) Mr. C is required to pay tax under **reverse charge** on inward supplies, though his own turnover is only ₹8 lakh.
(iii) Ms. D, an agriculturist, supplies produce cultivated out of her own land.

**Answer:**
Registration is governed by Sections 22, 23 and 24 of the CGST Act.

**(i) Supplier making only RCM outward supplies — Not liable.** Under Section 23(1)(a) / the notification exempting such persons, a person engaged **exclusively in supplies on which the whole of tax is payable by the recipient under reverse charge** is **not required to register**, regardless of turnover. Hence Mr. B (₹60 lakh) need not register.

**(ii) Person liable to pay RCM — Compulsory registration.** Section 24(iii) mandates **compulsory registration**, irrespective of the threshold, for persons who are **required to pay tax under reverse charge**. Therefore Mr. C must register even though his turnover (₹8 lakh) is below the threshold.

**(iii) Agriculturist — Not liable.** Section 23(1)(b) provides that an **agriculturist**, to the extent of supply of produce out of cultivation of land, is **not liable to registration**. Hence Ms. D need not register.

**Conclusion:** Compulsory registration applies only to Mr. C (RCM payer, Section 24(iii)); Mr. B (exclusive RCM outward supplier) and Ms. D (agriculturist) are exempt under Section 23.

*(Full-marks tip: Distinguish the two RCM situations — a person whose *outward* supplies are all under RCM is exempt from registration (Section 23), whereas a person liable to *pay* RCM on inward supplies must compulsorily register (Section 24(iii)). Mixing these up is the standard error.)*

### Q82. Ch: GST – Time & Value of Supply — Value with Subsidy (Section 15) (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** M/s Green Agro Ltd. supplies a batch of goods. From the following, compute the **transaction value** and GST payable @ 18%. All amounts are exclusive of GST unless stated.

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| List price of goods | 5,00,000 |
| Municipal tax charged separately on the supply | 20,000 |
| Packing charges | 15,000 |
| Subsidy received from a **private trust** (directly linked to price; not from Government) | 30,000 |
| Subsidy received from the **State Government** (linked to price) | 50,000 |
| Discount of 2% on list price, shown on the invoice | 10,000 |
| Interest for delayed payment actually recovered from the customer | 5,000 |

**Solution:**

### Q83. Ch: GST – Time & Value of Supply — Value between Related/Distinct Persons (Rule 28) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** (a) State how the value of a supply between **related or distinct persons** (other than through an agent) is determined under Rule 28 of the CGST Rules, 2017. (b) Head Office (Mumbai) of ABC Ltd. transfers goods to its Branch (Chennai), a distinct person. Open market value ₹1,00,000; goods of like kind and quality valued at ₹95,000; the branch is **eligible for full ITC**. HO issues the invoice at ₹80,000. Determine the value of supply.

**Answer:**
**(a) Rule 28 — Related/distinct persons.** The value of a supply of goods or services between **distinct persons (Section 25(4)/(5))** or **related persons** (other than through an agent) shall be:
1. the **open market value (OMV)** of such supply;
2. if OMV is not available, the value of supply of **goods or services of like kind and quality**;
3. if value cannot be determined under (1) or (2), the value as per **Rule 30 (cost + 10%)** or **Rule 31 (reasonable means)**, in that order.

**First proviso:** where goods are intended for **further supply as such** by the recipient, the value may, at the supplier's option, be **90% of the price charged by the recipient** to his unrelated customer.

**Second proviso (the key relief):** where the **recipient is eligible for full input tax credit**, the value declared in the invoice shall be deemed to be the **open market value** of the goods or services.

**(b) Application.** HO (Mumbai) and Branch (Chennai) are **distinct persons** under Section 25(4); hence Rule 28 applies. Although OMV is ₹1,00,000, the **second proviso** applies because the recipient branch is **eligible for full ITC** — the whole transaction is revenue-neutral. Therefore the **value declared in the invoice, ₹80,000, is deemed to be the open market value** and is accepted as the value of supply.

**Conclusion:** The value of supply is **₹80,000** (invoice value), by virtue of the second proviso to Rule 28, since the recipient is entitled to full ITC.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner specifically tests the second proviso — when the recipient is eligible for full ITC, any invoice value is deemed OMV, so ₹80,000 stands even though OMV is ₹1,00,000. Marks are lost for mechanically taking OMV ₹1,00,000 and ignoring the full-ITC proviso.)*

### Q84. Ch: GST – Time & Value of Supply — Time of Supply of Services & RCM (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** Determine the **time of supply** in each independent case under Sections 13 and the RCM provisions of the CGST Act, 2017.

| Case | Facts |
|---|---|
| A | Date of provision of service: 10-Aug; Invoice issued: 20-Aug (within 30 days); Payment received: 25-Aug. (Forward charge) |
| B | Date of provision of service: 10-Aug; Invoice issued: 25-Sep (i.e., beyond 30 days); Payment received: 5-Sep. (Forward charge) |
| C | Import of service under **reverse charge**: Date of payment entered in recipient's books: 15-Oct; Date of invoice by foreign supplier: 30-Sep; Payment debited from bank: 18-Oct. |
| D | Supply of **vouchers** by a supplier where the supply (goods) is identifiable at the time of issue of the voucher; voucher issued 1-Nov, redeemed 20-Dec. |

**Solution:**

**WN — Governing rules.**
- **Section 13(2) (forward charge):** time of supply of services is the **earliest of** — (a) date of invoice if issued within the period prescribed under Section 31 (30 days), or date of provision of service if the invoice is **not** issued within that period; and (b) date of receipt of payment; whichever is earlier.
- **Section 13(3) (reverse charge):** time of supply is the **earlier of** — (a) date of payment as entered in the books of the recipient or date of debit in his bank account, whichever is earlier; or (b) the date **immediately following 60 days** from the date of invoice. For **import of service from an associated enterprise**, it is the date of entry in the books or date of payment, whichever is earlier.
- **Section 12(4)/13(4) (vouchers):** if the supply is **identifiable** at the point of issue, time of supply is the **date of issue** of the voucher; otherwise the **date of redemption**.

**Determination.**

| Case | Reasoning | Time of Supply |
|---|---|---|
| A | Invoice (20-Aug) issued within 30 days → compare invoice date (20-Aug) vs payment (25-Aug); earliest | **20-Aug** |
| B | Invoice issued beyond 30 days → take date of provision (10-Aug) vs payment (5-Sep); earliest | **10-Aug** (date of service provision) |
| C | RCM: earlier of payment date in books (15-Oct)/bank debit (18-Oct) = 15-Oct, **or** 61st day from invoice (30-Sep + 60 = 29-Nov, so 30-Nov). Earlier = 15-Oct | **15-Oct** |
| D | Voucher with identifiable supply → date of issue | **1-Nov** |

**Answer:** Time of supply — Case A: **20-Aug**; Case B: **10-Aug**; Case C: **15-Oct**; Case D: **1-Nov**.

*(Full-marks tip: In forward charge, the crucial test is whether the invoice was issued within 30 days — if not, the date of provision of service replaces the invoice date. In RCM, remember the 60-day rule and that the "date of payment" limb uses the earlier of book-entry and bank-debit dates.)*

### Q85. Ch: GST – Input Tax Credit — ITC on inputs/capital goods sent to job-worker u/s 143 (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** M/s Precision Forgings Ltd., a registered manufacturer in Pune, sends inputs and capital goods to a job-worker for further processing. Examine the availability of input tax credit and the consequences under section 143 of the CGST Act, 2017 read with section 19, in each of the following independent situations:

| Case | Facts |
|------|-------|
| (a) | Inputs sent directly to the job-worker's premises from the vendor's place on 05.04.2024 without being first brought to the principal's factory. |
| (b) | Inputs sent to the job-worker on 10.05.2024 are not received back within 1 year; they are actually received back on 20.08.2025. |
| (c) | Capital goods (moulds and dies) sent to the job-worker on 01.06.2024 are not returned within 3 years. |
| (d) | Moulds, dies, jigs and fixtures sent to the job-worker and not returned within 3 years. |

**Answer:**
The principal is entitled to take input tax credit on inputs and capital goods sent to a job-worker under section 19 of the CGST Act, 2017, and the conditions and time-limits for their receipt back are governed by section 143.

**(a) Inputs sent directly to job-worker's premises:** Under section 19(2) of the CGST Act, 2017, the principal is entitled to take credit of input tax on inputs even if the inputs are directly sent to a job-worker without being first brought to his place of business. The credit is fully available. The one-year period for receiving the goods back is reckoned **from the date of receipt of inputs by the job-worker** [proviso to section 143(1)]. Hence credit is available on 05.04.2024 and the one-year clock runs from the date the job-worker receives the inputs.

**(b) Inputs not received back within 1 year:** Under section 143(3) of the CGST Act, 2017, where the inputs sent for job-work are not received back by the principal within **one year** of being sent out (or, for direct despatch, from the date of receipt by the job-worker), it shall be **deemed that such inputs had been supplied by the principal to the job-worker on the day they were sent out**. The principal must therefore pay tax on such deemed supply along with interest. When the inputs are actually received back on 20.08.2025, the principal may take re-credit of the tax so paid.

**(c) & (d) Capital goods / moulds & dies not returned within 3 years:** Under section 143(4), if **capital goods** are not received back within **three years**, they are deemed to have been supplied on the day they were sent out. **However**, under the proviso to section 143(4)/(5), the deeming provision **does not apply to moulds, dies, jigs, fixtures or tools** sent to the job-worker for job-work. Therefore:
- Case (c): moulds and dies are covered by the exception — **no deemed supply**, no tax payable on non-return.
- Case (d): same exception applies — **no deemed supply** for moulds, dies, jigs and fixtures.

**Conclusion:** ITC is available in all four cases; deemed supply with interest arises only in case (b); no deemed supply arises for moulds/dies/jigs/fixtures in cases (c) and (d).

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards distinguishing the 1-year rule for inputs from the 3-year rule for capital goods, and specifically citing the moulds/dies/jigs/fixtures exclusion. A common deduction is forgetting that the time-limit runs from the job-worker's date of receipt when goods are sent directly.)*

### Q92. Ch: GST – Registration — Cancellation of registration (Sec 29 / Rule 21, 21A) (Marks: 6) [Application]

### Q93. Ch: GST – Registration — Revocation of cancellation (Sec 30 / Rule 23) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the provisions relating to **revocation of cancellation of registration** under section 30 of the CGST Act, 2017 read with rule 23 of the CGST Rules, 2017, including the time-limit, the requirement of filing pending returns, and the forms used.

**Answer:**
Under **section 30(1)** of the CGST Act, 2017, where the registration of a person has been **cancelled by the proper officer on his own motion** (suo motu, i.e., not on the taxpayer's own request), the registered person may apply for **revocation of cancellation**.

**Time-limit:** The application must be made within **ninety (90) days** from the date of service of the cancellation order. This period may be **extended by a further period not exceeding 180 days** by the Additional/Joint Commissioner or Commissioner on being satisfied of sufficient cause (proviso to section 30(1)). Thus, the maximum outer limit can extend to 90 + 180 days.

**Procedure (Rule 23):**
1. The person applies in **Form GST REG-21** to the proper officer.
2. Where the registration was cancelled for **failure to furnish returns**, the application for revocation shall be filed **only after** furnishing all pending returns and paying the tax due along with **interest, penalty and late fee**.
3. If satisfied, the proper officer revokes the cancellation by an order in **Form GST REG-22** within **30 days** of the application.
4. If not satisfied, the officer issues a **notice in Form GST REG-23**; the applicant replies in **Form GST REG-24** within 7 working days; and the officer disposes of the reply within 30 days.

**Conclusion:** Revocation is available only for suo-motu cancellations, on an application in REG-21 within 90 days (extendable up to 180 more days), after clearing all pending returns and dues; the officer orders revocation in REG-22 within 30 days.

*(Full-marks tip: Note that revocation is NOT available where the taxpayer voluntarily applied for cancellation, and that pending returns/dues must be cleared first. Common deduction: quoting only 30 days as the application limit — that is the officer's disposal period, not the applicant's.)*

### Q94. Ch: GST – Tax Invoice, Payment & Returns — Tax invoice contents & time-limit of issue (Sec 31 / Rule 46) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the **time-limit for issuing a tax invoice** under section 31 of the CGST Act, 2017 for supply of goods and supply of services. Also state any five particulars that a tax invoice must contain under rule 46 of the CGST Rules, 2017.

**Answer:**
Under **section 31** of the CGST Act, 2017, a registered person supplying taxable goods or services must issue a tax invoice within the prescribed time.

**Time-limit for goods [section 31(1)]:**
- Where the supply **involves movement** of goods — before or at the time of **removal** of goods for supply to the recipient.
- Where the supply **does not involve movement** — before or at the time of **delivery** of goods or making available to the recipient.
- **Continuous supply of goods** [section 31(4)] — where successive statements of account/successive payments are involved, the invoice is issued **before or at the time each statement is issued or each payment is received**.
- **Goods sent on approval / sale-or-return** [section 31(7)] — invoice issued **before or at the time of supply, or six months from the date of removal, whichever is earlier**.

**Time-limit for services [section 31(2) read with rule 47]:** A tax invoice for services must be issued **within 30 days** from the date of supply of service. For an **insurer, banking company, financial institution or NBFC**, the period is **45 days**.

**Five particulars under rule 46:**
1. Name, address and **GSTIN** of the supplier.
2. A **consecutive serial number** (up to 16 characters, unique for a financial year).
3. **Date of issue** of the invoice.
4. Name, address and GSTIN/UIN of the recipient (and, if unregistered and value ≥ ₹50,000, address of delivery with State).
5. **HSN code** of goods/services, description, quantity, total value, taxable value, rate and amount of tax (CGST/SGST/IGST separately), place of supply for inter-State, and whether tax is payable on **reverse charge** — with signature/digital signature of the supplier.

**Conclusion:** Goods — invoice by removal/delivery; services — within 30 days (45 for banking/NBFC); and the invoice must carry the rule 46 mandatory particulars.

*(Full-marks tip: Quote the 30-day (45 for banks) service rule and the goods movement/no-movement distinction. Common deduction: mixing up the 30-day service limit with goods.)*

### Q95. Ch: GST – Tax Invoice, Payment & Returns — Debit notes & credit notes (Sec 34) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Explain the provisions of **section 34** of the CGST Act, 2017 relating to issue of **credit notes and debit notes**, including the time-limit for declaring a credit note in returns. Then decide, with reasons, whether a credit note or debit note is to be issued in the following independent cases by M/s Vertex Ltd. (original invoice dated 15.05.2024):

| Case | Facts |
|------|-------|
| (a) | Goods worth ₹1,00,000 returned by the buyer as defective. |
| (b) | Taxable value in the original invoice was under-charged by ₹20,000. |
| (c) | Post-sale discount of ₹10,000 given as per a pre-agreed contract, ITC of which is reversed by the recipient. |
| (d) | Buyer sanctioned a rate difference resulting in higher price of ₹15,000. |

**Answer:**
Under **section 34(1)** of the CGST Act, 2017, where a tax invoice has been issued and the **taxable value or tax charged is found to exceed** the actual value/tax, or where goods are **returned** or found **deficient**, the supplier may issue a **credit note** to the recipient. Under **section 34(3)**, where the **taxable value or tax charged is less** than the actual, the supplier shall issue a **debit note** (which includes a supplementary invoice).

**Time-limit for credit notes [section 34(2)]:** Details of a credit note must be declared in the return for the month during which it is issued, but **not later than 30th November** following the end of the financial year in which the supply was made, **or** the date of furnishing the annual return, **whichever is earlier**. The tax liability is adjusted **only if the recipient reverses the corresponding ITC**. **Debit notes** have **no such time-limit** — but ITC on a debit note is available up to 30th November of the following FY reckoned from the debit-note's own date [section 16(4)].

**Application:**

| Case | Document | Reason |
|------|----------|--------|
| (a) | **Credit note** | Goods returned — value/tax originally charged now exceeds actual [section 34(1)]. |
| (b) | **Debit note** | Taxable value was under-charged — supplier must charge the differential [section 34(3)]. |
| (c) | **Credit note** | Post-sale discount known/agreed before supply and linked to invoices; permissible reduction only if recipient reverses proportionate ITC [section 15(3)(b) + section 34]. |
| (d) | **Debit note** | Upward rate revision → additional value/tax → debit note [section 34(3)]. |

**Conclusion:** Credit notes in cases (a) and (c) (subject to the 30 November limit and recipient's ITC reversal); debit notes in cases (b) and (d) (no upper time-limit for issue).

*(Full-marks tip: Stress that a credit note reduces liability only if the recipient's ITC is correspondingly reversed, and quote the 30-November limit. Common deduction: applying the 30-Nov limit to debit notes.)*

### Q96. Ch: GST – Tax Invoice, Payment & Returns — GSTR-1 vs GSTR-2B / GSTR-3B (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between **Form GSTR-1, Form GSTR-2B and Form GSTR-3B**. State the due dates and explain how GSTR-1 and GSTR-2B are linked in the process of availing input tax credit.

**Answer:**
These are the core monthly return forms under section 37, section 38 and section 39 of the CGST Act, 2017 read with the CGST Rules.

| Basis | GSTR-1 | GSTR-2B | GSTR-3B |
|-------|--------|---------|---------|
| Nature | Statement of **outward supplies** (section 37) | **Auto-drafted, static** ITC statement (section 38) | Monthly **summary return** cum payment (section 39) |
| Filed by | Every registered supplier (not composition/ISD/CTP-exempt) | System-generated — not filed by taxpayer | Every regular registered person |
| Content | Details of invoices, debit/credit notes of outward supplies | ITC available/ineligible, based on suppliers' GSTR-1/IFF/GSTR-5/6 | Summary of outward supplies, ITC claimed, net tax payable |
| Due date | **11th** of the following month (monthly); IFF/quarterly under QRMP | Generated on **14th** of the following month | **20th** of the following month (or 22nd/24th staggered under QRMP) |
| Editable | Yes, by supplier | **No** — static snapshot | Yes, by taxpayer |

**Linkage (GSTR-1 → GSTR-2B → ITC):** When a supplier files **GSTR-1** (or IFF), the invoice details flow to the recipient's **GSTR-2B**, which is a static statement generated on the 14th. Under **section 16(2)(aa)** read with **rule 36(4)**, a recipient can avail ITC in his **GSTR-3B only to the extent the invoice is reflected in GSTR-2B**. Credit not appearing in GSTR-2B (e.g., because the supplier has not filed GSTR-1) is **not admissible**. Thus GSTR-1 filed by the supplier is the "source" that populates the recipient's GSTR-2B, which in turn caps the ITC claimable in GSTR-3B. Rule 88C/GSTR-3B vs GSTR-1 mismatch may also trigger intimation in **Form DRC-01B**.

**Conclusion:** GSTR-1 (outward, 11th) auto-populates GSTR-2B (14th), which determines admissible ITC in GSTR-3B (20th); ITC is restricted strictly to GSTR-2B-reflected credit.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner wants the 11th → 14th → 20th chain and the 16(2)(aa)/36(4) matching rule. Common deduction: calling GSTR-2B a "return to be filed" — it is auto-drafted and static.)*

### Q97. Ch: GST – Tax Invoice, Payment & Returns — Late fee & interest on delayed filing (Sec 47 & 50) (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** M/s Horizon Ltd. (regular taxpayer, monthly filer) was required to file its GSTR-3B for September 2024 by 20.10.2024 but actually filed it on **04.11.2024**. Its net tax liability for September 2024 was ₹4,00,000, of which ₹2,50,000 was discharged from the electronic credit ledger and ₹1,50,000 was payable in cash (paid on 04.11.2024). Compute the **late fee under section 47** and **interest under section 50** payable. (Assume the return is not a NIL return; CGST + SGST late fee is ₹50 per day capped as per notification; interest rate 18% p.a.; take a 365-day year.)

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Period of delay**
Due date 20.10.2024; filed 04.11.2024. Delay = 11 days (21 Oct to 31 Oct = 11 days; 1 Nov to 4 Nov = 4 days) → **15 days**.
(Days from 21.10.2024 to 04.11.2024 = 15 days.)

**WN-2: Late fee under section 47**
For a non-NIL return, late fee is ₹25 CGST + ₹25 SGST = **₹50 per day**.
Late fee = 15 days × ₹50 = **₹750** (CGST ₹375 + SGST ₹375). (Well within the prescribed cap.)

**WN-3: Interest under section 50**
Interest under section 50(1) is levied @ 18% p.a. on the tax paid **in cash** (net cash liability), i.e., on the portion of output tax paid by debiting the electronic cash ledger, for the period of delay. As per the proviso to section 50(1)/rule 88B, interest is charged only on the net tax liability paid through the cash ledger.
Interest base = ₹1,50,000 (cash portion). ITC-utilised portion (₹2,50,000) does **not** attract interest.
Interest = 1,50,000 × 18% × (15 ÷ 365) = 1,50,000 × 0.18 × 0.041096 = **₹1,109** (rounded).

Formula: Interest = Principal × Rate × (Days ÷ 365) = 1,50,000 × 18/100 × 15/365 = ₹1,109.59 ≈ **₹1,110**.

**Statement Showing Late Fee and Interest — GSTR-3B, Sept 2024**

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|-------------|-----------|
| Days of delay | 15 |
| Late fee @ ₹50/day (CGST 375 + SGST 375) | 750 |
| Interest @ 18% p.a. on ₹1,50,000 (cash) for 15 days | 1,110 |
| **Total late fee + interest** | **1,860** |

**Answer:** Late fee = **₹750**; Interest = **₹1,110** (approx); total additional payment = **₹1,860**. Interest is charged only on the ₹1,50,000 net cash liability, not on the ₹2,50,000 discharged through ITC.

*(Full-marks tip: Post-2021 proviso to section 50(1) charges interest only on the net cash-paid tax when the return is filed after due date — a favourite examiner point. Common deduction: charging 18% on the full ₹4,00,000.)*

### Q98. Ch: GST – Tax Invoice, Payment & Returns — Electronic ledgers & order of discharge (Sec 49) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the three **electronic ledgers/registers** maintained on the common portal under section 49 of the CGST Act, 2017 and rule 86/87/88. State the **order** in which every taxable person shall discharge his tax and other dues under section 49(8).

**Answer:**
Under **section 49** of the CGST Act, 2017 read with the CGST Rules, three ledgers are maintained on the common portal:

| Ledger | Rule | Nature / Use |
|--------|------|--------------|
| **Electronic Cash Ledger** | Rule 87, Form GST PMT-05 | Reflects deposits made through challan (PMT-06); used to pay **tax, interest, penalty, fee or any other amount**. |
| **Electronic Credit Ledger** | Rule 86, Form GST PMT-02 | Reflects **input tax credit** availed; used **only** to pay **output tax** (not interest, penalty, late fee, or reverse-charge liability). |
| **Electronic Liability Register** | Rule 85, Form GST PMT-01 | Records all **liabilities** (tax, interest, penalty, etc.) of the person, debited as dues arise and credited as they are discharged. |

**Restriction on credit ledger [section 49(4)]:** The amount in the electronic **credit ledger** may be used only for payment of **output tax**; it cannot be used for interest, penalty, late fee or tax under reverse charge — these must be paid **in cash**.

**Order of discharge [section 49(8)]:** Every taxable person shall discharge his tax and other dues in the following order:
1. **Self-assessed tax and other dues of previous tax periods** first;
2. **Self-assessed tax and other dues of the current tax period** next;
3. **Any other amount payable** including demand under section 73 or 74, and any amount determined by the officer.

**Conclusion:** Cash ledger pays everything; credit ledger pays only output tax; the liability register tracks all dues; and dues are cleared previous-period-first, then current-period, then other demands.

*(Full-marks tip: Emphasise that ITC (credit ledger) cannot pay interest/penalty/late fee/RCM — only cash can. Common deduction: allowing credit ledger for reverse-charge tax.)*

### Q100. Ch: GST – Tax Invoice, Payment & Returns — Bill of supply, receipt/refund/payment vouchers & continuous supply (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between a **tax invoice** and a **bill of supply**, and briefly state when a **receipt voucher, refund voucher and payment voucher** are issued under the CGST Act, 2017.

**Answer:**
Under section 31 of the CGST Act, 2017 and rules 46–55 of the CGST Rules, different documents are prescribed for different situations.

**Tax invoice vs Bill of supply:**

| Basis | Tax invoice [Sec 31(1)/(2), Rule 46] | Bill of supply [Sec 31(3)(c), Rule 49] |
|-------|--------------------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| When issued | On a **taxable** supply by a normal registered person | On supply of **exempt goods/services**, or by a **composition** taxpayer |
| Tax shown | Tax is **charged and shown** separately | **No tax** is shown/charged |
| ITC | Recipient can avail ITC | No ITC (no tax charged) |

**Other vouchers:**
- **Receipt voucher [section 31(3)(d), Rule 50]:** Issued by a registered person **on receipt of an advance payment** with respect to any supply of goods or services; it evidences the advance and the tax thereon (where applicable).
- **Refund voucher [section 31(3)(e), Rule 51]:** Issued where a receipt voucher was issued for an advance but **no supply is subsequently made and no tax invoice is issued** — the advance is refunded and a refund voucher is issued against such receipt voucher.
- **Payment voucher [section 31(3)(g), Rule 52]:** Issued by a **recipient** who is liable to pay tax under **reverse charge**, at the time of making payment to the supplier, in respect of inward supplies on which he pays tax on RCM basis.

**Conclusion:** A tax invoice is for taxable supplies (tax shown), a bill of supply for exempt/composition supplies (no tax); a receipt voucher records an advance, a refund voucher its return where no supply follows, and a payment voucher a reverse-charge payment to the supplier.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards mapping each voucher to its exact rule (50/51/52) and clarifying that the payment voucher is issued by the RECIPIENT under RCM. Common deduction: confusing receipt and payment vouchers.)*
