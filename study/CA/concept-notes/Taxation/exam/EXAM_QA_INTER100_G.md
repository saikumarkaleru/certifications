### Q1. Ch: Basic Concepts — Default Tax Regime under Section 115BAC(1A) (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** For A.Y. 2025-26, explain the position of the concessional tax regime under section 115BAC as the "default" regime for an individual. State how such an individual (having income from business/profession, and one not having such income) may exercise the option to be taxed under the normal (old) regime, and the consequence of exercising and withdrawing that option.

**Answer:**
With effect from A.Y. 2024-25, the tax regime under section 115BAC(1A) has been made the **default** regime for an individual, HUF, AOP (other than a co-operative society), BOI and artificial juridical person. Consequently, unless the assessee affirmatively opts out, the income of such person is computed and charged to tax at the concessional slab rates prescribed in section 115BAC(1A), after forgoing the specified deductions and exemptions listed in section 115BAC(2).

Section 115BAC(6) permits the assessee to **opt out** of the default regime and be taxed under the normal (old) provisions. The manner of exercising this option depends upon the nature of income:

| Particulars | Assessee having income from business or profession | Assessee not having income from business or profession |
|---|---|---|
| Form / manner of opting out | Furnish **Form 10-IEA** on or before the due date u/s 139(1) for the relevant assessment year | Exercise the option **along with the return of income** furnished u/s 139(1) |
| Number of times option can be exercised | Only **once** the assessee can switch back to the default regime; thereafter he can never again opt for the old regime so long as he has business/profession income | The option is available **every year** — he may choose either regime each year |

Where an individual having business/profession income opts out through Form 10-IEA and later withdraws that option, the withdrawal is permitted **only once**, and after such withdrawal he shall not be eligible to exercise the option again unless he ceases to have income from business or profession.

**Conclusion:** For A.Y. 2025-26, section 115BAC(1A) applies by default; an individual with business income must file Form 10-IEA within the section 139(1) due date to be taxed under the old regime, whereas an individual without business income may simply choose the beneficial regime in his return each year.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner rewards the precise linkage of Form 10-IEA to those with business income and the "only once" restriction on switching; a common deduction is stating that the old regime is the default — it is not, w.e.f. A.Y. 2024-25.)*

### Q2. Ch: Basic Concepts — Rebate u/s 87A and Marginal Relief under the Default Regime (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** Mr. Karan, a resident individual aged 38 years, has a total income of **₹7,10,000** for A.Y. 2025-26, all of which is normally chargeable to tax at slab rates. He has not opted out of the default tax regime under section 115BAC(1A). Compute his tax liability, clearly demonstrating the availability (or otherwise) of rebate u/s 87A and any marginal relief.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Tax on total income of ₹7,10,000 under section 115BAC(1A)**

| Slab (₹) | Rate | Tax (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| Up to 3,00,000 | Nil | Nil |
| 3,00,001 – 7,00,000 | 5% | 20,000 |
| 7,00,001 – 7,10,000 | 10% | 1,000 |
| **Total** | | **21,000** |

**WN-2: Rebate under section 87A**
Under the default regime, rebate u/s 87A is allowed where total income does **not exceed ₹7,00,000**, up to a maximum of ₹25,000. Since Mr. Karan's total income of ₹7,10,000 **exceeds ₹7,00,000, no rebate u/s 87A is available.**

**WN-3: Marginal relief u/s 87A (proviso)**
Under the default regime, marginal relief is available where total income marginally exceeds ₹7,00,000 — the income-tax payable shall not exceed the amount by which the total income exceeds ₹7,00,000.
- Income exceeding ₹7,00,000 = ₹7,10,000 – ₹7,00,000 = **₹10,000**
- Tax computed (WN-1) = ₹21,000, which exceeds ₹10,000.
- Therefore tax payable is restricted to ₹10,000; **Marginal relief = ₹21,000 – ₹10,000 = ₹11,000**

**Computation of Tax Liability of Mr. Karan (A.Y. 2025-26)**

| Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Tax on total income (WN-1) | 21,000 |
| Less: Marginal relief (WN-3) | (11,000) |
| Tax after marginal relief | 10,000 |
| Add: Health & Education Cess @ 4% | 400 |
| **Total Tax Liability** | **10,400** |

**Answer:** Tax liability of Mr. Karan for A.Y. 2025-26 = **₹10,400**.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner specifically tests the newly introduced marginal relief on the ₹7,00,000 threshold under the default regime; simply denying 87A and charging ₹21,840 loses marks. State the "tax shall not exceed excess income" principle explicitly.)*

### Q3. Ch: Basic Concepts — Surcharge and Marginal Relief (₹50 lakh threshold) (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** Mr. Vikram, a resident individual aged 45 years, has opted out of the default regime (i.e., he is taxed under the old/normal regime). His total income for A.Y. 2025-26 is **₹51,00,000**. Compute his tax liability, showing the working for surcharge and marginal relief.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Income-tax on ₹51,00,000 (old regime slab rates)**

| Slab (₹) | Rate | Tax (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| Up to 2,50,000 | Nil | Nil |
| 2,50,001 – 5,00,000 | 5% | 12,500 |
| 5,00,001 – 10,00,000 | 20% | 1,00,000 |
| Above 10,00,000 (i.e., on 41,00,000) | 30% | 12,30,000 |
| **Total income-tax** | | **13,42,500** |

**WN-2: Surcharge**
Total income exceeds ₹50 lakh but not ₹1 crore, hence surcharge @ **10%** = 13,42,500 × 10% = **₹1,34,250**.
Tax + surcharge = 13,42,500 + 1,34,250 = **₹14,76,750**.

**WN-3: Marginal relief**
Tax on total income of ₹50,00,000 (no surcharge, as income does not exceed ₹50 lakh):
= 12,500 + 1,00,000 + (30% of 40,00,000) = 12,500 + 1,00,000 + 12,00,000 = **₹13,12,500**.
- Income exceeding ₹50,00,000 = ₹1,00,000.
- (Tax + surcharge on ₹51,00,000) – (Tax on ₹50,00,000) = 14,76,750 – 13,12,500 = ₹1,64,250.
- This exceeds the excess income of ₹1,00,000 by ₹64,250.
- **Marginal relief = ₹64,250**; surcharge after relief = 1,34,250 – 64,250 = **₹70,000**.

**Computation of Tax Liability of Mr. Vikram (A.Y. 2025-26)**

| Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Income-tax (WN-1) | 13,42,500 |
| Add: Surcharge after marginal relief (WN-3) | 70,000 |
| Tax + surcharge | 14,12,500 |
| Add: Health & Education Cess @ 4% | 56,500 |
| **Total Tax Liability** | **14,69,000** |

**Answer:** Tax liability of Mr. Vikram = **₹14,69,000**.

*(Full-marks tip: marginal relief ensures the additional tax + surcharge on income above ₹50 lakh does not exceed the additional income; showing the ₹13,12,500 benchmark on ₹50 lakh is the crux — omitting it forfeits the marginal-relief marks.)*

### Q4. Ch: Basic Concepts — Comparison of Old and New (Default) Regime (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** Mr. Aditya, a salaried individual aged 45 years, furnishes the following particulars for the previous year 2024-25. Advise which tax regime is more beneficial for A.Y. 2025-26 and compute the tax liability under the beneficial regime.

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Basic salary (₹75,000 p.m.) | 9,00,000 |
| House Rent Allowance received | 2,40,000 |
| Other fully taxable allowances | 3,60,000 |
| Rent paid for accommodation in Delhi | 3,00,000 |
| Deduction u/s 80C (LIC + PPF) | 1,50,000 |
| Deduction u/s 80D (medical insurance) | 25,000 |
| Deduction u/s 80CCD(1B) (NPS – self) | 50,000 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1: HRA exemption u/s 10(13A) [relevant only under old regime]**
Salary for HRA = Basic (no DA) = ₹9,00,000. Least of the following is exempt:
(a) Actual HRA received = ₹2,40,000
(b) Rent paid – 10% of salary = 3,00,000 – 90,000 = ₹2,10,000
(c) 50% of salary (Delhi = metro) = ₹4,50,000
**Least = ₹2,10,000 exempt**; taxable HRA = 2,40,000 – 2,10,000 = ₹30,000.

**WN-2: Gross salary and Income under head Salaries**

| Particulars | Old Regime (₹) | New Regime (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| Basic salary | 9,00,000 | 9,00,000 |
| HRA (taxable portion) | 30,000 | 2,40,000 |
| Other taxable allowances | 3,60,000 | 3,60,000 |
| Gross Salary | 12,90,000 | 15,00,000 |
| Less: Standard deduction u/s 16(ia) | (50,000) | (75,000) |
| Income under head Salaries | 12,40,000 | 14,25,000 |

**WN-3: Total Income**

| Particulars | Old Regime (₹) | New Regime (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| Income under head Salaries | 12,40,000 | 14,25,000 |
| Less: 80C / 80D / 80CCD(1B) (not allowed in new regime) | (2,25,000) | Nil |
| **Total Income** | **10,15,000** | **14,25,000** |

**WN-4: Tax under Old Regime on ₹10,15,000**
= 12,500 + 1,00,000 + (30% of 15,000) = 12,500 + 1,00,000 + 4,500 = ₹1,17,000
Add cess @ 4% = ₹4,680 → **₹1,21,680**.

**WN-5: Tax under New Regime on ₹14,25,000**
= (5% of 4,00,000) + (10% of 3,00,000) + (15% of 2,00,000) + (20% of 2,25,000)
= 20,000 + 30,000 + 30,000 + 45,000 = ₹1,25,000
Add cess @ 4% = ₹5,000 → **₹1,30,000**.

**Statement Showing Comparison of Tax Liability (A.Y. 2025-26)**

| Regime | Total Income (₹) | Tax + Cess (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| Old (normal) regime | 10,15,000 | 1,21,680 |
| New (default) regime | 14,25,000 | 1,30,000 |

**Answer:** The **old regime is more beneficial** — tax liability of **₹1,21,680** as against ₹1,30,000 under the default regime; a saving of **₹8,320**. Mr. Aditya (having no business income) should exercise the option in his return u/s 139(1).

*(Full-marks tip: show BOTH computations fully; the examiner rewards the standard-deduction difference (₹75,000 vs ₹50,000), the loss of HRA/Chapter VI-A under the new regime, and a clear "beneficial regime" conclusion with the ₹ saving.)*

### Q5. Ch: Basic Concepts — Surcharge and Marginal Relief (₹1 crore threshold) (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** Ms. Nisha, a resident individual aged 50 years taxed under the old regime, has a total income of **₹1,01,00,000** for A.Y. 2025-26 (comprising business income only). Compute her tax liability with full working for surcharge and marginal relief.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Income-tax on ₹1,01,00,000 (old regime)**
= 12,500 + 1,00,000 + (30% of 91,00,000) = 1,12,500 + 27,30,000 = **₹28,42,500**.

**WN-2: Surcharge @ 15%** (income exceeds ₹1 crore but not ₹2 crore)
= 28,42,500 × 15% = **₹4,26,375**; Tax + surcharge = **₹32,68,875**.

**WN-3: Marginal relief at ₹1 crore**
Tax + surcharge on income of exactly ₹1,00,00,000 (surcharge @ 10%):
- Income-tax on ₹1 crore = 1,12,500 + (30% of 90,00,000) = 1,12,500 + 27,00,000 = ₹28,12,500
- Surcharge @ 10% = ₹2,81,250 → Tax + surcharge = ₹30,93,750
Maximum permissible tax + surcharge = 30,93,750 + (income above ₹1 crore = 1,00,000) = **₹31,93,750**.
Actual tax + surcharge (WN-2) = ₹32,68,875.
**Marginal relief = 32,68,875 – 31,93,750 = ₹75,125**; surcharge after relief = 4,26,375 – 75,125 = ₹3,51,250.

**Computation of Tax Liability of Ms. Nisha (A.Y. 2025-26)**

| Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Income-tax (WN-1) | 28,42,500 |
| Add: Surcharge after marginal relief | 3,51,250 |
| Tax + surcharge | 31,93,750 |
| Add: Health & Education Cess @ 4% | 1,27,750 |
| **Total Tax Liability** | **33,21,500** |

**Answer:** Tax liability of Ms. Nisha = **₹33,21,500**.

*(Full-marks tip: at the ₹1 crore threshold the benchmark surcharge is 10% (not nil); computing marginal relief against a nil-surcharge figure is the common error.)*

### Q6. Ch: Basic Concepts — Rebate u/s 87A: Scope and Restrictions (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Discuss the eligibility conditions and quantum of rebate under section 87A for A.Y. 2025-26 under both the old regime and the default regime u/s 115BAC(1A). Is the rebate available against tax on long-term capital gains taxable u/s 112A?

**Answer:**
Rebate u/s 87A is available only to a **resident individual** (not to an HUF, firm, company, or non-resident). It is deducted from the income-tax computed on total income before adding health and education cess. The eligibility limits and quantum differ between the two regimes:

| Particulars | Old (normal) regime | Default regime u/s 115BAC(1A) |
|---|---|---|
| Total income ceiling | Up to ₹5,00,000 | Up to ₹7,00,000 |
| Maximum rebate | Lower of tax payable or ₹12,500 | Lower of tax payable or ₹25,000 |
| Marginal relief on rebate | Not available | Available where income marginally exceeds ₹7,00,000 |

The rebate is allowed against the income-tax computed on the total income **before cess**. However, a specific restriction applies: rebate u/s 87A **shall not be allowed against tax on long-term capital gains chargeable u/s 112A** (i.e., LTCG on equity shares/units of equity-oriented funds). The rebate is therefore computed only on the tax attributable to income other than such 112A capital gains.

**Conclusion:** A resident individual gets a rebate up to ₹12,500 (old regime, income ≤ ₹5 lakh) or up to ₹25,000 (default regime, income ≤ ₹7 lakh); in neither case is it allowed against tax on LTCG u/s 112A.

*(Full-marks tip: the "resident individual only" condition and the 112A carve-out are the two points examiners look for; comparing both regimes in a table earns the presentation mark.)*

### Q7. Ch: Residential Status — Determination for a Citizen Leaving for Employment (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Mr. Arvind, an Indian citizen, left India for the first time on **15th September 2024** to take up employment in Dubai, and remained there throughout the rest of the previous year. During P.Y. 2024-25 his income accruing/arising in India (i.e., income other than income from foreign sources) is **₹18,00,000**, and he is not liable to tax in any other country (Dubai levies no personal income-tax). Determine his residential status for A.Y. 2025-26.

**Answer:**
**Step 1 – Basic conditions u/s 6(1):** An individual is resident if he satisfies either (a) stay in India ≥ 182 days in the previous year, or (b) stay ≥ 60 days in the previous year and ≥ 365 days in the four preceding previous years.

Days of stay in India in P.Y. 2024-25 (1 April 2024 to 15 September 2024):
April 30 + May 31 + June 30 + July 31 + August 31 + September 15 = **168 days**.

Since Mr. Arvind is an Indian citizen **leaving India for the purpose of employment**, the second basic condition is inapplicable and the first condition stands modified — the period of "60 days" in section 6(1)(c) is substituted by **182 days** [Explanation 1(a) to section 6(1)]. He must therefore be in India for at least 182 days. His stay of 168 days is **less than 182 days**, so he does **not** satisfy the basic condition and is prima facie a **non-resident** u/s 6(1).

**Step 2 – Deemed residence u/s 6(1A):** An Indian citizen having total income (other than income from foreign sources) **exceeding ₹15,00,000**, who is not liable to tax in any other country by reason of domicile/residence, shall be deemed to be resident in India, **provided he is not a resident u/s 6(1)**. Here:
- He is not a resident u/s 6(1) — condition satisfied.
- Indian income = ₹18,00,000 > ₹15,00,000 — condition satisfied.
- He is not liable to tax in Dubai — condition satisfied.
Therefore Mr. Arvind is a **deemed resident** u/s 6(1A).

**Step 3 – Type of residence:** A person who is a resident only by virtue of section 6(1A) is always treated as **Resident but Not Ordinarily Resident (RNOR)** [section 6(6)(d)].

**Conclusion:** Mr. Arvind is a **Resident but Not Ordinarily Resident (RNOR)** for A.Y. 2025-26.

*(Full-marks tip: candidates routinely stop at "non-resident" after the 168-day count; the deemed-residence test u/s 6(1A) and its automatic RNOR status u/s 6(6)(d) are exactly what this fresh framing tests.)*

### Q8. Ch: Residential Status — Scope of Total Income (ROR / RNOR / NR) (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** From the following particulars of income of Mr. Sameer for the previous year 2024-25, compute his total income for A.Y. 2025-26 if he is (i) Resident and Ordinarily Resident, (ii) Resident but Not Ordinarily Resident, and (iii) Non-Resident.

| S.No. | Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Income from a business in Germany, business being controlled from India (income received in Germany) | 4,00,000 |
| 2 | Income from profession in India, received in India | 6,00,000 |
| 3 | Income from house property in London, received in London | 3,00,000 |
| 4 | Interest on bonds of a UK company (₹1,00,000 received in India, balance received in UK) | 2,00,000 |
| 5 | Past untaxed foreign income of P.Y. 2021-22 brought into India during 2024-25 | 5,00,000 |
| 6 | Salary earned in India but received in Nepal | 1,50,000 |
| 7 | Dividend from a foreign company, received abroad | 80,000 |
| 8 | Agricultural income in Bangladesh, received there | 1,20,000 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Guiding principles (Section 5 – Scope of Total Income)**
- **ROR:** taxable on global income — income received/accruing in India and income accruing outside India.
- **RNOR:** taxable on Indian income + income from a business controlled from / profession set up in India (foreign income otherwise not taxable).
- **NR:** taxable only on income received/deemed to be received in India, or accruing/deemed to accrue in India.
- Item 5 (past income brought into India) is **not an income of the previous year** and is not taxable for any status.

**Statement Showing Computation of Total Income of Mr. Sameer (A.Y. 2025-26)**

| S.No. | Particulars | ROR (₹) | RNOR (₹) | NR (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Business in Germany, controlled from India | 4,00,000 | 4,00,000 | Nil |
| 2 | Profession in India, received in India | 6,00,000 | 6,00,000 | 6,00,000 |
| 3 | House property in London, received in London | 3,00,000 | Nil | Nil |
| 4 | Interest on UK bonds — ₹1,00,000 received in India | 2,00,000 | 1,00,000 | 1,00,000 |
| 5 | Past foreign income brought into India | Nil | Nil | Nil |
| 6 | Salary earned in India, received in Nepal (accrues in India) | 1,50,000 | 1,50,000 | 1,50,000 |
| 7 | Dividend from foreign company, received abroad | 80,000 | Nil | Nil |
| 8 | Agricultural income in Bangladesh, received there | 1,20,000 | Nil | Nil |
| | **Total Income** | **18,50,000** | **12,50,000** | **8,50,000** |

**Notes:**
- Item 1 is foreign income but, being from a business **controlled from India**, it is taxable for both ROR and RNOR; for a NR it is not taxable.
- Item 4: only the ₹1,00,000 **received in India** is taxable for RNOR and NR; the full ₹2,00,000 is taxable for ROR.
- Item 6: salary for services rendered in India **accrues in India** [section 9(1)(ii)] and is therefore taxable for all three statuses irrespective of place of receipt.

**Answer:** Total income — ROR **₹18,50,000**; RNOR **₹12,50,000**; NR **₹8,50,000**.

*(Full-marks tip: the split of the UK-bond interest (received-in-India portion), the "business controlled from India" treatment for RNOR, and excluding the past income "brought in" are the three high-value adjustments; a one-line reason against each item earns the notes marks.)*

### Q9. Ch: Residential Status — Conditions for RNOR and Deemed Residence (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** State the additional conditions under section 6(6) that determine whether a resident individual is "Ordinarily Resident" or "Not Ordinarily Resident". Also explain the two categories of persons who are treated as "Not Ordinarily Resident" even though they may not be resident under the basic conditions.

**Answer:**
Once an individual is found to be **resident** under section 6(1), his further classification into "Ordinarily Resident" (ROR) or "Not Ordinarily Resident" (RNOR) is governed by section 6(6).

An individual is **Resident but Not Ordinarily Resident (RNOR)** if he satisfies **any one** of the following:
1. He has been a **non-resident in India in 9 out of the 10** previous years preceding the relevant previous year; **or**
2. He has been in India for a period of **729 days or less during the 7** previous years preceding the relevant previous year.

Conversely, to be **Ordinarily Resident (ROR)** he must be a resident in at least 2 of the 10 preceding previous years **and** must have been in India for 730 days or more in the 7 preceding previous years — i.e., he must fail both the above RNOR tests.

In addition, the following two categories are **specifically treated as RNOR** by the Finance Act amendments, even though they may not be resident under the basic conditions:

| Category | Provision | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Indian citizen / PIO visiting India with high Indian income | Section 6(6)(c) | An Indian citizen or person of Indian origin whose total income (other than foreign-source income) exceeds ₹15 lakh, and who is in India for 120 days or more but less than 182 days, is resident — and such person is RNOR |
| Deemed resident | Section 6(6)(d) | An individual deemed to be resident u/s 6(1A) (Indian citizen with Indian income > ₹15 lakh, not liable to tax in any other country) is RNOR |

**Conclusion:** An individual is RNOR if he satisfies either of the two conditions in section 6(6)(a), and additionally the categories under section 6(6)(c) and 6(6)(d) are statutorily deemed RNOR.

*(Full-marks tip: state both alternative conditions using the exact "9 out of 10 / 729 days in 7" wording; adding the 6(6)(c) and 6(6)(d) special RNOR categories lifts a 3-mark answer to full marks.)*

### Q10. Ch: Residential Status — Residence of HUF and Company (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain how the residential status of (a) a Hindu Undivided Family and (b) a company is determined under the Income-tax Act, 1961, for A.Y. 2025-26.

**Answer:**
**(a) Hindu Undivided Family [Section 6(2)]:**
A HUF is said to be **resident** in India in any previous year **unless** the control and management of its affairs is situated **wholly outside India** during that year. Thus, if the control and management is wholly or partly in India, the HUF is resident.

Once resident, the HUF is further classified using the conditions in section 6(6) applied to its **karta** — the HUF is Resident but Not Ordinarily Resident if the karta satisfies either of the two additional conditions (non-resident in 9 out of 10 preceding years, or in India for 729 days or less in the 7 preceding years); otherwise it is Ordinarily Resident.

**(b) Company [Section 6(3)]:**
A company is said to be resident in India in any previous year if:
1. It is an **Indian company**; **or**
2. Its **place of effective management (POEM)** in that year is in India.

"Place of effective management" means a place where **key management and commercial decisions** that are necessary for the conduct of the business of the entity as a whole are, in substance, made. An Indian company is **always resident** irrespective of POEM; a foreign company is resident only if its POEM is in India during the relevant year.

**Conclusion:** A HUF is resident unless its control and management is wholly outside India; a company is resident if it is an Indian company or its POEM is in India.

*(Full-marks tip: for HUF, remember the ROR/RNOR sub-classification is tested on the karta; for a company, the "always resident" rule for Indian companies and the POEM definition are the marking points.)*

### Q11. Ch: Residential Status — Determination and Application to Income (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Mrs. Meera, an Indian citizen, had been residing and working abroad continuously for the past 14 years. She returned to India permanently on **1st April 2024** and stayed in India throughout the previous year 2024-25. During the seven previous years preceding P.Y. 2024-25, her total stay in India (on occasional visits) was only 190 days. For P.Y. 2024-25 she earned (i) ₹8,00,000 salary for services rendered in India, and (ii) ₹2,50,000 rent from a house property situated in Canada, received in Canada. Determine her residential status and compute the income taxable in India for A.Y. 2025-26.

**Answer:**
**Step 1 – Basic condition u/s 6(1):** Mrs. Meera was in India for the whole of P.Y. 2024-25, i.e., 365 days, which is ≥ 182 days. She therefore satisfies the first basic condition and is a **resident** for A.Y. 2025-26.

**Step 2 – Additional conditions u/s 6(6):** She must be tested for ROR/RNOR:
1. She was a **non-resident** abroad for the past 14 years, hence she was non-resident in **9 out of the 10** preceding previous years — this RNOR condition is satisfied; **and**
2. Her stay in India in the **7 preceding previous years was only 190 days**, i.e., **729 days or less** — this RNOR condition is also satisfied.

Since she satisfies (in fact, both of) the conditions under section 6(6), she is a **Resident but Not Ordinarily Resident (RNOR)**.

**Step 3 – Income taxable in India (Scope u/s 5 for RNOR):**

| Particulars | Taxable? | ₹ |
|---|---|---|

### Q29. Ch: Income from House Property — Property Held as Stock-in-Trade & Composite Rent (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** Explain the tax treatment in the following independent situations for A.Y. 2025-26, giving the relevant section:
(a) M/s Skyline Developers, a real-estate builder, holds 4 completed but unsold flats as stock-in-trade. Two flats remained unsold for 3 years since the certificate of completion (obtained in F.Y. 2022-23).
(b) Mr. Naveen lets out a fully furnished house with fittings for a composite rent of ₹80,000 per month; the letting of the building and amenities is inseparable.
(c) Mr. Ojas lets out a building for ₹40,000 p.m. and separately provides lift and security services for ₹10,000 p.m.; the two are separable.

**Answer:**

**(a) Unsold stock-in-trade — Sec 23(5):**
Where property, consisting of building or land appurtenant thereto, is held as **stock-in-trade** and is not let during the whole or any part of the previous year, the annual value up to **two years** from the end of the financial year in which the completion certificate is obtained shall be taken as **Nil**. Completion certificate obtained in F.Y. 2022-23; the two-year period expires at the end of F.Y. 2024-25. From A.Y. 2026-27 onwards, notional annual value becomes taxable.

**(b) Inseparable composite rent — Sec 56(2)(i):**

Hence ₹80,000 × 12 = ₹9,60,000 is taxable under Other Sources/PGBP, not house property.

**(c) Separable composite rent:**
Where the two lettings are **separable**, the rent attributable to the **building** (₹40,000 × 12 = ₹4,80,000) is taxable under **"Income from House Property"**, and the amount for amenities/services (₹10,000 × 12 = ₹1,20,000) is taxable under **"Other Sources"** or **PGBP**.

**Conclusion:** (a) Annual value Nil for A.Y. 2025-26 u/s 23(5); (b) whole composite rent taxable under Other Sources/PGBP as inseparable; (c) rent bifurcated — building portion under House Property, services under Other Sources.

### Q31. Ch: PGBP — General Deductions u/s 37(1) & Personal/Capital Expenditure (Marks: 5) [Theory + Application]
**Question:** State, with reasons and the relevant section, whether the following are allowable while computing business income for A.Y. 2025-26:

### Q34. Ch: PGBP vs House Property — Head of Income & Business Connection (Marks: 5) [Theory + Application]
**Question:** Decide, with reasons and relevant provisions, the correct head of income in each independent case for A.Y. 2025-26:
(a) A company whose main object and business is to let out properties and earn rent from commercial complexes it owns.
(b) Rental income earned by a manufacturing company from temporarily letting out a portion of its factory building lying idle.
(c) Paying-guest accommodation run by an individual providing food, cleaning and other services as a regular activity.
(d) A warehousing company that lets out godowns and provides pest-control, security and loading services as part of an integrated warehousing activity.

**Answer:**

**(a) Company letting properties as its business:**
Even where letting is the object of the company, income from ownership of building is generally assessed under **"Income from House Property"**, since the head is determined by the nature of income (ownership of property), not the motive — following *East India Housing & Land Development Trust Ltd. v. CIT*. However, where the letting is a **complex commercial activity** with substantial services, it may fall under PGBP (*Chennai Properties* / *Rayala Corporation*). Absent such services, the rent is taxable under **House Property**.

**(b) Idle factory temporarily let:**
Where a **commercial asset** is temporarily let out during a lull, the rent partakes the character of business income and is taxable under **"Profits and Gains of Business or Profession"**, as the asset continues to be a business asset exploited commercially.

**(c) Paying-guest accommodation with services:**
The dominant activity is provision of **services** (food, cleaning) along with occupation; this is an organised commercial activity taxable under **"PGBP"** (or Other Sources), not House Property, since income arises from services rendered rather than mere ownership.

**(d) Integrated warehousing with services:**
Warehousing coupled with pest-control, security and handling is a **composite commercial activity**; the income is taxable under **"Profits and Gains of Business or Profession"** (following *Sultan Brothers* / *CIT v. National Storage*), as the letting is inseparable from the services forming an integrated business.

**Conclusion:** (a) House Property (unless substantial services); (b), (c) and (d) — Profits and Gains of Business or Profession.

*(Full-marks tip: Head of income turns on nature, not motive — cite East India Housing for pure rent and Chennai Properties/Sultan Brothers for the exceptions where organised services convert rent into business income. Naming at least one case earns the analytical mark.)*

### Q40. Ch: Capital Gains — Period of Holding & Short-term vs Long-term Capital Asset (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the concept of "period of holding" under section 2(42A) and distinguish between a short-term capital asset and a long-term capital asset for different categories of assets.

**Answer:**
Under section 2(42A) of the Income-tax Act, 1961, a **short-term capital asset** means a capital asset held by an assessee for not more than the prescribed period immediately preceding the date of its transfer; any asset held for a longer period is a **long-term capital asset** under section 2(29A). The period of holding is reckoned from the date of acquisition to the date immediately preceding the date of transfer, and it determines both the rate of tax and the availability of indexation. The prescribed periods differ by category of asset, as summarised below.

| Category of capital asset | Held for NOT more than | Classification if within the period |
|---|---|---|
| Listed securities (equity/preference shares), units of equity-oriented fund, units of UTI, zero-coupon bonds | 12 months | Short-term (else long-term) |
| Unlisted shares and immovable property (land or building) | 24 months | Short-term (else long-term) |
| Any other capital asset (e.g., gold, jewellery, unlisted debentures, other movable assets) | 36 months | Short-term (else long-term) |

Special rules also apply: in the case of assets acquired by gift, will or inheritance, the period of holding of the previous owner is included by virtue of section 2(42A) read with section 49(1); in the case of bonus shares, the period is reckoned from the date of allotment of the bonus shares; and for a right entitlement, from the date of the offer.

**Conclusion:** The classification into short-term or long-term hinges on the period of holding measured against the section-specified threshold for that class of asset, which in turn governs indexation and the applicable rate of tax under sections 111A/112/112A.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the correct 12/24/36-month thresholds mapped to the right asset class and the inclusion of the previous owner's holding period for gifted/inherited assets. Confusing the 24-month period for immovable property with 36 months is a common error.)*

### Q43. Ch: Income from Other Sources — Chargeability u/s 56, Deductions u/s 57 & Disallowances u/s 58 (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** "Income from Other Sources is a residuary head of income." Explain the scope of chargeability under section 56, and state the important deductions allowable under section 57 and the expenses expressly disallowed under section 58.

**Answer:**
Under section 56(1), income of every kind which is not chargeable to income-tax under any of the other four heads (salaries, house property, profits and gains of business or profession, and capital gains) and which is not excluded from total income is chargeable under the head **"Income from Other Sources"**; hence it is described as the **residuary head**. Section 56(2) additionally lists certain incomes that are always taxable under this head even though they may otherwise seem to fall elsewhere.

**Incomes specifically chargeable under section 56(2)** include: dividends; income from winnings from lotteries, crossword puzzles, races, card games and other games of any sort or gambling (section 56(2)(ib)); interest on securities, if not chargeable as business income; income from letting of machinery, plant or furniture on hire (and where such letting is inseparable from letting of building); any sum received under a Keyman insurance policy not taxable as salary/business income; and any money or property received without or for inadequate consideration falling within section 56(2)(x).

**Deductions allowable under section 57** include: (i) any reasonable sum paid as commission or remuneration for realising dividend or interest on securities; (ii) in the case of family pension, a deduction of the lower of ₹15,000 or one-third of such pension under section 57(iia); (iii) any other expenditure (not being capital expenditure) laid out wholly and exclusively for the purpose of earning such income under section 57(iii); and (iv) in respect of income by way of interest on compensation/enhanced compensation, a deduction of 50% of such interest.

**Expenses expressly disallowed under section 58** include: any personal expenses of the assessee; any interest or salary payable outside India on which tax has not been deducted at source; wealth-tax; and (crucially) any expenditure in connection with winnings from lotteries, crossword puzzles, races or gambling, as such income is taxed at a flat rate under section 115BB with no deduction permitted.

**Conclusion:** Section 56 casts the charge on residuary and specified incomes, section 57 permits deductions for expenditure incurred wholly and exclusively to earn such income, and section 58 blocks personal, TDS-defaulting and casual-income-related expenses.

*(Full-marks tip: Give at least four items each from sections 56(2), 57 and 58. Linking the section 58 disallowance of lottery-related expenses to the flat-rate charge under section 115BB shows examination maturity.)*

### Q47. Ch: Clubbing & Set-off — Cross Transfers & Conversion into HUF Property u/s 64(2) (Marks: 4) [Application]
**Question:** Examine the following situations and state, with reasons, in whose hands the income is chargeable for A.Y. 2025-26:
(a) Mr. Kunal gifts ₹5,00,000 to Mrs. Lalit (Mr. Lalit's wife), and on the same day Mr. Lalit gifts ₹5,00,000 to Mrs. Kunal (Mr. Kunal's wife). Each lady invests the amount and earns interest of ₹45,000.
(b) Mr. Manav, owner of self-acquired property, converts it into the property of his Hindu Undivided Family (of which he is a member) without adequate consideration. The HUF earns income of ₹60,000 from such property during the year.

**Answer:**

**(a) Cross transfers:** Where two transfers are intimately connected so as to form part of a single transaction, and each transfer is made in consideration of the other, the transfers are treated as **indirect transfers** by each spouse to his own wife, so as to attract the clubbing provisions of section 64(1)(iv). The gifts are reciprocal and made on the same day; hence the interest of ₹45,000 earned by Mrs. Kunal is clubbed in the hands of **Mr. Kunal**, and the interest of ₹45,000 earned by Mrs. Lalit is clubbed in the hands of **Mr. Lalit**, as if each had transferred the amount to his own wife. This principle is supported by the decision in *CIT v. Keshavji Morarji*.

**(b) Conversion of individual property into HUF property (section 64(2)):** Where an individual converts his separate/self-acquired property into property belonging to the HUF (or throws it into the common stock) otherwise than for adequate consideration, the income derived from such converted property is **clubbed in the hands of the individual (Mr. Manav)**. Accordingly, the income of ₹60,000 is taxable in the hands of Mr. Manav and not the HUF. Even after a partition of the HUF, the income from the converted property attributable to the spouse's share continues to be clubbed in Mr. Manav's hands.

**Conclusion:** (a) ₹45,000 each is clubbed in the hands of Mr. Kunal and Mr. Lalit respectively; (b) ₹60,000 is clubbed in the hands of Mr. Manav under section 64(2).

*(Full-marks tip: Naming the "indirect/cross-transfer" doctrine and citing section 64(2) for HUF conversion earns the marks. Add the post-partition continuation of clubbing on the spouse's share for a complete answer.)*

### Q50. Ch: Clubbing & Set-off — Carry Forward Rules for Different Losses (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Prepare a comparative statement showing, for each of the following, the head against which set-off is permitted and the period of carry forward: (i) house property loss, (ii) business loss (non-speculative), (iii) speculation business loss, (iv) loss of a specified business under section 35AD, (v) short-term and long-term capital loss, and (vi) unabsorbed depreciation.

**Answer:**
The set-off and carry-forward of losses is governed by sections 70 to 80 read with section 32(2). The permissible set-off in subsequent years and the maximum carry-forward period differ by the nature of the loss, as summarised below.

| Nature of loss | Section | Set-off in subsequent years allowed against | Carry-forward period |
|---|---|---|---|

### Q58. Ch: Advance Tax/TDS/TCS — Sec 234C Relaxation for Capital Gains / Casual Income (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** State the circumstances under section 234C in which no interest is chargeable even though an advance-tax instalment falls short. Also explain the relief where income arises from capital gains or winning from lotteries after an instalment due date.

**Answer:**
Section 234C charges interest for deferment of advance-tax instalments. However, the proviso to section 234C(1) provides relief so that an assessee is not penalised for shortfalls that arise from income which could not have been estimated in advance.

**No interest is chargeable u/s 234C in the following cases:**
1. **First-time shortfall on estimated business income:** If the shortfall in payment of the advance-tax instalment is on account of underestimation or failure to estimate the amount of **capital gains**, **income of the nature referred to in section 2(24)(ix)** (winnings from lotteries, crossword puzzles, races, card games, gambling etc.), income under the head "Profits and gains of business or profession" **where the income accrued for the first time**, or **dividend income** (other than deemed dividend u/s 2(22)(e)), then **no interest** is chargeable on that amount — **provided** the assessee has paid the whole of the tax attributable to such income (as part of the remaining instalments during the year, or if it arises after 15 March, by 31 March of the previous year).

2. **Small shortfall condition (main computation):** No interest if the advance tax paid on or before 15 June is not less than 12% and on or before 15 September is not less than 36% of the tax due on the returned income.

**Illustrative application:** If an assessee earns a capital gain of ₹5,00,000 on 20-12-2024 (after the 15-Dec instalment), the tax on this gain need not have been paid in the 15-June, 15-Sept and 15-Dec instalments; if the assessee pays that tax in the 15-March 2025 instalment, no section 234C interest is chargeable on the capital-gains component.

**Conclusion:** Interest under section 234C is not attracted on shortfalls arising from capital gains, casual income, first-time business income or dividend, provided the tax thereon is paid in the remaining instalment(s) falling due after such income arises (or by 31 March if it arises after the last instalment).

### Q61. Ch: Advance Tax/TDS/TCS — Consequences of Failure to Deduct/Pay TDS (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Discuss the consequences under the Income-tax Act, 1961 where a person fails to deduct tax at source or, having deducted, fails to pay it to the credit of the Central Government.

**Answer:**
The Act imposes both interest, disallowance and penal consequences on a person who defaults in the deduction or payment of tax at source.

1. **Interest under section 201(1A):** Where any person fails to deduct tax, or after deducting fails to pay it, he is liable to pay simple interest —
   - @ **1% per month or part** from the date on which tax was **deductible** to the date on which it is actually **deducted** (failure to deduct); and
   - @ **1.5% per month or part** from the date of **deduction** to the date of actual **payment** (failure to pay after deduction).

2. **Assessee-in-default — section 201(1):** The deductor is treated as an **assessee in default** in respect of the tax not deducted/paid, and it can be recovered from him. However, he is not treated as in default if the payee has filed his return, offered the income, and paid tax (with a CA certificate in Form 26A).

3. **Disallowance under section 40(a)(ia):** In computing business income, **30%** of the sum on which tax was deductible but not deducted/paid (to a resident) is **disallowed**; it is allowed in the year of subsequent payment/deduction.

4. **Penalty under section 271C:** Penalty equal to the amount of tax not deducted may be levied. Failure to pay deducted tax may attract prosecution under **section 276B** (rigorous imprisonment 3 months to 7 years with fine).

5. **Late filing of TDS return — section 234E and 271H:** Late fee of ₹200 per day (up to the TDS amount) and penalty ₹10,000 to ₹1,00,000 for non-filing/incorrect filing.

**Conclusion:** A TDS default triggers interest u/s 201(1A), status of assessee-in-default u/s 201(1), 30% expenditure disallowance u/s 40(a)(ia), and penalty/prosecution u/s 271C/276B.

### Q63. Ch: Return Filing & Assessment — Belated, Revised & Updated Returns (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the provisions relating to belated return (Sec 139(4)), revised return (Sec 139(5)) and updated return (Sec 139(8A)). Can a belated return be revised? Also state the time limits for AY 2025-26.

**Answer:**
The Act permits a return to be filed after the due date, corrected, and even updated after the assessment year, subject to specified conditions and time limits.

**Belated return — Section 139(4):** Where a person has not furnished the return within the time allowed u/s 139(1), he may furnish a belated return at any time **before three months prior to the end of the relevant assessment year** or before completion of assessment, whichever is earlier. For AY 2025-26, the belated return may be filed up to **31st December 2025**. A belated return attracts fee u/s 234F and interest u/s 234A, and certain losses cannot be carried forward.

**Revised return — Section 139(5):** If a person, having furnished a return u/s 139(1) or 139(4), discovers any omission or wrong statement, he may furnish a revised return at any time up to **31st December 2025** (three months before the end of the AY) or before completion of assessment, whichever is earlier. **A belated return CAN now be revised** (post-amendment, a return filed u/s 139(4) is expressly revisable).

**Updated return — Section 139(8A):** Any person, whether or not he has furnished a return, may furnish an **updated return** of income within **48 months (extended from 24 months w.e.f. 01-04-2025)** from the end of the relevant assessment year, on payment of additional tax. It cannot be filed to declare a loss, reduce tax liability, or increase a refund. Additional tax ranges from 25% to 70% of the aggregate tax and interest depending on how late it is filed.

|---|---|---|
| Belated | 139(4) | 31-12-2025 |
| Revised | 139(5) | 31-12-2025 |
| Updated | 139(8A) | within 48 months from end of AY 2025-26 |

**Conclusion:** Belated and revised returns for AY 2025-26 may be filed up to 31-12-2025; a belated return is now revisable; an updated return may be filed within 48 months of the end of the AY subject to additional tax.

### Q64. Ch: Return Filing & Assessment — Sec 139(1) Seventh Proviso (Compulsory Filing) & Sec 234F Fee (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** State the circumstances under the seventh proviso to section 139(1) under which an individual, though otherwise not required to file a return, must compulsorily furnish a return of income. Also explain the fee for default in furnishing return under section 234F.

**Answer:**
Ordinarily an individual is required to file a return only if his total income exceeds the basic exemption limit. However, the **seventh proviso to section 139(1)** casts an obligation to file even where income is below the exemption limit if any of the following conditions is satisfied during the previous year:

1. Deposited an aggregate amount **exceeding ₹1 crore** in one or more **current accounts** with a bank;
2. Incurred expenditure **exceeding ₹2 lakh** on **foreign travel** for himself or any other person;
3. Incurred expenditure **exceeding ₹1 lakh** towards **electricity consumption**;
4. Other conditions notified — e.g., where **total sales/turnover/gross receipts in business exceed ₹60 lakh**, in **profession exceed ₹10 lakh**, **TDS/TCS is ₹25,000 or more** (₹50,000 for senior citizens), or **savings bank deposits aggregate ₹50 lakh or more**.

**Fee for default — Section 234F:** Where a person required to furnish a return u/s 139 fails to do so within the due date u/s 139(1), a fee is levied:
- **₹5,000** if the return is furnished after the due date; but
- **₹1,000** if the total income does not exceed ₹5,00,000.

**Conclusion:** An individual must compulsorily file a return under the seventh proviso if he crosses any of the specified high-value thresholds (₹1 crore current-account deposit, ₹2 lakh foreign travel, ₹1 lakh electricity, etc.), and a late return attracts a fee of ₹5,000 (₹1,000 where total income ≤ ₹5 lakh) under section 234F.

### Q69. Ch: GST – Supply — Composite vs Mixed Supply (Marks: 6) [Application]

| Case | Bundle offered for a single price | GST rates of individual items |
|------|-----------------------------------|-------------------------------|
| (i) | Sale of an air-conditioner along with compulsory installation and a 1-year warranty | AC 28%; installation 18% |
| (ii) | A festive hamper containing chocolates, a wall clock, aerated drinks and a silk tie, sold for ₹4,000 | Chocolates 18%; wall clock 12%; aerated drinks 28%; tie 12% |
| (iii) | Supply of goods, packing of goods and transportation to the buyer's premises, invoiced together | Goods 12%; packing 18%; transport 5% |
| (iv) | A works contract for renovation of an immovable property (material + labour) | — |

**Answer:** (theory)
The tests flow from section 2(30) (composite supply), section 2(74) (mixed supply), section 2(90) (principal supply) and the charging rules in **section 8** of the CGST Act, 2017. A **composite supply** is one made by a taxable person comprising two or more taxable supplies **naturally bundled and supplied in conjunction** in the ordinary course of business, one of which is a principal supply; under **section 8(a)** it is treated as a supply of that principal supply and taxed at the principal supply's rate. A **mixed supply** is a combination of two or more supplies made for a single price which does **not** constitute a composite supply; under **section 8(b)** it is treated as supply of that particular item which attracts the **highest rate** of tax.

**Case (i):** Supply of the AC with installation and warranty is **naturally bundled** — a buyer expects installation with an appliance. It is a **composite supply**, the **AC being the principal supply**; the whole is taxed at **28%** (the AC rate) under section 8(a).

**Case (ii):** Chocolates, a clock, aerated drinks and a tie are **not naturally bundled** and each can be supplied separately; sold for a single price, this is a **mixed supply**. Under section 8(b) the whole hamper is taxed at the **highest rate among the items, i.e. 28%** (aerated drinks).

**Case (iii):** Supply of goods with packing and transportation to the buyer is naturally bundled in the ordinary course of business — a **composite supply** with **goods as the principal supply**; taxed at **12%** under section 8(a).

**Case (iv):** A **works contract** as defined in section 2(119) is, by virtue of **para 6(a) of Schedule II**, treated as a **supply of service**; it is a composite supply and is chargeable to GST as a service (generally 18%), **not** split into goods and services.

| Case | Nature | Principal supply | Rate applied |
|------|--------|------------------|--------------|
| (i) | Composite [Sec 8(a)] | Air-conditioner | 28% |
| (ii) | Mixed [Sec 8(b)] | — (highest rate) | 28% |
| (iii) | Composite [Sec 8(a)] | Goods | 12% |
| (iv) | Composite (works contract – service, Sch II para 6(a)) | Service | 18% |

**Conclusion:** (i) and (iii) are composite supplies taxed at the principal supply's rate; (ii) is a mixed supply taxed at the highest rate (28%); (iv) is a works contract taxed as a service.

### Q70. Ch: GST – Supply — Schedule I: Supply Without Consideration (Marks: 5) [Application]

| (ii) | Employee-to-employer services in employment | **Neither** (not a supply) | Sch III, para 1 |
| (iii) | Renting of a commercial building | **Supply of service** | Sch II, para 2(b) |
| (iv) | Sale of land | **Neither** (not a supply) | Sch III, para 5 |
| (v) | Transfer of title with property passing in future (hire purchase) | **Supply of goods** | Sch II, para 1(c) |
| (vi) | Functions of a Member of Parliament | **Neither** (not a supply) | Sch III, para 3 |

Explaining the principle: where **title in goods passes** (whether now or at a future certain date), the activity is a **supply of goods** [para 1(a)/1(c) of Schedule II]; where only the **right to use** goods is transferred **without title**, or where **immovable property is rented**, it is a **supply of service** [paras 1(b), 2(b) and 5(f)]. Schedule III excludes employment services, sale of land and completed buildings, and functions of constitutional/statutory functionaries from the ambit of "supply" altogether.

**Conclusion:** (i) and (iii) are services; (v) is goods; (ii), (iv) and (vi) fall in Schedule III and are not supplies at all.

### Q72. Ch: GST – Supply — Scope of Supply u/s 7 (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** "The expression 'supply' under section 7 of the CGST Act, 2017 is wide but not unlimited." Explain the **scope of supply** under section 7, and briefly state the effect of **section 7(2)**.

**Answer:** (theory)
**Section 7(1)** of the CGST Act, 2017 defines the expression **"supply"** in an inclusive manner. It includes:
- **(a)** all forms of supply of goods or services or both such as **sale, transfer, barter, exchange, licence, rental, lease or disposal** made or agreed to be made **for a consideration** by a person **in the course or furtherance of business**;
- **(b)** **import of services** for a consideration whether or not in the course or furtherance of business;
- **(c)** the activities specified in **Schedule I**, made or agreed to be made **without a consideration**; and
- per **section 7(1A)**, where an activity constitutes a supply, it shall be treated **either as a supply of goods or of services** as referred to in **Schedule II**.

Thus the two essential ingredients for section 7(1)(a) are **consideration** and **furtherance of business**; import of services [7(1)(b)] dispenses with the business requirement, and Schedule I activities [7(1)(c)] dispense with consideration.

**Section 7(2)** carves out the limitation: notwithstanding section 7(1), the activities or transactions specified in **Schedule III** (e.g. services by an employee, sale of land, sale of a completed building, actionable claims other than specified ones), and such activities of the Government as may be notified, shall be treated as **neither a supply of goods nor a supply of services**.

**Conclusion:** Section 7(1)/(1A) casts a wide net over transactions for consideration in furtherance of business (plus import of services and Schedule I), while section 7(2) read with Schedule III keeps specified activities entirely outside the scope of supply.

*(Full-marks tip: list the four limbs (a)–(c) and 7(1A) with the twin tests of consideration and furtherance of business, then contrast with the 7(2) exclusion — omitting section 7(1A)/Schedule II linkage is a common deduction.)*

### Q75. Ch: GST – Charge & RCM — Forward Charge, RCM & Section 9(3)/(4) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between the **forward charge** and **reverse charge** mechanisms under the CGST Act, 2017. Explain the scope of **section 9(3)** and **section 9(4)**, and state the meaning of reverse charge under **section 2(98)**.

**Answer:** (theory)
Under **section 9(1)**, GST is normally levied on the **supplier** who collects it from the recipient and pays it to the Government — this is the **forward charge mechanism**. **Reverse charge** is defined in **section 2(98)** as the liability to pay tax by the **recipient** of the supply instead of the supplier.

The two enabling provisions are:
- **Section 9(3):** the Government may, on the recommendations of the Council, **notify categories of supply** of goods or services on which tax shall be paid by the **recipient** on reverse charge (e.g. legal services by an advocate, GTA services, sponsorship, services by a director to a company). All provisions of the Act apply to such recipient as if he were the person liable to pay tax.
- **Section 9(4):** where a **registered person** receives supplies from an **unregistered supplier**, tax shall be paid by the registered recipient on reverse charge — but only for such **classes of registered persons** and **specified supplies** as are notified (e.g. a promoter procuring specified goods/services in real estate).

| Basis | Forward charge | Reverse charge |
|-------|----------------|----------------|
| Person liable | Supplier [Sec 9(1)] | Recipient [Sec 9(3)/(4); 2(98)] |
| Payment of tax | From cash/ITC | **Cash only** (ITC of RCM not usable to pay RCM) |
| Registration | Threshold-based | Recipient liable to register mandatorily [Sec 24] |
| Composition dealer | Can opt | Must pay RCM at normal rates even if under composition |

**Conclusion:** Forward charge places the tax burden on the supplier (section 9(1)); reverse charge shifts it to the recipient in the notified cases under sections 9(3) and 9(4), as defined in section 2(98).

*(Full-marks tip: quote section 2(98) verbatim and give at least two notified RCM examples for section 9(3); a common deduction is confusing 9(3) (notified supplies) with 9(4) (URD supplies to notified registered persons).)*

### Q81. Ch: GST – Time & Value of Supply — Time of Supply of Goods (Marks: 6) [Problem]

### Q87. Ch: GST – Input Tax Credit — Conditions for Availing ITC u/s 16 (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** "Mere possession of a tax invoice is not enough to avail input tax credit." Explain the cumulative conditions that a registered person must satisfy under Section 16 of the CGST Act, 2017 to be entitled to input tax credit.

**Answer:** Section 16(1) of the CGST Act, 2017 entitles every registered person to take credit of input tax charged on any inward supply of goods or services which are used or intended to be used in the course or furtherance of his business, subject to the conditions prescribed. Section 16(2) then lays down that, notwithstanding anything in Sec 16(1), no registered person shall be entitled to the credit unless **all** the following conditions are cumulatively satisfied:

(i) He is in possession of a **tax invoice or debit note** issued by a registered supplier, or such other tax-paying document (clause a).

(ii) The **details of the invoice/debit note have been furnished by the supplier** in his statement of outward supplies (GSTR-1/IFF) and such details have been **communicated to the recipient** in Form GSTR-2B — clause (aa). Further, under clause (ba) read with Sec 38, the credit in respect of the said supply must **not be restricted** in GSTR-2B.

(iii) He has **received the goods or services** or both. By the *bill-to-ship-to* deeming fiction (Explanation), goods delivered to a third person on the direction of the registered person are deemed received by him (clause b).

(iv) The **tax charged has actually been paid** to the Government by the supplier, in cash or through admissible ITC (clause c).

(v) He has **furnished the return** under Section 39 (clause d).

Two further riders apply: under the **second proviso to Sec 16(2)**, if the recipient fails to pay the supplier the value of supply along with tax **within 180 days** of the date of invoice, an amount equal to the ITC availed must be added to his output tax liability with interest (re-availed on subsequent payment). Under **Section 16(3)**, if depreciation has been claimed on the tax component of the cost of capital goods under the Income-tax Act, ITC on that tax component is not allowed. Under **Section 16(4)**, ITC in respect of an invoice/debit note cannot be availed after **30th November** following the end of the financial year to which such invoice pertains, or the date of furnishing the annual return, whichever is earlier.

**Conclusion:** Possession of the invoice is only the first of five cumulative conditions; unless the supply is reflected in GSTR-2B, the goods/services are received, the tax is paid to the Government, the return is filed and the time-limit u/s 16(4) is met, the credit cannot be validly availed.

*(Full-marks tip: list all five conditions of 16(2) with the clause letters (a, aa, b, c, d) and add the 180-day proviso, Sec 16(3) depreciation bar and the 16(4) time-limit — the examiner allots a mark for each rider beyond the bare five conditions.)*

### Q89. Ch: GST – Registration — Persons Liable and Procedure of Registration (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the procedure for obtaining registration under the GST law as laid down in Section 25 read with Rule 8 and Rule 9 of the CGST Rules, 2017. Also state the effective date of registration.

**Answer:** Under **Section 25(1)**, every person liable to be registered under Sec 22 or Sec 24 must apply for registration in every State/Union territory from which he makes a taxable supply, **within 30 days** from the date on which he becomes liable to registration (a casual taxable person/non-resident taxable person applies at least **5 days** prior to commencement of business). The procedure prescribed under **Rule 8 and Rule 9** is:

(i) **Declaration (Part A):** The applicant declares his PAN, mobile number and e-mail address in **Part A of Form GST REG-01** on the common portal. The PAN is validated online; the mobile and e-mail are verified through a one-time password (OTP). On successful verification a **Temporary Reference Number (TRN)** is generated.

(ii) **Application (Part B):** Using the TRN, the applicant electronically submits **Part B of Form GST REG-01**, duly signed/verified through EVC or DSC, along with the specified documents, and obtains an **acknowledgement in Form GST REG-02**.

(iii) **Scrutiny and query:** Where the application is found deficient, the proper officer issues a notice in **Form GST REG-03** within the prescribed period; the applicant furnishes a reply/clarification in **Form GST REG-04** within 7 working days.

(iv) **Grant or rejection:** If the officer is satisfied (or fails to take action within the prescribed period, in which case registration is deemed granted), registration is granted in **Form GST REG-06** with a 15-digit GSTIN. If not satisfied, the application is rejected by a speaking order in **Form GST REG-05**.

**Effective date of registration (Rule 10):** Where the application is submitted **within 30 days** of becoming liable, the effective date of registration is the **date on which the person became liable** to registration. Where the application is submitted **after 30 days**, the effective date is the **date of grant** of registration.

**Conclusion:** Registration follows a two-part electronic application (REG-01) leading to a GSTIN in REG-06, and its effective date turns on whether the applicant applied within the 30-day window.

| Case | Situation |

### Q95. Ch: GST – Input Tax Credit — Time Limit and 180-Day Reversal (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Examine and answer the following independent situations with reference to the provisions of the CGST Act, 2017 relating to input tax credit:

| Case | Situation |
|---|---|
| (a) | Pragati Ltd. received a tax invoice dated 15th March 2025 (FY 2024-25). It intends to avail the ITC of ₹90,000 thereon while filing GSTR-3B in December 2025. Can it do so? |
| (b) | Rachana Ltd. availed ITC of ₹60,000 on an invoice dated 1st May 2025 but did not pay the supplier the value plus tax within 180 days. What are the consequences? |
| (c) | Sankalp Ltd. purchased capital goods and claimed depreciation under the Income-tax Act on the full cost including the GST component of ₹1,20,000. Can it also avail ITC of ₹1,20,000? |

**Answer:**

**(a) Time limit u/s 16(4) — Pragati Ltd.:** Section 16(4) provides that ITC in respect of an invoice pertaining to a financial year cannot be availed after the **30th November** following the end of that financial year, or the date of furnishing the annual return, whichever is earlier. The invoice dated 15-03-2025 pertains to **FY 2024-25**; the last date to avail its credit is **30th November 2025**. Since Pragati Ltd. proposes to avail it in the GSTR-3B for December 2025 (i.e., after 30-11-2025), the credit of ₹90,000 has **lapsed** and **cannot** be availed.

**(b) 180-day non-payment — Rachana Ltd.:** As per the **second and third provisos to Sec 16(2)** read with Rule 37, where a recipient fails to pay the supplier the **value of supply along with tax within 180 days** from the date of invoice, an amount equal to the ITC availed shall be **added to his output tax liability**, along with **interest under Sec 50**, in the return for the tax period following expiry of 180 days. Rachana Ltd. must therefore **reverse ₹60,000 with interest**. However, the credit can be **re-availed** (without any time limit under Sec 16(4)) once the payment to the supplier is subsequently made.

**(c) Depreciation on tax component — Sankalp Ltd.:** Section 16(3) provides that where a registered person has claimed **depreciation on the tax component** of the cost of capital goods under the Income-tax Act, 1961, the ITC on that tax component **shall not be allowed**. As Sankalp Ltd. capitalised and claimed depreciation on the ₹1,20,000 GST component, it **cannot** also avail ITC of ₹1,20,000. It must choose either depreciation on the tax component **or** ITC — not both.

**Conclusion:** (a) Credit lapses — not available beyond 30-11-2025; (b) ₹60,000 must be reversed with interest, re-availed on payment; (c) ITC of ₹1,20,000 is not available as depreciation was claimed on the tax component.

*(Full-marks tip: for (a) apply the "30th November of the next FY" limit (not 30 September, which is outdated); for (b) stress reversal **with interest** and the right to re-avail; for (c) cite Sec 16(3) and the either/or principle.)*

### Q96. Ch: GST – Registration — Casual Taxable Person and Threshold (Marks: 6) [Application]

**Conclusion:** (a) Yes — separate CTP registration in U.P. with advance tax, 90-day validity; (b) No — agriculturist exempt u/s 23; (c) No — turnover below the applicable Special Category threshold.

### Q97. Ch: GST – Tax Invoice, Payment & Returns — Time Limit for Invoice, Bill of Supply and Vouchers (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** With reference to Sections 31 and 34 of the CGST Act, 2017, explain: (i) the time limit for issue of a tax invoice for supply of goods and of services; (ii) when a bill of supply is to be issued; and (iii) the situations warranting a receipt voucher, a refund voucher and a payment voucher.

**Answer:**

**(i) Time limit for issue of tax invoice (Sec 31):**
For supply of **goods** [Sec 31(1)], the invoice must be issued **before or at the time of** — (a) **removal** of goods where the supply involves movement, or (b) **delivery/making available** to the recipient in any other case.

|---|---|
| **Receipt voucher** [Sec 31(3)(d)] | On **receipt of an advance** payment for a supply of goods or services, evidencing the advance. |
| **Refund voucher** [Sec 31(3)(e)] | Where an advance was received and a receipt voucher issued, but **subsequently no supply is made** and no tax invoice is issued — the refund of the advance is evidenced by a refund voucher. |
| **Payment voucher** [Sec 31(3)(g)] | Issued by a **recipient liable to pay tax under reverse charge**, at the time of making payment to the supplier. |

**Conclusion:** The timing of the invoice differs for goods (at removal/delivery) and services (within 30/45 days); exempt/composition suppliers issue a bill of supply; and receipt, refund and payment vouchers respectively evidence advances received, advances refunded, and reverse-charge payments.
