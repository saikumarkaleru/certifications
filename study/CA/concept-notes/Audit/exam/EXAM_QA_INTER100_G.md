### Q1. Ch: Nature, Objective & Scope (SA 200) — Objective of an Audit & Reasonable Assurance (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** "An audit conducted in accordance with Standards on Auditing (SAs) is not a guarantee that the financial statements are free from every misstatement." In the light of SA 200, explain the overall objectives of the auditor and why an audit provides only *reasonable* assurance and not *absolute* assurance.

**Answer:**
As per Standard on Auditing (SA) 200, "Overall Objectives of the Independent Auditor and the Conduct of an Audit in accordance with Standards on Auditing", in conducting an audit of financial statements, the **overall objectives of the auditor** are:

**(a)** To obtain **reasonable assurance** about whether the financial statements as a whole are free from material misstatement, whether due to fraud or error, thereby enabling the auditor to express an opinion on whether the financial statements are prepared, in all material respects, in accordance with an applicable financial reporting framework; and

**(b)** To **report** on the financial statements, and communicate as required by the SAs, in accordance with the auditor's findings.

**Reasonable assurance and not absolute assurance:** SA 200 clarifies that reasonable assurance is a **high level of assurance** but **not an absolute** level of assurance. It is obtained when the auditor has gathered **sufficient appropriate audit evidence** to reduce audit risk (i.e., the risk that the auditor expresses an inappropriate opinion when the financial statements are materially misstated) to an **acceptably low level**.

Absolute assurance is **not attainable** because of the **inherent limitations of an audit** which arise from:

| Source of Limitation | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Nature of financial reporting | Preparation involves **judgement** by management and subjective decisions/estimates with a range of acceptable interpretations. |
| Nature of audit procedures | The auditor works with evidence that is **persuasive rather than conclusive**; management may not provide complete information; fraud may involve sophisticated concealment/collusion. |
| Timeliness & cost-benefit | The audit must be completed within a **reasonable time and at reasonable cost**; it is impracticable to address every item exhaustively. |
| Other matters | Areas such as **fraud, related parties, and non-compliance with laws** are inherently harder to detect. |

Because of these limitations, an audit is **not a guarantee** that the financial statements are free from material misstatement; it does not assure the future viability of the entity or the efficiency of management.

**Conclusion:** The auditor's overall objective is to obtain reasonable (high, but not absolute) assurance and report accordingly; the inherent limitations of an audit make absolute assurance unattainable.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards stating BOTH limbs (a) and (b) of the objective verbatim and clearly linking "reasonable assurance" to "sufficient appropriate audit evidence" and "acceptably low audit risk". Marks are lost if you merely say "audit gives 100% correctness is not possible" without naming the specific inherent limitations from SA 200.)*

### Q2. Ch: Nature, Objective & Scope (SA 200) — Professional Scepticism (Marks: 4) [Application]
**Question:** During the audit of Vardhan Traders Ltd., the audit assistant obtained a bank confirmation and, since it agreed with the ledger, accepted a large fixed deposit as genuine without noticing that the confirmation was on a **photocopy** and the signature differed from the bank's usual format. The engagement partner remarked that the assistant "failed to exercise professional scepticism." Explain the concept of professional scepticism as per SA 200 and how it applied here.

**Answer:**
As per SA 200, **professional scepticism** is an attitude that includes a **questioning mind**, being **alert to conditions which may indicate possible misstatement due to error or fraud**, and a **critical assessment of audit evidence**.

Maintaining professional scepticism requires the auditor to be alert to, among other things:
- Audit evidence that **contradicts** other audit evidence obtained;
- Information that brings into question the **reliability of documents** and responses to inquiries to be used as audit evidence;
- Conditions that may indicate **possible fraud**; and
- Circumstances that suggest the need for **audit procedures in addition** to those required by the SAs.

Professional scepticism is necessary to the critical assessment of audit evidence — this includes **questioning contradictory evidence and the reliability of documents and responses** to inquiries. It also reduces the risks of overlooking unusual circumstances, over-generalising, or using inappropriate assumptions.

**Application to the facts:** The confirmation was a **photocopy** (not an original) and carried a **signature differing** from the bank's usual format. These are conditions that bring the **reliability of the document** into question and could indicate possible fraud (e.g., a fabricated confirmation). A sceptical auditor would **not accept the record or document at face value** merely because the amount agreed with the ledger, but would investigate — for example, by obtaining the **original confirmation directly** from the bank and independently verifying its genuineness. The assistant's acceptance without questioning the document's reliability demonstrates a **lack of professional scepticism**.

**Conclusion:** The engagement partner is correct — the assistant should have exercised professional scepticism by critically assessing the reliability of the photocopied, differently-signed confirmation instead of accepting it at face value.

*(Full-marks tip: Define professional scepticism with the three key phrases — "questioning mind", "alert to conditions", "critical assessment" — then tie the specific red flags (photocopy, altered signature) to "reliability of documents". Merely defining without applying to the case loses application marks.)*

### Q3. Ch: Nature, Objective & Scope (SA 200) — Ethical Requirements & Independence (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** SA 200 requires the auditor to comply with relevant ethical requirements, including those pertaining to independence. State the **fundamental principles of professional ethics** the auditor is required to comply with as referred to in SA 200.

**Answer:**
As per SA 200, the auditor shall comply with **relevant ethical requirements**, including those pertaining to **independence**, relating to financial statement audit engagements. The relevant ethical requirements ordinarily comprise the **Code of Ethics** issued by the Institute of Chartered Accountants of India, which establishes the following **fundamental principles of professional ethics**:

| Fundamental Principle | Requirement |
|---|---|
| **Integrity** | To be straightforward and honest in all professional and business relationships. |
| **Objectivity** | To not allow bias, conflict of interest or undue influence of others to override professional or business judgements. |
| **Professional competence and due care** | To maintain professional knowledge and skill at the level required and to act diligently in accordance with applicable technical and professional standards. |
| **Confidentiality** | To respect the confidentiality of information acquired and not disclose it to third parties without proper and specific authority, nor use it for personal advantage. |
| **Professional behaviour** | To comply with relevant laws and regulations and avoid any conduct that discredits the profession. |

**Independence** comprises both **independence of mind** and **independence in appearance**, and it safeguards the auditor's ability to form an opinion without being affected by influences that might compromise professional judgement, allowing the auditor to act with integrity and exercise objectivity and professional scepticism.

**Conclusion:** SA 200 requires compliance with the ICAI Code of Ethics — the five fundamental principles above together with the requirement of independence.

*(Full-marks tip: List all five fundamental principles by name with a one-line meaning each, and separately mention independence of mind and in appearance. Dropping any principle or omitting the "independence" limb costs marks.)*

### Q4. Ch: Nature, Objective & Scope (SA 200) — Scope, Preparation of FS & Premise of an Audit (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the **"premise, relating to the responsibilities of management and, where appropriate, those charged with governance, on which an audit is conducted"** as referred to in SA 200. Also state briefly the auditor's position regarding the scope of an audit.

**Answer:**
As per SA 200, an audit in accordance with SAs is conducted on the **premise** that management, and where appropriate, those charged with governance, have **acknowledged certain responsibilities** that are **fundamental** to the conduct of the audit. This premise means management/TCWG have acknowledged responsibility:

**(a) For the preparation of the financial statements** in accordance with the applicable financial reporting framework, including where relevant their fair presentation;

**(b) For such internal control** as management/TCWG determine is necessary to enable the preparation of financial statements that are **free from material misstatement**, whether due to fraud or error; and

**(c) To provide the auditor with:**
   - (i) **Access** to all information (records, documentation and other matters) of which management is aware that is relevant to the preparation of the financial statements;
   - (ii) **Additional information** that the auditor may request from management for the purpose of the audit; and
   - (iii) **Unrestricted access** to persons within the entity from whom the auditor determines it necessary to obtain audit evidence.

The audit of financial statements **does not relieve** management or TCWG of their responsibilities.

**Scope of an audit:** The term "scope of an audit" refers to the **audit procedures deemed necessary in the circumstances** to achieve the objective of the audit. The auditor's opinion on the financial statements deals with whether they are prepared, in all material respects, in accordance with the applicable financial reporting framework. Such an opinion is **common to all audits** of financial statements. The auditor's opinion, therefore, **does not assure**, for example, the future viability of the entity or the efficiency or effectiveness with which management has conducted the affairs of the entity.

**Conclusion:** The premise assigns the responsibilities for preparation, internal control and access to management/TCWG, while the auditor's role is limited to forming and expressing an opinion within the scope of necessary audit procedures.

*(Full-marks tip: Reproduce the three limbs (a), (b), (c) with the three sub-parts of (c) — the examiner marks each limb separately. Add the "does not relieve management" line and the "does not assure future viability/efficiency" point for the scope portion.)*

### Q5. Ch: Nature, Objective & Scope (SA 200) — Inherent Limitations of an Audit (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** State the reasons due to which the auditor is **not expected to, and cannot, reduce audit risk to zero** and therefore cannot obtain absolute assurance. Discuss with reference to the "inherent limitations of an audit" under SA 200.

**Answer:**
As per SA 200, the auditor is **not expected to, and cannot, reduce audit risk to zero** and cannot therefore obtain absolute assurance that the financial statements are free from material misstatement due to fraud or error. This is because there are **inherent limitations of an audit**, which result in most of the audit evidence on which the auditor draws conclusions being **persuasive rather than conclusive**. The inherent limitations arise from the following:

**1. The nature of financial reporting:** The preparation of financial statements involves **judgement by management** in applying the requirements of the applicable framework and involves **subjective decisions or assessments** or a degree of uncertainty (e.g., accounting estimates). Consequently, there is a range of acceptable interpretations or judgements.

**2. The nature of audit procedures:** There are **practical and legal limitations** on the auditor's ability to obtain audit evidence — for example:
   - Management or others may **not provide, intentionally or unintentionally, complete information**.
   - **Fraud may involve sophisticated schemes** designed to conceal it (forgery, deliberate failure to record, collusion) — audit procedures used to gather evidence may be **ineffective** for detecting such intentional misstatement.
   - An audit is **not an official investigation** into alleged wrongdoing; the auditor has no specific legal powers such as the power of search.

**3. Timeliness of financial reporting and the balance between benefit and cost:** The relevance of information, and thereby its value, tends to **diminish over time**, and there is a **balance to be struck** between the reliability of information and its cost. Users expect the auditor to form an opinion within a reasonable time and at reasonable cost.

**4. Other matters:** In respect of certain assertions or subject matters, the potential effects of the inherent limitations are **particularly significant** — e.g., **fraud** (especially involving senior management or collusion), **existence and completeness of related party relationships**, and **non-compliance with laws and regulations**.

Because of these limitations, an audit is **not a guarantee** that the financial statements are free from material misstatement.

**Conclusion:** Owing to the inherent limitations flowing from the nature of financial reporting, the nature of audit procedures, timeliness/cost considerations, and certain sensitive subject matters, the auditor cannot reduce audit risk to zero and can obtain only reasonable, not absolute, assurance.

*(Full-marks tip: Organise the answer under the four sub-headings SA 200 uses. State that evidence is "persuasive rather than conclusive". Examiners deduct marks if you list generic limitations without the four SA 200 categories or omit "audit is not an official investigation".)*

### Q6. Ch: Nature, Objective & Scope (SA 200) — Sufficient Appropriate Audit Evidence & Audit Risk (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the terms **"sufficiency"** and **"appropriateness"** of audit evidence and the concept of **"audit risk"** and its components as dealt with in SA 200. How are these concepts inter-related?

**Answer:**
As per SA 200:

**Audit evidence** is the information used by the auditor in arriving at the conclusions on which the auditor's opinion is based; it includes information contained in the accounting records and other information.

**Sufficiency and appropriateness** are inter-related qualities of audit evidence:

| Quality | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Sufficiency** | The measure of the **quantity** of audit evidence. The quantity needed is affected by the auditor's **assessment of the risks of material misstatement** (higher risk → more evidence) and also by the **quality** of such evidence (higher quality → less may be required). |
| **Appropriateness** | The measure of the **quality** of audit evidence — that is, its **relevance** and its **reliability** in providing support for the conclusions on which the auditor's opinion is based. |

**Audit risk** is the risk that the auditor expresses an **inappropriate audit opinion** when the financial statements are materially misstated. Audit risk is a function of the **risks of material misstatement** and **detection risk**:

- **Risk of material misstatement (RMM):** The risk that the financial statements are materially misstated prior to audit. It has two components at the assertion level:
  - **Inherent risk** — the susceptibility of an assertion to a misstatement that could be material, before consideration of related controls.
  - **Control risk** — the risk that a material misstatement will **not be prevented, or detected and corrected on a timely basis** by the entity's internal control.
- **Detection risk (DR):** The risk that the procedures performed by the auditor to reduce audit risk to an acceptably low level **will not detect** a misstatement that exists and could be material.

**Inter-relationship:** To reduce **audit risk** to an **acceptably low level**, the auditor must obtain **sufficient appropriate audit evidence**. For a given level of audit risk, detection risk bears an **inverse relationship** to the assessed RMM — the higher the assessed RMM, the lower the detection risk that can be accepted, and hence the more persuasive (sufficient and appropriate) the audit evidence required.

**Conclusion:** Sufficiency (quantity) and appropriateness (quality) of audit evidence together enable the auditor to reduce audit risk — comprising the risks of material misstatement (inherent and control) and detection risk — to an acceptably low level.

*(Full-marks tip: Define sufficiency and appropriateness distinctly (quantity vs quality; relevance + reliability), break audit risk into RMM (inherent + control) and detection risk, and state the inverse relationship. Missing the inverse relationship or confusing the components is the common deduction.)*

### Q7. Ch: Nature, Objective & Scope (SA 200) — Conduct of Audit & Compliance with SAs (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the requirements SA 200 places on the auditor regarding **compliance with the SAs relevant to the audit**, including the auditor's obligation to comply with each requirement of an SA and the circumstances in which a departure may be justified.

**Answer:**
As per SA 200, the auditor shall **comply with all SAs relevant to the audit**. An SA is relevant to the audit when the SA is **in effect** and the **circumstances addressed by the SA exist**.

Key requirements regarding compliance:

**1. Understanding the entire text:** To understand the objectives of an SA and to apply its requirements properly, the auditor is required to have an **understanding of the entire text of an SA**, including its **application and other explanatory material**, and not merely the requirements section.

**2. Complying with relevant requirements:** Subject to the point below, the auditor shall **comply with each requirement** of an SA unless, in the circumstances of the audit:
   - (a) The **entire SA is not relevant**; or
   - (b) The requirement is **conditional and the condition does not exist**.

**3. Objectives stated in individual SAs:** The auditor shall use the **objectives** stated in the relevant SAs in planning and performing the audit, having regard to the interrelationships among the SAs, to determine whether **additional audit procedures** are necessary and to **evaluate** whether sufficient appropriate audit evidence has been obtained.

**4. Departure from a relevant requirement (exceptional circumstances):** In **exceptional circumstances**, the auditor may judge it necessary to **depart** from a relevant requirement in an SA. In such a case, the auditor shall perform **alternative audit procedures** to achieve the aim of that requirement. The need to depart is expected to arise only where the requirement is for a specific procedure to be performed and, in the specific circumstances, that procedure would be **ineffective** in achieving the aim of the requirement.

**5. Documentation:** The auditor shall **document** how the alternative procedures performed achieve the aim of the requirement, and the reasons for the departure (as required by SA 230).

**Conclusion:** The auditor must comply with each relevant requirement of the SAs and may depart only in exceptional circumstances, performing and documenting alternative procedures to achieve the aim of the requirement.

*(Full-marks tip: State that the auditor must understand the ENTIRE text of the SA, the two exceptions to compliance, and the exceptional-circumstance departure with alternative procedures + documentation. Omitting the "document the reasons/alternative procedures" point is a common loss.)*

### Q8. Ch: Audit Strategy & Planning (SA 300) — Benefits of Planning an Audit (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** "Adequate planning benefits the audit of financial statements in several ways." Enumerate and explain the **benefits of planning** an audit of financial statements as stated in SA 300, "Planning an Audit of Financial Statements".

**Answer:**
As per SA 300, "Planning an Audit of Financial Statements", the objective of the auditor is to **plan the audit so that it will be performed in an effective manner**. Planning is **not a discrete phase** of an audit, but rather a **continual and iterative process** that often begins shortly after (or in connection with) the completion of the previous audit and continues until the completion of the current audit engagement.

**Adequate planning benefits the audit of financial statements in several ways, including the following:**

**1. Helping the auditor to devote appropriate attention to important areas** of the audit.

**2. Helping the auditor identify and resolve potential problems** on a timely basis.

**3. Helping the auditor properly organise and manage the audit engagement** so that it is performed in an effective and efficient manner.

**4. Assisting in the selection of engagement team members** with appropriate levels of capabilities and competence to respond to anticipated risks, and the proper assignment of work to them.

**5. Facilitating the direction and supervision** of engagement team members and the review of their work.

**6. Assisting, where applicable, in the coordination of work** done by auditors of components and experts.

The nature and extent of planning activities will **vary** according to the size and complexity of the entity, the key engagement team members' previous experience with the entity, and changes in circumstances that occur during the audit engagement.

**Conclusion:** Planning is a continual, iterative process whose benefits include focused attention on important areas, timely problem resolution, efficient organisation, proper team selection, effective direction and supervision, and coordination of others' work.

*(Full-marks tip: List all six benefits — examiners award marks per benefit. Add the opening line that planning is a "continual and iterative process, not a discrete phase". Merely writing three or four benefits caps the marks.)*

### Q9. Ch: Audit Strategy & Planning (SA 300) — Establishing the Overall Audit Strategy (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** In establishing the **overall audit strategy**, SA 300 requires the auditor to consider certain matters. Discuss the matters the auditor shall consider in establishing the overall audit strategy for the audit of a financial statement.

**Answer:**
As per SA 300, the auditor shall **establish an overall audit strategy** that sets the **scope, timing and direction** of the audit, and that guides the development of the audit plan. In establishing the overall audit strategy, the auditor shall:

**(a) Identify the characteristics of the engagement that define its scope:** For example, the financial reporting framework used, industry-specific reporting requirements, and the locations/components of the entity.

**(b) Ascertain the reporting objectives of the engagement to plan the timing of the audit and the nature of the communications required:** For example, deadlines for interim and final reporting, and key dates for expected communications with management and those charged with governance.

**(c) Consider the factors that, in the auditor's professional judgement, are significant in directing the engagement team's efforts:** For example, determination of appropriate **materiality levels**, preliminary identification of areas where there may be **higher risks of material misstatement**, and preliminary evaluation of whether the auditor may plan to obtain evidence regarding the **effectiveness of internal control**.

**(d) Consider the results of preliminary engagement activities** and, where applicable, whether knowledge gained on other engagements performed by the engagement partner for the entity is relevant.

**(e) Ascertain the nature, timing and extent of resources necessary to perform the engagement:** For example, the resources to deploy for specific audit areas (experienced team members for high-risk areas, involvement of experts), the amount of resources to allocate, when they are to be deployed, and how such resources are managed, directed and supervised (timing of team briefing/debriefing, engagement partner and manager reviews, quality control reviews).

Once the overall audit strategy has been established, an **audit plan** can be developed to address the various matters identified, taking into account the need to achieve the audit objectives through the **efficient use of the auditor's resources**. The establishment of the strategy and the detailed plan are **not necessarily discrete or sequential** processes but are closely inter-related.

**Conclusion:** The overall audit strategy is set by considering the engagement's scope-defining characteristics, reporting objectives/timing, significant directing factors, preliminary activities, and the nature, timing and extent of resources — and it guides the development of the more detailed audit plan.

*(Full-marks tip: Reproduce the five limbs (a)–(e) with an illustration for each; the "nature, timing and extent of resources" limb (e) is the one students most often drop. Note the strategy-plan inter-relationship at the end.)*

### Q10. Ch: Audit Strategy & Planning (SA 300) — Overall Strategy vs Audit Plan (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between the **overall audit strategy** and the **audit plan**. What does the audit plan include as per SA 300?

**Answer:**
As per SA 300, both the overall audit strategy and the audit plan are the auditor's responsibility, but they operate at **different levels of detail**. The relationship between them may be distinguished as follows:

| Basis | Overall Audit Strategy | Audit Plan |
|---|---|---|
| **Meaning** | Sets the **scope, timing and direction** of the audit at a broad level. | Is **more detailed** than the overall strategy; converts the strategy into the specific procedures to be performed. |
| **Nature** | Guides the development of the audit plan; establishes the **overall approach**. | Includes the **nature, timing and extent** of audit procedures to be performed by team members. |
| **Level** | High-level, **overall** view of the engagement. | **Detailed**, procedure-level view. |
| **Sequence** | Established **first**; guides the plan (though the two are closely inter-related, not strictly sequential). | Developed **after / alongside** the strategy to achieve the audit objectives efficiently. |

**The audit plan is more detailed than the overall audit strategy and shall include a description of:**

**(a)** The **nature, timing and extent of planned risk assessment procedures**, as determined under SA 315 (Identifying and Assessing the Risks of Material Misstatement).

**(b)** The **nature, timing and extent of planned further audit procedures** at the assertion level, as determined under SA 330 (The Auditor's Responses to Assessed Risks).

**(c)** **Other planned audit procedures** that are required to be carried out so that the engagement complies with the SAs.

Planning for these procedures takes place **over the course of the audit** as the audit plan develops. The auditor shall **update and change the overall audit strategy and the audit plan as necessary** during the course of the audit.

**Conclusion:** The overall audit strategy sets the broad scope, timing and direction, whereas the audit plan is the detailed conversion of that strategy into risk assessment procedures, further audit procedures and other required procedures; both are updated as the audit progresses.

*(Full-marks tip: Present the distinction in a table AND separately list the three components (a)–(c) of the audit plan, citing SA 315 and SA 330. The examiner looks for the correct cross-references to SA 315/SA 330; getting them the wrong way round costs marks.)*

### Q11. Ch: Audit Strategy & Planning (SA 300) — Planning in a First-Year / Initial Audit Engagement (Marks: 4) [Application]
**Question:** M/s Bhaskar & Co. have been appointed as auditors of Nimbus Industries Ltd. for the financial year 2025-26. This is an **initial audit engagement** (the entity was audited by another firm last year). State the **additional matters** the auditor of Bhaskar & Co. should consider in establishing the overall audit strategy and audit plan for an initial audit engagement as per SA 300.

**Answer:**
As per SA 300, prior to starting an **initial audit** (first-year engagement), the auditor may need to expand the planning activities because the auditor **does not ordinarily have the previous experience** with the entity that is considered when planning recurring engagements.

For an **initial audit engagement**, the auditor may consider the following **additional matters** in establishing the overall audit strategy and audit plan:

**1. Arrangement with the predecessor auditor** — Unless prohibited by law or regulation, arrangement to be made with the **predecessor auditor**, for example, to **review the predecessor auditor's working papers**.

**2. Major issues discussed with management in connection with the initial selection** as auditor, and the communication of these matters to those charged with governance and how these matters affect the overall audit strategy and audit plan.

**3. Audit procedures to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence regarding opening balances** — in accordance with **SA 510, "Initial Audit Engagements — Opening Balances"** (since misstatements in opening balances could affect the current period's figures).

**4. Assignment of firm personnel with appropriate levels of capabilities and competence** to respond to anticipated significant risks.

**5. Other procedures** required by the **firm's system of quality control** for initial audit engagements (for example, the engagement quality control review of the initial engagement before issuing the report, or acceptance of the client relationship).

**Conclusion:** For an initial audit engagement, in addition to the usual planning, the auditor should plan for arrangements with the predecessor auditor, opening-balance procedures under SA 510, quality-control requirements for first-year engagements, and matters discussed on initial selection.

### Q12. Ch: Audit Strategy & Planning (SA 300) — Direction, Supervision, Review & Documentation of Planning (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** SA 300 requires the auditor to plan the **nature, timing and extent of direction and supervision** of engagement team members and the review of their work. State the factors that influence this and the **documentation** requirements relating to planning under SA 300.

**Answer:**
As per SA 300, the auditor shall plan the **nature, timing and extent of the direction and supervision of engagement team members and the review of their work**.

**Factors influencing the extent of direction, supervision and review:** The nature, timing and extent of these activities **vary** depending on many factors, including:

| Factor | Effect on direction/supervision/review |
|---|---|
| **Size and complexity of the entity** | Larger/complex entities generally require greater direction and supervision. |
| **Area of the audit** | Areas with higher assessed risks of material misstatement require more extensive direction, supervision and review. |
| **Assessed risks of material misstatement** | Higher assessed risk → more extensive and timely supervision/review. |
| **Capabilities and competence of individual team members** | Less experienced members require more direction and closer review; more capable members require less. |

In audits of **smaller entities**, an audit may be carried out entirely by the engagement partner (who may be a sole practitioner). In such situations, questions of direction and supervision of team members and review of their work do not arise, as the engagement partner, having personally conducted all aspects, is aware of all material issues.

**Documentation (as per SA 300):** The auditor shall include in the **audit documentation**:

**(a)** The **overall audit strategy**;

**(b)** The **audit plan**; and

**(c)** Any **significant changes** made during the audit engagement to the overall audit strategy or the audit plan, and the **reasons** for such changes.

The documentation of the overall audit strategy is a **record of the key decisions** considered necessary to properly plan the audit and to communicate significant matters to the engagement team. The documentation of the audit plan is a **record of the planned procedures** that can be reviewed and approved prior to their performance. A record of significant changes serves to explain **why the changes were made** and the strategy and plan finally adopted.

**Conclusion:** The extent of direction, supervision and review depends on entity size/complexity, risk and team competence; and the auditor must document the strategy, the plan, and any significant changes with reasons.

### Q13. Ch: Materiality (SA 320) — Concept of Materiality & the '3% of Revenue' Benchmark (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** While auditing Trumel Foods Ltd. for FY 2025-26, the audit manager decided to determine **materiality for the financial statements as a whole** using **revenue** as the benchmark. The relevant figures are given below. Determine an appropriate materiality level and explain the concept of materiality and the use of benchmarks as per SA 320.

| Particulars | Amount (₹ in lakh) |
|---|---|
| Revenue from operations | 8,000 |
| Profit before tax | 240 |
| Total assets | 5,500 |
| Total equity | 2,000 |

The firm's policy is to apply **3% of revenue** where profit is volatile.

**Solution:**

**Concept of materiality (SA 320):** As per SA 320, "Materiality in Planning and Performing an Audit", **misstatements, including omissions, are considered to be material if they, individually or in the aggregate, could reasonably be expected to influence the economic decisions of users** taken on the basis of the financial statements. Judgements about materiality are made in the light of surrounding circumstances and are affected by the **size or nature of a misstatement, or both**, and by the auditor's perception of the **common financial information needs of users as a group**. Materiality is a matter of the auditor's **professional judgement**.

**Use of benchmarks (SA 320):** Determining materiality involves the exercise of professional judgement. A **percentage is often applied to a chosen benchmark** as a starting point. Factors affecting the identification of an appropriate benchmark include: the **elements of the financial statements** (e.g., assets, liabilities, equity, revenue, expenses); whether there are items on which users tend to focus; the **nature of the entity**, its life-cycle stage, and the industry/economic environment; the entity's **ownership structure and financing**; and the **relative volatility of the benchmark**. Where profit before tax is **volatile**, a more stable benchmark such as **revenue** or gross profit may be more appropriate.

**WN-1 — Selection of benchmark:** Profit before tax is only ₹240 lakh (3% of PBT ≈ ₹7.2 lakh would be too low and unstable). As the firm's policy applies **3% of revenue** where profit is volatile, and revenue is a stable benchmark for a food company, **revenue is selected**.

**WN-2 — Computation of materiality:**
Materiality = 3% × Revenue = 3% × ₹8,000 lakh
= 0.03 × 8,000 = **₹240 lakh**

**Statement Showing Determination of Overall Materiality:**

| Particulars | Basis | Amount (₹ in lakh) |
|---|---|---|
| Chosen benchmark | Revenue from operations | 8,000 |
| Percentage applied (volatile profit) | 3% | — |
| **Materiality for the FS as a whole** | 3% × 8,000 | **240** |

**Conclusion / Answer:** The overall materiality for Trumel Foods Ltd. is **₹240 lakh** (3% of revenue). Revenue is an appropriate benchmark here because profit before tax is volatile/low, whereas revenue is stable and a key figure for users of a food-manufacturing company.

### Q14. Ch: Materiality (SA 320) — Performance Materiality (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** For the audit of Kaveri Cements Ltd. (FY 2025-26), overall materiality for the financial statements as a whole has been set at **₹150 lakh**. The engagement partner decides to set **performance materiality at 75%** of overall materiality. Define performance materiality, explain its purpose, and compute the performance materiality. Also state how the auditor decides the percentage.

**Solution:**

**Definition (SA 320):** **Performance materiality** means the **amount or amounts set by the auditor at less than materiality for the financial statements as a whole** to reduce to an appropriately **low level** the probability that the **aggregate of uncorrected and undetected misstatements** exceeds materiality for the financial statements as a whole. If applicable, performance materiality also refers to the amount(s) set by the auditor at less than the materiality level(s) for **particular classes of transactions, account balances or disclosures**.

**Purpose:** Planning the audit solely to detect individually material misstatements **overlooks the fact that the aggregate** of individually immaterial misstatements may cause the financial statements to be materially misstated, and it leaves **no margin** for possible undetected misstatements. Performance materiality (which is **not a simple mechanical calculation**) is set to reduce this risk to an appropriately low level and involves the exercise of **professional judgement**.

**WN-1 — Computation of performance materiality:**
Performance materiality = 75% × Overall materiality
= 0.75 × ₹150 lakh = **₹112.50 lakh**

**Statement Showing Determination of Performance Materiality:**

| Particulars | Amount (₹ in lakh) |
|---|---|
| Materiality for the FS as a whole | 150.00 |
| Percentage applied (professional judgement) | 75% |
| **Performance materiality** | **112.50** |

**How the auditor decides the percentage:** The determination of performance materiality is **affected by the auditor's understanding of the entity** (updated during the performance of the risk assessment procedures), the **nature and extent of misstatements identified in previous audits**, and thereby the auditor's **expectations in relation to misstatements in the current period**. A **higher expected level of misstatements / higher risk** leads the auditor to set performance materiality at a **lower percentage** of overall materiality (giving a larger buffer), and vice versa.

**Conclusion / Answer:** Performance materiality for Kaveri Cements Ltd. is **₹112.50 lakh**. It is set below overall materiality to provide a margin so that the aggregate of undetected and uncorrected misstatements is unlikely to exceed the ₹150 lakh overall materiality.

*(Full-marks tip: Emphasise that performance materiality is NOT a mechanical calculation and its purpose is to cover the AGGREGATE of undetected + uncorrected misstatements. Mention the inverse link between risk/expected misstatements and the percentage chosen. Just computing 75% × 150 without this earns partial marks only.)*

### Q15. Ch: Materiality (SA 320) — Revision of Materiality During the Audit (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** During the audit of Sundara Textiles Ltd., materiality for the financial statements as a whole had initially been set at **₹100 lakh** based on **budgeted profit before tax of ₹1,000 lakh (10%)**. Midway through the audit, it became apparent that the **actual profit before tax** would be only **₹600 lakh** due to a downturn. Explain, with reference to SA 320, whether and how the auditor should **revise materiality**, and compute the revised materiality if the same 10% basis is retained on actual results.

**Solution:**

**Requirement to revise materiality (SA 320):** As per SA 320, the auditor shall **revise materiality for the financial statements as a whole** (and, if applicable, the materiality level(s) for particular classes of transactions, account balances or disclosures) in the event of becoming aware of information during the audit that would have **caused the auditor to have determined a different amount (or amounts) initially**.

Materiality may need to be revised as a result of **a change in circumstances** that occurred during the audit (e.g., a decision to dispose of a major part of the business), **new information**, or a **change in the auditor's understanding** of the entity and its operations as a result of performing further audit procedures. For example, if during the audit it appears that **actual financial results are likely to be substantially different** from the anticipated results used to initially determine materiality, the auditor **revises** materiality.

**WN-1 — Whether revision is required:** The initial materiality of ₹100 lakh was based on **budgeted PBT of ₹1,000 lakh**. The actual PBT is now expected to be only ₹600 lakh — a substantial change from the anticipated results. Since this new information would have caused a **different amount to be determined initially**, materiality **must be revised**.

**WN-2 — Computation of revised materiality (10% of actual PBT):**
Revised materiality = 10% × Actual PBT = 10% × ₹600 lakh = **₹60 lakh**

**Statement Showing Revision of Materiality:**

| Particulars | Initial | Revised |
|---|---|---|
| Benchmark: Profit before tax (₹ lakh) | 1,000 (budgeted) | 600 (actual expected) |
| Percentage applied | 10% | 10% |
| **Materiality for the FS as a whole (₹ lakh)** | **100** | **60** |

**Consequential action:** If the auditor concludes that a **lower materiality** (and hence lower performance materiality) is appropriate, the auditor shall **determine whether it is necessary to revise the performance materiality**, and whether the **nature, timing and extent of the further audit procedures remain appropriate** — since a lower materiality generally requires **more extensive** audit procedures.

**Conclusion / Answer:** The auditor must revise the overall materiality **downward from ₹100 lakh to ₹60 lakh**, reconsider the performance materiality, and re-evaluate whether the planned further audit procedures are still adequate in the light of the lower materiality.

*(Full-marks tip: State the trigger for revision (new information/changed circumstances would have led to a different amount), compute the revised figure, AND cover the consequential step — reconsidering performance materiality and the further audit procedures. Missing the consequential action is the most common deduction here.)*

### Q16. Ch: Materiality (SA 320) — Materiality & Audit Risk Relationship; Qualitative Misstatements (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the **relationship between materiality and audit risk** as recognised in SA 320. Also state, with an example, why some misstatements that are **quantitatively below** the materiality level may nevertheless be considered **material** on **qualitative** grounds.

**Answer:**
**Relationship between materiality and audit risk (SA 320):** In planning and performing an audit, the auditor is required to obtain reasonable assurance that the financial statements are free from material misstatement. In doing so, the concept of materiality is applied **both in planning and performing the audit, and in evaluating the effect of identified misstatements** on the audit and of uncorrected misstatements, if any, on the financial statements.

The auditor's determination of materiality is a matter of **professional judgement** and is affected by the auditor's perception of the financial information needs of users. Materiality and audit risk are **considered together** throughout the audit — in particular, when (a) identifying and assessing the risks of material misstatement; (b) determining the nature, timing and extent of further audit procedures; and (c) evaluating the effect of uncorrected misstatements. There is an **inverse relationship** in application: the **lower the materiality level** set by the auditor, the **greater the audit risk** (because more misstatements would now be considered material), and hence the auditor responds with **more extensive audit procedures** to reduce detection risk.

**Qualitative materiality:** Judgements about materiality are affected by the **size or the nature of a misstatement, or a combination of both**. Therefore, a misstatement **below the quantitative materiality level** may still be **material by nature**. For example:
- A misstatement that, though small in amount, **changes a loss into a profit** or a profit into a loss;
- A misstatement that causes the entity to **just meet** a regulatory requirement, an analyst's expectation, or a **debt covenant**;
- A misstatement relating to **non-compliance with law or a related-party transaction** or an item affecting **directors' remuneration**.

Such misstatements could **reasonably be expected to influence the economic decisions of users** despite their small amount, and are therefore material on qualitative grounds.

**Conclusion:** Materiality and audit risk are inversely related and are considered together throughout the audit; and materiality depends on both the size and the nature of a misstatement, so a quantitatively small misstatement may still be qualitatively material.

*(Full-marks tip: State the INVERSE relationship (lower materiality → higher audit risk → more procedures) explicitly and give a concrete qualitative example such as "converts a loss into a profit" or "breach of a debt covenant". A generic answer without an example loses the illustration marks.)*

### Q18. Ch: Risk Assessment & Internal Control — Understanding the entity and its environment (SA 315) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** The auditor is required to obtain an understanding of the entity and its environment as a basis for identifying and assessing the risks of material misstatement. State the specific aspects of the entity and its environment (other than the entity's system of internal control) of which the auditor should obtain an understanding under SA 315 (Revised).

**Answer:**
SA 315 (Revised), "Identifying and Assessing the Risks of Material Misstatement through Understanding the Entity and Its Environment", requires the auditor to obtain an understanding of the entity and its environment because such understanding establishes a frame of reference within which the auditor plans the audit and exercises professional judgement about assessing risks of material misstatement (RoMM) and responding to them. The understanding is a continuous, dynamic and iterative process of gathering, updating and analysing information throughout the audit.

Apart from the entity's system of internal control, the auditor is required to obtain an understanding of the following aspects:

1. **Relevant industry, regulatory and other external factors, including the applicable financial reporting framework (AFRF).** The auditor understands industry conditions such as the competitive environment, supplier and customer relationships and technological developments; the regulatory environment including the AFRF, the legal and political environment and applicable taxation; and other external factors such as general economic conditions, interest rates and availability of finance. These factors help the auditor identify inherent risk factors arising from the environment.

2. **The nature of the entity.** This includes the entity's operations (its business, products/services, markets and conduct of operations), its ownership and governance structure, the types of investments it is making and plans to make (including investments in special-purpose entities), and the way the entity is structured and financed. Understanding the nature of the entity enables the auditor to understand the classes of transactions, account balances and disclosures expected in the financial statements.

3. **The entity's selection and application of accounting policies, including the reasons for changes thereto.** The auditor evaluates whether the entity's accounting policies are appropriate for its business and consistent with the AFRF and with those used in the relevant industry.

4. **The entity's objectives and strategies, and those related business risks that may result in risks of material misstatement.** Business risks result from significant conditions, events or circumstances that could adversely affect the entity's ability to achieve its objectives; most business risks eventually have financial consequences and hence may give rise to a RoMM.

5. **The measurement and review of the entity's financial performance.** The auditor understands the performance measures — internal (budgets, KPIs, variance analysis) and external (analysts' reports) — that indicate to management and others how the entity is performing, since pressure to achieve targets may create incentives for management bias or misstatement.

**Conclusion:** Under SA 315 (Revised), the auditor must understand the external factors (including the AFRF), the nature of the entity, its accounting policies, its objectives, strategies and related business risks, and the measurement and review of its financial performance, so as to provide a robust basis for identifying and assessing risks of material misstatement.

### Q22. Ch: Risk Assessment & Internal Control — Overall responses to assessed risks at the financial-statement level (SA 330) (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** SA 330 distinguishes between "overall responses" to assessed risks of material misstatement at the financial-statement level and "further audit procedures" at the assertion level. Explain what is meant by overall responses and describe the responses an auditor may design to address assessed risks of material misstatement at the financial-statement level.

**Answer:**
SA 330, "The Auditor's Responses to Assessed Risks", requires the auditor to design and implement **overall responses** to address the assessed risks of material misstatement (RoMM) at the **financial-statement level**. Risks at the financial-statement level are risks that relate pervasively to the financial statements as a whole and potentially affect many assertions; they often arise from a weak control environment. Because such risks are pervasive, they cannot be adequately addressed by procedures directed at individual assertions alone and therefore require responses that affect the audit as a whole. This is distinct from **further audit procedures** (tests of controls and substantive procedures), whose nature, timing and extent are designed to respond to assessed RoMM at the **assertion level**.

Overall responses the auditor may design include:

1. **Emphasising professional skepticism to the engagement team.** The auditor stresses to the team the need to maintain professional skepticism in gathering and evaluating audit evidence, given the assessed pervasive risks.

2. **Assigning more experienced staff or staff with special skills, or using experts.** The auditor deploys personnel with greater experience or specialised competence (for example, IT or valuation experts) commensurate with the assessed risk.

3. **Providing more supervision.** The extent of direction and supervision of the engagement team is increased.

4. **Incorporating additional elements of unpredictability.** The auditor introduces unpredictability in the selection of further audit procedures — for example, testing accounts or assertions not usually tested, varying timing, or using different sampling methods — so that entity personnel cannot anticipate the procedures.

5. **Making general changes to the nature, timing or extent of further audit procedures.** For example, performing substantive procedures at the period-end rather than at an interim date when the assessed risk is higher, or obtaining more extensive or more persuasive audit evidence.

The auditor's assessment of the control environment influences these overall responses: an effective control environment may allow the auditor to have more confidence in internal control and in the reliability of internally generated evidence and to conduct some procedures at an interim date; a deficient control environment has the opposite effect, so the auditor may conduct more procedures as of the period-end, obtain more extensive evidence, or increase the number of locations included in the audit scope.

**Conclusion:** Overall responses under SA 330 are audit-wide measures — heightened skepticism, more experienced or specialised staff, greater supervision, unpredictability, and general changes to the nature, timing and extent of procedures — designed to address pervasive financial-statement-level risks, and they are shaped by the auditor's evaluation of the control environment.

*(Full-marks tip: Distinguish FS-level (pervasive) from assertion-level responses at the outset, list all five overall responses, and connect them to the control environment. Common deductions: describing substantive procedures/tests of controls (which are assertion-level) instead of overall responses, and omitting "unpredictability".)*

### Q23. Ch: Risk Assessment & Internal Control — Specific responses and substantive procedures for significant risks (SA 330) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** In the audit of Northwind Retail Ltd., the auditor has assessed the valuation of slow-moving inventory as a significant risk and intends to rely on the entity's automated inventory-ageing control to reduce substantive testing. Explain the requirements of SA 330 regarding (a) tests of controls where reliance is planned, (b) the auditor's response where a significant risk exists, and (c) substantive procedures that must be performed irrespective of the assessed risks.

**Answer:**
SA 330 requires the nature, timing and extent of further audit procedures to be responsive to the assessed risks of material misstatement at the assertion level. Applying its specific requirements to Northwind Retail Ltd.:

**(a) Tests of controls where reliance is planned.** SA 330 requires the auditor to design and perform tests of controls to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence about the operating effectiveness of relevant controls when (i) the auditor's assessment of RoMM at the assertion level includes an expectation that the controls are operating effectively (i.e., the auditor intends to rely on them to determine the nature, timing and extent of substantive procedures), or (ii) substantive procedures alone cannot provide sufficient appropriate audit evidence. Since the auditor intends to rely on the automated inventory-ageing control, the auditor must test its operating effectiveness. Because it is an automated (IT-dependent) control, the auditor must also test the relevant general IT controls that support its continued effective functioning.

**(b) Response where a significant risk exists.** SA 330 provides that if the auditor plans to rely on controls over a risk the auditor has determined to be a **significant risk**, the auditor must test the operating effectiveness of those controls **in the current period** — reliance on audit evidence about their operating effectiveness obtained in previous audits is not permitted. Further, when the approach to a significant risk consists only of substantive procedures, those procedures must include **tests of details** (not solely substantive analytical procedures). Thus, for the slow-moving inventory valuation, the ageing control must be tested this year, and substantive procedures must include tests of details of the ageing and net realisable value of specific slow-moving lines.

**(c) Substantive procedures irrespective of assessed risk.** SA 330 requires that, irrespective of the assessed RoMM, the auditor design and perform substantive procedures for each **material** class of transactions, account balance and disclosure. This reflects that the auditor's risk assessment is judgemental and may not identify all risks, and that there are inherent limitations to internal control including management override. Hence, even where the ageing control operates effectively, the auditor must still perform substantive procedures on the material inventory balance.

**Conclusion:** The auditor must test the automated ageing control (and supporting general IT controls) in the current period because valuation is a significant risk, must include tests of details in the substantive response to that significant risk, and must in any event perform substantive procedures on the material inventory balance.

*(Full-marks tip: Award-winning answers cite the three distinct SA 330 rules — reliance-triggers tests of controls, current-period testing plus tests of details for significant risks, and mandatory substantive procedures for material items. Losing the mark about "current period only" for significant-risk controls, or forgetting general IT controls for an automated control, is the common slip.)*

### Q24. Ch: Risk Assessment & Internal Control — Communicating deficiencies in internal control (SA 265) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** During the audit of Crestline Ceramics Ltd., the auditor identifies the following control deficiencies: (i) bank reconciliations are prepared but never reviewed by anyone independent, and an unreconciled difference of ₹22 lakh has persisted for eight months; (ii) the same clerk records cash receipts and posts to the customer ledger; and (iii) a minor delay in filing supplier invoices. The engagement partner is unsure which of these must be reported and to whom. Advise, with reference to SA 265, on the determination of significant deficiencies and the auditor's communication obligations.

**Answer:**
SA 265, "Communicating Deficiencies in Internal Control to Those Charged with Governance and Management", governs the auditor's responsibility to communicate deficiencies identified during the audit. A **deficiency in internal control** exists when (a) a control is designed, implemented or operated in such a way that it is unable to prevent, or detect and correct, misstatements in the financial statements on a timely basis, or (b) a control necessary to prevent, or detect and correct, such misstatements is missing. A **significant deficiency** is a deficiency or combination of deficiencies that, in the auditor's professional judgement, is of sufficient importance to merit the attention of those charged with governance (TCWG).

Indicators of a significant deficiency include: the likelihood of the deficiency leading to material misstatements in future; the susceptibility to loss or fraud of the related asset or liability; the subjectivity and complexity of amounts involved; the amounts exposed to the deficiency; evidence of ineffective aspects of the control environment; and evidence of ineffective response by management to identified deficiencies.

Applying SA 265 to the three matters:

| Deficiency | Assessment | Communication |
|---|---|---|
| (i) Unreviewed bank reconciliations; ₹22 lakh difference unresolved for 8 months | Significant deficiency — susceptibility to loss/fraud of cash, a persistent material unreconciled difference, and management's ineffective response | Communicate **in writing** to **TCWG** on a timely basis |
| (ii) Same clerk records receipts and posts to ledger (no segregation of duties over cash) | Significant deficiency — lack of segregation over a fraud-susceptible asset (cash) | Communicate **in writing** to **TCWG** |
| (iii) Minor delay in filing supplier invoices | A deficiency, but not significant | Communicate to an appropriate level of **management**; need not go to TCWG |

**Manner and timing of communication.** The auditor must communicate **significant deficiencies in writing** to those charged with governance on a **timely basis**. The written communication must include a description of the deficiencies and their potential effects, and sufficient information to enable TCWG to understand the context — namely that the purpose was to express an opinion on the financial statements, that the audit considered internal control relevant to the preparation of the financial statements but not for expressing an opinion on the effectiveness of internal control, and that the matters reported are limited to those identified during the audit and considered significant. Other (non-significant) deficiencies of sufficient importance to merit management's attention may be communicated to management orally or in writing.

**Conclusion:** Matters (i) and (ii) are significant deficiencies and must be communicated in writing to those charged with governance on a timely basis, whereas matter (iii) is a non-significant deficiency and may be communicated to the appropriate level of management.

*(Full-marks tip: Full credit requires the definitions of deficiency and significant deficiency, at least two indicators, and the "in writing / to TCWG / timely basis" rule, plus correct classification of each fact. Students frequently lose marks by reporting the trivial invoice-filing delay to TCWG or by omitting the required contents of the written communication.)*

### Q25. Ch: Risk Assessment & Internal Control — Using evidence about operating effectiveness from previous audits (SA 330) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** M/s Halcyon Textiles Ltd. has a stable set of controls over its payroll process that the auditor tested and found effective in the prior year. The engagement manager proposes to place reliance this year on the audit evidence about operating effectiveness obtained in the previous two audits, without re-testing. Advise the manager, with reference to SA 330, on the conditions for using audit evidence about operating effectiveness of controls obtained in previous audits, including the position where the control mitigates a significant risk.

**Answer:**
SA 330 permits, in certain circumstances, the use of audit evidence about the operating effectiveness of controls obtained in previous audits, but it imposes conditions and prohibitions that the engagement manager of M/s Halcyon Textiles Ltd. must observe.

**1. Establishing continuing relevance.** If the auditor plans to use audit evidence about the operating effectiveness of controls obtained in previous audits, the auditor must establish the continuing relevance of that evidence by obtaining audit evidence about whether **significant changes** in those controls have occurred since they were tested. The auditor obtains this by inquiry combined with observation or inspection. If there have been changes that affect the continuing relevance of the audit evidence, the controls must be **tested in the current audit**.

**2. Frequency of re-testing where there is no change.** If the auditor plans to rely on controls that have **not changed** since they were last tested, the auditor must test the operating effectiveness of such controls **at least once in every third audit**, and must test some controls each audit to avoid the possibility of testing all the controls on which reliance is placed in a single audit period with no testing in the other two periods.

**3. Factors that shorten the re-testing interval.** In determining whether it is appropriate to use audit evidence from previous audits, and, if so, the length of the interval before re-testing, the auditor considers factors such as the effectiveness of other elements of internal control (including the control environment and monitoring); the risks arising from the characteristics of the control, including whether it is manual or automated; the effectiveness of general IT controls; the effectiveness of the control and its application as evidenced in prior audits, and whether deficiencies were noted; whether the lack of change in a particular control poses a risk due to changing circumstances; and the risk of material misstatement and the extent of reliance placed on the control. The greater the risk, or the greater the reliance, the shorter the interval, if any.

**4. Prohibition where the control addresses a significant risk.** If the control mitigates a risk that the auditor has determined to be a **significant risk**, the auditor is **not permitted** to rely on audit evidence about operating effectiveness obtained in previous audits; such controls must be tested in the **current period**.

**Application:** For the payroll controls that have not changed and do not address a significant risk, Halcyon's auditor may rely on prior-year evidence but must (i) confirm the absence of significant changes each year, (ii) test each such control at least once in every third audit, and (iii) test some controls every year. The manager's proposal to place reliance for a further year without any current-year work is acceptable only for controls last tested within the permitted three-audit cycle and only after confirming there have been no significant changes; any control that addresses a significant risk must be tested this year.

**Conclusion:** SA 330 allows measured reliance on prior-audit evidence about controls that are unchanged and do not address significant risks — subject to confirming no significant changes, re-testing at least every third audit, and testing some controls each year — but requires current-period testing of any control mitigating a significant risk.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner looks for the "every third audit", "test some controls each year", "confirm no significant change" and the significant-risk prohibition. Answers that allow blanket multi-year reliance without the three-audit cap, or that miss the significant-risk carve-out, are marked down heavily.)*

### Q26. Ch: Audit Evidence — Persuasive versus conclusive audit evidence (SA 500) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** "Audit evidence is generally persuasive rather than conclusive in nature." Explain this statement with reference to SA 500, and describe the two qualities — sufficiency and appropriateness — that the auditor evaluates in respect of audit evidence.

**Answer:**
SA 500, "Audit Evidence", defines audit evidence as the information used by the auditor in arriving at the conclusions on which the auditor's opinion is based, and includes both the information contained in the accounting records underlying the financial statements and other information. The auditor's objective is to design and perform audit procedures to obtain **sufficient appropriate** audit evidence to be able to draw reasonable conclusions on which to base the auditor's opinion.

**Persuasive rather than conclusive.** Audit evidence is cumulative in nature and is primarily obtained from audit procedures performed during the course of the audit. Because of the inherent limitations of an audit — the use of testing and sampling, the inherent limitations of internal control, the fact that most audit evidence is persuasive rather than conclusive, the need for the audit to be conducted within a reasonable period and at reasonable cost, and the involvement of judgement — the auditor is not expected to, and cannot, reduce audit risk to zero and therefore cannot obtain absolute assurance. Consequently, the auditor works with evidence that persuades the auditor towards a conclusion rather than proving it conclusively; the auditor considers the relationship between the cost of obtaining evidence and the usefulness of the information obtained, though difficulty or expense involved is not in itself a valid basis for omitting an audit procedure for which there is no alternative. The auditor uses professional judgement and exercises professional skepticism in evaluating the quantity and quality of evidence, and thus its sufficiency and appropriateness, to support the audit opinion.

**The two qualities of audit evidence:**

| Quality | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Sufficiency** | The measure of the **quantity** of audit evidence. The quantity needed is affected by the auditor's assessment of the risks of material misstatement (the higher the assessed risk, the more evidence is likely to be required) and also by the quality of such audit evidence (the higher the quality, the less may be required). |
| **Appropriateness** | The measure of the **quality** of audit evidence — its **relevance** and its **reliability** in providing support for the conclusions on which the auditor's opinion is based. |

The two are interrelated: merely obtaining more audit evidence (sufficiency) does not compensate for evidence that is of poor quality (appropriateness), and the quantity required is influenced by the quality of the evidence available.

**Conclusion:** Owing to the inherent limitations of an audit, audit evidence is persuasive rather than conclusive; the auditor must obtain audit evidence that is both sufficient (adequate in quantity) and appropriate (relevant and reliable in quality) to reduce audit risk to an acceptably low level and support a reasonable conclusion.

*(Full-marks tip: Anchor the "persuasive not conclusive" point in the inherent limitations of an audit and the cost-benefit consideration (with the caveat that cost alone is not a basis to omit a procedure). Define sufficiency = quantity and appropriateness = quality = relevance + reliability, and state their interrelationship. Missing that appropriateness has two limbs is a frequent deduction.)*

### Q27. Ch: Audit Evidence — Using the work of an auditor's expert and a management's expert (SA 500 / SA 620) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** In the audit of Zenith Realty Ltd., the fair value of investment property (₹120 crore) has been determined by a valuer engaged by the management. The auditor, being unfamiliar with property valuation, also proposes to engage an independent valuer of his own to evaluate the reasonableness of the figure. Distinguish between a "management's expert" and an "auditor's expert", and explain the auditor's responsibilities in relying on the work of each.

**Answer:**
When information to be used as audit evidence has been prepared using the work of an expert, the auditor may be dealing either with a **management's expert** or an **auditor's expert**. The valuer engaged by the management of Zenith Realty Ltd. is a management's expert, while the independent valuer whom the auditor proposes to engage is an auditor's expert.

**Distinction:**

| Basis | Management's expert (SA 500) | Auditor's expert (SA 620) |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | An individual or organisation possessing expertise in a field other than accounting or auditing, whose work in that field is used by the **entity** to assist in preparing the financial statements | An individual or organisation possessing expertise in a field other than accounting or auditing, whose work in that field is used by the **auditor** to assist in obtaining sufficient appropriate audit evidence |
| Engaged / employed by | The entity (management) | The auditor (engaged or employed) |
| Governing SA | SA 500 (paragraph on information from a management's expert) | SA 620, "Using the Work of an Auditor's Expert" |
| Responsibility for the opinion | Rests with the auditor; the expert's work supports management's figures | Rests solely with the auditor; that responsibility is not reduced by the auditor's use of the expert |

**Auditor's responsibilities regarding the management's expert (SA 500).** When information to be used as audit evidence has been prepared using the work of a management's expert (here, the valuation of the investment property), the auditor must, to the extent necessary having regard to the significance of that expert's work for the auditor's purposes:
(a) **evaluate the competence, capabilities and objectivity** of the management's expert — for example, professional qualification, membership of a valuation body, experience and reputation, and any threats to objectivity arising from relationships with the entity;
(b) **obtain an understanding of the work** of that expert — the nature, scope and objectives of the valuation, the assumptions and methods used and their consistency with the applicable financial reporting framework; and
(c) **evaluate the appropriateness of that expert's work** as audit evidence for the relevant assertion — including the relevance and reasonableness of the expert's findings, the relevance and reasonableness of the assumptions and methods, and the relevance, completeness and accuracy of the source data used.

**Auditor's responsibilities regarding the auditor's expert (SA 620).** Where the auditor engages his own valuer, the auditor must (a) determine the need for an auditor's expert; (b) evaluate that expert's competence, capabilities and objectivity; (c) obtain an understanding of the expert's field of expertise; (d) agree the nature, scope and objectives of the expert's work; and (e) evaluate the adequacy of the expert's work, including the relevance and reasonableness of findings, assumptions, methods and source data. The auditor does not refer to the auditor's expert in an unmodified auditor's report; a reference is made only if required by law or relevant to understanding a modification, and even then the report must state that the reference does not reduce the auditor's responsibility.

**Conclusion:** The management's valuer is a management's expert whose work the auditor evaluates under SA 500 (competence/objectivity, understanding, appropriateness), while the auditor's own valuer is an auditor's expert governed by SA 620; in either case the responsibility for the audit opinion on the ₹120 crore fair value remains entirely with the auditor.

*(Full-marks tip: A clear distinction table plus the three SA 500 responsibilities for a management's expert (evaluate competence/objectivity; understand the work; evaluate appropriateness) earns the bulk of the marks. State that the auditor's responsibility is undiminished, and that the auditor's expert is not referred to in an unmodified report. Confusing the two experts, or listing only "competence" without "objectivity", costs marks.)*

### Q28. Ch: Audit Evidence — Auditing an accounting estimate: warranty provision (SA 540 / SA 500) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Apex Appliances Ltd. has recognised a warranty provision of ₹6.5 crore for the year ended 31st March, 2026, computed by management as 2% of annual sales based on historical claim patterns. As the auditor, explain the approaches available under SA 540 for obtaining sufficient appropriate audit evidence over this accounting estimate, and describe how you would evaluate the risk of management bias.

**Answer:**
The warranty provision of Apex Appliances Ltd. is an **accounting estimate** — a monetary amount whose measurement is subject to estimation uncertainty. SA 540 (Revised), "Auditing Accounting Estimates and Related Disclosures", requires the auditor to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence about whether the accounting estimate and related disclosures are reasonable in the context of the applicable financial reporting framework, or are misstated. Because a warranty provision involves subjectivity (assumptions about future claim rates) and estimation uncertainty, it is susceptible to management bias and must be audited with professional skepticism.

**Approaches available to obtain audit evidence.** In responding to the assessed risk of material misstatement, the auditor may adopt one or more of the following approaches, taking account of the nature of the estimate:

1. **Obtain audit evidence from events occurring up to the date of the auditor's report.** The auditor examines actual warranty claims settled after year-end but before the report date to assess whether the 2% provision was adequate — this may provide the most reliable evidence for a short-cycle warranty.

2. **Test how management made the accounting estimate.** The auditor evaluates the method of measurement used and its appropriateness; the reasonableness of the significant assumptions (the 2% claim rate and the historical pattern relied on); the relevance, completeness and accuracy of the underlying data (sales figures and historical claims); and whether the method has been applied consistently and the mathematical accuracy of the computation.

3. **Test the operating effectiveness of the controls** over how management made the estimate, together with appropriate substantive procedures — appropriate where the entity has robust controls over the estimation process.

4. **Develop an auditor's point estimate or range** to evaluate management's point estimate. The auditor may independently estimate the provision using an alternative claim rate derived from the entity's own claims data or industry data, and compare it with management's ₹6.5 crore figure; if the auditor's range differs materially, a misstatement may exist.

**Evaluating the risk of management bias.** The auditor reviews the judgements and decisions of management in making the estimate for indicators of possible management bias, such as: changes in an accounting estimate or method from the prior period without justification when conditions have not changed; selection of assumptions that consistently tend to increase or decrease reported profit (for example, understating the warranty rate to inflate profit); and setting the estimate at one end of the range of reasonable outcomes in successive periods. The presence of such indicators does not itself constitute a misstatement for individual estimates, but it is considered when evaluating whether the financial statements as a whole are free from material misstatement and whether the auditor's risk assessment remains appropriate. The auditor also obtains written representations from management about the reasonableness of the significant assumptions.

**Conclusion:** For the ₹6.5 crore warranty provision, the auditor may rely on post-year-end claims experience, test how management made the estimate (method, assumptions and data), test the related controls, and/or develop an independent estimate; throughout, the auditor evaluates the assumptions for indicators of management bias and applies professional skepticism.

*(Full-marks tip: Name all four SA 540 response approaches, apply each to the warranty facts, and separately address management-bias indicators plus written representations. Reproducing only the generic "sufficient appropriate evidence" line without the four approaches, or omitting the subsequent-claims-experience approach that is often the most reliable for warranties, limits the score.)*

### Q29. Ch: Audit Evidence — Inventory held by third parties (SA 501) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** As at 31st March, 2026, a material portion of the raw material of Brightline Chemicals Ltd. (₹9 crore) is stored at a third-party bonded warehouse, and the auditor is unable to attend a physical count at that location. Explain the auditor's responsibilities under SA 501 in respect of inventory that is under the custody and control of a third party, and the further procedures where doubt arises about the third party's integrity.

**Answer:**
SA 501, "Audit Evidence — Specific Considerations for Selected Items", addresses the existence and condition of inventory. Where inventory is material to the financial statements, the auditor must obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence regarding its existence and condition. Ordinarily this is done by attending the physical inventory count; however, when inventory under the custody and control of a **third party** is material, SA 501 prescribes specific procedures.

**Procedures for inventory held by a third party.** When inventory under the custody and control of a third party is material to the financial statements, the auditor must obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence regarding its existence and condition by performing **one or both** of the following:

1. **Requesting confirmation from the third party** as to the quantities and condition of the inventory held on behalf of the entity (a direct external confirmation regarding the ₹9 crore of raw material held at the bonded warehouse); and/or

2. **Performing inspection or other audit procedures appropriate in the circumstances** — for example, attending, or arranging for another auditor to attend, the third party's physical count of the inventory if practicable.

**Where doubt arises about the third party's integrity and objectivity.** When the auditor obtains information giving rise to doubt about the integrity and objectivity of the third party, the auditor considers performing additional procedures, which may include:

(a) **attending, or arranging for another auditor to attend, the third party's physical count** of the inventory;
(b) **obtaining another auditor's report, or a service auditor's report** (for example, under SA 402), on the adequacy of the third party's internal control for ensuring that the inventory is properly counted and safeguarded;
(c) **inspecting documentation** regarding inventory held by third parties — for example, warehouse receipts; or
(d) **requesting confirmation from other parties** when the inventory has been pledged as collateral.

**Application to Brightline Chemicals Ltd.:** Since the ₹9 crore raw material is material and the auditor cannot attend the count, the auditor should obtain direct confirmation from the bonded warehouse of the quantities and condition held, supplemented as necessary by inspection of warehouse receipts. If any information casts doubt on the warehouse operator's integrity or objectivity, the auditor should escalate to attending the count (personally or through another auditor) or obtaining a service auditor's report on the warehouse's controls.

**Conclusion:** For the third-party-held raw material, the auditor obtains evidence of existence and condition by confirmation from the warehouse and/or inspection procedures, and, where the third party's integrity is in doubt, performs the additional procedures specified in SA 501, such as attending the count or obtaining a service/other auditor's report.

*(Full-marks tip: Give the two primary SA 501 procedures (confirmation and/or inspection) and then the four additional procedures triggered by doubt about the third party's integrity. Students who stop at "obtain confirmation" without the integrity-doubt escalation, or who confuse this with attending the entity's own count, lose the second half of the marks.)*

### Q30. Ch: Audit Evidence — Reconciliation of a positive confirmation reply (SA 505) (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** In the audit of Meridian Traders Ltd., a positive external confirmation was sent to a customer, Falcon Stores, whose ledger balance in Meridian's books at 31st March, 2026 was ₹8,45,000. Falcon Stores confirmed a balance of ₹6,90,000. On investigation, the auditor gathered the following information. Prepare a statement reconciling the two balances, determine the amount (if any) requiring adjustment or further audit work, and state the assertion addressed.

| # | Information gathered |
|---|---|
| 1 | Goods invoiced by Meridian for ₹1,20,000 were despatched on 30-03-2026 but received by Falcon on 03-04-2026; Falcon records purchases on receipt |
| 2 | A cheque of ₹90,000 sent by Falcon on 28-03-2026 was received and recorded by Meridian on 02-04-2026 |
| 3 | A credit note of ₹40,000 for a sales return, agreed by Meridian, was recorded by Falcon but not yet posted in Meridian's ledger |
| 4 | Falcon had wrongly debited Meridian's account with ₹25,000 relating to another supplier |

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Nature of each reconciling item.**
- Item 1 (goods in transit ₹1,20,000): Timing difference. Meridian has correctly recorded the sale on despatch; Falcon will record on receipt (03-04-2026). No adjustment in Meridian's books.
- Item 2 (cash in transit ₹90,000): Timing difference. Falcon reduced its payable on 28-03; Meridian recorded receipt on 02-04. No adjustment to the 31-03 receivable in Meridian's books (correctly still outstanding at year-end), but supports existence and cut-off.
- Item 3 (credit note ₹40,000): Error in **Meridian's** books — the agreed sales-return credit note has not been posted. Meridian must **reduce** the receivable by ₹40,000.
- Item 4 (₹25,000 wrongly debited by Falcon): Error in **Falcon's** books; no adjustment in Meridian's books, but explains part of the difference.

**WN-2: Reconciliation Statement.**

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Balance as per Falcon Stores' confirmation (credit balance owed to Meridian) | 6,90,000 |
| Add: Goods in transit — invoiced by Meridian, not yet received by Falcon (Item 1) | 1,20,000 |
| Add: Sales-return credit note wrongly omitted by Falcon's counterpart — actually error in Meridian's books, so treat via Meridian side (see below) | — |
| Add: Amount wrongly debited by Falcon to another supplier's account (Item 4) | 25,000 |
| Less: Cheque sent by Falcon on 28-03, not yet recorded by Meridian (Item 2) | (90,000) |
| **Adjusted balance per Falcon (reconciled to Meridian, before Meridian's own error)** | **7,45,000** |

**WN-3: Correcting Meridian's own book error (Item 3).**
Meridian's ledger balance ₹8,45,000 is overstated by the unposted credit note of ₹40,000. Corrected balance in Meridian's books = ₹8,45,000 − ₹40,000 = **₹8,05,000**.

**Reconciliation of the two corrected balances:**

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Corrected balance per Meridian's books (₹8,45,000 − ₹40,000) | 8,05,000 |
| Less: Goods in transit not yet recorded by Falcon (Item 1) | (1,20,000) |
| Add: Cheque in transit not yet recorded by Meridian (Item 2) — reduces receivable per Meridian only after 31-03; already excluded from Falcon | — |
| Add back / adjust: Cash in transit ₹90,000 (recorded by Falcon, not by Meridian) | 90,000 |
| Less: Amount wrongly debited by Falcon (₹25,000) | (25,000) |
| **Balance per Falcon's confirmation** | **7,50,000** |

Reconciled residual difference: ₹7,50,000 vs confirmed ₹6,90,000 leaves ₹60,000 — being cash in transit ₹90,000 partly offset — indicating the ₹90,000 cheque is the balancing timing item; the confirmed ₹6,90,000 ties to ₹8,05,000 − ₹1,20,000 (goods in transit, added back for Falcon) is reconciled through the timing and error items above.

**Simplified reconciliation (OR — direct method):**

| Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Balance per Meridian (books) | 8,45,000 |
| Less: Credit note not posted by Meridian (Meridian's error — adjust books) | (40,000) |
| Less: Goods in transit — recorded by Meridian, not yet by Falcon (timing) | (1,20,000) |
| Less: Cheque in transit — recorded by Falcon, not yet by Meridian (timing) | (90,000) |
| Add: Amount wrongly debited by Falcon to another supplier (Falcon's error) | 25,000 |
| **Balance per Falcon Stores' confirmation** | **6,20,000** |

The unreconciled ₹70,000 (₹6,90,000 confirmed − ₹6,20,000 derived) is the net of the two timing/error items that fall on Falcon's side and requires the auditor to obtain supporting documentation (despatch proof for Item 1, bank credit for Item 2, and Falcon's correction of Item 4).

**Adjustment required in Meridian's books:** Only **Item 3 (₹40,000)** is a genuine error in Meridian's books requiring adjustment — reduce trade receivables and record the sales return. Items 1, 2 and 4 are timing differences / the counterparty's error, requiring the auditor to obtain corroborating documentation (goods despatch note and shipping proof, bank statement showing the ₹90,000 credit in April, and Falcon's rectification of the ₹25,000) rather than an adjustment.

**Assertion addressed:** The external confirmation primarily addresses the **existence** and **rights** assertions for trade receivables, and, through the cut-off items (goods and cash in transit), the **accuracy, valuation and allocation** and **cut-off** assertions.

**Answer:** Meridian must adjust its books by ₹40,000 (unposted credit note), reducing trade receivables from Falcon to **₹8,05,000**; the remaining differences (₹1,20,000 goods in transit, ₹90,000 cash in transit and ₹25,000 wrongly debited by Falcon) are timing differences / counterparty error to be corroborated by documentary evidence. The confirmation addresses the existence, rights and cut-off assertions.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards correctly classifying each item as (a) auditee's error needing adjustment, (b) timing difference, or (c) counterparty error, and stating the assertion tested. The single adjustment here is the ₹40,000 credit note; treating timing differences as adjustments to the auditee's balance is the classic error that loses marks.)*

### Q31. Ch: Audit Evidence — Opening balances where the prior period was modified (SA 510) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** CA Rohan has been appointed as auditor of Silverline Pharma Ltd. for the year ended 31st March, 2026 — his first year on the engagement. The predecessor auditor had issued a **qualified opinion** on the year ended 31st March, 2025 because of an inability to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence over a ₹3 crore inventory of expired stock whose write-down could not be verified. Explain CA Rohan's responsibilities under SA 510 regarding opening balances and the effect of the prior-period modification on his current-year report.

**Answer:**
SA 510, "Initial Audit Engagements — Opening Balances", governs CA Rohan's responsibilities, this being an initial audit engagement (Silverline Pharma Ltd. was audited by a predecessor in the prior period). **Opening balances** are the account balances that exist at the beginning of the period, based upon the closing balances of the prior period, and reflect the effects of transactions and events of prior periods and accounting policies applied in the prior period.

(a) determining whether the prior period's closing balances have been correctly brought forward to the current period, or, where appropriate, restated;
(b) determining whether the opening balances reflect the application of appropriate accounting policies; and
(c) performing one or more of the following:
   - where the prior-year financial statements were audited, reviewing the **predecessor auditor's working papers** to obtain evidence about the opening balances;
   - evaluating whether audit procedures performed in the **current period** provide evidence relevant to the opening balances; or
   - performing **specific audit procedures** to obtain evidence regarding the opening balances.

Therefore, CA Rohan must first determine whether the matter giving rise to the predecessor's qualification — the unverified ₹3 crore of expired inventory — remains unresolved and continues to be material to the current period.

**Conclusion:** Being an initial engagement, CA Rohan must obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence over the opening balances (by reviewing the predecessor's working papers, using current-period procedures, or performing specific procedures); and because the prior period carried a qualified opinion over the ₹3 crore expired inventory, he must modify his current-year report under SA 705/SA 710 to the extent that matter remains relevant and material to the 2026 financial statements.

### Q32. Ch: Audit Evidence — Facts discovered after the financial statements have been issued (SA 560) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Titan Motors Ltd.'s financial statements for the year ended 31st March, 2026 were approved and the audited financial statements were issued on 20th May, 2026. On 12th July, 2026, before the AGM, the auditor becomes aware that a major customer had been declared insolvent on 25th April, 2026, rendering a receivable of ₹4.2 crore (included in the year-end balance) irrecoverable — a condition that existed at the balance-sheet date. Explain the auditor's responsibilities under SA 560 regarding facts that become known after the financial statements have been issued.

**Answer:**
SA 560, "Subsequent Events", deals with the auditor's responsibilities relating to subsequent events. It distinguishes three periods. The situation of Titan Motors Ltd. falls in the third — **facts which become known to the auditor after the financial statements have been issued**. The date of the auditor's report was on or before 20th May, 2026; the financial statements were issued on 20th May, 2026; and the fact came to the auditor's knowledge on 12th July, 2026, i.e., after issuance.

**Nature of the event.** The customer's insolvency was declared on 25th April, 2026 but the auditor must consider whether the condition existed at the balance-sheet date. Insolvency of a debtor shortly after the year-end that confirms the debtor's inability to pay for balances outstanding at the year-end is an **adjusting event** — it provides evidence of a condition (the debtor's non-recoverability) existing at 31st March, 2026. Had it been known before the report date, the ₹4.2 crore should have been provided for. The financial statements as issued are therefore materially misstated.

**Auditor's responsibilities under SA 560 after the financial statements are issued.** When, after the financial statements have been issued, a fact becomes known to the auditor that, had it been known at the date of the auditor's report, may have caused the auditor to amend the report, the auditor must:

1. **Discuss the matter with management and, where appropriate, those charged with governance.**

2. **Determine whether the financial statements need amendment** and, if so, inquire how management intends to address the matter in the financial statements.

3. If management **amends the financial statements**, the auditor must:
   (a) carry out the audit procedures necessary in the circumstances on the amendment;
   (b) review the steps taken by management to ensure that anyone in receipt of the previously issued financial statements, together with the auditor's report thereon, is informed of the situation;
   (c) unless the circumstances permit dating the report as of a later date, extend the audit procedures on subsequent events to the date of the new auditor's report and date the new report no earlier than the date of approval of the amended financial statements; and
   (d) provide a **new or amended auditor's report** that includes an **Emphasis of Matter** paragraph or **Other Matter** paragraph referring to a note in the amended financial statements that explains the reason for the amendment and referring to the earlier report issued by the auditor.

4. If management **does not take the necessary steps** to ensure that anyone in receipt of the previously issued financial statements is informed of the situation and does not amend the financial statements where the auditor considers amendment is necessary, the auditor must **notify management and those charged with governance** that the auditor will seek to prevent future reliance on the auditor's report. If, despite such notification, management or TCWG do not take these necessary steps, the auditor must take **appropriate action to seek to prevent reliance** on the auditor's report, acting on the basis of the auditor's legal rights and obligations (and, where appropriate, after obtaining legal advice).

**Application to Titan Motors Ltd.:** Since the insolvency confirms a condition existing at 31st March, 2026 and the ₹4.2 crore is material, the auditor should discuss the matter with management and TCWG, and management should amend the financial statements to provide for the irrecoverable receivable before the AGM. The auditor should then perform procedures on the amendment, extend subsequent-events procedures, and issue a new report with an Emphasis of Matter / Other Matter paragraph referring to the reason for the amendment. If management refuses to amend, the auditor must take action to prevent reliance on the previously issued report.

**Conclusion:** The debtor's insolvency is an adjusting subsequent event discovered after issuance of the financial statements; under SA 560 the auditor must discuss with management/TCWG, and where the statements are amended, issue a new/amended report with an EOM or Other Matter paragraph, and where they are not, take steps to prevent reliance on the audit report.

*(Full-marks tip: Correctly place the event in the third SA 560 period (after issuance), identify it as an adjusting event, and give both branches — management amends (new report with EOM/Other Matter paragraph) and management refuses (steps to prevent reliance). ### Q33. Ch: Audit Evidence — Going concern: material uncertainty and reporting (SA 570) (Marks: 8) [Application]
**Question:** During the audit of Horizon Steel Ltd. for the year ended 31st March, 2026, the auditor identifies the following: accumulated losses have eroded 70% of net worth; a ₹150 crore term loan has been recalled by the lender for default; and net current liabilities exceed net current assets by ₹85 crore. Management has prepared cash-flow forecasts assuming (i) a fresh equity infusion of ₹120 crore from a promoter, for which only a non-binding letter of intent exists, and (ii) restructuring of the recalled loan, discussions for which are at a preliminary stage. Explain the auditor's responsibilities under SA 570 and the reporting outcomes depending on the adequacy of disclosure.

**Answer:**
SA 570, "Going Concern", establishes the auditor's responsibilities in the audit of financial statements relating to going concern and the implications for the auditor's report. Under the going concern basis of accounting, the financial statements are prepared on the assumption that the entity will continue in operation for the foreseeable future.

**Identification of events casting doubt.** SA 570 lists financial, operating and other events or conditions that may cast significant doubt on the entity's ability to continue as a going concern. In Horizon Steel Ltd., several **financial indicators** are present: substantial erosion of net worth (accumulated losses of 70% of net worth); the term loan of ₹150 crore recalled by the lender on default (an inability to comply with the terms of loan agreements / withdrawal of financial support); and net current liabilities exceeding net current assets by ₹85 crore (adverse key financial ratios and a net current liability position). These indicators, individually and cumulatively, cast **significant doubt** on the entity's ability to continue as a going concern.

**Auditor's responsibilities.** The auditor must:
1. **Obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence** regarding, and conclude on, the appropriateness of management's use of the going concern basis of accounting, and remain alert throughout the audit for evidence of events or conditions casting significant doubt.
2. **Evaluate management's assessment** of the entity's ability to continue as a going concern, covering the same period used by management (at least twelve months from the balance-sheet date under the framework).
3. Where events or conditions have been identified, **perform additional audit procedures**, including: analysing and discussing cash-flow, profit and other relevant forecasts with management; evaluating the reliability of the underlying data and the reasonableness of the assumptions; considering whether any additional facts have become available since management's assessment; and evaluating management's plans for future action and whether the outcome of these plans is likely to improve the situation and is feasible in the circumstances.
4. **Evaluate management's plans and their feasibility.** Here the auditor evaluates the two mitigating factors: the ₹120 crore promoter equity infusion is supported only by a **non-binding letter of intent** (not a firm commitment), and the loan restructuring is at a **preliminary** stage. Since neither mitigating factor is firmly committed or substantially assured, they may not be sufficient to remove the doubt.
5. **Request written representations** from management (and TCWG) regarding their plans for future action and the feasibility of those plans.

**Auditor's conclusion — a material uncertainty.** A **material uncertainty** exists when the magnitude of its potential impact and likelihood of occurrence is such that appropriate disclosure of the nature and implications of the uncertainty is necessary for fair presentation. Given the recalled loan, eroded net worth and net current liability position, and mitigating plans that are not firmly committed, the auditor would ordinarily conclude that a **material uncertainty related to going concern exists**, but that the use of the going concern basis remains appropriate (assuming the auditor does not conclude the entity will be unable to continue).

**Reporting outcomes (SA 570 with SA 705):**

| Situation | Reporting outcome |
|---|---|
| Going concern basis appropriate; material uncertainty exists; **disclosure in the financial statements is adequate** | Express an **unmodified opinion** and include a separate section headed **"Material Uncertainty Related to Going Concern"** drawing attention to the note and stating that these events/conditions indicate a material uncertainty and that the opinion is not modified in this respect |
| Going concern basis appropriate; material uncertainty exists; **disclosure is inadequate** | Express a **qualified opinion** or **adverse opinion** as appropriate under SA 705, and state in the Basis for Qualified/Adverse Opinion section that a material uncertainty exists that has not been adequately disclosed |
| **Use of going concern basis is inappropriate** (e.g., management intends to liquidate or has no realistic alternative) | Express an **adverse opinion** if the financial statements have nevertheless been prepared on a going concern basis |
| Management **unwilling to make or extend its assessment** when requested | Consider the implications for the report, which may include a **qualified opinion or disclaimer** owing to inability to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence |

**Conclusion:** Multiple financial indicators cast significant doubt on Horizon Steel Ltd.'s ability to continue as a going concern; with mitigating plans that are not firmly committed, a material uncertainty exists. If the financial statements adequately disclose that uncertainty, the auditor issues an unmodified opinion with a "Material Uncertainty Related to Going Concern" section; if disclosure is inadequate, a qualified or adverse opinion is warranted.

*(Full-marks tip: An 8-mark answer must (i) identify the going-concern indicators, (ii) list the additional procedures and the evaluation of management's plans (highlighting that the LOI and restructuring are not firm), (iii) define material uncertainty, and (iv) give the full reporting matrix (adequate disclosure → EOM-style GC section with unmodified opinion; inadequate → qualified/adverse; inappropriate basis → adverse). Missing the "Material Uncertainty Related to Going Concern" heading, or conflating it with an Emphasis of Matter paragraph, is a frequent error.)*

### Q34. Ch: Audit Evidence — Reliability of written representations (SA 580) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** "Although written representations provide necessary audit evidence, they do not provide sufficient appropriate audit evidence on their own." In light of SA 580, explain the reliability of written representations and the course of action available to the auditor where there is doubt as to their reliability or where management refuses to provide requested written representations.

**Answer:**
SA 580, "Written Representations", deals with the auditor's responsibility to obtain written representations from management and, where appropriate, those charged with governance. A **written representation** is a written statement by management provided to the auditor to confirm certain matters or to support other audit evidence. Written representations are a source of necessary audit evidence: if management does not provide, or the auditor is unable to obtain, requested written representations, the auditor may be unable to conclude, and this may affect the opinion.

**Reliability and limitations of written representations.** Although written representations provide necessary audit evidence, they do **not** provide sufficient appropriate audit evidence on their own about any of the matters with which they deal. Furthermore, the fact that management has provided reliable written representations does not affect the nature or extent of other audit evidence that the auditor obtains about the fulfilment of management's responsibilities or about specific assertions. Written representations are therefore complementary to, and not a substitute for, other audit evidence the auditor expects to be reasonably available.

**Doubt as to reliability (SA 580).** Written representations are inconsistent with other audit evidence in some circumstances, and the auditor investigates. If concerns about the competence, integrity, ethical values or diligence of management, or about its commitment to or enforcement of these, are such that the auditor concludes that the written representations are **not reliable**, the auditor:
(a) takes appropriate actions, including determining the possible effect on the reliability of representations (oral or written) and audit evidence in general; and
(b) if the auditor concludes that the written representations are not reliable, particularly where representations about management's responsibilities are inconsistent with other audit evidence, the auditor may be unable to rely on those representations. In such cases, and where the auditor concludes that there is sufficient doubt about the integrity of management such that written representations required (about responsibilities) are not reliable, the auditor is unable to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence and **disclaims an opinion** on the financial statements in accordance with SA 705.

**Requested written representations not provided (SA 580).** If management does not provide one or more of the requested written representations, the auditor must:
(a) **discuss the matter** with management;
(b) **re-evaluate the integrity of management** and evaluate the effect that this may have on the reliability of representations (oral or written) and audit evidence in general; and
(c) **take appropriate actions**, including determining the possible effect on the opinion.

Specifically, the auditor **disclaims an opinion** on the financial statements if (i) the auditor concludes that there is sufficient doubt about the integrity of management such that the written representations about management's responsibilities are not reliable, or (ii) management does not provide the written representations about its responsibilities (for the preparation of the financial statements, completeness of information, etc.).

**Conclusion:** Written representations are necessary but not, by themselves, sufficient appropriate audit evidence, and they cannot replace other evidence expected to be available. Where the auditor doubts their reliability or management refuses to provide the required representations about its responsibilities, the auditor re-evaluates management's integrity and, in the extreme, disclaims an opinion under SA 705.

*(Full-marks tip: State both limitations — not sufficient on their own, and no reduction in other evidence required — then give the two separate SA 580 scenarios (doubt as to reliability; representations not provided) with the disclaimer outcome for the responsibility-related representations. Answers that treat written representations as substantive proof of the matters represented, or omit the disclaimer consequence, are marked down.)*

### Q35. Ch: Audit Sampling & Analytical Procedures — Sampling Approaches and Suitability of Haphazard and Block Selection (SA 530) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** CA Nirmala, while planning the substantive testing of purchase vouchers of Vaayu Industries Ltd., is undecided between a statistical and a non-statistical sampling approach. Her articled assistant suggests that, to save time, they should simply pick the first 40 vouchers from each of three files ("block selection") and, for the balance sheet items, "just choose whichever items look convenient" (haphazard selection). Explain the meaning of statistical and non-statistical sampling and comment on the appropriateness of block selection and haphazard selection as sample selection methods under SA 530.

**Answer:** As per SA 530 "Audit Sampling", audit sampling is the application of audit procedures to less than 100% of items within a population of audit relevance such that all sampling units have a chance of selection, in order to provide the auditor a reasonable basis on which to draw conclusions about the entire population.

**Statistical sampling** is an approach that has two essential characteristics: (i) random selection of the sample items, and (ii) the use of probability theory to evaluate sample results, including measurement of sampling risk. Any sampling approach that does not possess both these characteristics is regarded as **non-statistical sampling**. The decision on which approach to use is a matter of the auditor's judgement; SA 530 clarifies that the approach chosen does not, by itself, affect the reliability of the audit evidence, provided the sample is representative and properly evaluated.

The methods of selecting the sample recognised by SA 530 include random selection, systematic selection, monetary-unit (value-weighted) selection, haphazard selection and block selection.

| Method | Nature | Auditor's use |
|---|---|---|
| Haphazard selection | Sample selected without following a structured technique, though the auditor avoids conscious bias or predictability | Not appropriate when using **statistical** sampling because it does not permit measurement of sampling risk; even in non-statistical sampling the auditor must ensure no bias (e.g., avoiding only easily located items) |
| Block selection | Selection of a contiguous block of items (e.g., all vouchers of a particular week) | Generally **not** an appropriate method because most populations are so structured that items in a sequence share similar characteristics but differ from the rest of the population; a block is unlikely to be representative |

Applying the above, the assistant's suggestion of picking the **first 40 vouchers of each file (block selection)** is inappropriate, since those vouchers relate to a specific period/sequence and are unlikely to be representative of the whole year's purchases; systematic or monetary-unit selection would be preferable. **Haphazard selection** for balance sheet items is not permissible if CA Nirmala intends a statistical approach, and even under a non-statistical approach she must guard against selecting only convenient or unusual items, which introduces bias.

**Conclusion:** CA Nirmala may choose either a statistical or a non-statistical approach as a matter of judgement, but block selection should be avoided and haphazard selection should not be used where sampling risk is to be measured statistically; a structured method such as random, systematic or monetary-unit selection is more appropriate.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner rewards naming all five/ six selection methods and stating specifically WHY block selection is normally inappropriate — because sequential items share characteristics — and WHY haphazard selection is incompatible with statistical sampling. A common deduction is confusing "haphazard" with "random"; state clearly that haphazard is not random.)*

### Q36. Ch: Audit Sampling & Analytical Procedures — Factors Influencing Sample Size for Tests of Details and Direction of Change (SA 530) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** For the audit of Meghana Textiles Ltd., the engagement partner has revised certain planning parameters. State, with brief reasons, the effect (increase / decrease / negligible) on the sample size for **tests of details** of each of the following changes, as contemplated in Appendix 3 to SA 530: (i) an increase in the auditor's assessment of the risk of material misstatement; (ii) an increase in the tolerable misstatement; (iii) an increase in the amount of misstatement the auditor expects to find; (iv) an increase in the use of other substantive procedures directed at the same assertion; (v) stratification of the population where appropriate.

**Answer:** SA 530 "Audit Sampling", in Appendix 3, sets out the factors that influence the sample size for tests of details. Sample size is determined by the level of sampling risk the auditor is willing to accept; the lower the risk the auditor is willing to accept, the larger the sample size needs to be. The direction of change for each factor is as follows:

| # | Change in factor | Effect on sample size | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| (i) | Increase in the auditor's assessment of the **risk of material misstatement** | **Increase** | The higher the RMM, the lower the acceptable detection risk, requiring more persuasive evidence, hence a larger sample |
| (ii) | Increase in the **tolerable misstatement** | **Decrease** | The larger the misstatement the auditor is willing to accept, the smaller the sample size needs to be |
| (iii) | Increase in the **misstatement the auditor expects to find** | **Increase** | The greater the expected misstatement, the larger the sample needs to be to conclude that actual misstatement is not more than tolerable misstatement |
| (iv) | Increase in the **use of other substantive procedures directed at the same assertion** | **Decrease** | The more assurance obtained from other procedures, the lower the assurance required from sampling, permitting a smaller sample |
| (v) | **Stratification** of the population where appropriate | **Decrease** | Effective stratification reduces the variability of items within each stratum, allowing a reduced overall sample size without a proportional increase in sampling risk |

**Conclusion:** The revisions have opposing effects — factors (i) and (iii) enlarge the sample, while (ii), (iv) and (v) reduce it; the net sample size must be re-computed considering all changes together.

Do not confuse the tests-of-details factors (Appendix 3) with the tests-of-controls factors (Appendix 2) — e.g., "expected rate of deviation" belongs to controls, "expected misstatement" to details. ### Q37. Ch: Audit Sampling & Analytical Procedures — Projecting Sample Results and Treatment of an Anomaly (SA 530) (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** CA Devendra is performing substantive tests of details over the "Purchases" of Konark Ceramics Ltd. He selects a sample from the population of recorded purchase invoices and examines each selected item. The relevant data is given below. He identifies that one of the misstatements arose because a supplier's invoice was erroneously posted twice due to a one-off failure of the accounting software patch installed only for a single day; the duplication has since been corrected and cannot recur. Compute the projected misstatement, decide how the anomaly is to be treated, and advise whether the population can be accepted.

| Particulars | Amount / Number |
|---|---|
| Book value of the population (recorded purchases) | ₹ 90,00,000 |
| Number of items (sampling units) in the population | 900 invoices |
| Book value of the sample examined | ₹ 12,00,000 |
| Number of items in the sample | 150 invoices |
| Total overstatement misstatement found in the sample | ₹ 24,000 |
| Of which — misstatement due to the one-day duplicate posting | ₹ 9,000 |
| Tolerable misstatement set for purchases | ₹ 1,80,000 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Identify the anomalous misstatement.**
Under SA 530, an **anomaly** is a misstatement or deviation that is demonstrably not representative of misstatements or deviations in a population. The duplicate posting of ₹ 9,000 arose from a one-day software failure that has been corrected and cannot recur; CA Devendra therefore has a high degree of certainty that it is not representative of the population. It qualifies as an anomaly and must be **excluded** when projecting misstatements to the population (SA 530, para 14 and A18).

Non-anomalous misstatement in the sample = ₹ 24,000 − ₹ 9,000 = **₹ 15,000**.

**WN-2: Project the non-anomalous misstatement to the population (ratio method).**
Projected misstatement = (Non-anomalous misstatement in sample ÷ Book value of sample) × Book value of population
= (₹ 15,000 ÷ ₹ 12,00,000) × ₹ 90,00,000
= 0.0125 × ₹ 90,00,000 = **₹ 1,12,500**.

**WN-3: Add back the anomalous misstatement.**
Per SA 530 para 14, although the anomaly is excluded from projection, its actual effect must still be considered. The anomalous misstatement is added to the projected figure:
Total estimated misstatement = ₹ 1,12,500 (projected) + ₹ 9,000 (anomaly) = **₹ 1,21,500**.

**Statement Showing Evaluation of Sample Results — Purchases of Konark Ceramics Ltd.**

| Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Projected misstatement (non-anomalous, ratio method) | 1,12,500 |
| Add: Anomalous misstatement (actual) | 9,000 |
| **Total estimated misstatement in the population** | **1,21,500** |
| Tolerable misstatement | 1,80,000 |
| Estimated misstatement as % of tolerable misstatement | 67.5% |

**Evaluation:** The total estimated misstatement of ₹ 1,21,500 is **below** the tolerable misstatement of ₹ 1,80,000. However, per SA 530 para 13, the auditor must evaluate whether the projected misstatement, together with an allowance for **sampling risk**, exceeds tolerable misstatement. As the estimated misstatement (₹ 1,21,500) is close to (67.5% of) the tolerable misstatement, the possibility that actual misstatement exceeds tolerable misstatement is not remote. CA Devendra should therefore consider requesting management to investigate the identified misstatements and the potential for further misstatement, and/ or extending the sample or performing alternative procedures before concluding.

**OR (Cross-check — is the sample representative?):** If, after considering sampling risk, the auditor concludes that the sample does not provide a reasonable basis (estimated misstatement plus sampling-risk allowance exceeds ₹ 1,80,000), he must perform additional audit procedures; if it does provide a reasonable basis, he may accept the population subject to correction of the identified errors.

**Answer:** Projected misstatement = **₹ 1,12,500**; the ₹ 9,000 duplicate posting is an **anomaly** to be excluded from projection but added back separately, giving a total estimated misstatement of **₹ 1,21,500** against a tolerable misstatement of **₹ 1,80,000**. As the estimate is close to the tolerable limit, CA Devendra should perform further procedures/ seek correction before accepting the population.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner specifically checks that the anomaly is EXCLUDED from projection but its actual amount is ADDED BACK — students who project the full ₹ 24,000 lose marks. Also state the "projected + sampling-risk allowance vs. tolerable" test; simply comparing ₹ 1,21,500 < ₹ 1,80,000 and stopping is treated as an incomplete conclusion.)*

### Q41. Ch: Audit Sampling & Analytical Procedures — Analytical Procedures as Risk Assessment vs. Final Overall Review (SA 315 / SA 520) (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between the use of analytical procedures at the **risk assessment stage** and their use at the **overall review (final) stage** of the audit, and state whether analytical procedures are mandatory at each of these stages.

**Answer:** Analytical procedures — evaluations of financial information through analysis of plausible relationships among both financial and non-financial data — are employed by the auditor at different stages of the audit for different objectives, as contemplated in SA 315 and SA 520.

| Basis | Risk assessment stage | Overall review (final) stage |
|---|---|---|
| Governing SA | SA 315, "Identifying and Assessing the Risks of Material Misstatement" (as risk assessment procedures) | SA 520, "Analytical Procedures" (para 6) |
| Objective | To obtain an understanding of the entity and its environment and to identify areas of potential risk of material misstatement | To assist the auditor in forming an overall conclusion as to whether the financial statements are consistent with the auditor's understanding of the entity |
| Nature of data | Often uses data aggregated at a high level; results give a broad initial indication | Reviews the audited figures for previously unrecognised risks and unusual/ unexpected relationships |
| Mandatory? | **Yes** — analytical procedures are required as a risk assessment procedure under SA 315 | **Yes** — SA 520 para 6 requires the auditor to design and perform analytical procedures near the end of the audit |
| Consequence of unusual result | Identifies risks to be responded to in the audit plan | May indicate a previously unrecognised risk, requiring the auditor to revise the assessed risks and modify further procedures |

By contrast, the use of analytical procedures as **substantive procedures** (SA 520 para 5) during the response phase is **not** mandatory — it is one option, alongside tests of details, that the auditor may choose.

**Conclusion:** Analytical procedures are **mandatory** both at the risk assessment stage (SA 315) and at the overall review stage (SA 520 para 6), but their use as substantive procedures is optional; the objectives at each stage differ as tabulated above.

### Q45. Ch: Audit of Items of Financial Statements — Audit of a Decommissioning / Site Restoration Provision (AS 29) (Marks: 5) [Application]

### Q50. Ch: Audit Sampling & Analytical Procedures — Substantive Analytical Procedure over Subscription Revenue (SA 520) (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** CA Rukmini is auditing the subscription revenue of Nabhas Digital Services Ltd., an OTT streaming company, for the year ended 31 March 2026. She decides to corroborate the recorded revenue using a substantive analytical procedure built from non-financial operating data. Using the data below, develop the expected revenue, quantify the difference from the recorded amount, and explain how she should follow it up and whether a substantive analytical procedure is a suitable approach here.

| Particulars | Data |
|---|---|
| Average number of active paying subscribers during the year | 50,000 |
| Average monthly subscription (ARPU) per subscriber | ₹ 250 |
| Recorded subscription revenue for the year | ₹ 15,90,00,000 |
| Threshold (acceptable difference) for investigation | ₹ 50,00,000 |
| Note | One-time activation fees of ₹ 55,00,000 are separately included within the recorded revenue above |

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Develop the independent expectation of subscription revenue.**
Expected subscription revenue = Average subscribers × Monthly ARPU × 12 months
= 50,000 × ₹ 250 × 12
= 50,000 × ₹ 3,000 = **₹ 15,00,00,000**.

**WN-2: Compute the raw difference from recorded revenue.**

**Statement Showing Substantive Analytical Review of Subscription Revenue — Nabhas Digital Services Ltd.**

| Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Expected subscription revenue (WN-1) | 15,00,00,000 |
| Recorded revenue (as booked) | 15,90,00,000 |
| **Raw difference (recorded − expected)** | **90,00,000** |
| Threshold for investigation | 50,00,000 |

The raw difference of ₹ 90,00,000 exceeds the ₹ 50,00,000 threshold and therefore requires investigation.

**WN-3: Refine the expectation for the reconciling item.**
The recorded revenue includes ₹ 55,00,000 of **one-time activation fees**, which are not part of the recurring subscription stream captured by the subscriber-×-ARPU model. Adjusting for this:
Recorded recurring subscription revenue = ₹ 15,90,00,000 − ₹ 55,00,000 = **₹ 15,35,00,000**.
Refined difference = ₹ 15,35,00,000 − ₹ 15,00,00,000 = **₹ 35,00,000**.

**Evaluation under SA 520:**
- After adjusting the expectation for the identified reconciling item (activation fees), the residual difference of **₹ 35,00,000 is below the threshold of ₹ 50,00,000** and could be regarded as acceptable — but only after CA Rukmini **corroborates** the ₹ 55,00,000 activation-fee explanation with audit evidence (e.g., the revenue break-up report, sample activation invoices).
- She must also test the **reliability of the operating data** (subscriber count and ARPU) used to build the expectation, per SA 520 para 5(b) — confirming that the average subscriber figure is drawn from a system subjected to effective controls, and that ARPU reflects actual approved price plans.
- She should consider whether the expectation is **sufficiently precise**: disaggregating by month or by subscription plan (e.g., basic vs. premium) would sharpen it and reduce the risk that offsetting misstatements are concealed within the annual total.

**Suitability of the approach:** Subscription revenue is a large volume of homogeneous, predictable transactions driven by a stable relationship (subscribers × price), making it **well suited** to a substantive analytical procedure. | Particulars | Amount |

| Borrowings outstanding at the end of the year | ₹ 24,00,00,000 |

### Q52. Ch: Audit in an Automated Environment — The Four Domains of IT General Controls (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** While planning the audit of Nexus Retail Ltd., which runs its entire business on an integrated ERP, your senior instructs you to obtain an understanding of the entity's IT General Controls (ITGCs) "domain by domain" before you place any reliance on the automated application controls. **Answer:**
IT General Controls (ITGCs) are the policies and procedures that relate to many applications and support the effective functioning of application controls by helping to ensure the continued proper operation of information systems; the auditor considers them as part of obtaining an understanding of the entity's system of internal control under **SA 315 "Identifying and Assessing the Risks of Material Misstatement"**.

If a programmer can change the application's logic without authorisation, or if an unauthorised user can gain access and process transactions, then even a perfectly designed application control cannot be relied upon. Hence, before testing and relying on automated application controls, the auditor must be satisfied that the relevant ITGCs are operating effectively; where ITGCs are weak, the auditor cannot adopt a "test once" approach and must fall back on substantive procedures.

ITGCs are conventionally classified into the following principal domains:

| Domain | What it governs | Illustrative control |
|---|---|---|
| **Access security** | Who can log in and what they can do; prevention of unauthorised access | Unique user IDs, strong passwords, role-based access, timely removal of leavers, review of privileged/superuser access |
| **Program change management** | Changes to existing applications | Changes are requested, authorised, tested and approved before being moved to the production (live) environment |
| **Program development / acquisition** | New systems and major implementations | System development life-cycle controls, user acceptance testing, sign-off before go-live |
| **Computer operations** | Day-to-day running of the environment | Scheduling and monitoring of batch jobs, backup and recovery, incident management, physical and environmental security of the data centre |

**Conclusion:** ITGCs are pervasive controls over access, changes, development and operations; because the reliability of every automated application control depends on them, the auditor must understand and, where reliance is intended, test each relevant ITGC domain before relying on the application controls it supports.

### Q58. Ch: Audit in an Automated Environment — Interface Controls & the Systematic-Error Multiplier (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Cascade Ltd. runs two separate systems — a front-end sales/point-of-sale system and a back-end financial ERP — connected by an automated interface that transfers each day's sales into the general ledger. You discover that the interface's tax-mapping logic was configured incorrectly on 1 April and remained unchanged all year, applying GST at 12% to a product category that should attract 18%. Explain the nature of this weakness, why a single interface/configuration error behaves differently from a manual error, and the auditor's approach.

**Answer:**
An **interface** is an automated routine that passes data from one application to another; **interface controls** (completeness/accuracy checks, control-total reconciliations between the two systems) ensure that what leaves the source system arrives complete and accurate in the destination. The tax-mapping logic here is a **configurable application control** whose parameter (the GST rate for a category) was set up wrongly and never changed.

**Why an automated/configuration error behaves differently — the "systematic error multiplier":** a manual error is generally *random and isolated* — a human may misclassify one transaction today and get the next one right. An automated control, by contrast, applies the *same logic identically to every single transaction it processes*. Therefore a configuration error is **systematic and pervasive**: from 1 April onward, *every* sale in that category was taxed at the wrong rate, uniformly, for the whole year. The consequence is two-sided:

- **Adverse side:** the misstatement is potentially large because it is repeated across the entire population without exception.
- **Favourable side for audit strategy:** because the control operates consistently, once the auditor establishes *how* it behaves, the error is highly predictable and precisely quantifiable — the auditor can recompute the correct tax over the full population using a CAAT and measure the exact misstatement.

**Auditor's approach:**
- Confirm the configuration (rate table effective 1 April) and that no change was made (change-management evidence).
- Use a CAAT to extract all affected sales and recompute GST at 18% vs the 12% charged; the difference is the misstatement in the GST liability and, correspondingly, in revenue/receivables.
- Evaluate whether the error is material; report the interface/configuration control deficiency and the missing periodic review of tax-rate configuration to those charged with governance.

**Conclusion:** A single mis-configured interface control produces a *systematic, population-wide* misstatement rather than a scattered one; the auditor exploits this consistency by recomputing the correct amounts over the whole population and quantifying the error precisely, while reporting the underlying configuration/change-control weakness.

### Q61. Ch: Audit Documentation & Quality — Confidentiality, Custody, Integrity, Accessibility & Retrievability (Marks: 5) [Theory]

These characteristics are:

| Characteristic | What the controls must ensure |
|---|---|
| **Confidentiality** | Documentation is protected from unauthorised access; client information is not disclosed, consistent with the member's duty of confidentiality |
| **Safe custody** | The documentation is physically/electronically protected from loss, theft or damage (secure storage, restricted premises/server access) |

### Q66. Ch: Audit in an Automated Environment — How IT Assists the Auditor & Categories of CAATs (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between the auditor *auditing* an automated environment and the auditor *using* technology to perform the audit. Explain the ways in which IT/data analytics assists the auditor, and identify the broad categories of Computer-Assisted Audit Techniques (CAATs).

**Answer:**
It is useful to separate two ideas. *Auditing in an automated environment* means the entity's records and controls are IT-based, so the auditor must understand and, where relevant, test the IT controls. *Using technology in the audit* means the auditor deploys software tools to perform audit procedures more effectively — the two are complementary.

**Ways in which IT / data analytics assists the auditor:**
- **Analysis of complete populations** rather than samples, eliminating sampling risk over the tested attribute.
- **Speed and efficiency** in processing large volumes of data.
- **Re-performance / recalculation** of computations (depreciation, interest, tax) across the whole file.
- **Identification of exceptions and anomalies** — duplicates, gaps in sequences, out-of-range values, unusual journals posted at odd times or by unusual users.
- **Correlation and trend analysis** to focus attention on higher-risk items and improve the quality of audit evidence.

**Broad categories of CAATs:**

| Category | Nature |
|---|---|
| **Test data** | The auditor processes its own data (including deliberately invalid data) through the client's application to see whether the programmed controls operate as intended |
| **Audit software / generalised audit software (data analytics)** | Software used to read, extract, re-perform and analyse the entity's *actual* data (e.g., duplicate detection, recalculation, sampling, ageing) |
| **Embedded audit facilities (continuous auditing)** | Modules built into the client's system to capture/flag transactions of audit interest as they are processed |

**Conclusion:** Auditing in an automated environment concerns understanding and testing the entity's IT controls, whereas CAATs are the auditor's own technology tools; together, data analytics lets the auditor test whole populations, recompute and spot anomalies, using test data, audit software and embedded facilities.

### Q67. Ch: Audit in an Automated Environment — Evaluating an ITGC Deficiency & Its Effect on Controls Reliance (Cross-topic) (Marks: 8) [Application]
**Question:** For Pinnacle Ltd., you had planned a controls-reliance strategy over the automated three-way-match control in purchasing (a "test once" approach). While testing IT General Controls you find that: (a) during the year the entity implemented a program change to the purchasing module *without* documented testing or approval before it went live; and (b) a group of users retained access to *migrate program changes into production* even though they also *develop* those changes. Explain, step by step, how these findings affect your planned reliance on the automated three-way-match control, what you must document, and your revised audit response.

**Answer:**
This question integrates the automated-environment reliance model with the documentation requirements of SA 230.

**Step 1 — Identify the domain and the deficiencies.** Both findings sit in the **program change management** domain of IT General Controls (with a segregation-of-duties overlay):
- **(a)** A production change without documented testing/approval breaks the *change-authorisation and testing* control — a change could have introduced an error into the application logic.
- **(b)** The same users can *develop* and *migrate to production* — a failure of segregation of duties in change management; a developer could move unauthorised or untested code into the live system.

**Step 2 — Link ITGCs to the application control.** The auditor's ability to adopt a "test once and rely for the year" approach to an automated control rests on the assumption that the control's programmed logic **did not change** during the period, which in turn depends on effective change-management ITGCs. Here, that assumption fails: because changes could be made without testing/approval and by users who bypass segregation of duties, the auditor **cannot assume** the three-way-match logic remained unchanged and effective throughout the year.

**Step 3 — Effect on the planned reliance strategy.** The planned "test once" controls-reliance strategy over the automated three-way match is **no longer appropriate**. Under **SA 330 "The Auditor's Responses to Assessed Risks"**, when the general controls that support an automated control are deficient, the auditor cannot rely on operating effectiveness across the period.

**Step 4 — Revised audit response.**
- Determine **whether the purchasing module's three-way-match logic was in fact changed** during the year; if a change occurred, test the control **separately for each period** (before and after the change) and evaluate whether the changed logic is correct.
- **Increase substantive procedures** over purchases and payables (e.g., re-perform matching on a sample, or use a CAAT to test the whole population for unmatched/overpaid items), rather than relying on the automated control.
- Evaluate whether the deficiencies constitute a **significant deficiency in internal control** to be communicated to those charged with governance under **SA 265**.

**Step 5 — What must be documented (SA 230).** Record: the ITGC deficiencies identified and how they were identified; the evaluation that reliance on the automated control is not appropriate and the reasons; the revised nature, timing and extent of procedures; the results of the substantive/CAAT work; the significant judgement that the "test once" assumption failed; and the communication of the deficiency to TCWG — all to the "experienced auditor" standard.

**Conclusion:** Because change-management ITGCs are deficient, the assumption underpinning "test once" reliance on the automated three-way match collapses; the auditor must test the control per period of change if any occurred, shift to substantive/CAAT procedures, consider an SA 265 communication, and fully document the changed judgement and response under SA 230.

*(Full-marks tip: Award-winning answers explicitly connect "test once" reliance to the *change-management assumption* and then walk the reader from deficiency → collapse of assumption → SA 330 revised response → SA 265 communication → SA 230 documentation. Answers that merely say "do more substantive testing" without the reliance-logic chain lose the higher marks.)*

### Q69. Ch: Company Audit — Disqualifications of an Auditor u/s 141(3) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Examine, with reference to the relevant provisions of the Companies Act, 2013, whether the following persons are qualified to be appointed / continue as the statutory auditor of **Meridian Textiles Ltd.**:
(a) CA. Rohan, who holds 300 equity shares of face value ₹10 each in **Sunbeam Yarns Ltd.**, the holding company of Meridian Textiles Ltd.
(b) CA. Kavya, whose brother has taken a loan of ₹8 lakh from Meridian Textiles Ltd. against the security of his personal residential flat.
(c) A partnership firm **M/s Rao & Co.**, one of whose partners is also acting as the whole-time Company Secretary of Meridian Textiles Ltd.
(d) CA. Imran, who was convicted by a court of an offence involving fraud four years ago.

**Answer:**
The eligibility, qualifications and disqualifications of an auditor are governed by **Section 141 of the Companies Act, 2013**, read with **Rule 10 of the Companies (Audit and Auditors) Rules, 2014**. The four situations are examined against the specific sub-clauses of Section 141(3):

**(a) Holding of securities [Section 141(3)(d)(i)]:** A person is disqualified if he, his relative or partner **holds any security of or interest in** the company, its holding, subsidiary, associate company or a subsidiary of such holding company. A relative, however, may hold securities of face value **not exceeding ₹1,00,000**. Here the disqualification test applies to the person himself with **no monetary threshold** — the ₹1 lakh relaxation is available only to a *relative*. CA. Rohan personally holds 300 shares of ₹10 each (₹3,000) in **Sunbeam Yarns Ltd.**, the holding company. Since he himself holds a security in the holding company, he is **disqualified**, irrespective of the small value.

**(b) Indebtedness / guarantee or security [Section 141(3)(d)(iii)]:** A person is disqualified if he, his relative or partner **has given a guarantee or provided any security** in connection with the indebtedness of any **third person** to the company (its holding, subsidiary or associate) in excess of **₹1,00,000**. Here it is CA. Kavya's **brother** who has himself taken the loan of ₹8 lakh on the security of his *own* flat; neither Kavya, nor her relative *as a guarantor for a third person*, nor her partner has given a guarantee/security for someone else's debt to the company. The clause is therefore **not attracted**, and CA. Kavya is **not disqualified**.

**(c) Person in employment / holding office [Section 141(3)(c) and 141(3)(f)]:** A person who is in the **employment of the company** is disqualified, and so is a firm any of whose partners is disqualified. A partner of M/s Rao & Co. is the **whole-time Company Secretary** (an officer in employment) of Meridian Textiles Ltd. That partner is therefore disqualified, and by virtue of the firm-level disqualification the whole firm **M/s Rao & Co. is disqualified**.

**(d) Conviction for fraud [Section 141(3)(h)]:** A person who has been **convicted by a court of an offence involving fraud** is disqualified for a period of **ten years** from the date of such conviction. CA. Imran was convicted only **four years** ago; the ten-year period has not elapsed, so he continues to be **disqualified**.

**Conclusion:** (a) Rohan — disqualified; (b) Kavya — **not** disqualified; (c) M/s Rao & Co. — disqualified; (d) Imran — disqualified.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards citing the exact sub-clause of 141(3). The classic trap is (a) vs relative-threshold — the ₹1 lakh relaxation is ONLY for a relative, never for the auditor himself; and (b) — the guarantee clause bites only when the guarantee/security is given for a THIRD person's debt to the company, not when a relative borrows on his own security.)*

### Q71. Ch: Company Audit — Casual Vacancy u/s 139(8) (Marks: 4) [Application]

### Q72. Ch: Company Audit — Removal of Auditor before Expiry of Term u/s 140(1) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** The Board of Directors of **Galaxy Cements Ltd.** is dissatisfied with its statutory auditor and wishes to **remove him before the expiry of his term**. The Managing Director asks you, as a professional, to explain the exact procedure and safeguards under the Companies Act, 2013 for such removal, and the consequence if the procedure is not followed. Also state the auditor's right to make a **representation** when the company proposes not to re-appoint him.

**Answer:**
**Removal before expiry of term is governed by Section 140(1)** read with **Rule 7 of the Companies (Audit and Auditors) Rules, 2014**. An auditor appointed under Section 139 may be removed before the expiry of his term **only** by following the prescribed procedure, which contains three cumulative safeguards:

1. A **special resolution** of the company must be passed for the removal.
2. **Previous approval of the Central Government** (power delegated to the Regional Director) must be obtained. The application to the Central Government is to be made **in Form ADT-2 within 30 days** of the resolution of the **Board**.
3. The company must hold the **general meeting within 60 days** of receipt of the Central Government's approval, to pass the special resolution.
4. Before taking any action for removal, the auditor concerned must be given a **reasonable opportunity of being heard**.

The correct **sequence** is: Board resolution → application to Central Government in Form ADT-2 (within 30 days of Board resolution) → on receipt of CG approval, general meeting within 60 days to pass the special resolution, after giving the auditor an opportunity of being heard.

**Consequence of non-compliance:** If the removal is effected without the special resolution, or without the prior approval of the Central Government, the removal is **invalid/void** and the auditor continues in office.

**Right of representation on proposed non-re-appointment [Section 140(4)]:** Where it is proposed to appoint **someone other than the retiring auditor** (or expressly to provide that the retiring auditor shall not be re-appointed), a **special notice** is required. On receipt of such notice, the company must **forthwith send a copy to the retiring auditor**. The retiring auditor is entitled to make a **written representation** (of reasonable length) to the company and to request that it be circulated to the members; the company must (unless received too late) state the fact of the representation in the notice and **send a copy to every member**. If it is not sent, the auditor may require it to be **read out at the meeting**, and in every case the auditor has a **right to be heard orally** at the meeting. This right may be curtailed only if the Tribunal, on the company's application, is satisfied that the right is being abused to secure **needless publicity for defamatory matter**.

**Conclusion:** Removal before term needs a special resolution + prior Central Government approval (Form ADT-2 within 30 days) + opportunity of being heard; non-compliance renders the removal void. On proposed non-re-appointment, the retiring auditor enjoys the statutory right of representation and to be heard under Section 140(4).

*(Full-marks tip: Distinguish clearly between removal BEFORE term (Sec 140(1) — special resolution + CG approval) and non-RE-appointment at AGM (Sec 140(4) — special notice + representation). ### Q73. Ch: Company Audit — Fixing of Auditor's Remuneration u/s 142 (Marks: 4) [Application]

In FY 2025-26, apart from the audit fee of ₹6,00,000 fixed in the general meeting, **Horizon Polymers Ltd.** reimbursed its auditor ₹45,000 of out-of-pocket travelling expenses incurred for the audit and separately paid ₹2,50,000 for a taxation-representation assignment (a permitted service). Which of these amounts form part of the "remuneration" required to be disclosed, and how is the first auditor's remuneration fixed?

**Answer:**
The remuneration of the auditor is governed by **Section 142** of the Companies Act, 2013.

**How remuneration is fixed [Section 142(1)]:** The remuneration of the auditor of a company shall be **fixed in its general meeting** or in such manner as may be determined therein. However, the Board may fix the remuneration of the **first auditor**.

**What remuneration includes [Explanation to Section 142]:** The "remuneration" of the auditor is **in addition to the fee payable** and shall **include**:
(a) the **expenses, if any, incurred by the auditor in connection with the audit** of the company; and
(b) any **facility** extended to him;
**but does NOT include** any remuneration paid to him for **any other service rendered** by him at the request of the company.

**Application to Horizon Polymers Ltd.:**

| Payment | Amount (₹) | Part of "remuneration"? | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Audit fee fixed in general meeting | 6,00,000 | Yes | Core audit fee |
| Reimbursement of travelling expenses for audit | 45,000 | **Yes** | Expenses in connection with the audit — expressly included by the Explanation |
| Fee for taxation-representation (other service) | 2,50,000 | **No** | Remuneration for "other service rendered" — expressly excluded |
| **Total remuneration as auditor** | **6,45,000** | — | 6,00,000 + 45,000 |

**Fixing for the first auditor:** The remuneration of the first auditor may be **fixed by the Board of Directors**, whereas for subsequent auditors it is fixed by the members in general meeting.

**Conclusion:** The auditor's remuneration to be disclosed is **₹6,45,000** (audit fee ₹6,00,000 + audit-related expenses ₹45,000); the ₹2,50,000 for the separate taxation service is not part of the auditor's remuneration. The first auditor's remuneration is fixed by the Board.

### Q74. Ch: Company Audit — Appointment of First Auditor u/s 139(6) & 139(7) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** With reference to the appointment of the **first auditor**, advise in the following independent situations:
(a) **Pioneer Devices Ltd.**, a newly incorporated company (not a Government company), whose Board failed to appoint the first auditor within 30 days of incorporation.
(b) **Bharat Rail Components Ltd.**, a Government company, in which the C&AG did not appoint the first auditor within 60 days of registration.
State the time limits and the persons responsible in each case.

**Answer:**
The appointment of the **first auditor** is governed by **Section 139(6)** (companies other than Government companies) and **Section 139(7)** (Government companies).

**(a) Pioneer Devices Ltd. — Section 139(6):** The first auditor of a company, **other than a Government company**, shall be appointed by the **Board of Directors within 30 days** of the **date of registration** of the company. If the Board **fails** to appoint within 30 days, it shall **inform the members**, who shall appoint the first auditor within **90 days at an extraordinary general meeting (EGM)**. The first auditor so appointed holds office **till the conclusion of the first annual general meeting**. Since Pioneer's Board failed within 30 days, the members must now appoint the first auditor within 90 days at an EGM.

**(b) Bharat Rail Components Ltd. — Section 139(7):** In the case of a **Government company** (or a company owned/controlled, directly or indirectly, by the Central/State Government), the first auditor shall be appointed by the **Comptroller and Auditor-General of India (C&AG) within 60 days** of the date of registration. If the **C&AG does not appoint** within 60 days, the **Board of Directors** shall appoint the first auditor within the **next 30 days**; and on failure of the Board, it shall inform the members, who shall appoint within **60 days at an EGM**. In every case the first auditor holds office **till the conclusion of the first annual general meeting**. Here, as the C&AG failed within 60 days, the Board must appoint within the next 30 days.

The two routes are contrasted below:

| Basis | Non-Government company [S.139(6)] | Government company [S.139(7)] |
|---|---|---|
| Primary appointing authority | Board of Directors | C&AG |
| Primary time limit | 30 days of registration | 60 days of registration |
| On default | Members at EGM within 90 days | Board within next 30 days; then members at EGM within 60 days |
| Tenure | Till conclusion of first AGM | Till conclusion of first AGM |

**Conclusion:** (a) The members of Pioneer Devices Ltd. must appoint the first auditor within 90 days at an EGM. (b) On the C&AG's default, the Board of Bharat Rail Components Ltd. must appoint the first auditor within the next 30 days.

*(Full-marks tip: Keep the two cascades separate — non-Govt: Board 30 days → members 90 days; Govt: C&AG 60 days → Board 30 days → members 60 days. State that the first auditor holds office only "till the conclusion of the first AGM" — omitting the tenure line costs a mark.)*

| Case | Facts as on / during FY 2025-26 |

### Q76. Ch: Company Audit — Re-appointment of Retiring Auditor u/s 139(9) & 139(10) (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the conditions under which a **retiring auditor** may be re-appointed at an annual general meeting, and what happens where at any annual general meeting **no auditor is appointed or re-appointed**, under the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:**
The re-appointment of a retiring auditor is governed by **Section 139(9)** and the consequence of a failure to appoint is governed by **Section 139(10)** of the Companies Act, 2013.

**Re-appointment of retiring auditor [Section 139(9)]:** Subject to the provisions of Section 139(1) and the rules, a **retiring auditor may be re-appointed** at an annual general meeting **if**:
(a) he is **not disqualified** for re-appointment;
(b) he has **not given the company a notice in writing of his unwillingness** to be re-appointed; and
(c) a **special resolution has not been passed** at that meeting appointing some other auditor or providing expressly that he shall not be re-appointed.

**Where no auditor is appointed or re-appointed [Section 139(10)]:** Where at any annual general meeting **no auditor is appointed or re-appointed**, the **existing auditor shall continue to be the auditor** of the company. This ensures continuity of the audit function so that the company is never left without an auditor.

These provisions must also be read with the **default term regime of Section 139(1)** — an auditor is appointed to hold office from the conclusion of one AGM till the conclusion of the sixth AGM (subject to ratification/rotation rules), so re-appointment normally arises at the end of such a term.

**Conclusion:** A retiring auditor is re-appointed if he is not disqualified, has not expressed unwillingness, and no special resolution appointing another/barring him has been passed; and if a meeting fails to appoint anyone, the existing auditor simply continues under Section 139(10).

*(Full-marks tip: List all three cumulative conditions of 139(9) as a set, and separately state the 139(10) "existing auditor continues" rule. A frequent error is stating only two conditions or confusing 139(10) with the casual-vacancy provision — they are distinct.)*

### Q77. Ch: Audit Report — Types of Modified Opinions (SA 705) (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Under SA 705 (Revised), "Modifications to the Opinion in the Independent Auditor's Report", explain the **three types of modified opinion** and the two circumstances that give rise to a modification. Present, in a table, how the auditor decides which type of modified opinion to express.

**Answer:**
**SA 705 (Revised)** deals with the auditor's responsibility to issue an appropriate report when he concludes that a modification to the opinion is necessary.

**Circumstances requiring a modification:** The auditor modifies the opinion when:
1. he concludes that, based on the audit evidence obtained, the **financial statements as a whole are not free from material misstatement**; or
2. he is **unable to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence** to conclude that the financial statements as a whole are free from material misstatement (a *limitation of scope*).

**The three types of modified opinion:**
- **Qualified opinion:** Expressed when (a) the auditor, having obtained sufficient appropriate audit evidence, concludes that misstatements are **material but NOT pervasive**; or (b) the auditor is **unable to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence**, but concludes that the possible effects of undetected misstatements, if any, could be **material but NOT pervasive**. The opinion states the financial statements give a true and fair view **"except for"** the effects of the matter.
- **Adverse opinion:** Expressed when the auditor, having obtained sufficient appropriate audit evidence, concludes that misstatements are **both material AND pervasive** to the financial statements. The auditor states that the financial statements **do NOT give a true and fair view**.
- **Disclaimer of opinion:** Expressed when the auditor is **unable to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence** and concludes that the possible effects of undetected misstatements could be **both material AND pervasive**; or, in rare cases involving multiple uncertainties, he concludes it is not possible to form an opinion. The auditor **does not express an opinion**.

The choice depends on (i) the **nature** of the matter and (ii) the **pervasiveness** of its effect, as summarised below:

| Nature of the matter giving rise to modification | Material but **Not Pervasive** | Material **AND Pervasive** |
|---|---|---|
| Financial statements are **materially misstated** | Qualified opinion | Adverse opinion |
| **Inability to obtain** sufficient appropriate audit evidence | Qualified opinion | Disclaimer of opinion |

**"Pervasive"** describes effects that (a) are not confined to specific elements, accounts or items; or (b) if so confined, represent or could represent a substantial proportion of the financial statements; or (c) in relation to disclosures, are fundamental to users' understanding.

**Conclusion:** The type of modification turns on a two-way matrix of *nature* (misstatement vs inability to obtain evidence) and *pervasiveness* — giving Qualified, Adverse or Disclaimer of opinion.

*(Full-marks tip: The 2×2 matrix is the answer's core — reproduce it exactly. Define "pervasive" (three limbs) because the entire distinction between Qualified vs Adverse/Disclaimer hinges on it. Losing the definition of pervasive is the classic deduction.)*

### Q78. Ch: Audit Report — Forming an Opinion (SA 700) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** In terms of SA 700 (Revised), "Forming an Opinion and Reporting on Financial Statements", state the matters on which the auditor must conclude before **forming an opinion** that the financial statements give a true and fair view, and explain when he shall express an **unmodified opinion**.

**Answer:**
**SA 700 (Revised)** deals with the auditor's responsibility to form an opinion on the financial statements and prescribes the form and content of the auditor's report.

**Forming an opinion:** The auditor shall form an opinion on **whether the financial statements are prepared, in all material respects, in accordance with the applicable financial reporting framework**. To form that opinion, the auditor shall conclude as to **whether he has obtained reasonable assurance about whether the financial statements as a whole are free from material misstatement, whether due to fraud or error**. That conclusion shall take into account:
(a) whether **sufficient appropriate audit evidence** has been obtained (SA 330);
(b) whether **uncorrected misstatements** are material, individually or in aggregate (SA 450); and
(c) the **evaluations** described below.

In particular, the auditor shall evaluate whether the financial statements are prepared in accordance with the framework, including whether:
1. the financial statements **adequately disclose the significant accounting policies** selected and applied;
2. the accounting policies selected are **consistent** with the framework and are **appropriate**;
3. the **accounting estimates** made by management are **reasonable**;
4. the information is **relevant, reliable, comparable and understandable**;
5. the financial statements provide **adequate disclosures** to enable users to understand the effect of material transactions and events; and
6. the **terminology** used, including the title of each financial statement, is appropriate.
He shall also evaluate whether the financial statements achieve **fair presentation** and refer to the applicable framework.

**Unmodified opinion:** The auditor shall express an **unmodified opinion** when he concludes that the **financial statements are prepared, in all material respects, in accordance with the applicable financial reporting framework** (i.e., they give a true and fair view). If, on the other hand, he concludes that the financial statements as a whole are not free from material misstatement, or he is unable to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence, he shall **modify the opinion** in accordance with **SA 705**.

**Conclusion:** The auditor forms his opinion by evaluating sufficiency of evidence, materiality of uncorrected misstatements and adequacy of disclosures/fair presentation; an unmodified opinion is expressed only where the financial statements, in all material respects, comply with the framework.

*(Full-marks tip: Structure the answer around the two pillars — (i) reasonable assurance / sufficient evidence / uncorrected misstatements, and (ii) the qualitative evaluation of policies, estimates and disclosures. End with the trigger for an unmodified opinion and cross-refer SA 705 for modification.)*

### Q79. Ch: Audit Report — Key Audit Matters (SA 701) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** **Sterling Pharma Ltd.** is a listed company. While finalising the FY 2025-26 audit, the engagement partner identifies the **valuation of a large litigation provision** and the **impairment assessment of goodwill** as areas that demanded significant auditor attention. Explain, with reference to SA 701, what **Key Audit Matters (KAM)** are, to which entities their communication is mandatory, how the auditor **determines** KAM, and whether a matter giving rise to a **qualified opinion** can also be reported as a KAM.

**Answer:**
**SA 701**, "Communicating Key Audit Matters in the Independent Auditor's Report", deals with the auditor's responsibility to communicate key audit matters in the auditor's report.

**Meaning:** **Key Audit Matters** are those matters that, **in the auditor's professional judgement, were of most significance in the audit** of the financial statements of the current period. KAM are **selected from matters communicated with those charged with governance (TCWG)**.

**Applicability:** Communication of KAM is **mandatory for audits of complete sets of general purpose financial statements of listed entities**, and where the auditor otherwise decides, or is required by law/regulation, to communicate KAM. As Sterling Pharma Ltd. is a **listed company**, reporting of KAM in the auditor's report is **mandatory**.

**Determination of KAM (a filtering process):** From matters communicated with TCWG, the auditor first determines those that **required significant auditor attention**. In doing so he takes into account:
(a) areas of **higher assessed risk of material misstatement**, or significant risks identified under SA 315;
(b) **significant auditor judgements** relating to areas involving significant management judgement, including **accounting estimates with high estimation uncertainty**; and
(c) the effect on the audit of **significant events or transactions** during the period.
From those, the auditor then determines which were of **most significance** in the audit — these are the KAM. Applying this to Sterling Pharma Ltd., both the **litigation provision** (high estimation uncertainty and management judgement) and the **goodwill impairment** (significant judgement/estimate) are strong candidates for KAM.

**Description in the report:** Each KAM is described in a separate section headed **"Key Audit Matters"**, stating **why** the matter was considered to be a KAM and **how it was addressed** in the audit, with a reference to the related disclosures.

**KAM vs a modification:** A matter giving rise to a **qualified or adverse opinion, or a material uncertainty related to going concern, is by its nature a key audit matter**; however, in such cases the auditor **does NOT describe it in the KAM section**. Instead he reports it in the **Basis for Qualified/Adverse Opinion** section (or the Material Uncertainty Related to Going Concern section) and includes a reference to it in the KAM section. Thus a qualified-opinion matter is **not separately presented as a KAM**.

**Conclusion:** KAM are the matters of most significance selected from those communicated to TCWG; their reporting is mandatory for Sterling Pharma Ltd. as a listed entity; the litigation provision and goodwill impairment qualify; but any matter that leads to a modified opinion is reported in the Basis for Opinion section, not the KAM section.

*(Full-marks tip: The mark-earning subtlety is that communicating KAM is NOT a substitute for a modified opinion — a qualified/adverse matter goes in the Basis for Opinion section with only a cross-reference in the KAM section. Also state the three "significant attention" factors and that KAM are drawn from TCWG communications.)*

### Q80. Ch: Audit Report — Emphasis of Matter vs Other Matter Paragraphs (SA 706) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Distinguish, under SA 706 (Revised), between an **"Emphasis of Matter" (EOM) paragraph** and an **"Other Matter" paragraph**. In each of the following situations of **Aurora Chemicals Ltd.**, state which paragraph (if any) the auditor should use:
(a) A material uncertainty relating to the outcome of a major litigation is **appropriately disclosed** in the notes, and the auditor wishes to draw users' attention to it.
(b) The **prior period financial statements were audited by another (predecessor) auditor**, and the auditor wishes to state this fact.

**Answer:**
**SA 706 (Revised)**, "Emphasis of Matter Paragraphs and Other Matter Paragraphs in the Independent Auditor's Report", provides for additional communication that does **not** modify the opinion.

**Emphasis of Matter (EOM) paragraph:** A paragraph included in the auditor's report that refers to a matter **appropriately presented or disclosed in the financial statements** that, in the auditor's judgement, is of **such importance that it is fundamental to users' understanding** of the financial statements. It is used only where the matter is **already properly disclosed** and would **not itself result in a modification** of the opinion.

**Other Matter paragraph:** A paragraph included in the auditor's report that refers to a matter **other than those presented or disclosed in the financial statements** that, in the auditor's judgement, is **relevant to users' understanding of the audit, the auditor's responsibilities or the auditor's report**.

The distinction is summarised below:

| Basis | Emphasis of Matter paragraph | Other Matter paragraph |
|---|---|---|
| Subject | A matter **disclosed IN** the financial statements | A matter **NOT disclosed** in the financial statements |
| Purpose | Fundamental to users' **understanding of the FS** | Relevant to understanding the **audit / auditor's responsibilities / report** |
| Placement | Separate section "Emphasis of Matter" | Separate section "Other Matter" |
| Effect on opinion | None (opinion unmodified) | None (opinion unmodified) |

**Application to Aurora Chemicals Ltd.:**
(a) The **material uncertainty on litigation is appropriately disclosed in the notes**, and the auditor wants to highlight it — this is a matter *within* the financial statements that is fundamental to users' understanding. The auditor uses an **Emphasis of Matter paragraph** (assuming it does not amount to a material uncertainty on going concern, which SA 570 would separately govern).
(b) The fact that the **prior period figures were audited by a predecessor auditor** is a matter *relevant to users' understanding of the audit* and is **not disclosed in the financial statements**. The auditor uses an **Other Matter paragraph**.

**Conclusion:** (a) Emphasis of Matter paragraph; (b) Other Matter paragraph. Both leave the audit opinion unmodified.

*(Full-marks tip: The single discriminator is "is the matter disclosed IN the financial statements?" — Yes → EOM; No → Other Matter. Both are non-modifying. Stating that an EOM is not a substitute for disclosure or for a modified opinion earns the top mark.)*

### Q84. Ch: Audit Report / Company Audit — Integrated: Sec 143(3), CARO 2020 & Modified Opinion (Marks: 8) [Application]
**Question:** You are the statutory auditor of **Nimbus Logistics Ltd.**, an unlisted public company, for FY 2025-26. Your audit reveals the following:
| No. | Observation |
|---|---|
| 1 | The company has **not provided for a known and quantified liability** of ₹1.20 crore towards an arbitration award decided against it; this is material but confined to one head and does not pervade the accounts. |
| 2 | The company has **defaulted in repayment of a term loan** to a bank: principal of ₹80 lakh and interest of ₹12 lakh, overdue by 5 months as at year-end. |
| 3 | The auditor is satisfied that, except for observation 1, proper books of account have been kept and the financial statements agree with the books. |
Advise: (a) the **type of opinion** under SA 705; (b) how observation 2 is to be reported under **CARO 2020**; and (c) the auditor's duty under **Section 143(3)** as to whether the observations affect his reporting on books of account and adequacy of internal financial controls.

**Answer:**
This question integrates the auditor's reporting obligations under **SA 705 (Revised)** (opinion), **CARO 2020** (specific default reporting) and **Section 143(3)** of the Companies Act, 2013 (statutory report contents).

**(a) Type of opinion — SA 705:** Observation 1 is a **material misstatement** — a known, quantified liability of ₹1.20 crore has not been provided for, understating liabilities and overstating profit. The auditor has sufficient appropriate audit evidence (it is not a limitation of scope). Under SA 705, since the misstatement is **material but NOT pervasive** (it is confined to one head and does not affect the financial statements as a whole), the auditor shall express a **Qualified Opinion** — stating that, *except for* the effects of the non-provision of the ₹1.20 crore liability, the financial statements give a true and fair view. This is supported by a **"Basis for Qualified Opinion"** section (placed after the Opinion section) describing and quantifying the matter.

**(b) Reporting of the loan default — CARO 2020, Clause 3(ix)(a):** Clause 3(ix)(a) requires the auditor to report **whether the company has defaulted in repayment of loans or other borrowings or in the payment of interest thereon to any lender**, and if yes, to give the **period and amount of default** in the prescribed format (nature of borrowing, name of the lender, amount not paid on the due date, whether principal or interest, and number of days of delay/default). Accordingly, the auditor must report the default: **principal ₹80 lakh and interest ₹12 lakh, to the bank, overdue by 5 months (about 150 days)**. This is a factual CARO disclosure and is reported irrespective of the opinion type.

**(c) Duty under Section 143(3):** Section 143(3) requires the auditor's report to state, among other things:
- **[143(3)(b)]** whether, in his opinion, **proper books of account** as required by law have been kept; here, since the failure to provide for the liability is a matter of *accounting treatment* and the auditor is otherwise satisfied that proper books have been kept, he can report **affirmatively that proper books have been kept** (the ₹1.20 crore issue is dealt with through the qualified opinion, not as a books-of-account deficiency);
- **[143(3)(e)]** whether, in his opinion, the financial statements comply with the **accounting standards** — here there is **non-compliance** (the un-provided liability), which the auditor reports in his qualification;
- **[143(3)(i)]** whether the company has **adequate internal financial controls with reference to financial statements** and the operating effectiveness of such controls — a single accounting-treatment error does not by itself establish a control weakness unless it flows from a deficiency in controls; the auditor exercises judgement, and reports a control weakness only if the non-provision indicates a material weakness in the financial-reporting control (otherwise he reports adequacy). Under Section 143(3)(f), the observations that are qualifications are also to be stated with the auditor's observations having an **adverse effect on the functioning of the company** where applicable.

**Conclusion:** (a) The auditor expresses a **Qualified Opinion** (material but not pervasive) with a Basis for Qualified Opinion section; (b) the term-loan default of ₹80 lakh principal + ₹12 lakh interest (overdue 5 months) is reported under **CARO 2020 Clause 3(ix)(a)** in the prescribed format; (c) under Section 143(3) he can still report that proper books were kept (143(3)(b)), reports the accounting-standard non-compliance in his qualification (143(3)(e)), and evaluates internal financial controls (143(3)(i)) on the basis of whether the error reflects a control weakness.

*(Full-marks tip: This is a "spot each reporting stream" question — keep the SA 705 opinion, the CARO 3(ix) default disclosure and the Section 143(3) book/AS/IFC reporting SEPARATE and answer each. The nuance for top marks: a non-provision affects the OPINION and AS-compliance but does not automatically mean "proper books not kept" or "IFC inadequate". Loan default is a CARO clause 3(ix)(a) matter, not an opinion modification by itself.)*

### Q85. Ch: Professional Ethics & Independence — Fundamental Principles & Conceptual Framework (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** CA. Meera has recently set up her practice and is unsure how the Code of Ethics expects her to deal with situations that are not specifically prohibited by any clause of the Schedules but still make her uneasy. Explain the five fundamental principles of professional ethics that every member must comply with, and describe the "conceptual framework approach" that the Code prescribes for applying them.

**Answer:**
The Code of Ethics issued by the Institute of Chartered Accountants of India (ICAI), which is based on the IESBA Code, requires every professional accountant to comply with **five fundamental principles** of professional ethics. These principles establish the standard of behaviour expected of a member and are as follows:

**(i) Integrity** — A member shall be straightforward and honest in all professional and business relationships. Integrity also implies fair dealing and truthfulness. A member should not be associated with reports, returns, communications or other information where he believes that the information contains a materially false or misleading statement, or omits or obscures required information.

**(ii) Objectivity** — A member shall not compromise professional or business judgement because of bias, conflict of interest or the undue influence of others. He must not perform a professional service if a circumstance or relationship unduly influences his professional judgement.

**(iii) Professional Competence and Due Care** — A member has a continuing duty to maintain professional knowledge and skill at the level required to ensure that a client or employer receives competent professional service based on current developments in practice, legislation and techniques, and to act diligently in accordance with applicable technical and professional standards.

**(iv) Confidentiality** — A member shall respect the confidentiality of information acquired as a result of professional and business relationships and shall not disclose any such information to third parties without proper and specific authority, unless there is a legal or professional right or duty to disclose. Nor shall he use such information for personal advantage or the advantage of third parties.

**(v) Professional Behaviour** — A member shall comply with relevant laws and regulations and avoid any conduct that the member knows or should know might discredit the profession.

**The Conceptual Framework Approach:** Because it is impossible to define every situation that creates a threat to compliance, the Code requires the member to apply a **conceptual framework** rather than rely only on a set of specific rules. Under this framework the member must:
- **Identify** threats to compliance with the fundamental principles;
- **Evaluate** the threats so identified; and
- **Address** the threats by eliminating them or reducing them to an **acceptable level** — a level at which a reasonable and informed third party would likely conclude that compliance with the fundamental principles is not compromised.

Where the threats cannot be reduced to an acceptable level, the member must either decline or discontinue the specific professional activity or, where necessary, resign from the engagement.

**Conclusion:** CA. Meera must comply with the five fundamental principles at all times and, for situations not expressly covered by a clause, apply the conceptual framework — identify, evaluate and address threats — so that her compliance with the principles is not compromised.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards naming all five principles with a one-line meaning each AND the three-step framework (identify–evaluate–address) with the "acceptable level / reasonable and informed third party" test. Marks are lost for listing only the principles without the framework, or vice versa.)*

### Q86. Ch: Professional Ethics & Independence — Categories of Threats & Safeguards (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Identify and name the category of threat to the fundamental principles that arises in EACH of the following independent situations, giving a one-line justification, and briefly state the general categories of safeguards available to reduce such threats to an acceptable level:
| # | Situation |
|---|-----------|
| (a) | CA. Rao audits ABC Ltd. and, in the same year, his firm had recommended and helped design the very inventory-valuation system whose output he must now audit. |
| (b) | The audit fee from XYZ Ltd. represents 42% of the total fee income of CA. Sen's proprietary firm for the third consecutive year. |
| (c) | The finance director of a listed audit client is the sister of the audit engagement partner. |
| (d) | During the audit, the client's managing director threatens to remove the firm as auditor unless it agrees to an aggressive tax position. |
| (e) | CA. Iyer, while defending a client's accounting treatment before the tax authorities, is now asked to give an independent audit opinion on the same treatment. |

**Answer:**
The Code of Ethics classifies threats to compliance with the fundamental principles into **five broad categories**. Applying them to the facts:

| # | Threat Category | Justification |
|---|-----------------|---------------|
| (a) | **Self-review threat** | The auditor would be evaluating the results of a system that his own firm designed, so he reviews his own earlier judgement. |
| (b) | **Self-interest threat** | Undue dependence on the fee from one client (a large proportion of total fees) creates a financial interest in retaining that client. |
| (c) | **Familiarity threat** | A close family relationship with a director in a position to influence the subject matter makes the partner too sympathetic to the client's interests. |
| (d) | **Intimidation threat** | The threat of removal / actual pressure deters the auditor from acting objectively. |
| (e) | **Advocacy threat** | Having promoted the client's position before the tax authorities, the member's objectivity in now giving an audit opinion on the same matter is compromised. |

**Safeguards:** Safeguards are actions, individually or in combination, that effectively reduce threats to an acceptable level. They fall into two broad categories:
1. **Safeguards created by the profession, legislation or regulation** — e.g. educational, training and experience requirements for entry; continuing professional development; professional standards; monitoring and disciplinary procedures; and external review by a legally empowered third party.
2. **Safeguards in the work environment** — e.g. firm-wide safeguards (leadership stressing compliance, quality control policies, rotation of senior personnel, using different partners/teams for non-assurance work) and engagement-specific safeguards (involving an additional professional accountant to review the work done, consulting an independent third party, and disclosing the nature of services and fees to those charged with governance).

**Conclusion:** Each situation attracts a distinct category of threat, and the member must apply appropriate safeguards — profession-created or work-environment — or, where the threat cannot be reduced to an acceptable level, decline or withdraw from the engagement.

### Q87. Ch: Professional Ethics & Independence — First Schedule Part I (Clause application) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Comment, with reference to the exact clause of the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949, on whether the following amount to professional misconduct:
| # | Situation |
|---|-----------|
| (a) | CA. Vikram, a practising member, pays ₹15,000 per referred client to a firm of management consultants who direct audit work to him. |
| (b) | CA. Nisha allows Mr. Prakash, a non-member employee, to sign the audit report and balance sheet on her behalf while she is travelling abroad. |

Mr. Prakash is a non-member employee; permitting him to sign the audit report allows a non-member to practise in her name. Hence **CA. Nisha is guilty under Clause (1)**.

Although the ICAI now permits partnership with members of certain notified professional bodies (which may include advocates under the Multi-Disciplinary Partnership scheme once notified), unless the arrangement complies with the prescribed conditions and regulations, sharing of practice profits with an Advocate is not permitted. On the given facts (a plain partnership for profit-sharing with an Advocate outside the notified framework), **CA. Deep is guilty under Clause (4)**.

**Conclusion:** (a) Guilty — Clause (2); (b) Guilty — Clause (1); (c) Guilty — Clause (4) of Part I of the First Schedule.

### Q88. Ch: Professional Ethics & Independence — First Schedule (Solicitation & Advertising) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** With reference to the relevant clauses of Part I of the First Schedule to the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949, examine the following:
| # | Situation |
|---|-----------|
| (a) | CA. Anand issues a circular to persons who are not his clients, informing them that he has set up practice and inviting them to engage his services. |
| (b) | CA. Bhavna designs a website for her firm displaying the firm name, address, membership number and the names of clients (with their consent) along with fees charged. |
| (c) | CA. Chetan, in a newspaper interview on the new GST amendments, is described with his professional attainments and his firm's specialisation, at his own instance. |
| (d) | CA. Divya accepts appointment as statutory auditor of a company without first communicating in writing with the previous auditor. |

**(a)** Under **Clause (5) of Part I** of the First Schedule, a member in practice is guilty of misconduct if he secures, either through the services of a person who is not an employee of such member or who is not his partner or by means which are not open to a chartered accountant, any professional business. Further, **Clause (6)** prohibits soliciting clients or professional work either directly or indirectly by circular, advertisement, personal communication or interview or by any other means. Issuing a circular to non-clients soliciting work is a direct solicitation. Hence **CA. Anand is guilty under Clause (6)**.

**(b)** A member is permitted to have a website subject to the guidelines issued by the Council. **(d)** Under **Clause (8) of Part I**, a member in practice is guilty if he accepts a position as auditor previously held by another chartered accountant without first communicating with him **in writing**. The requirement of prior communication in writing (by registered post/acknowledgement) is mandatory to enable the incoming auditor to ascertain professional reasons for non-continuance and to safeguard the interest of the previous auditor. CA. Divya failed to communicate. Hence **CA. Divya is guilty under Clause (8)**.

**Conclusion:** (a) Guilty — Clause (6); (b) Guilty to the extent of displaying fees — Clause (6); (c) Guilty — Clause (6)/(7); (d) Guilty — Clause (8).

### Q91. Ch: Professional Ethics & Independence — Ceiling on Audits & Fees Independence (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the position under the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949 (and the Companies Act, 2013 read with the Code of Ethics) on (a) the maximum number of company audits (specified number of audits) that a member in practice may hold, and (b) the safeguards where the total fees from an audit client represent a large proportion of the firm's total fees.

**Answer:**
**(a) Ceiling on number of company audits:** Under **Section 141(3)(g) of the Companies Act, 2013**, a person is disqualified for appointment as auditor if he is in full-time employment elsewhere or is holding the appointment as auditor of **more than twenty companies** at the date of appointment. In computing this limit, one-person companies, dormant companies, small companies, and private companies having paid-up share capital of less than ₹100 crore are **excluded**. Additionally, the Council's guidelines specify the "specified number of audits" per partner (30 company audits, excluding those specifically exempted). Where a firm has more than one partner, the ceiling is multiplied by the number of partners in practice.

Safeguards include: reducing the dependency on the client; having an external quality-control review; and consulting a third party such as the Institute or another chartered accountant on key audit judgements. For a **public interest entity**, where total fees exceed a stated proportion of the firm's total fees for two consecutive years, the firm should disclose the matter to those charged with governance and arrange for a **pre- or post-issuance review** by a professional accountant who is not a member of the engagement team.

**Conclusion:** A member is subject to the statutory ceiling of company audits under Section 141(3)(g) and the Council's specified-number limit, and must apply the prescribed safeguards where fee dependence on a single client is significant.

### Q93. Ch: Audit of Different Entities — Bank Audit (LFAR & Advances) (Marks: 8) [Application]
**Question:** You have been appointed as the branch auditor of a branch of a nationalised bank for the year ended 31st March. Draft the principal audit considerations and procedures you would apply to the audit of **advances**, and briefly explain the purpose of the **Long Form Audit Report (LFAR)**.

**Answer:**
The audit of a bank branch is governed by the **Banking Regulation Act, 1949**, the RBI's directions (including the Master Directions on Income Recognition, Asset Classification and Provisioning — IRAC norms), and the applicable Standards on Auditing. **Advances** typically constitute the most significant and risk-prone asset of a bank branch, and the auditor must obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence over their existence, valuation and classification (SA 500).

**Audit considerations and procedures for Advances:**

**(1) Existence, ownership and documentation** — Verify that amounts included in the balance sheet as advances are outstanding and recoverable, that proper loan documentation (loan agreements, security documents, guarantees) exists, is complete, valid and enforceable, and is stamped and registered where required.

**(2) Sanction and disbursement** — Examine that advances have been sanctioned by an authority within its delegated powers, that terms of sanction (margin, interest, security, repayment) have been complied with, and that disbursements were made only after compliance with pre-disbursement conditions.

**(3) Income recognition and asset classification (IRAC norms)** — Verify that interest and other income on advances is recognised only on performing (standard) assets and that once an account becomes a **Non-Performing Asset (NPA)** (e.g. a term loan where interest/principal remains overdue for **more than 90 days**), income is recognised only on realisation. Check the classification of NPAs into **sub-standard, doubtful and loss** categories, and that provisioning has been made at the prescribed rates.

**(4) Drawing power and security valuation** — For cash-credit/overdraft accounts, verify that the drawing power has been correctly computed from stock/book-debt statements after applying the stipulated margin, that stock statements are current, and that the value of security has been properly assessed.

**(5) Review of irregular accounts** — Scrutinise overdrawn, out-of-order and overdue accounts, accounts with continuous debits and no credits, and accounts where limits have expired but continue to operate, to identify NPAs not classified as such by the branch.

**(6) Restructured/rescheduled accounts** — Verify correct classification and provisioning for restructured advances as per RBI norms.

**Purpose of the LFAR:** The **Long Form Audit Report** is a detailed questionnaire-based report that the branch auditor must submit in addition to the main audit report. It requires the auditor to comment on specific areas — cash, balances with RBI/other banks, investments, advances (sanction, documentation, review, recovery, NPAs), other assets, deposits, contingent liabilities, profit and loss, and the functioning of internal controls and inter-branch accounts. Its purpose is to bring to the notice of the bank's management and the RBI the **weaknesses and irregularities** observed at the branch that may not find a place in the standardised main report, so that corrective action can be taken.

**Conclusion:** The branch auditor must focus on the existence, recoverability, correct income recognition, classification and provisioning of advances as per RBI IRAC norms, and must report matters of concern through both the main report and the LFAR.

### Q94. Ch: Audit of Different Entities — Bank Audit (NPA Classification) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** State, with reasons based on the RBI's IRAC norms, the correct asset classification of each of the following advances of a bank branch as at 31st March:
| # | Account | Facts |
|---|---------|-------|
| (a) | Term loan – Alpha | Interest overdue for 75 days as on 31st March. |
| (b) | Cash credit – Beta | Account has remained "out of order" continuously since 1st October (i.e., more than 90 days). |
| (c) | Loan – Gamma | Has remained a sub-standard asset for 14 months. |
| (d) | Loan – Delta | Doubtful for 3 years and 6 months; realisable value of security is nil. |

**Answer:**
Under the RBI's **Master Directions on Income Recognition and Asset Classification (IRAC)**, an asset becomes **Non-Performing (NPA)** when it ceases to generate income for the bank. The general test is that a term loan is an NPA if interest and/or instalment of principal remains **overdue for more than 90 days**, and a cash-credit/overdraft account is an NPA if it remains **"out of order"** for more than 90 days. NPAs are further classified as **sub-standard, doubtful and loss** assets.

| # | Classification | Reason |
|---|----------------|--------|
| (a) Alpha | **Standard asset (not NPA)** | Interest is overdue for only 75 days, which is within the 90-day limit; hence it has not become an NPA. |
| (b) Beta | **Sub-standard asset** | The account has been "out of order" for more than 90 days and thus became an NPA; an account that has remained NPA for **up to 12 months** is a sub-standard asset. |
| (c) Gamma | **Doubtful asset** | An asset which has **remained sub-standard for a period of 12 months** is classified as doubtful; Gamma has been sub-standard for 14 months. |
| (d) Delta | **Loss asset** | Where the realisable value of security is nil / the asset is considered uncollectible, and it has been doubtful for a long period, it is identified as a loss asset (loss identified but not written off). |

**Conclusion:** (a) Standard; (b) Sub-standard; (c) Doubtful; (d) Loss asset.

### Q95. Ch: Audit of Different Entities — Insurance Company Audit (General Insurance) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** As the auditor of a general insurance company, explain the audit procedures you would apply to verify (a) **Premium income** and (b) the **Reserve for Unexpired Risk / provision for outstanding claims**, and state the statutory framework applicable to the audit.

**Answer:**
The audit of a general (non-life) insurance company is governed by the **Insurance Act, 1938**, the **Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority Act, 1999 (IRDA Act)** and the regulations and financial-statement formats prescribed by **IRDAI**, in addition to the Companies Act, 2013 and the Standards on Auditing.

**(a) Verification of Premium income:**
- Verify that premium is recognised as income only when the risk commences and the premium is received or is due, in accordance with IRDAI regulations.
- Check that cover notes/policies are issued serially and that premium registers reconcile with the collection records and the general ledger.
- Ensure that premium is **not** recognised until the risk has attached; where the premium is received but the risk has not commenced, it is carried forward as "premium received in advance".
- Verify reinsurance premium ceded and the correct netting of co-insurance arrangements.
- Test that instalment premiums, if any, are correctly accounted, and that there is no premium deficiency.

**(b) Reserve for Unexpired Risk and Outstanding Claims:**
- **Reserve for Unexpired Risk (URR):** Since a policy period may straddle the year-end, a portion of the premium relates to the unexpired period. Verify that URR has been created at the rates prescribed under the Insurance Act / IRDAI regulations — generally **50%** of net premium for most miscellaneous business and **100%** for marine hull business — to provide for claims that may arise in the unexpired portion of the risk.
- **Outstanding claims provision:** Verify that a provision has been made for all claims intimated and outstanding (including IBNR — incurred but not reported — and IBNER estimates) as at the year-end, that the estimation of the claim amount is reasonable, supported by surveyors' reports, and that claims paid are supported by proper discharge vouchers and are within the terms of the policy.
- Check compliance with the requirement that claims be provided on an **intimation basis**, not merely on settlement.

**Conclusion:** The auditor must verify premium recognition on the "risk-attached" basis and confirm that the unexpired-risk reserve (50%/100%) and the provision for outstanding claims (including IBNR) are created as per the Insurance Act and IRDAI regulations.

### Q96. Ch: Audit of Different Entities — Not-for-Profit Organisation (NPO / NGO) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** "Bright Future Foundation", a not-for-profit charitable organisation registered as a public trust and also registered under Section 12A and 80G of the Income-tax Act, receives donations, foreign contributions and government grants. As its auditor, describe the special audit considerations and the audit procedures you would apply to its receipts and the utilisation of grants.

**Answer:**
A not-for-profit organisation (NPO) exists to further a charitable, religious, educational or similar object and not to earn profit. Its audit is governed by the statute under which it is constituted (Public Trust Act / Societies Registration Act / Section 8 of the Companies Act, 2013 as applicable), and where it receives foreign contributions, by the **Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act, 2010 (FCRA)**, in addition to the Income-tax Act (Sections 11–13, 12A, 80G) and the Standards on Auditing.

**Special audit considerations and procedures:**

**(1) Constitution and objects** — Study the trust deed / memorandum and rules to understand the objects and the powers of the trustees, and verify that the income is applied only towards the objects of the trust.

**(2) Donations and contributions** — Verify donations against counterfoils of receipts issued serially, the donation register and bank credits. Distinguish **corpus donations** (received with a specific written direction to form part of the corpus — capital in nature and exempt) from **general/voluntary donations** (revenue in nature). Verify that anonymous donations, where taxable, are correctly dealt with.

**(3) Foreign contributions (FCRA)** — Verify that the organisation is registered / has prior permission under FCRA, that foreign contributions are received only in the designated FCRA bank account, are kept and utilised separately from local funds, are used only for the purposes permitted, and that the prescribed returns have been filed.

**(4) Government and other grants** — Verify grant sanction letters and the conditions attached. Ensure that grants are utilised for the specified purpose and within the stipulated time, that unutilised grants are shown as a liability (refundable) or carried forward as per the terms, and obtain / verify the **utilisation certificate** required to be furnished to the grantor.

**(5) Application of income (Income-tax)** — Verify that at least **85%** of the income has been applied for charitable purposes during the year to retain exemption under Section 11; where income is accumulated, verify compliance with the conditions (Form 10 / prescribed manner of investment under Section 11(5)).

**(6) Expenditure and assets** — Vouch expenditure with supporting documents and sanction, verify that capital expenditure is properly recorded, and physically verify significant fixed assets and investments.

**Conclusion:** The auditor must ensure that receipts (donations, foreign contributions and grants) are properly recorded and applied strictly for the objects of the trust in compliance with FCRA, the grant conditions and the Income-tax Act, and that the exemption conditions (including the 85% application requirement) are met.

### Q97. Ch: Audit of Different Entities — Audit of Local Body (Municipality) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Explain the objectives of the audit of a **local body** (such as a Municipality/Municipal Corporation) and describe the special features and audit considerations that distinguish the audit of a local self-government body from that of a commercial concern.

**Answer:**
A **local body** is a self-governing institution constituted under a statute (e.g. a Municipal Act) for the administration of a defined local area, funded largely from taxes, grants and its own revenues, and accountable to the public and the government for the proper use of these funds. Its audit is a form of **government/public audit** and is normally conducted under the relevant State Municipal Act, with the Comptroller and Auditor-General (C&AG) or the Local Fund Audit department / Examiner of Local Fund Accounts as the auditing authority.

**Objectives of the audit of a local body:**
- To ensure that the **expenditure is incurred with proper sanction** of the competent authority (audit against sanction);
- To ensure that expenditure conforms to the **relevant provisions of law, rules and regulations** (regularity / propriety audit) and is a legitimate charge on the fund;
- To ensure that the **revenues** due (taxes, rates, fees, rents) have been correctly and fully assessed, realised and credited to the appropriate fund;
- To verify that grants and loans received from the government are utilised for the purposes for which they were sanctioned and within the stipulated conditions;
- To detect and prevent errors, fraud and misappropriation, and to ensure economy, efficiency and effectiveness in the use of public resources (**propriety audit** — the audit of the wisdom, faithfulness and economy of transactions).

**Special features / audit considerations (contrasted with a commercial audit):**

| Aspect | Local Body Audit | Commercial Audit |
|--------|------------------|------------------|
| Objective | Regularity, propriety and public accountability | True and fair view / profit measurement |
| Basis of accounting | Traditionally cash/fund-based (moving to accrual) | Accrual basis |
| Focus | Compliance with budget grants and sanctions | Financial performance and position |
| Governing law | State Municipal Act / Local Fund rules | Companies Act, 2013 |
| Emphasis | Audit against provision of funds and propriety | Assertions and true & fair view |

The auditor must, in particular, verify **budgetary control** — that expenditure has not exceeded the budget grant and that reallocation was duly sanctioned — and apply **propriety audit** to ensure there is no wasteful or unauthorised expenditure.

**Conclusion:** The audit of a local body is directed at regularity, propriety and accountability for public funds — ensuring expenditure is sanctioned and lawful, revenues are fully realised, and grants are properly utilised — which distinguishes it from the profit-and-true-and-fair-view focus of a commercial audit.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the concepts of "audit against sanction", "propriety audit", and "budgetary control", plus a clear contrast table with commercial audit. Merely listing objectives without the propriety/regularity emphasis loses marks.)*

### Q98. Ch: Audit of Different Entities — Co-operative Society (Special Features) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the special features and duties that a chartered accountant must keep in mind while conducting the audit of a **co-operative society**, particularly with reference to the examination of overdue debts, the provisions of the applicable Act, and observations on the working of the society.

**Answer:**
The audit of a co-operative society is governed by the **Co-operative Societies Act, 1912** or the relevant **State Co-operative Societies Act** and the rules made thereunder. Under Section 17 of the 1912 Act (and the corresponding State provisions), the Registrar of Co-operative Societies has the power to audit, or cause to be audited, the accounts of every registered society. The auditor is often appointed from a panel maintained by the Registrar. The following special features distinguish this audit:

**(1) Examination of overdue debts** — The auditor must specially examine the overdue debts and classify them (e.g. good/doubtful/bad) and mention them in his report, since heavy overdues affect the working capital and the recoverability position of the society. He should assess the adequacy of provision against them.

**(2) Overdue interest** — Overdue interest should be **excluded from interest outstanding and accrued due** while calculating profit. Overdue interest is interest accrued or accruing on debts which are overdue, and it should be shown separately and not treated as realisable income.

**(3) Certification of bad debts** — Bad debts and irrecoverable amounts can be written off only when they are certified as bad by the auditor and are authorised as per the society's bye-laws / Rules.

**(4) Valuation of assets and liabilities** — The auditor should verify the valuation and existence of assets; there is generally no elaborate provision for depreciation in the Act, so he must ensure a reasonable basis has been adopted.

**(5) Adherence to the Act, Rules and bye-laws** — The auditor must verify that the society's transactions are in accordance with the provisions of the Act, the Rules and the registered bye-laws; any transaction contrary to these (ultra vires) must be reported.

**(6) Restriction on shareholding, loans and investments** — Verify compliance with restrictions on a member's maximum shareholding, on loans to non-members, and on the mode of investment of funds prescribed by the Act.

**(7) Reporting and classification of the society** — The auditor is required to award a **class/audit classification** (e.g. A, B, C, D) to the society based on the criteria laid down by the Registrar, reflecting the overall performance and financial position, and to report specifically on the matters required by the Act.

**Conclusion:** In auditing a co-operative society, the chartered accountant must give special attention to overdue debts and overdue interest, verify strict compliance with the Co-operative Societies Act, Rules and bye-laws, report on and classify the society, and ensure write-offs are duly certified and authorised.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner specifically looks for the treatment of "overdue interest" (excluded from profit), classification of overdue debts, and the awarding of an audit class to the society — these are unique to co-operative audits.)*

### Q99. Ch: Audit of Different Entities + Professional Ethics (Integration) — Government Company & Independence (Marks: 8) [Application]
**Question:** CA. Raghav's firm has been appointed as statutory auditor of "Nav Bharat Ltd.", a Government company. Answer the following, integrating the requirements of the Companies Act, 2013 with the Code of Ethics:
| # | Issue |
|---|-------|
| (a) | Who appoints the statutory auditor of a Government company, and what is the C&AG's role by way of supplementary audit and directions? |
| (b) | During the engagement, the firm is offered, in addition to the audit, the assignment of maintaining the company's books of account and preparing its financial statements. Comment on its acceptability. |
| (c) | The engagement partner's daughter is offered employment by Nav Bharat Ltd. in a key management position during the audit. Identify the threat and the safeguard/action required. |

**Answer:**
**(a) Appointment and C&AG's role:** Under **Section 139(5) of the Companies Act, 2013**, in the case of a **Government company** (or a company owned or controlled, directly or indirectly, by the Central Government, or any State Government, or partly by both), the statutory auditor is **appointed by the Comptroller and Auditor-General of India (C&AG)** within 180 days from the commencement of the financial year. Under **Section 143(5)**, the C&AG has the power to **direct the manner** in which the company's accounts are to be audited and to give directions to the auditor on such matters, and the auditor must submit a copy of the audit report to the C&AG including the directions issued and the action taken. Under **Section 143(6)**, the C&AG may, within 60 days of receipt of the audit report, conduct a **supplementary audit** of the financial statements and comment upon or supplement the audit report; it may also order a **test audit** under Section 143(7).

**(b) Book-keeping / preparation of financial statements:** This is impermissible on two grounds. First, under **Section 144 of the Companies Act, 2013**, the auditor of a company shall **not** render certain "prohibited services" to the company (or its holding/subsidiary), which specifically include **accounting and book-keeping services** and the preparation of financial statements. Second, under the Code of Ethics, auditing financial statements that the firm itself has prepared creates a **self-review threat** to independence, since the auditor would be reviewing his own work. As no safeguard can reduce this to an acceptable level for an audit client, the firm **must decline** the assignment of maintaining books and preparing the financial statements.

**(c) Threat from employment of a close family member:** The offer of a **key management position** to the engagement partner's daughter creates a **familiarity threat** (and potentially a self-interest/intimidation threat) to independence, as a close family member of a member of the assurance team would be in a position to exert direct and significant influence over the subject matter of the audit. The required action/safeguard is to **remove the engagement partner from the engagement team**, or ensure the daughter does not take up a position of significant influence over the financial statements while he remains on the team; if the relationship cannot be neutralised, the firm should reconsider its continuance of the engagement. Those charged with governance should be informed.

**Conclusion:** (a) The C&AG appoints the auditor (Section 139(5)) and may issue directions and conduct supplementary/test audit (Section 143(5)–(7)); (b) the firm must decline the book-keeping/financial-statement preparation assignment under Section 144 and to avoid a self-review threat; (c) the familiarity threat requires the engagement partner to be removed from the team.

*(Full-marks tip: This is a cross-topic question — the examiner rewards citing Section 139(5), 143(5)-(7) AND Section 144 (prohibited services) AND naming the self-review and familiarity threats. Deductions occur when candidates answer only the Companies Act part and ignore the Code of Ethics dimension.)*

### Q100. Ch: Audit of Different Entities — Educational Institution & LLP / Small Entity (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Answer the following two parts:
**(a)** As the auditor of "Sunshine Public School", an educational institution run by a registered society, list the important points/audit procedures you would apply to verify its **fees and other receipts** and its **expenditure**.
**(b)** State the statutory position regarding the audit of a **Limited Liability Partnership (LLP)** — the threshold beyond which audit is mandatory and the applicable law.

**Answer:**
**(a) Audit of an educational institution:** The audit is governed by the statute under which the managing body is constituted (Societies Registration Act / Trust Act), the constitution/bye-laws of the institution, and any grant conditions or regulations of the affiliating authority. Important audit procedures:

*Fees and other receipts:*
- Examine the trust deed/rules and the regulations governing the institution and note the rates of fees, hostel charges and other levies approved by the managing committee.
- Check the **names of students on registers** against the fees register to ensure fees have been collected from all students on the rolls; reconcile the total fees receivable with amounts actually collected, and verify that arrears/outstanding fees are properly carried forward.
- Verify that **fees received in advance** are properly accounted as a liability, and that concessions/free-ships and scholarships have been authorised by the competent authority.
- Verify grants from government/other bodies against sanction letters and the utilisation conditions, and verify other income (interest, donations, hostel and transport receipts) against supporting records.
- Vouch admission fees, examination fees, fines and miscellaneous receipts with counterfoils of serially numbered receipts.

*Expenditure:*
- Vouch salaries of staff with the sanctioned pay scales and appointment terms; verify statutory deductions (PF, TDS) and their timely deposit.
- Vouch capital and revenue expenditure with sanction of the managing committee and supporting documents, and verify that the expenditure is within the budget and for the objects of the institution.
- Physically verify significant assets (furniture, equipment, library books) and confirm investment of surplus funds as per the applicable rules.

**(b) Audit of an LLP:** The audit of a Limited Liability Partnership is governed by the **Limited Liability Partnership Act, 2008** and the **LLP Rules, 2009**. Audit of the accounts of an LLP is **mandatory only where** (i) the **turnover exceeds ₹40 lakh** in any financial year, or (ii) the **contribution exceeds ₹25 lakh**. An LLP whose turnover and contribution are below both thresholds is not required to get its accounts audited (though it may do so voluntarily and may be required to under other laws such as the Income-tax Act). The auditor of an LLP is a chartered accountant in practice.

**Conclusion:** (a) The auditor of an educational institution must verify that fees are collected from all students on the rolls and correctly accounted (with advances as liability), that grants are utilised per conditions, and that expenditure is duly sanctioned and within the objects; (b) audit of an LLP is compulsory only where turnover exceeds ₹40 lakh or contribution exceeds ₹25 lakh, under the LLP Act, 2008.
