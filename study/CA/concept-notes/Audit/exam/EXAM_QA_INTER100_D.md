### Q1. Ch: Nature, Objective & Scope (SA 200) — Fundamental / Ethical Principles Governing an Audit (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** SA 200 states that the auditor shall comply with relevant ethical requirements, including those pertaining to independence, relating to financial statement audit engagements. Enumerate the **fundamental principles** with which the auditor is required to comply and explain each in brief.

**Answer:** SA 200, "Overall Objectives of the Independent Auditor and the Conduct of an Audit in Accordance with Standards on Auditing," read with the Code of Ethics issued by the Institute of Chartered Accountants of India, requires the auditor to comply with the relevant ethical requirements. These ethical requirements ordinarily comprise the following **five fundamental principles**:

**(i) Integrity** — The auditor should be straightforward and honest in all professional and business relationships. Integrity also implies fair dealing and truthfulness. The auditor should not be associated with reports, returns, communications or other information where he believes that the information contains a materially false or misleading statement.

**(ii) Objectivity** — The auditor should not allow bias, conflict of interest or undue influence of others to override professional or business judgements. He should refrain from performing a professional service if a circumstance or relationship biases or unduly influences his professional judgement.

**(iii) Professional Competence and Due Care** — The auditor has a continuing duty to maintain professional knowledge and skill at the level required to ensure that a client receives competent professional service based on current developments in practice, legislation and techniques. He should act diligently and in accordance with applicable technical and professional standards.

**(iv) Confidentiality** — The auditor should respect the confidentiality of information acquired as a result of professional and business relationships and should not disclose any such information to third parties without proper and specific authority, unless there is a legal or professional right or duty to disclose. Nor should he use such information for his personal advantage or that of third parties.

**(v) Professional Behaviour** — The auditor should comply with relevant laws and regulations and should avoid any action that discredits the profession.

| Principle | Core requirement |
|---|---|
| Integrity | Straightforward and honest |
| Objectivity | No bias, conflict of interest or undue influence |
| Professional competence & due care | Maintain skill; act diligently per standards |
| Confidentiality | Do not disclose or misuse client information |
| Professional behaviour | Comply with laws; avoid discrediting profession |

**Conclusion:** Compliance with these five fundamental principles, together with independence, forms the ethical foundation on which every audit engagement is conducted under SA 200.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner rewards naming all five principles AND a one-line explanation of each; mark deductions occur when students merely list the names, or confuse "confidentiality" with a blanket bar that ignores the legal/professional duty-to-disclose exception.)*

### Q2. Ch: Nature, Objective & Scope (SA 200) — Elements of an Assurance Engagement (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** "An assurance engagement performed by a practitioner will consist of certain elements." In the context of the Framework for Assurance Engagements, identify and briefly explain the **five elements** of an assurance engagement.

**Answer:** As per the "Framework for Assurance Engagements" issued by ICAI, an assurance engagement is an engagement in which a practitioner expresses a conclusion designed to enhance the degree of confidence of the intended users, other than the responsible party, about the outcome of the evaluation or measurement of a subject matter against criteria. An assurance engagement performed by a practitioner will consist of the following **five elements**:

**(i) A three-party relationship** — This involves a **practitioner** (the person who provides the assurance, e.g., the auditor), a **responsible party** (the person responsible for the subject matter, e.g., the management preparing the financial statements) and the **intended users** (the persons for whom the practitioner prepares the assurance report, e.g., shareholders). The responsible party and the intended users may be from different or the same entities.

**(ii) An appropriate subject matter** — The subject matter, and subject matter information, of an assurance engagement can take many forms, such as financial performance or conditions (for which the subject matter information may be the recognition, measurement, presentation and disclosure in financial statements), non-financial performance, physical characteristics, systems and processes, or behaviour. An appropriate subject matter is identifiable and capable of consistent evaluation or measurement against the criteria.

**(iii) Suitable criteria** — Criteria are the benchmarks used to evaluate or measure the subject matter, including, where relevant, benchmarks for presentation and disclosure. Suitable criteria exhibit the characteristics of relevance, completeness, reliability, neutrality and understandability. Criteria can be established (e.g., Accounting Standards) or specifically developed.

**(iv) Sufficient appropriate evidence** — The practitioner plans and performs the assurance engagement with an attitude of professional skepticism to obtain sufficient appropriate evidence about whether the subject matter information is free from material misstatement. **Sufficiency** is the measure of the quantity of evidence and **appropriateness** is the measure of its quality — its relevance and reliability.

**(v) A written assurance report** — The practitioner provides a written report containing a conclusion that conveys the assurance obtained about the subject matter information. In a reasonable assurance engagement the conclusion is expressed in a positive form; in a limited assurance engagement it is expressed in a negative form.

**Conclusion:** These five elements — the three-party relationship, an appropriate subject matter, suitable criteria, sufficient appropriate evidence, and a written report — must all be present for an engagement to qualify as an assurance engagement.

*(Full-marks tip: examiners look for all five elements correctly named and the three parties correctly identified; a frequent deduction is omitting "suitable criteria" or confusing the responsible party with the intended user.)*

### Q3. Ch: Nature, Objective & Scope (SA 200) — Reasonable Assurance vs Absolute Assurance / Inherent Limitations (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** An audit conducted in accordance with SAs is designed to obtain reasonable assurance, and not absolute assurance. Explain why the auditor is **not able to obtain absolute assurance** and cannot reduce audit risk to zero.

**Answer:** As per SA 200, reasonable assurance is a **high, but not absolute, level of assurance**. It is obtained when the auditor has obtained sufficient appropriate audit evidence to reduce audit risk (that is, the risk that the auditor expresses an inappropriate opinion when the financial statements are materially misstated) to an acceptably low level. The auditor is not able to obtain absolute assurance because there are **inherent limitations of an audit**, which result in most of the audit evidence on which the auditor draws conclusions being persuasive rather than conclusive. The inherent limitations of an audit arise from:

**(i) The nature of financial reporting** — The preparation of financial statements involves judgement by management in applying the requirements of the applicable financial reporting framework, and many financial statement items involve subjective decisions, assessments or a degree of uncertainty (e.g., accounting estimates).

**(ii) The nature of audit procedures** — There are practical and legal limits on the auditor's ability to obtain audit evidence. For example, management or others may not provide, intentionally or unintentionally, the complete information relevant to the preparation of the financial statements; fraud may involve sophisticated and carefully organised schemes designed to conceal it; and an audit is not an official investigation into alleged wrongdoing, so the auditor is not given specific legal powers such as the power of search.

**(iii) The need for the audit to be conducted within a reasonable period of time and at a reasonable cost** — There is an expectation that the auditor will form an opinion within a reasonable period and at a reasonable cost, recognising that it is impracticable to address all information that may exist or to pursue every matter exhaustively on the assumption that information is in error or fraudulent until proved otherwise.

Additionally, certain assertions or subject matters — such as the occurrence of fraud involving senior management or collusion, the existence of related party relationships, or the occurrence of non-compliance with laws and regulations — have particular significance in the way inherent limitations affect the auditor's ability to detect material misstatements.

**Conclusion:** Because of these inherent limitations, an audit cannot provide, and the auditor cannot be expected to provide, absolute assurance that the financial statements are free from material misstatement; hence audit risk can never be reduced to zero.

*(Full-marks tip: full marks require linking "persuasive not conclusive" evidence to the three sources of inherent limitation; students commonly lose marks by writing that the auditor should have detected all frauds.)*

### Q4. Ch: Nature, Objective & Scope (SA 200) — Professional Skepticism (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** "The auditor shall plan and perform an audit with professional skepticism." Define professional skepticism as per SA 200 and state the circumstances in which the auditor is required to be particularly alert while maintaining professional skepticism.

**Answer:** SA 200 defines **professional skepticism** as an attitude that includes a **questioning mind**, being alert to conditions which may indicate possible misstatement due to error or fraud, and a **critical assessment of audit evidence**. Maintaining professional skepticism throughout the audit is necessary if the auditor is, for example, to reduce the risks of overlooking unusual circumstances, over-generalising when drawing conclusions from audit observations, and using inappropriate assumptions in determining the nature, timing and extent of the audit procedures and evaluating the results thereof.

Professional skepticism is necessary to the critical assessment of audit evidence. This includes being alert to, among other matters, the following:
(i) Audit evidence that **contradicts** other audit evidence obtained;
(ii) Information that brings into **question the reliability** of documents and responses to inquiries to be used as audit evidence;
(iii) Conditions that may indicate **possible fraud**; and
(iv) Circumstances that suggest the need for audit procedures **in addition to** those required by the SAs.

Maintaining professional skepticism throughout the audit also assists the auditor in reducing the risk of, for example, using inappropriate assumptions in determining the nature, timing and extent of the audit procedures. The auditor may accept records and documents as genuine unless he has reason to believe the contrary; nevertheless, he is required to consider the reliability of information to be used as audit evidence.

**Conclusion:** Professional skepticism — a questioning mind and critical assessment of evidence — must be maintained throughout the audit to obtain reasonable assurance.

*(Full-marks tip: state the exact definition (questioning mind + alert + critical assessment) and give the four "alert to" situations; a common deduction is treating skepticism as suspicion of dishonesty rather than an alert, questioning attitude.)*

### Q5. Ch: Nature, Objective & Scope (SA 200) — Scope of Audit and Independence (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** CA Nandini is appointed as the statutory auditor of Zephyr Ltd. During the audit, the Managing Director informs her that the scope of her audit is limited only to the profit and loss account, since the balance sheet has already been "reviewed internally," and further requests her not to communicate certain related party balances to those charged with governance as a matter of goodwill. Comment on the auditor's position with reference to SA 200 as regards the **scope of audit** and the **overall objectives of the auditor**.

**Answer:** As per SA 200, the scope of an audit of financial statements is determined by the auditor having regard to the requirements of the relevant legislation, the pronouncements of the Institute, and the terms of the audit engagement. However, the terms of engagement **cannot restrict the scope of an audit in relation to matters which are prescribed by legislation or by the pronouncements of the Institute**. The overall objectives of the auditor under SA 200 are:
(i) to obtain reasonable assurance about whether the financial statements **as a whole** are free from material misstatement, whether due to fraud or error, thereby enabling the auditor to express an opinion on whether the financial statements are prepared, in all material respects, in accordance with the applicable financial reporting framework; and
(ii) to report on the financial statements, and communicate as required by the SAs, in accordance with the auditor's findings.

**Analysis of facts:**
- The Managing Director's attempt to confine the audit to the profit and loss account only is a **limitation on scope** that is inconsistent with the statutory duty of the auditor under the Companies Act, 2013, to report on the financial statements as a whole (including the balance sheet). The auditor's opinion covers the financial statements taken as a whole; she cannot accept such a management-imposed restriction.
- The request not to communicate related party balances to those charged with governance conflicts with the auditor's duty to communicate relevant matters and to maintain **integrity, objectivity and professional skepticism**. Suppressing such communication as a "goodwill" gesture would compromise the fundamental ethical principles under SA 200.

**Conclusion:** CA Nandini should not accept the restriction; the scope of a statutory audit cannot be curtailed by management below what is prescribed by law and ICAI pronouncements, and she must fulfil her overall objectives of obtaining reasonable assurance on the financial statements as a whole and reporting/communicating accordingly. If the limitation persists, she should consider its effect on her report (e.g., qualification or disclaimer).

*(Full-marks tip: connect the answer to the exact SA 200 rule that engagement terms cannot override statutory scope, and to the two overall objectives; deductions occur if the student merely says "the MD is wrong" without the statutory basis.)*

### Q6. Ch: Nature, Objective & Scope (SA 200) — Preparation of Financial Statements: Premise of an Audit (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** An audit in accordance with SAs is conducted on the premise that management and, where appropriate, those charged with governance have acknowledged certain responsibilities that are fundamental to the conduct of the audit. State these responsibilities as per SA 200.

**Answer:** As per SA 200, an audit of financial statements is conducted on the **premise that management and, where appropriate, those charged with governance have acknowledged and understand that they have the following responsibilities** that are fundamental to the conduct of an audit in accordance with SAs. That is, responsibility:

**(i)** For the **preparation of the financial statements** in accordance with the applicable financial reporting framework, including where relevant their fair presentation;

**(ii)** For such **internal control** as management and, where appropriate, those charged with governance determine is necessary to enable the preparation of financial statements that are free from material misstatement, whether due to fraud or error; and

**(iii)** To **provide the auditor** with:
(a) **Access** to all information of which management and, where appropriate, those charged with governance are aware that is relevant to the preparation of the financial statements such as records, documentation and other matters;
(b) **Additional information** that the auditor may request from management and, where appropriate, those charged with governance for the purpose of the audit; and
(c) **Unrestricted access** to persons within the entity from whom the auditor determines it necessary to obtain audit evidence.

This premise is also referred to as the "premise, relating to the responsibilities of management and, where appropriate, those charged with governance, on which an audit is conducted."

**Conclusion:** The auditor's ability to conduct the audit depends upon management's acknowledgement of these three fundamental responsibilities, which together constitute the premise of the audit.

*(Full-marks tip: the three responsibilities — preparation of FS, internal control, and access/information — must all appear; students often forget the "unrestricted access to persons" limb.)*

### Q7. Ch: Nature, Objective & Scope (SA 200) — Compliance with SAs / Objective Not Achievable (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Discuss the requirements of SA 200 regarding **compliance with the Standards on Auditing** and the auditor's course of action where an individual objective stated in a relevant SA cannot be achieved.

**Answer:** As per SA 200, the auditor shall **comply with all SAs relevant to the audit**. An SA is relevant to the audit when the SA is in effect and the circumstances addressed by the SA exist. To achieve the overall objectives of the auditor, the auditor shall use the objectives stated in the relevant SAs in planning and performing the audit, having regard to the interrelationships among the SAs, to:
(i) determine whether any audit procedures **in addition** to those required by the SAs are necessary in pursuance of the objectives stated in the SAs; and
(ii) **evaluate** whether sufficient appropriate audit evidence has been obtained.

The auditor shall have an **understanding of the entire text of an SA**, including its application and other explanatory material, to understand its objectives and to apply its requirements properly. The auditor shall **comply with each requirement of an SA** unless, in the circumstances of the audit, (a) the entire SA is not relevant; or (b) the requirement is not relevant because it is conditional and the condition does not exist. In **exceptional circumstances**, the auditor may judge it necessary to **depart** from a relevant requirement in an SA; in such a case, the auditor shall perform **alternative audit procedures** to achieve the aim of that requirement, and shall **document** the reasons for the departure and how the alternative procedures achieve the aim.

**Objective not achievable:** If an objective in a relevant SA **cannot be achieved**, the auditor shall evaluate whether this prevents the auditor from achieving the overall objectives of the auditor and thereby requires the auditor, in accordance with the SAs, to **modify the auditor's opinion or withdraw** from the engagement (where withdrawal is possible under applicable law or regulation). Failure to achieve an objective represents a **significant matter** requiring documentation in accordance with SA 230.

**Conclusion:** The auditor must comply with each relevant SA requirement; departures are permitted only in exceptional circumstances with alternative procedures and documentation, and an unachievable objective may compel modification of opinion or withdrawal.

*(Full-marks tip: reward for both limbs — the "understand entire text / comply with each requirement" rule and the "objective not achievable → modify/withdraw + document as significant matter under SA 230" consequence.)*

### Q8. Ch: Audit Strategy & Planning (SA 300) — Establishing the Overall Audit Strategy (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** SA 300 requires the auditor to establish an overall audit strategy that sets the scope, timing and direction of the audit, and that guides the development of the audit plan. State the **matters the auditor shall consider** in establishing the overall audit strategy.

**Answer:** As per SA 300, "Planning an Audit of Financial Statements," the auditor shall establish an **overall audit strategy** that sets the scope, timing and direction of the audit, and that guides the development of the audit plan. In establishing the overall audit strategy, the auditor shall:

**(i) Identify the characteristics of the engagement that define its scope** — For example, the financial reporting framework used, industry-specific reporting requirements, the locations of the components of the entity, the nature of business segments, the reporting currency, the need for statutory audits of components, and the availability of the work of internal auditors and the extent of reliance on such work.

**(ii) Ascertain the reporting objectives of the engagement to plan the timing of the audit and the nature of the communications required** — For example, the entity's timetable for reporting (interim and final stages), the organisation of meetings with management and those charged with governance, discussions regarding the expected type and timing of reports, and the discussion with management regarding expected communications on the status of audit work.

**(iii) Consider the factors that, in the auditor's professional judgement, are significant in directing the engagement team's efforts** — For example, the determination of appropriate materiality levels, preliminary identification of areas where there may be higher risks of material misstatement, preliminary identification of material components and account balances, and evaluation of whether the auditor may plan to obtain evidence regarding the effectiveness of internal control.

**(iv) Consider the results of preliminary engagement activities** and, where applicable, whether knowledge gained on other engagements performed by the engagement partner for the entity is relevant — This includes activities under SA 220 (client acceptance/continuance) and SA 210 (agreeing the terms of engagement).

**(v) Ascertain the nature, timing and extent of resources necessary to perform the engagement** — For example, the selection of the engagement team (including where necessary the engagement quality control reviewer), assignment of audit work to team members including appropriately experienced members for high-risk areas, engagement budgeting, and the manner in which resources are managed, directed and supervised.

**Conclusion:** By addressing these matters, the overall audit strategy assists the auditor to determine the resources and their deployment, and provides the framework within which the detailed audit plan is developed.

*(Full-marks tip: examiners award marks for all five heads with an example under each; the "resources — nature, timing and extent" head is the one most often omitted.)*

### Q9. Ch: Audit Strategy & Planning (SA 300) — Changes to Planning Decisions / Modifications to Strategy and Plan (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** "Planning is not a discrete phase of an audit, but rather a continual and iterative process." In this context, explain, with reference to SA 300, how the auditor is required to **update and change the overall audit strategy and the audit plan** as necessary during the course of the audit.

**Answer:** As per SA 300, planning is **not a discrete phase** of an audit, but rather a **continual and iterative process** that often begins shortly after (or in connection with) the completion of the previous audit and continues until the completion of the current audit engagement. The auditor shall **update and change** the overall audit strategy and the audit plan **as necessary during the course of the audit**.

As a result of unexpected events, changes in conditions, or the audit evidence obtained from the results of audit procedures, the auditor may need to modify the overall audit strategy and audit plan and thereby the resulting planned nature, timing and extent of further audit procedures, based on the revised consideration of assessed risks. This may be the case when information comes to the auditor's attention that differs significantly from the information available when the auditor planned the audit procedures. For example, audit evidence obtained through the performance of substantive procedures may **contradict** the audit evidence obtained through tests of controls.

The following are illustrations of when planning decisions may be revised:
(i) **Discovery of a matter** during the audit that indicates a higher risk of material misstatement in a particular account balance or class of transactions than originally assessed — requiring the auditor to reassess risk and modify the planned procedures;
(ii) **New information** obtained about the entity and its environment (for example, a significant change in the business) that alters the auditor's earlier expectations; and
(iii) Where **audit evidence contradicts** other evidence or where controls are found not to be operating effectively, requiring a shift in the extent of substantive procedures.

Planning the nature, timing and extent of specific further audit procedures depends on the outcome of the risk assessment procedures. In addition, the auditor may begin the execution of further audit procedures for some classes of transactions, account balances and disclosures before planning all remaining further audit procedures.

**Conclusion:** Because planning is continual and iterative, the auditor must remain alert and revise the overall audit strategy and the audit plan whenever changed circumstances or contradictory audit evidence so require, documenting significant changes and the reasons therefor under SA 230.

*(Full-marks tip: link the answer to "iterative process," give at least one concrete trigger (e.g., contradictory evidence or higher assessed risk), and mention documentation of the change under SA 230.)*

### Q10. Ch: Audit Strategy & Planning (SA 300) — Relationship Between Overall Audit Strategy and Audit Plan (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between the **Overall Audit Strategy** and the **Audit Plan** as envisaged by SA 300, and explain the relationship between the two.

**Answer:** SA 300 requires the auditor to plan the audit so that it will be performed in an effective manner, and this involves establishing an overall audit strategy and developing an audit plan. Although distinct, the two are closely inter-related, as a change in one may result in a consequential change in the other.

| Basis | Overall Audit Strategy | Audit Plan |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Sets the **scope, timing and direction** of the audit and guides the development of the audit plan | Is **more detailed** than the overall strategy and includes the nature, timing and extent of audit procedures to be performed |
| Level | **Broader** framework | **Detailed** approach flowing from the strategy |
| Contents | Characteristics defining scope, reporting objectives/timing, significant factors directing effort, results of preliminary activities, and resources | (a) Nature, timing and extent of planned **risk assessment procedures** (SA 315); (b) Nature, timing and extent of planned **further audit procedures** at the assertion level (SA 330); and (c) **other planned audit procedures** required to comply with the SAs |
| Purpose | Helps the auditor determine the resources necessary and how they are to be deployed | Helps to carry out the audit strategy into specific procedures for members of the engagement team |
| Sequence | Established **first** | Developed **after** the strategy |

**Relationship:** Once the overall audit strategy has been established, an audit plan can be developed to address the various matters identified in the strategy, taking into account the need to achieve the audit objectives through the efficient use of the auditor's resources. The establishment of the overall audit strategy and the detailed audit plan are **not necessarily sequential or discrete processes**, but are closely inter-related since changes in one may result in consequential changes to the other.

**Conclusion:** The overall audit strategy provides the broad direction, while the audit plan converts that direction into specific procedures; both are interdependent and iterative.

*(Full-marks tip: present the distinction in a table AND state the three contents of the audit plan under SA 300 (risk assessment procedures, further audit procedures, other procedures); a common deduction is omitting the "further audit procedures at assertion level per SA 330" limb.)*

### Q11. Ch: Audit Strategy & Planning (SA 300) — Documentation of Planning (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the matters that the auditor is required to **include in the audit documentation** in respect of planning, as per SA 300.

**Answer:** As per SA 300, the auditor shall **document**:
**(a)** The **overall audit strategy**;
**(b)** The **audit plan**; and
**(c)** Any **significant changes** made during the audit engagement to the overall audit strategy or the audit plan, and the **reasons for such changes**.

The explanatory material to SA 300 clarifies the purpose of each:
(i) The documentation of the **overall audit strategy** is a record of the key decisions considered necessary to properly plan the audit and to communicate significant matters to the engagement team. For example, the auditor may summarise the overall audit strategy in the form of a memorandum that contains key decisions regarding the overall scope, timing and conduct of the audit.
(ii) The documentation of the **audit plan** is a record of the planned nature, timing and extent of risk assessment procedures and further audit procedures at the assertion level in response to the assessed risks. It also serves as a record of the proper planning of the audit procedures that can be reviewed and approved prior to their performance. The auditor may use standard audit programmes or audit completion checklists, tailored as needed to reflect the particular engagement circumstances.
(iii) The documentation of **significant changes** to the originally planned overall audit strategy and audit plan, and the resulting changes to the planned nature, timing and extent of audit procedures, explains **why** the significant changes were made and the overall strategy and audit plan finally adopted for the audit. It also reflects the appropriate response to the significant changes occurring during the audit.

**Conclusion:** Documentation of the strategy, the plan and significant changes (with reasons) evidences that the audit was properly planned in accordance with SA 300 and SA 230.

*(Full-marks tip: name all three documentation items; the significant-changes-with-reasons item is the most frequently missed.)*

### Q12. Ch: Audit Strategy & Planning (SA 300) — Benefits of Planning / Preliminary Engagement Activities (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** (a) State the **benefits of adequate planning** of an audit of financial statements as recognised in SA 300. (b) At the beginning of the current audit engagement, what **preliminary engagement activities** is the auditor required to undertake?

**Answer:** **(a) Benefits of adequate planning:** As per SA 300, adequate planning benefits the audit of financial statements in several ways, including the following. Planning helps the auditor to:
(i) devote **appropriate attention to important areas** of the audit;
(ii) **identify and resolve potential problems** on a timely basis;
(iii) **properly organise and manage** the audit engagement so that it is performed in an effective and efficient manner;
(iv) **select engagement team members** with appropriate levels of capabilities and competence to respond to anticipated risks, and the proper assignment of work to them;
(v) **facilitate the direction and supervision** of engagement team members and the review of their work; and
(vi) assist, where applicable, in the **coordination of work** done by auditors of components and experts.

**(b) Preliminary engagement activities:** The auditor shall undertake the following activities at the beginning of the current audit engagement:
(i) Performing procedures required by **SA 220, "Quality Control for an Audit of Financial Statements,"** regarding the **continuance of the client relationship** and the specific audit engagement;
(ii) Evaluating **compliance with relevant ethical requirements**, including independence, in accordance with SA 220; and
(iii) Establishing an **understanding of the terms of the engagement**, as required by **SA 210, "Agreeing the Terms of Audit Engagements."**

The purpose of performing these preliminary engagement activities is to help the auditor ensure that he has considered any events or circumstances that may adversely affect his ability to plan and perform the audit engagement, and to establish that he has maintained the necessary independence and ability to perform the engagement, and that there is no issue with management integrity that may affect the auditor's willingness to continue the engagement.

**Conclusion:** Adequate planning yields the six benefits above, and it is grounded in the three preliminary engagement activities relating to client continuance, ethics/independence, and the terms of engagement.

*(Full-marks tip: benefits should be given as complete sentences (not one word each); the three preliminary activities must be tied to SA 220 and SA 210 by reference.)*

### Q13. Ch: Materiality (SA 320) — Materiality and Performance Materiality; Determination for the Financial Statements as a Whole (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** With reference to SA 320, explain the concept of **materiality** in the context of an audit, define **performance materiality**, and describe how the auditor determines materiality **for the financial statements as a whole** when establishing the overall audit strategy.

**Answer:** As per SA 320, "Materiality in Planning and Performing an Audit," financial reporting frameworks often discuss the concept of materiality in the context of the preparation and presentation of financial statements. Although frameworks may discuss materiality in different terms, they generally explain that:
(i) **Misstatements**, including omissions, are considered to be **material** if they, individually or in the aggregate, could reasonably be expected to **influence the economic decisions of users** taken on the basis of the financial statements;
(ii) Judgements about materiality are made in the light of **surrounding circumstances**, and are affected by the **size or nature** of a misstatement, or a combination of both; and
(iii) Judgements about matters that are material to users of the financial statements are based on a consideration of the **common financial information needs of users as a group**. The possible effect of misstatements on specific individual users, whose needs may vary widely, is not considered.

**Performance materiality** means the amount or amounts set by the auditor at **less than materiality for the financial statements as a whole** to reduce to an appropriately low level the probability that the aggregate of uncorrected and undetected misstatements exceeds materiality for the financial statements as a whole. If applicable, performance materiality also refers to the amount or amounts set by the auditor at less than the materiality level or levels for particular classes of transactions, account balances or disclosures.

**Determination of materiality for the financial statements as a whole:** When establishing the overall audit strategy, the auditor shall **determine materiality for the financial statements as a whole**. Determining materiality involves the **exercise of professional judgement**. A percentage is often applied to a chosen **benchmark** as a starting point in determining materiality. Factors that may affect the identification of an appropriate benchmark include:
(i) the elements of the financial statements (e.g., assets, liabilities, equity, revenue, expenses);
(ii) whether there are items on which the attention of the users of the particular entity's financial statements tends to be focused (e.g., for the purpose of evaluating financial performance, users may tend to focus on profit, revenue or net assets);
(iii) the nature of the entity, where it is in its life cycle, and the industry and economic environment in which it operates;
(iv) the entity's ownership structure and the way it is financed; and
(v) the relative volatility of the benchmark.

Examples of benchmarks that may be appropriate include categories of reported income such as **profit before tax, total revenue, gross profit and total expenses, total equity or net asset value**. Where profit before tax from continuing operations is volatile, other benchmarks may be more appropriate.

**Conclusion:** Materiality is judged in relation to the influence on users' economic decisions; performance materiality is set below overall materiality to provide a margin for aggregation risk; and materiality for the financial statements as a whole is determined by applying professional judgement, typically a percentage of an appropriate benchmark.

*(Full-marks tip: full marks need the definition of both "materiality" and "performance materiality" plus the benchmark-and-professional-judgement basis; students often confuse performance materiality with overall materiality.)*

### Q14. Ch: Materiality (SA 320) — Materiality for Particular Classes of Transactions, Account Balances or Disclosures (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** SA 320 requires the auditor, in certain circumstances, to determine materiality level(s) for **particular classes of transactions, account balances or disclosures** at a level lower than materiality for the financial statements as a whole. Discuss the requirement and the factors that may indicate the existence of one or more such particular classes.

**Answer:** As per SA 320, if, in the specific circumstances of the entity, there is **one or more particular classes of transactions, account balances or disclosures** for which **misstatements of lesser amounts** than materiality for the financial statements as a whole could reasonably be expected to influence the economic decisions of users taken on the basis of the financial statements, the auditor shall **also determine the materiality level or levels** to be applied to those particular classes of transactions, account balances or disclosures.

Factors that may indicate the existence of one or more particular classes of transactions, account balances or disclosures for which misstatements of lesser amounts than materiality for the financial statements as a whole could reasonably be expected to influence the economic decisions of users include the following:
**(i)** Whether **law, regulation or the applicable financial reporting framework** affects users' expectations regarding the measurement or disclosure of certain items (for example, related party transactions, and the remuneration of management and those charged with governance).
**(ii)** The **key disclosures** in relation to the industry in which the entity operates (for example, research and development costs for a pharmaceutical company).
**(iii)** Whether attention is **focused on a particular aspect** of the entity's business that is separately disclosed in the financial statements (for example, a newly acquired business).

Where such particular classes exist, the auditor must apply the specifically determined (lower) materiality levels to them, in addition to the overall materiality applied to the financial statements as a whole, and must determine **performance materiality** for those classes as well.

**Conclusion:** Beyond overall materiality, the auditor determines separate, lower materiality levels for particular classes/balances/disclosures where smaller misstatements could influence users' decisions, guided by legal/framework expectations, key industry disclosures and specially-focused aspects of the business.

*(Full-marks tip: the three indicating factors with their illustrative examples (related-party/management remuneration, R&D for pharma, newly acquired business) are the mark-earning content; also note that performance materiality is set for these classes too.)*

### Q15. Ch: Materiality (SA 320) — Revision of Materiality as the Audit Progresses (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the circumstances in which the auditor is required to **revise materiality** for the financial statements as a whole (and, where applicable, the materiality level(s) for particular classes of transactions, account balances or disclosures) as the audit progresses, and the auditor's consequent responsibilities as per SA 320.

**Answer:** As per SA 320, the auditor shall **revise materiality** for the financial statements as a whole (and, if applicable, the materiality level or levels for particular classes of transactions, account balances or disclosures) in the event of becoming aware of **information during the audit that would have caused the auditor to have determined a different amount (or amounts) initially**.

Materiality for the financial statements as a whole (and, if applicable, materiality levels for particular classes of transactions, account balances or disclosures) may need to be **revised** as a result of:
(i) a **change in circumstances** that occurred during the audit (for example, a decision to dispose of a major part of the entity's business);
(ii) **new information**; or
(iii) a **change in the auditor's understanding of the entity and its operations** as a result of performing further audit procedures.

For example, if during the audit it appears as though **actual financial results are likely to be substantially different** from the anticipated period-end financial results that were used initially to determine materiality for the financial statements as a whole, the auditor revises that materiality.

**Consequential responsibility:** If the auditor concludes that a **lower materiality** for the financial statements as a whole (and, if applicable, materiality level or levels for particular classes) than that initially determined is appropriate, the auditor shall determine **whether it is necessary to revise performance materiality**, and whether the nature, timing and extent of the further audit procedures **remain appropriate**.

**Conclusion:** Materiality is not fixed; the auditor must revise it (and reassess performance materiality and the planned procedures) whenever new information, changed circumstances or a changed understanding indicates that a different amount is appropriate.

*(Full-marks tip: state the three triggers for revision and, crucially, the consequential duty to reconsider performance materiality and the appropriateness of further audit procedures — this second limb is where marks are lost.)*

### Q16. Ch: Materiality (SA 320) — Computation of Materiality Benchmarks (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** CA Ishaan, while planning the audit of Meridian Manufacturing Ltd. for the year ended 31st March, 2026, is determining materiality for the financial statements as a whole. The following data is extracted from the entity's draft financial statements:

| Particulars | Amount (₹ in lakhs) |
|---|---|
| Total Revenue | 8,000 |
| Profit Before Tax (PBT) | 500 |
| Total Assets | 6,000 |
| Total Expenses | 7,500 |

**WN-1: Materiality under each benchmark**

| Benchmark | Amount (₹ lakhs) | % applied | Materiality (₹ lakhs) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Profit Before Tax | 500 | 5% | 500 × 5% = **25.00** |
| Total Revenue | 8,000 | 1% | 8,000 × 1% = 80.00 |
| Total Assets | 6,000 | 2% | 6,000 × 2% = 120.00 |

**WN-2: Selected benchmark**

**WN-3: Performance materiality**
Performance materiality = 75% of overall materiality (set below overall materiality to allow for aggregation risk, per SA 320).
= ₹25.00 lakhs × 75% = **₹18.75 lakhs**

**WN-4: Clearly trivial threshold**
= 5% of overall materiality = ₹25.00 lakhs × 5% = **₹1.25 lakhs**
(Amounts below this are clearly trivial and, per SA 450, need not be accumulated.)

|---|---|---|

### Q17. Ch: Materiality (SA 320) — Documentation and Interrelationship with Audit Risk (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** (a) State the matters relating to materiality that the auditor is required to **include in the audit documentation** as per SA 320. (b) Explain the **relationship between materiality and audit risk** and how materiality is applied by the auditor in planning and performing the audit and in evaluating misstatements.

**Answer:** **(a) Documentation:** As per SA 320, the auditor shall **include in the audit documentation** the following amounts and the factors considered in their determination:
(i) **Materiality for the financial statements as a whole;**
(ii) If applicable, the **materiality level or levels for particular classes of transactions, account balances or disclosures;**
(iii) **Performance materiality;** and
(iv) Any **revision** of (i) – (iii) as the audit progressed.

**(b) Materiality and audit risk:** In conducting an audit of financial statements, the overall objectives of the auditor are to obtain reasonable assurance about whether the financial statements as a whole are free from material misstatement. The auditor obtains reasonable assurance by obtaining sufficient appropriate audit evidence to **reduce audit risk to an acceptably low level**. Materiality and audit risk are considered **throughout the audit**, in particular, when:
(i) **identifying and assessing** the risks of material misstatement (SA 315);
(ii) **determining the nature, timing and extent** of further audit procedures (SA 330); and
(iii) **evaluating the effect of uncorrected misstatements**, if any, on the financial statements and in forming the opinion in the auditor's report (SA 450).

There is an **inverse relationship** between materiality and the level of audit risk — the higher the materiality level, the lower the audit risk, and vice versa. The auditor takes this inverse relationship into account when determining the nature, timing and extent of audit procedures. For example, if, after planning specific audit procedures, the auditor determines that the acceptable materiality level is lower, audit risk is increased; the auditor compensates for this by either (a) reducing the assessed risk of material misstatement, where this is possible, and supporting the reduced level by carrying out extended or additional tests of control; or (b) reducing detection risk by modifying the nature, timing and extent of the planned substantive procedures.

The concept of materiality is applied by the auditor both in **planning and performing the audit**, and in **evaluating the effect** of identified misstatements on the audit and of uncorrected misstatements, if any, on the financial statements. Materiality determined when planning the audit does not necessarily establish an amount below which uncorrected misstatements, individually or in the aggregate, will always be evaluated as immaterial; the circumstances related to some misstatements may cause the auditor to evaluate them as material even if they are below materiality.

**Conclusion:** The auditor must document overall materiality, materiality for particular classes, performance materiality and any revisions; and must apply materiality — recognising its inverse relationship with audit risk — throughout risk assessment, designing procedures and evaluating misstatements.

*(Full-marks tip: reward for the four documentation items AND the inverse materiality-audit risk relationship with the compensating response (reduce RMM via more control tests, or reduce detection risk via more substantive procedures); the "circumstances may make a below-materiality misstatement material" point earns the final mark.)*

### Q18. Ch: Risk Assessment & Internal Control — Components of Internal Control (COSO) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** SA 315 (Revised) adopts the five inter-related components of internal control described in the COSO framework. Enumerate and briefly explain each of these five components as the auditor is required to understand them.

**Answer:** Internal control, as defined in SA 315 (Revised) "Identifying and Assessing the Risks of Material Misstatement through Understanding the Entity and Its Environment", is the process designed, implemented and maintained by those charged with governance, management and other personnel to provide reasonable assurance about the achievement of an entity's objectives with regard to reliability of financial reporting, effectiveness and efficiency of operations, and compliance with applicable laws and regulations. The Standard divides internal control into **five components**, and the auditor is required to obtain an understanding of each of them.

1. **Control Environment** — This is the foundation on which the other components rest. It includes the governance and management functions and the attitudes, awareness and actions of those charged with governance and management concerning the entity's internal control and its importance. The auditor evaluates whether management, under the oversight of those charged with governance, has created and maintained a culture of honesty and ethical behaviour, and whether the strengths in the control environment collectively provide an appropriate foundation for the other components.

2. **The Entity's Risk Assessment Process** — This is the entity's own process for identifying business risks relevant to financial reporting objectives, estimating their significance, assessing the likelihood of their occurrence and deciding upon actions to address and manage them. The auditor obtains an understanding of whether the entity has such a process and, if so, how management addresses risks arising from change (e.g., new products, new accounting standards, rapid growth).

3. **The Information System and Communication** relevant to financial reporting — This comprises the procedures and records established to initiate, record, process and report entity transactions and to maintain accountability for the related assets, liabilities and equity, together with how the entity communicates financial reporting roles, responsibilities and significant matters.

4. **Control Activities** — These are the policies and procedures that help ensure that management's directives are carried out; they include activities such as authorisation, performance reviews, information processing, physical controls and segregation of duties. The auditor obtains an understanding of those control activities relevant to the audit.

5. **Monitoring of Controls** — This is the process to assess the effectiveness of internal control performance over time. It includes assessing the design and operation of controls on a timely basis and taking necessary corrective actions, and it draws on internal audit and ongoing/separate evaluations.

| Component | Core question it answers |
|---|---|
| Control Environment | Is the "tone at the top" sound? |
| Risk Assessment Process | Does the entity identify and manage its own risks? |
| Information System & Communication | How are transactions captured, processed and reported? |
| Control Activities | What specific controls carry out management's directives? |
| Monitoring of Controls | Are the controls kept effective over time? |

**Conclusion:** The auditor must understand all five inter-related components because a deficiency in any one may undermine the reliability of the entire system and affect the assessment of the risks of material misstatement.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards naming all five components in the correct COSO sequence AND giving a distinguishing one-line explanation of each; a common deduction is confusing "control activities" with the whole of "internal control", or omitting "monitoring of controls".)*

### Q19. Ch: Risk Assessment & Internal Control — Inherent Limitations of Internal Control (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** "Internal control, however effective, can provide an entity with only reasonable assurance and not absolute assurance." In light of SA 315 (Revised), explain the inherent limitations of internal control.

**Answer:** SA 315 (Revised) recognises that internal control, no matter how well designed and operated, can provide an entity with only **reasonable assurance** about achieving the entity's financial reporting objectives, because of certain **inherent limitations** built into every system of internal control. These limitations are as follows:

1. **Human judgement in decision-making** can be faulty, and breakdowns in internal control can occur because of simple **human errors or mistakes** — for example, errors in the design of a control or in the manner in which a control operates.

2. **Collusion** — Controls, particularly those depending on segregation of duties, can be **circumvented by collusion** of two or more people, whether among employees or between employees and third parties.

3. **Management override** — The controls may be defeated by **management overriding the internal control**, e.g., by concluding side agreements not recorded in the books, or by directing staff to record fictitious entries.

4. **Cost-benefit consideration** — Management often makes judgements on the nature and extent of controls it chooses to implement and on the risks it chooses to assume; the **cost of a control should not exceed the benefit** expected to be derived from it, so certain risks are consciously not controlled.

5. **Controls tend to be directed at routine transactions** rather than non-routine transactions, so unusual or one-off transactions may escape the controls.

6. **Smaller entities** often have fewer employees, limiting the extent to which **segregation of duties** is practicable.

**Conclusion:** Because of these inherent limitations, the existence of a satisfactory system of internal control reduces, but does not eliminate, the probability of misstatement; the auditor therefore can never place complete reliance on internal control and must always perform some substantive procedures.

*(Full-marks tip: State expressly that limitations exist even in a well-designed system and give at least four distinct limitations — human error, collusion, management override and cost-benefit. Merely writing "controls can fail" without the named categories loses marks.)*

### Q20. Ch: Risk Assessment & Internal Control — Entity-Level vs Process-Level Controls (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between "entity-level controls" and "process-level (transaction-level) controls", and explain why the auditor's understanding of entity-level controls is significant.

**Answer:** In understanding the entity's system of internal control under SA 315 (Revised), controls are frequently classified by the level at which they operate — controls that operate across the whole entity ("entity-level controls") and controls that operate within a particular business process or transaction stream ("process-level" or "transaction-level" controls).

| Basis | Entity-Level Controls | Process-Level (Transaction-Level) Controls |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Controls that have a pervasive effect on the entity's internal control as a whole | Controls embedded within a specific business process or class of transactions |
| Examples | Controls in the control environment, the entity's risk assessment process, board/audit committee oversight, entity-wide policies, monitoring of controls, IT general controls | Authorisation of a purchase order, three-way match of invoice-GRN-PO, bank reconciliation, credit approval before dispatch |
| Nature | Often indirect and pervasive; set the "tone" and framework | Direct; address specific assertions for a class of transactions or balances |
| Precision | Usually less precise, operate at a higher level | More precise, aimed at preventing/detecting specific misstatements |
| Assertion linkage | Do not by themselves address a specific assertion | Directly linked to specific assertions (e.g., occurrence, completeness) |

The auditor's understanding of **entity-level controls is significant** because these controls influence the effectiveness of the process-level controls: a weak control environment or ineffective monitoring may undermine even well-designed process controls, whereas strong entity-level controls may reduce (though never eliminate) the likelihood of misstatement. They therefore affect the auditor's overall assessment of the risks of material misstatement at the financial statement level and the nature, timing and extent of further audit procedures.

**Conclusion:** Both levels must be understood; entity-level controls set the environment within which process-level controls either succeed or fail, and hence they shape the auditor's risk assessment.

*(Full-marks tip: A clean table plus the point that entity-level controls are pervasive and influence process controls earns full marks; do not merely list examples without stating the pervasive/direct distinction.)*

### Q21. Ch: Risk Assessment & Internal Control — Significant Deficiencies (SA 265) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Under SA 265 "Communicating Deficiencies in Internal Control to Those Charged with Governance and Management", what factors may the auditor consider in determining whether a deficiency, or a combination of deficiencies, constitutes a "significant deficiency" that must be communicated in writing to those charged with governance?

**Answer:** SA 265 requires the auditor to determine whether, on the basis of the audit work performed, one or more deficiencies in internal control have been identified, and if so, whether any of them, individually or in combination, constitute a **significant deficiency**. A "significant deficiency in internal control" is a deficiency or combination of deficiencies that, in the auditor's professional judgement, is of sufficient importance to merit the attention of those charged with governance. The significance of a deficiency depends not only on whether a misstatement has actually occurred but also on the **likelihood and potential magnitude** of misstatements that could occur. In making this judgement, the auditor may consider the following factors:

1. The **likelihood of the deficiencies leading to material misstatements** in the financial statements in the future.
2. The **susceptibility to loss or fraud** of the related asset or liability.
3. The **subjectivity and complexity** of determining estimated amounts, such as fair value accounting estimates.
4. The **financial statement amounts** exposed to the deficiencies.
5. The **volume of activity** that has occurred or could occur in the account balance or class of transactions exposed to the deficiency.
6. The **importance of the controls** to the financial reporting process, e.g., general monitoring controls and controls over the prevention and detection of fraud, controls over the selection and application of significant accounting policies, and controls over significant transactions with related parties.
7. The **cause and frequency** of the exceptions detected as a result of the deficiencies.
8. The **interaction of the deficiency with other deficiencies** in internal control.

Indicators of significant deficiencies also include evidence of ineffective aspects of the control environment, an ineffective entity risk assessment process, misstatements detected by the auditor's procedures that were not prevented or detected by internal control, and restatement of previously issued financial statements to reflect a material misstatement.

**Conclusion:** Where the auditor concludes a deficiency is a significant deficiency, SA 265 requires it to be communicated in **writing** to those charged with governance on a timely basis; other (non-significant) deficiencies of sufficient importance are communicated to management.

*(Full-marks tip: Distinguish clearly that significant deficiencies go in writing to TCWG, while less-serious deficiencies of sufficient importance go to management; list at least five determining factors. Omitting the "written communication to TCWG" point is a common deduction.)*

### Q22. Ch: Risk Assessment & Internal Control — Responses to Assessed Risks (SA 330) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** CA Vandana is auditing Meridian Textiles Ltd. for the year ended 31 March 2026. During risk assessment she notes the following situations. For EACH, state, with reasons under SA 330 "The Auditor's Responses to Assessed Risks", the appropriate audit response.

| # | Situation |
|---|---|
| (i) | Controls over the revenue cycle are found, on testing, to be operating effectively and the risk of material misstatement in "occurrence of sales" is assessed as low. |
| (ii) | The entity's payroll is fully automated and unchanged from the prior year; controls tested last year were effective. |
| (iii) | A significant risk of management override is identified in respect of journal entries posted at period-end. |
| (iv) | Inventory is held at 40 scattered locations and the entity has weak controls over stock records. |

**Answer:** SA 330 requires the auditor to design and implement overall responses to address the assessed risks of material misstatement at the financial statement level, and to design and perform further audit procedures whose nature, timing and extent are **responsive to the assessed risks of material misstatement at the assertion level**. The higher the auditor's assessment of risk, the more persuasive the audit evidence required. Applying this to each situation:

**(i) Effective revenue controls, low assessed risk:** Because the controls have been tested and found to operate effectively, the auditor may adopt a combined approach — reliance on tests of controls supported by a **reduced extent of substantive procedures** for the "occurrence" assertion. However, SA 330 requires that **irrespective of the assessed risk, substantive procedures must be performed for each material class of transactions, account balance and disclosure**; controls testing alone is never sufficient.

**(ii) Automated, unchanged payroll:** Where controls have been tested in a prior audit, SA 330 permits the auditor to rely on that evidence only after obtaining audit evidence about whether **changes** have occurred since. Being automated, the control is likely to operate consistently unless the program is changed; the auditor may test the operating effectiveness at least **once in every third audit**, provided general IT controls over program changes are effective and there is no significant risk — and must test some controls each period rather than all in the same period. She should confirm no changes to the program and test relevant general IT controls.

**(iii) Significant risk of management override of journal entries:** For a significant risk, SA 330 requires the auditor to perform **substantive procedures specifically responsive to that risk**, and where the approach includes only substantive procedures (as is mandatory for management override under SA 240), those procedures shall include tests of details. The auditor should test the appropriateness of journal entries and other adjustments made at period-end and review estimates for bias. Reliance on controls alone is not appropriate for a significant risk.

**(iv) Weak inventory controls, 40 locations:** As controls over stock records are weak, a controls-reliance approach is not available; the auditor must adopt a **wholly substantive approach** with increased extent — attend physical counts at a sample of locations selected on the basis of materiality and risk, perform roll-forward/roll-back procedures, and increase the extent of testing to obtain sufficient appropriate evidence over "existence" and "completeness".

**Conclusion:** The response is calibrated to the assessed risk — reliance where controls are effective (always with some substantive work), triennial rotation for stable automated controls, mandatory tests of details for significant/override risks, and a substantive-only, extended approach where controls are weak.

*(Full-marks tip: The mark-winning point in (i) is that substantive procedures are mandatory for every material item regardless of control effectiveness; in (iii) that a significant risk always requires tests of details. Losing these two nuances is the common deduction.)*

### Q23. Ch: Risk Assessment & Internal Control — Risk Assessment Procedures (SA 315) (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the "risk assessment procedures" the auditor is required to perform under SA 315 (Revised) to obtain an understanding of the entity and its environment, including its internal control.

**Answer:** SA 315 (Revised) requires the auditor to perform **risk assessment procedures** to provide a basis for the identification and assessment of the risks of material misstatement at the financial statement and assertion levels. These procedures, by themselves, do not provide sufficient appropriate audit evidence on which to base the audit opinion; they are the foundation for designing further audit procedures. The risk assessment procedures shall include the following:

1. **Inquiries of management** and of others within the entity who, in the auditor's judgement, may have information likely to assist in identifying risks of material misstatement due to fraud or error — for example, those charged with governance, internal audit personnel, employees involved in initiating, processing or recording complex or unusual transactions, in-house legal counsel, and marketing or sales personnel.

2. **Analytical procedures** performed as risk assessment procedures, which may help identify the existence of unusual transactions or events, and amounts, ratios and trends that indicate matters with audit implications, including risks of material misstatement.

3. **Observation and inspection** — these procedures may support inquiries of management and others and provide information about the entity and its environment; examples include observation of entity operations, inspection of documents and records, and inspection of the entity's premises and plant facilities.

Additionally, where the auditor intends to use information obtained from the auditor's **previous experience** with the entity and from procedures performed in previous audits, the auditor shall determine whether changes have occurred that may affect its relevance. The engagement partner and other key members shall also **discuss** the susceptibility of the entity's financial statements to material misstatement.

**Conclusion:** Inquiry, analytical procedures, and observation and inspection together constitute the mandatory risk assessment procedures, but must be supplemented by further audit procedures to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence.

### Q24. Ch: Audit Evidence — Relevance and Reliability Hierarchy (SA 500) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** SA 500 "Audit Evidence" states that the reliability of audit evidence is influenced by its source and nature. Explain the generalisations the auditor may apply about the reliability of audit evidence, and distinguish "relevance" from "reliability".

**Answer:** Under SA 500, the auditor's objective is to obtain **sufficient appropriate audit evidence** to be able to draw reasonable conclusions on which to base the audit opinion. "Sufficiency" is the measure of the **quantity** of audit evidence, while "appropriateness" is the measure of its **quality** — that is, its **relevance** and its **reliability** in providing support for the conclusions on which the auditor's opinion is based.

**Relevance** deals with the logical connection with, or bearing upon, the purpose of the audit procedure and, where appropriate, the **assertion** under consideration. For example, a procedure to test the existence of debtors (confirmation) is relevant to the existence assertion but not to their valuation.

**Reliability** is influenced by the source and nature of the evidence, and by the circumstances under which it is obtained, including the controls over its preparation and maintenance. SA 500 offers the following useful generalisations, subject to important exceptions:

1. Audit evidence is more reliable when it is obtained from **independent sources outside the entity**.
2. Audit evidence generated **internally is more reliable when the related controls** imposed by the entity are effective.
3. Audit evidence obtained **directly by the auditor** (e.g., observation of the application of a control) is more reliable than evidence obtained indirectly or by inference (e.g., inquiry about the application of a control).
4. Audit evidence in **documentary form**, whether paper, electronic or other medium, is more reliable than evidence obtained orally.
5. Audit evidence provided by **original documents** is more reliable than evidence provided by photocopies or facsimiles, or documents that have been filmed, digitised or otherwise transformed.

| Attribute | Concerns | Answers |
|---|---|---|
| Relevance | Quality — connection to the assertion/purpose | Does this evidence bear on the assertion I am testing? |
| Reliability | Quality — trustworthiness of the evidence | Can I trust this evidence given its source and nature? |
| Sufficiency | Quantity | Do I have enough of it? |

**Conclusion:** The auditor evaluates both relevance and reliability of evidence; more reliable evidence and evidence directly relevant to the assertion reduce the quantity of evidence needed, but higher quality cannot fully compensate for a deficiency in quantity.

*(Full-marks tip: State all five reliability generalisations and clearly separate relevance (bearing on assertion) from reliability (trustworthiness). Noting that quality cannot fully compensate for poor quantity is a frequently-rewarded point.)*

### Q25. Ch: Audit Evidence — Using the Work of Management's Expert (SA 500) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** In the audit of Kaveri Infra Ltd. for FY 2025-26, management has valued its investment property using an independent valuer engaged by the company (a "management's expert"). The auditor intends to use this valuation report as audit evidence. Explain, under SA 500, the matters the auditor must evaluate before relying on the work of a management's expert.

**Answer:** Under SA 500 "Audit Evidence", where information to be used as audit evidence has been prepared using the work of a **management's expert** (an individual or organisation possessing expertise in a field other than accounting or auditing, whose work in that field is used by the entity to assist in preparing the financial statements), the auditor shall, to the extent necessary having regard to the significance of that expert's work for the auditor's purposes, do the following three things:

1. **Evaluate the competence, capabilities and objectivity of the management's expert.** The auditor considers the expert's qualifications, membership of a professional body, licence to practise, experience and reputation in the field. Regarding objectivity, the auditor considers threats such as the expert's relationship with the entity — for example, whether the valuer is financially or otherwise dependent on, or related to, Kaveri Infra Ltd. Information about competence, capabilities and objectivity may come from personal experience, discussions, published papers, or the expert's quality control policies.

2. **Obtain an understanding of the work of that management's expert.** This includes understanding the field of expertise (property valuation), whether relevant professional or industry standards (e.g., recognised valuation standards) apply, the assumptions and methods used and whether they are generally accepted and appropriate for financial reporting, the nature of the internal and external data used, and the appropriateness of that work as audit evidence for the relevant assertion (valuation of investment property).

3. **Evaluate the appropriateness of that expert's work as audit evidence** for the relevant assertion. This includes evaluating the relevance and reasonableness of the expert's findings or conclusions and their consistency with other audit evidence, the relevance and reasonableness of assumptions and methods used, and, where the expert's work involves use of source data significant to that work, the relevance, completeness and accuracy of that source data.

If the auditor concludes that the work of the management's expert is **not adequate** for the auditor's purposes, SA 500 requires the auditor to agree with management on the nature and extent of further work to be performed by the expert, or to perform **further audit procedures appropriate to the circumstances**, which may include the auditor engaging his own **auditor's expert** (dealt with in SA 620).

**Conclusion:** CA can rely on the valuer's report only after evaluating the valuer's competence, capabilities and objectivity, understanding the valuation work and assumptions, and evaluating whether the report is appropriate audit evidence for the valuation assertion; otherwise further procedures are required.

### Q26. Ch: Audit Evidence — Litigation and Claims (SA 501) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** During the audit of Sterling Pharma Ltd. for FY 2025-26, the auditor learns that the company is a defendant in a product-liability suit and in a tax dispute, both of which could materially affect the financial statements. Describe the audit procedures the auditor should perform in respect of litigation and claims, and the auditor's course of action if management refuses permission to communicate with the entity's external legal counsel, as required by SA 501.

**Answer:** SA 501 "Audit Evidence — Specific Considerations for Selected Items" requires the auditor to design and perform audit procedures in order to **identify litigation and claims** involving the entity which may give rise to a risk of material misstatement, including:

1. **Inquiry of management** and, where applicable, others within the entity, including in-house legal counsel.
2. **Reviewing minutes** of meetings of those charged with governance and correspondence between the entity and its external legal counsel.
3. **Reviewing legal expense accounts** in the books.

A letter of general inquiry requests the counsel to inform the auditor of any litigation and claims that the counsel is aware of, together with an assessment of the outcome and an estimate of the financial implications, including costs. **Conclusion:** The auditor must inquire, review minutes and legal expenses, and where risk exists communicate directly with external legal counsel via a letter of inquiry; a management-imposed scope limitation on such communication that cannot be overcome by alternative procedures leads to a modified opinion under SA 705.

### Q30. Ch: Audit Evidence — Subsequent Events (SA 560) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** The financial statements of Halcyon Steel Ltd. for the year ended 31 March 2026 are approved by the Board on 20 May 2026 and the auditor's report is dated the same day. Analyse the auditor's responsibilities under SA 560 "Subsequent Events" in the following independent situations.

| # | Event |
|---|---|

| (ii) | On 5 June 2026 (after the report was signed but before the financial statements were issued), a fire destroyed a factory; the auditor becomes aware of it. |

"Subsequent events" are events occurring between the date of the financial statements and the date of the auditor's report, and facts that become known to the auditor after the date of the auditor's report. AS 4 classifies these into **adjusting** events (providing evidence of conditions existing at the balance sheet date) and **non-adjusting** events.

**(i) Customer insolvency on 10 May 2026 (before report date):** This event occurs between the date of the financial statements (31 March 2026) and the date of the auditor's report (20 May 2026). If management fails to adjust, the auditor considers modification of the opinion.

**(ii) Fire on 5 June 2026 (after report date, before issue):** The auditor **does not have an obligation to perform any audit procedures** regarding the financial statements after the date of the auditor's report. If management amends and discloses, the auditor extends audit procedures to the date of the new report and **dates the report as of the new date** (or dual-dates). If management does not amend where amendment is required, and the report has not yet been provided to the entity, the auditor shall modify the opinion.

**Conclusion:** Situation (i) requires adjustment (adjusting event); (ii) requires disclosure and possible re-dating of the report (non-adjusting but material); (iii) requires the auditor to press for revision and, failing that, to prevent reliance on the report.

### Q32. Ch: Audit Evidence — Written Representations (SA 580) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain, under SA 580 "Written Representations", (a) the purpose and status of written representations as audit evidence, and (b) the auditor's course of action where management does not provide one or more of the requested written representations, or where the auditor has doubts about management's integrity.

**Answer:** SA 580 "Written Representations" deals with the auditor's responsibility to obtain written representations from management and, where appropriate, those charged with governance. A **written representation** is a written statement by management provided to the auditor to confirm certain matters or to support other audit evidence.

**(a) Purpose and status:** Written representations are **necessary information** that the auditor requires in connection with the audit of the entity's financial statements; accordingly, similar to responses to inquiries, written representations are **audit evidence**. However, although written representations provide necessary audit evidence, they **do not provide sufficient appropriate audit evidence on their own** about any of the matters with which they deal, and the fact that management has provided reliable written representations does not affect the nature or extent of other audit evidence that the auditor obtains about the fulfilment of management's responsibilities or about specific assertions. The auditor is required to request management to provide a written representation that it has **fulfilled its responsibility for the preparation of the financial statements** and for the completeness of information provided to the auditor, and other written representations required by other SAs. The date of the written representations shall be **as near as practicable to, but not after, the date of the auditor's report**, and shall cover all financial statements and periods referred to in the auditor's report.

**(b) Course of action on doubts / non-provision:**

- **Doubt about reliability of representations:** If the auditor has concerns about the competence, integrity, ethical values or diligence of management, or about its commitment to or enforcement of these, the auditor shall **determine the effect that such concerns may have on the reliability of representations (oral or written) and audit evidence in general**. In particular, if written representations are **inconsistent with other audit evidence**, the auditor shall perform audit procedures to attempt to resolve the matter; if the matter remains unresolved, the auditor shall reconsider the assessment of the competence, integrity, ethical values or diligence of management and determine the effect on the audit.

**Conclusion:** Written representations are audit evidence but never a substitute for other evidence; where they are unreliable or the fundamental representations about management's responsibilities are withheld, the auditor disclaims the opinion under SA 705.

### Q34. Ch: Audit Evidence — Sufficiency and Appropriateness; Tests of Controls Timing (SA 500 / SA 330) (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** (a) Explain the inter-relationship between the "sufficiency" and "appropriateness" of audit evidence and how the assessed risk of material misstatement affects the quantity of evidence. (b) Under SA 330, what must the auditor consider before relying on audit evidence about the operating effectiveness of controls obtained in **previous audits**?

**Answer:** **(a) Sufficiency and appropriateness:** Under SA 500, "**sufficiency**" is the measure of the **quantity** of audit evidence, and "**appropriateness**" is the measure of its **quality** — that is, its relevance and reliability. The two are inter-related: the quantity of audit evidence needed is affected by the auditor's **assessment of the risks of material misstatement** (the **higher** the assessed risk, the **more** audit evidence is likely to be required) and also by the **quality** of the audit evidence (the **higher** the quality, the **less** may be required). However, merely obtaining more audit evidence may not compensate for its poor quality — that is, a large quantity of unreliable or irrelevant evidence cannot cure a deficiency in appropriateness. Sufficiency and appropriateness are thus inter-related; the auditor must obtain evidence that is both sufficient in quantity and appropriate in quality to reduce audit risk to an acceptably low level and be able to draw reasonable conclusions on which to base the opinion. Whether sufficient appropriate audit evidence has been obtained is ultimately a matter of **professional judgement**.

**(b) Reliance on controls evidence from previous audits (SA 330):** If the auditor plans to use audit evidence about the operating effectiveness of controls obtained in previous audits, the auditor shall establish the **continuing relevance** of that evidence by obtaining audit evidence about whether **significant changes** in those controls have occurred subsequent to the previous audit. The auditor obtains this evidence by inquiry combined with observation or inspection. Then:

1. **If there have been changes** that affect the continuing relevance of the audit evidence from the previous audit, the auditor shall **test the controls in the current audit**.
2. **If there have not been such changes**, the auditor shall test the controls at least **once in every third audit** (i.e., may rely for two intervening periods), and shall test **some controls each audit** to avoid the possibility of testing all the controls on which the auditor relies in a single audit period with no testing in the subsequent two audit periods.
3. Regardless of the above, if the auditor plans to rely on controls over a **significant risk**, the auditor shall **test those controls in the current period** — reliance on prior-audit evidence is not permitted for controls that mitigate a significant risk.

The auditor also considers, in deciding whether it is appropriate to use audit evidence from previous audits, the effectiveness of other elements of internal control (control environment, monitoring, the entity's risk assessment process), the risks arising from the characteristics of the control (whether manual or automated), the effectiveness of general IT controls, the effectiveness of the control and its application, and whether the lack of a change in a particular control poses a risk due to changing circumstances.

**Conclusion:** Sufficiency (quantity) and appropriateness (quality) together determine whether the auditor has enough evidence — higher risk demands more, higher quality permits less, but quantity cannot substitute for quality; and prior-audit control evidence may be relied upon subject to a maximum three-year re-testing cycle, with mandatory current-year testing for changed controls and controls over significant risks.

*(Full-marks tip: Two precise numbers win marks in part (b): re-test at least once every third audit, and always test controls over a significant risk in the current period. In part (a), state that quantity cannot compensate for poor quality — a point examiners specifically look for.)*

### Q35. Ch: Audit Sampling & Analytical Procedures — Sampling Risk vs Non-Sampling Risk (SA 530) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** SA 530 "Audit Sampling" requires the auditor to consider two distinct components of the risk that his conclusion is wrong. Explain **sampling risk** and **non-sampling risk**, bring out the difference between them, and state how each can be reduced.

**Answer:**
Audit sampling is the application of audit procedures to less than 100% of the items within a population of audit relevance such that all sampling units have a chance of selection, in order to draw a conclusion about the entire population (SA 530, para 5(a)). Because the whole population is not examined, the auditor's conclusion is exposed to two kinds of risk.

**Sampling risk** is the risk that the auditor's conclusion based on a sample may be different from the conclusion that would have been reached had the entire population been subjected to the same audit procedure (SA 530, para 5(c)). It arises purely because a sample, and not the whole population, was tested. Sampling risk can lead to two types of erroneous conclusions:
- **Risk of incorrect acceptance / risk that controls are more effective than they actually are** — this affects **audit effectiveness** and is more likely to lead to an inappropriate audit opinion; and
- **Risk of incorrect rejection / risk that controls are less effective than they actually are** — this affects **audit efficiency**, as it usually leads to additional, ultimately unnecessary, work.

**Non-sampling risk** is the risk that the auditor reaches an erroneous conclusion **for any reason not related to sampling risk** (SA 530, para 5(d)). Examples are the use of inappropriate audit procedures, misinterpretation of audit evidence, or failure to recognise a misstatement or deviation that is in fact present in an item examined.

| Basis | Sampling risk | Non-sampling risk |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | Testing only a part (sample) of the population | Human/judgement factors unrelated to sample size |
| Present even if 100% tested? | No — eliminated by examining the whole population | Yes — can exist even in a 100% examination |
| How reduced | Increasing the sample size; using an appropriate selection method | Proper planning, direction, supervision and review; appropriate procedures |
| Measurable? | Can be measured/controlled (especially in statistical sampling) | Cannot be measured statistically |

**Conclusion:** Sampling risk is reduced by increasing sample size and selecting a representative sample, whereas non-sampling risk is reduced not by sample size but by adequate planning, supervision, review and correct application of procedures; the auditor must guard against both to keep overall detection risk acceptably low.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards clearly stating that non-sampling risk survives even a 100% check whereas sampling risk does not, and naming the two effectiveness/efficiency limbs of sampling risk. Common deduction: writing that "larger sample reduces both risks" — larger sample does NOT reduce non-sampling risk.)*

### Q36. Ch: Audit Sampling & Analytical Procedures — Methods of Selecting the Sample (SA 530) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** SA 530 permits the auditor to select sample items by several principal methods. Briefly explain the **methods of sample selection** available to the auditor, indicating for each whether it is appropriate for statistical or non-statistical sampling.

**Answer:**
Under SA 530, para A13 and Appendix 4, the auditor must select items in such a way that each sampling unit in the population has a chance of selection. The principal methods are:

**1. Random selection.** Items are selected so that every sampling unit has an equal probability of being chosen, applied through random number generators or random number tables. It is appropriate for **statistical** sampling because selection is objective and probability-based.

**2. Systematic selection.** The number of sampling units in the population is divided by the sample size to give a **sampling interval** (say, every 50th item), and after a random start each subsequent item at that interval is selected. The auditor must ensure the population is not structured so that the interval coincides with a pattern. It can be used for both statistical and non-statistical sampling.

**3. Monetary Unit Sampling (value-weighted selection).** The sampling unit is the individual monetary unit (e.g., a rupee); selection is thus biased towards **higher-value items**, so items of larger amounts have a greater chance of selection. It is a value-weighted statistical approach useful for testing for overstatement.

**4. Haphazard selection.** The auditor selects the sample without following a structured technique, but avoiding any conscious bias or predictability (e.g., not deliberately picking easy or unusual items). It is **not appropriate when using statistical sampling**, since it is not based on probability.

**5. Block selection.** Selection of a contiguous block of items (e.g., all vouchers of a particular week). Because items in a block tend to have similar characteristics and differ from the rest of the population, block selection is **ordinarily not an appropriate** means of sample selection; it may be used only rarely, supported by extra care.

| Method | Statistical? | Key feature |
|---|---|---|
| Random | Yes | Equal chance to all units |
| Systematic | Yes / No | Fixed interval after random start |
| Monetary Unit (value-weighted) | Yes | Larger amounts more likely selected |
| Haphazard | No | Unstructured but unbiased |
| Block | Generally avoid | Contiguous items; similar characteristics |

**Conclusion:** Random, systematic and monetary-unit selection support statistical evaluation; haphazard selection suits only non-statistical sampling; and block selection should ordinarily be avoided as it is unlikely to be representative.

*(Full-marks tip: State clearly that haphazard is NOT the same as random and cannot be used for statistical sampling, and that block selection is generally inappropriate. Losing the value-weighted nature of MUS costs a mark.)*

### Q37. Ch: Audit Sampling & Analytical Procedures — Stratification & Value-Weighted Selection (SA 530) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** CA. Meera is auditing the trade receivables of Vardhan Ltd. The ledger contains 4,000 customer accounts with a total book value of ₹24 crore. About 30 accounts, aggregating ₹9 crore, are individually large; the remaining 3,970 accounts (₹15 crore) are small and numerous. Meera wants to design an efficient substantive sampling plan. Explain (i) what **stratification** is and why it helps here, and (ii) how **value-weighted (monetary-unit) selection** would improve audit efficiency when her objective is to detect **overstatement** of receivables.

**Answer:**
**(i) Stratification (SA 530, para A8–A9).** Stratification is the process of dividing a population into sub-populations, each of which is a group of sampling units having **similar characteristics** (often monetary value). The objective is to reduce the variability of items within each stratum, which reduces sample size for a given level of sampling risk without increasing overall sampling risk. The results of a procedure applied to a sample of items **within a stratum can be projected only to the items that make up that stratum**; to conclude on the whole population, the auditor considers the risk of material misstatement in relation to whatever other strata make up the entire population.

Applying this to Vardhan Ltd.:
- The 30 large accounts (₹9 crore) are **individually significant** and can be examined **100%** — this is not sampling at all but a complete examination of a stratum, which removes sampling risk for the ₹9 crore that carries the greatest monetary exposure.
- The 3,970 small accounts (₹15 crore) form a homogeneous low-value stratum from which a **sample** can be drawn; because these items are similar, a modest sample gives a reliable conclusion for that stratum.

This concentrates audit effort on the value that matters most and makes the audit both efficient and effective.

**(ii) Value-weighted (monetary-unit) selection (SA 530, para A10, A54).** When the objective is to detect **overstatement**, the auditor can identify the sampling unit as the **individual monetary unit** in the population rather than each customer account. The auditor then selects specific rupees, and examines the particular account (the physical item) that contains each selected rupee. This approach automatically gives **larger balances a proportionately higher chance of selection**, because a large balance contains more monetary units. As a result:
- Audit effort is directed at the balances that could contain the largest overstatements;
- Sample size can be reduced; and
- When no misstatement is found, the conclusion is expressed directly in monetary terms.

A limitation is that value-weighted selection is inefficient for detecting **understatement** or when there are **zero or negative (credit) balances**, which need separate consideration.

**Conclusion:** Meera should examine the 30 large accounts (₹9 crore) in full as a separate stratum and apply value-weighted (monetary-unit) sampling to the ₹15 crore small-account stratum, focusing effort where the risk of overstatement in rupee terms is greatest.

*(Full-marks tip: State explicitly that a sample result within a stratum can only be projected to that stratum, and that value-weighting targets overstatement, not understatement. Merely defining stratification without applying it to the two strata loses application marks.)*

**Answer:** Total projected misstatement = **₹1,44,000**. ### Q39. Ch: Audit Sampling & Analytical Procedures — Evaluating Deviations in a Test of Controls (SA 530) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** While performing a test of controls over purchase authorisation, CA. Rohan planned a sample of 60 invoices with an expected deviation rate of 2% and a tolerable deviation rate of 6%. On testing, he found **5 deviations**, of which **1** was traced to a one-off breakdown of the approval software on a single day, since rectified. Explain how Rohan should evaluate these results, including whether the 1 deviation can be treated as an **anomaly**, and what conclusion he should reach.

**Answer:**
In a test of controls, the sample deviation rate is the auditor's best estimate of the deviation rate in the whole population (SA 530, para 14). The auditor evaluates the results by projecting the deviation rate and comparing it with the **tolerable deviation rate**.

**Sample deviation rate** = 5 deviations ÷ 60 items = **8.33%**, which is **higher than** both the expected rate (2%) and, more importantly, the tolerable deviation rate (6%).

**Can the 1 deviation be treated as an anomaly?** An **anomaly** is a misstatement or deviation that is demonstrably **not representative** of misstatements or deviations in the population (SA 530, para 5(e)). SA 530, para 13, permits treating a deviation as an anomaly only in the **extremely rare** circumstance where the auditor has obtained a **high degree of certainty** that it is not representative — for example, by establishing its specific cause and confirming that it does not affect the rest of the population. Here the single deviation arose from a **one-off software breakdown confined to a single day**, which has been established and rectified. If Rohan documents this cause and satisfies himself that no other transactions on other days were affected, that 1 deviation may be excluded as an anomaly.

**Re-evaluation after removing the anomaly:** Even after excluding 1 anomalous deviation, **4 deviations remain** out of 60, giving a deviation rate of **6.67%**, which **still exceeds** the tolerable deviation rate of 6%.

**Conclusion and action:** Because the deviation rate exceeds the tolerable rate even after treating one item as an anomaly, Rohan **cannot rely on the control as planned**. Under SA 530, para 15–16, he should: (i) request management to investigate the deviations and their cause; (ii) **not reduce** the planned substantive procedures as originally intended (i.e., reassess control risk upward); and (iii) consider whether the assessed risk of material misstatement and the nature, timing and extent of further substantive procedures need to be modified.

*(Full-marks tip: The trap is calling any convenient error an "anomaly". State the "extremely rare / high degree of certainty" bar, and note that even after the anomaly the residual rate still breaches tolerable, so control cannot be relied upon.)*

### Q41. Ch: Audit Sampling & Analytical Procedures — Suitability & Precision of Substantive Analytical Procedures (SA 520) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Before deciding to use substantive analytical procedures for a particular assertion, SA 520 requires the auditor to make certain determinations. Explain the matters the auditor must evaluate under SA 520, para 5, in determining the **suitability** of a substantive analytical procedure and in developing an **expectation** with sufficient **precision**.

**Answer:**
Under SA 520 "Analytical Procedures", para 5, when designing and performing substantive analytical procedures, whether alone or in combination with tests of details, the auditor shall:

**(a) Determine the suitability of the particular substantive analytical procedure for given assertions.** Substantive analytical procedures are generally **more suitable for large volumes of transactions that tend to be predictable over time** (e.g., interest, payroll, rent). They are **less suitable** where the relationship is not predictable or where there is a high risk of management override. The auditor also takes into account the assessed risk of material misstatement and any tests of details for the same assertion.

**(b) Evaluate the reliability of the data** from which the auditor's expectation is developed, having regard to the source, comparability, nature and relevance of the information available, and the **controls over its preparation** that are designed to ensure its completeness, accuracy and validity. Data produced by a well-controlled system is more reliable than data outside the entity's control environment.

**(c) Develop an expectation of recorded amounts or ratios and evaluate whether the expectation is sufficiently precise** to identify a misstatement that, individually or when aggregated with others, may cause the financial statements to be materially misstated. The precision of the expectation depends on the accuracy with which the expected result can be predicted, the **degree to which information can be disaggregated** (e.g., by location, product line or month rather than in aggregate), and the availability of both financial and non-financial information.

**(d) Determine the amount of any difference of recorded amounts from expected values that is acceptable without further investigation.** This threshold is influenced by materiality and the desired level of assurance, and is set having regard to the possibility that a combination of misstatements could aggregate to a material amount.

**Conclusion:** A substantive analytical procedure gives meaningful assurance only if the relationship is predictable, the underlying data is reliable, the expectation is precise (aided by disaggregation), and an appropriate acceptable-difference threshold is fixed; absent these, the auditor should rely on tests of details.

*(Full-marks tip: Reproduce all four limbs of SA 520 para 5, and stress that disaggregation improves precision. Merely writing "analytical procedures are useful for predictable data" without the reliability-of-data and precision limbs loses marks.)*

### Q42. Ch: Audit Sampling & Analytical Procedures — Analytical Procedures at the Overall Review Stage (SA 520) (Marks: 5) [Application]

He must **not** conclude and sign until the inconsistencies are satisfactorily explained and corroborated; if they reveal a previously unrecognised risk, he must extend procedures accordingly.

**6. Verify recognition of components in the P&L.** Check that **current service cost, interest cost, and actuarial gains/losses** are recognised as required by AS 15 — actuarial gains and losses are recognised **immediately in the Statement of Profit and Loss**.

**7. Verify disclosures.** Ensure the disclosures required by AS 15 (reconciliation of opening and closing obligation, principal actuarial assumptions, expense recognised) are made.

**Conclusion:** The auditor should not merely accept the provision figure; he must satisfy himself that the PUC method is used, the assumptions are reasonable, the employee data fed to the actuary is accurate, and the AS 15 recognition and disclosure requirements are met before accepting the gratuity provision.

### Q48. Ch: Audit of Items of Financial Statements — Purchases & Expense Cut-off (Completeness of Liabilities) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** While auditing Anmol Ltd., you are concerned about the **completeness of trade payables and expenses** — i.e., whether all liabilities relating to the year have been recorded (the risk of **understated purchases / unrecorded liabilities**). Describe the **audit procedures** you would perform to test the **purchase/expense cut-off** and to search for **unrecorded liabilities**.

**Answer:**
The principal risk for liabilities is **understatement (completeness)** — the assertion that all payables and expenses that should have been recorded have been recorded, in the correct period (**cut-off**). The auditor performs a directional test looking for **omitted** liabilities:

**1. Examine goods-received records around year-end.** Inspect the **Goods Received Notes (GRNs)/stores records** for the last few days before and the first few days after the balance-sheet date, and trace them to purchase invoices and the purchase register, to ensure that goods received **before** year-end are recorded as purchases/payables in the current year even if the invoice arrives later.

**2. Perform a search for unrecorded liabilities.** Examine **payments made after the year-end** (subsequent period cash book/bank statements) and **invoices received after the year-end**; determine whether any relate to goods/services received **before** the balance-sheet date, which should have been provided for as at year-end.

**3. Review the "invoices not yet booked" / provision for expenses.** Verify the completeness of the **provision for expenses** (electricity, professional fees, freight, etc.) for services consumed but not yet invoiced at year-end, by comparing with the prior year and known recurring expenses.

**4. Reconcile supplier statements.** Obtain **statements of account from major suppliers** and reconcile with the payables ledger; investigate any items on the supplier statement not appearing in the ledger — a classic source of unrecorded liabilities.

**5. Match purchases with inventory/cut-off.** Ensure consistency with the **inventory cut-off**: goods included in closing inventory (received before year-end) must have a corresponding **purchase/liability** recorded. A mismatch (goods in stock but no liability) reveals an unrecorded payable.

**6. Analytical review.** Compare the payables/purchases and payable-days ratio with the prior year to identify an unexpected fall that may indicate omitted liabilities.

**Conclusion:** By combining GRN cut-off testing, a subsequent-period search for unrecorded liabilities, supplier-statement reconciliations and consistency with inventory cut-off, the auditor obtains assurance that trade payables and expenses are **complete** and recorded in the correct period.

*(Full-marks tip: The key insight the examiner wants is that liabilities are tested for *understatement/completeness* (search subsequent payments and unbooked invoices), the mirror image of the *overstatement* testing done for assets. Linking purchase cut-off to inventory cut-off shows depth.)*

### Q50. Ch: Audit of Items of Financial Statements — Verification of Borrowing Costs Capitalised (AS 16) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Tejas Infra Ltd. has capitalised **₹90 lakh of borrowing costs** to a qualifying asset (a manufacturing plant under construction) during FY 2025-26 and has charged the balance interest to the Statement of Profit and Loss. As the auditor, explain the **audit procedures** to verify the **capitalisation of borrowing costs** under **AS 16**, and the conditions that must be satisfied.

**Answer:**
**AS 16 "Borrowing Costs"** requires that borrowing costs **directly attributable to the acquisition, construction or production of a qualifying asset** be **capitalised** as part of the cost of that asset; **all other** borrowing costs are recognised as an **expense** in the period incurred. A **qualifying asset** is one that necessarily takes a **substantial period of time** to get ready for its intended use or sale. The auditor's procedures are:

**1. Confirm the asset is a qualifying asset.** Verify from project records that the plant **necessarily takes a substantial period of time** to be ready for use, so that it qualifies for capitalisation of borrowing costs.

**2. Verify the amount eligible for capitalisation.**
- For **specific borrowings** (funds borrowed specifically to obtain the asset), verify that the amount capitalised is the **actual borrowing cost incurred** on that borrowing **less** any **income earned on temporary investment** of those funds.
- For **general borrowings** used to fund the asset, verify that the amount is computed by applying a **capitalisation rate** (the weighted-average rate of general borrowings) to the expenditure on the asset, and that the amount capitalised **does not exceed** the total borrowing cost actually incurred in the period.

**3. Verify the period of capitalisation.** Confirm that capitalisation **commenced** only when (i) expenditure on the asset is being incurred, (ii) borrowing costs are being incurred, and (iii) activities necessary to prepare the asset are in progress; that capitalisation was **suspended** during extended periods in which active development was interrupted; and that capitalisation **ceased** when the asset was **substantially ready** for its intended use.

**4. Recompute and reconcile.** Recompute the ₹90 lakh capitalised amount from loan statements and expenditure schedules, and reconcile the balance charged to the Statement of Profit and Loss.

**5. Verify disclosure.** Ensure disclosure of the **accounting policy** for borrowing costs and the **amount capitalised** during the period.

**Conclusion:** The auditor must confirm the asset is a qualifying asset, that only **directly attributable** costs (net of temporary investment income for specific borrowings, or capitalisation-rate-based for general borrowings) are capitalised for the correct period, and that the amount and policy are properly disclosed.

*(Full-marks tip: Full marks require the specific-vs-general borrowing distinction, the "less income on temporary investment" reduction for specific borrowings, and the commencement/suspension/cessation timing rules. Missing the "substantial period of time" qualifying-asset test loses a mark.)*

Depreciation = ₹1,00,00,000 ÷ 10 years = **₹10,00,000 per annum**.

The grant is treated as **deferred income** and recognised in the P&L **in proportion to depreciation** over the useful life.

### Q52. Ch: Audit in an Automated Environment — General Controls vs Application Controls (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** In an entity that uses information technology extensively, the auditor is required to understand the IT controls that are relevant to the audit. Distinguish between "General IT Controls" and "Application Controls", and state why the effectiveness of application controls depends on general controls.

**Answer:**
In an automated environment, controls implemented within the IT systems are broadly classified into General IT Controls (GITCs) and Application Controls, and the auditor must understand both categories because they operate together to ensure the integrity of the information produced by the system.

**General IT Controls** are policies and procedures that relate to *many* applications and support the effective functioning of application controls by helping to ensure the continued proper operation of information systems. They operate at an entity-wide or system-wide level. Examples include controls over data centre and network operations, program change management, access security (logical and physical access), and system software acquisition, change and maintenance.

**Application Controls** are manual or automated procedures that typically operate at a *business-process level* and apply to the processing of individual transactions by a particular application. They are designed to ensure the completeness, accuracy, validity and authorisation of transactions captured, processed and reported by that application. Examples include validation checks on input, sequence checks, matching of purchase order to invoice, and system-enforced authorisation limits.

The distinction may be summarised as follows:

| Basis | General IT Controls | Application Controls |
|---|---|---|
| Level of operation | Entity/system-wide (pervasive) | Business-process/transaction level |
| Purpose | Ensure continued and proper functioning of the IT environment | Ensure completeness, accuracy, validity and authorisation of transactions |
| Examples | Access security, change management, backup, data-centre operations | Input validation, calculation logic, reconciliation, three-way match |
| Relationship | Support and underpin application controls | Depend on general controls to remain effective |

**Why application controls depend on general controls:** An application control is only as reliable as the environment in which the program runs. If general controls over program changes are weak, a properly designed application control (e.g., a system-enforced credit limit) could be altered by an unauthorised change without detection; if access controls are weak, data validated by the application could be edited directly in the database, bypassing the control. Hence, even a well-designed application control cannot be relied upon unless the auditor is satisfied that the relevant general controls are operating effectively.

**Conclusion:** General IT controls provide the foundation on which application controls operate; the auditor therefore first evaluates GITCs and, only where these are effective, places reliance on the automated application controls.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner rewards the clear "application controls depend on GITCs" linkage with a concrete example; marks are lost for merely listing examples without explaining the dependency.)*

### Q53. Ch: Audit in an Automated Environment — Input, Processing and Output Controls (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Application controls in a computerised information system (CIS) environment are commonly categorised according to the stage of data flow at which they operate. Explain "Input Controls", "Processing Controls" and "Output Controls", giving two illustrative controls under each category.

**Answer:**
Application controls are designed to ensure the completeness and accuracy of records and the validity of the entries made in them, resulting from both manual and programmed processing. In a CIS environment they are conveniently classified by reference to the three stages through which data flows — input, processing and output.

**1. Input Controls:** These controls are designed to provide reasonable assurance that data received for processing has been properly authorised, converted into machine-sensible form and identified, and that data (including data transmitted over communication lines) has not been lost, suppressed, added, duplicated or otherwise improperly changed. In short, they ensure that only *valid, authorised and accurate* data enters the system. Illustrative controls:
- *Validation/edit checks* such as check-digit verification, range checks, field-format checks and reasonableness tests that reject invalid data at the point of entry.
- *Batch totals and control totals* (financial totals, hash totals, record counts) reconciled after entry to ensure completeness of input.

**2. Processing Controls:** These controls are designed to provide reasonable assurance that processing has been performed as intended for the particular application, i.e., that all transactions are processed as authorised, no authorised transactions are omitted, and no unauthorised transactions are added. They ensure the accuracy and completeness of processing once data is inside the system. Illustrative controls:
- *Sequence checks and run-to-run control totals* that confirm all records read in are processed and carried to the next stage.
- *System-enforced calculation and matching logic* (e.g., automatic computation of tax, matching of goods-receipt to purchase order), including exception reporting of items that fail the logic.

**3. Output Controls:** These controls are designed to provide reasonable assurance that the results of processing are accurate and are distributed only to authorised personnel. They protect the integrity and confidentiality of the information generated. Illustrative controls:
- *Reconciliation of output control totals with input and processing totals*, and review of output for reasonableness by a responsible official.
- *Controlled distribution of reports* — maintaining a distribution register and restricting sensitive output to authorised recipients only.

| Stage | Objective | Example controls |
|---|---|---|
| Input | Only valid, authorised, accurate data enters | Edit/validation checks; batch/control totals |
| Processing | Data processed completely and accurately as intended | Run-to-run totals; matching & calculation logic; exception reports |
| Output | Results accurate and distributed only to authorised persons | Output-to-input reconciliation; controlled report distribution |

**Conclusion:** Input, processing and output controls together form a continuum that safeguards the integrity of information from the point of capture to the point of delivery, and the auditor evaluates each stage to determine the reliability of system-generated data.

*(Full-marks tip: give two distinct examples per category; a common deduction is repeating the same control — e.g., "control totals" — under all three stages without differentiating its purpose.)*

### Q54. Ch: Audit in an Automated Environment — Risks in an Automated Environment (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** The use of IT systems and controls in an entity's financial reporting gives rise to specific risks that the auditor should be aware of. Discuss any five risks that arise in an automated environment.

**Answer:**
While information technology provides significant benefits such as speed, accuracy and consistency of processing, it also introduces specific risks to an entity's internal control that the auditor must consider when assessing the risks of material misstatement. The principal risks arising in an automated environment are as follows:

1. **Reliance on systems or programs that are inaccurately processing data, processing inaccurate data, or both.** If the underlying program logic is incorrect, errors are introduced systematically and repeated in every transaction, rather than occurring randomly as in manual processing.

2. **Unauthorised access to data** that may result in destruction of data or improper changes to data, including the recording of unauthorised or non-existent transactions, or inaccurate recording of transactions. Since data resides in electronic form, changes can be made without a visible physical trail.

3. **The possibility of IT personnel gaining access privileges beyond those necessary** to perform their assigned duties, thereby breaking down the segregation of duties. A person with excessive access could both initiate and conceal a transaction.

4. **Unauthorised changes to data in master files** (e.g., standing data such as prices, credit limits, vendor bank accounts), which affect all subsequent transactions processed using that data.

5. **Unauthorised changes to systems or programs**, and failure to make necessary changes to systems or programs, so that controls may be overridden or may no longer function as intended.

Additional risks include **loss of data or inability to access data as required** (e.g., due to inadequate backup) and **potential loss of audit trail**, since transactions may be initiated, authorised, processed and recorded electronically with no supporting paper documentation.

**Conclusion:** These risks are systematic and pervasive in nature; a single control weakness in the IT environment can therefore affect a large volume of transactions, which is why the auditor evaluates general and application controls before relying on system-generated information.

*(Full-marks tip: state at least five *distinct* risks and note the systematic/pervasive character of IT risk versus random manual error — that observation is what the examiner looks for beyond a bare list.)*

### Q56. Ch: Audit in an Automated Environment — Computer-Assisted Audit Techniques / Data Analytics (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain what is meant by "Computer-Assisted Audit Techniques (CAATs)" and state the circumstances in which their use may be particularly advantageous to the auditor. Give two examples of audit tests that can be performed using CAATs.

**Answer:**
**Meaning:** Computer-Assisted Audit Techniques (CAATs) refer to the application of auditing procedures using the computer as an audit tool. 1. **Recalculation and re-performance** — recomputing GST, depreciation, interest or discounts on the entire transaction file and comparing with the amounts recorded by the system.

**Conclusion:** CAATs enhance both the effectiveness and efficiency of the audit in an automated environment, particularly where transaction volumes are high and the audit trail is largely electronic.

### Q58. Ch: Audit in an Automated Environment — Automated vs Manual Controls (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Compare "automated controls" with "manual controls" in terms of their inherent characteristics, and explain the audit implications of each. State how a "manual control that depends on IT-generated information" should be viewed by the auditor.

**Answer:**
Internal controls in an entity may be automated (performed by the IT system), manual (performed by people), or a combination of both. The inherent characteristics of the two differ significantly, and this affects the auditor's testing strategy.

| Basis | Automated Controls | Manual Controls |
|---|---|---|
| Consistency of operation | Applied consistently to every transaction; not subject to human error, lapse of attention or judgement | Prone to human error, fatigue, and being overridden or circumvented |
| Segregation | Enforced by system configuration and access rights | Depends on organisational structure and supervision |
| Nature of error | Errors are systematic and repetitive (same error in every transaction) | Errors tend to be random and isolated |
| Susceptibility | Vulnerable to unauthorised program changes and access breaches | Vulnerable to collusion, management override and simple mistakes |
| Suitability | Better for high-volume, routine, rule-based transactions | Better for large, unusual, non-routine transactions requiring judgement |

**Audit implications:**
- For **automated controls**, because they operate uniformly, the auditor may be able to test the control *once* (or a small number of times) and, provided the relevant general IT controls (particularly over program change and access) are effective, conclude that it operated consistently throughout the period ("benefit of consistency"). Reliance therefore depends on the effectiveness of GITCs.
- For **manual controls**, the auditor generally needs to test a *sample* across the period because consistency cannot be assumed; the auditor also considers the competence and integrity of the persons performing the control and the possibility of override.

**Manual controls that depend on IT-generated information:** Some controls are performed manually but rely on reports or data produced by the system (e.g., a manager reviewing a system-generated exception report of overdue receivables). Such a control can be no more reliable than the completeness and accuracy of the underlying report. The auditor must therefore evaluate the **reliability of the IT-generated information** used in the control (its completeness and accuracy) in addition to testing the manual review itself; if the report is unreliable, the manual control is ineffective regardless of how diligently the review is performed.

**Conclusion:** Automated controls offer consistency but rely on strong general controls, whereas manual controls offer flexibility but risk random error and override; where a manual control uses IT-generated information, the auditor must satisfy himself of the reliability of that information.

*(Full-marks tip: the "benefit of consistency" for automated controls and the requirement to test the reliability of IT-generated information behind a manual control are the two high-value points examiners look for.)*

### Q59. Ch: Audit in an Automated Environment — Application: Input Controls (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** "GreenFoods Ltd" processes 2,000 vendor invoices per month through its accounts-payable module. In the current year, the auditor noted the following errors that had entered the accounting records: (a) an invoice was entered twice and paid twice; (b) an invoice with quantity "500" was keyed as "5000"; (c) an invoice was booked against a vendor code that did not exist in the master, and posted to a suspense account; and (d) the GST amount keyed did not agree with the rate applicable. For each error, identify the input control that, had it been in place and operating, would have prevented it.

**Answer:**
Each of the errors noted is attributable to the absence or failure of a specific input control. Input controls are designed to ensure that only valid, authorised and accurate data enters the system. The appropriate preventive control for each error is as follows:

| Error observed | Input control that would have prevented it | How the control works |
|---|---|---|
| (a) Invoice entered and paid twice | **Duplicate check (uniqueness/matching control)** | The system checks the vendor code + invoice number combination against invoices already recorded and rejects or flags a duplicate before entry is accepted. |
| (b) Quantity "500" keyed as "5000" | **Reasonableness/range check (and limit check)** | The system compares the keyed value against a predefined acceptable range (or against the linked purchase-order quantity) and flags values outside the range for review. |
| (c) Invoice booked to a non-existent vendor code | **Validity check (existence/master check)** | The system validates the entered vendor code against the vendor master file and rejects any code that does not exist, preventing posting to suspense. |
| (d) GST amount inconsistent with applicable rate | **Programmed calculation / matching check** | The GST is computed by the system from the taxable value and the applicable rate (or the keyed amount is matched to the system-computed amount), and any mismatch is rejected or reported as an exception. |

**Additional observation:** The recurrence of such errors across a high volume of transactions indicates weak input controls generally; the auditor should also consider whether **batch/control totals** are being reconciled and should extend substantive testing (using CAATs to scan for duplicates, out-of-range values and invalid codes across the full population) and consider the impact on the assessed control risk.

**Conclusion:** Errors (a) to (d) would each have been prevented by duplicate, reasonableness/range, validity and programmed-calculation checks respectively; their occurrence points to a deficiency in the design or operation of input controls in the accounts-payable module.

*(Full-marks tip: match each error to the *correct named* input control; a common deduction is giving a generic answer like "validation check" for all four instead of naming duplicate, range, validity and calculation checks specifically.)*

### Q60. Ch: Audit Documentation & Quality — Form, Content and Extent of Documentation (SA 230) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** As per SA 230 "Audit Documentation", the auditor is required to prepare documentation that is sufficient to enable an experienced auditor to understand the audit. State the factors that influence the form, content and extent of audit documentation.

**Answer:**
SA 230, "Audit Documentation", requires the auditor to prepare audit documentation that is sufficient to enable an experienced auditor, having no previous connection with the audit, to understand: (a) the nature, timing and extent of the audit procedures performed; (b) the results of those procedures and the audit evidence obtained; and (c) significant matters arising during the audit, the conclusions reached thereon, and significant professional judgements made in reaching those conclusions.

The **form, content and extent** of audit documentation depend on factors such as:

1. **The size and complexity of the entity.**
2. **The nature of the audit procedures** to be performed.
3. **The identified risks of material misstatement** — higher assessed risk generally warrants more extensive documentation.
4. **The significance of the audit evidence** obtained.
5. **The nature and extent of exceptions identified.**
6. **The need to document a conclusion or the basis for a conclusion** not readily determinable from the documentation of the work performed or evidence obtained.
7. **The audit methodology and tools used.**

**Nature of documentation:** Audit documentation may be recorded on paper or on electronic or other media, and includes, for example, audit programmes, analyses, issues memoranda, summaries of significant matters, letters of confirmation and representation, checklists and correspondence concerning significant matters. The auditor need not include superseded drafts, duplicates, or preliminary notes.

**Conclusion:** SA 230 does not prescribe a rigid format; rather, the form, content and extent of documentation are a matter of professional judgement determined by the above factors, always subject to the overarching "experienced auditor" test.

### Q61. Ch: Audit Documentation & Quality — Assembly of the Final Audit File / Time Limit (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the time limit within which the auditor should complete the assembly of the final audit file, and explain the nature of the changes that may and may not be made to audit documentation after the date of the auditor's report.

**Answer:**
As per SA 230 read with SQC 1, the auditor is required to assemble the audit documentation in an **audit file** and complete the administrative process of assembling the final audit file on a **timely basis after the date of the auditor's report**. SQC 1 provides that an appropriate time limit within which to complete the assembly of the final audit file is ordinarily **not more than 60 days after the date of the auditor's report**.

The assembly of the final audit file after the date of the auditor's report is an **administrative process** that does not involve the performance of new audit procedures or the drawing of new conclusions. During this period, the auditor may make changes to the documentation that are **administrative in nature**, such as:
- deleting or discarding superseded documentation;
- sorting, collating and cross-referencing working papers;
- signing off on completion checklists relating to the file-assembly process; and
- documenting audit evidence that the auditor had obtained, discussed and agreed with the relevant members of the engagement team **before** the date of the auditor's report.

After the assembly of the final audit file has been completed, the auditor **shall not delete or discard audit documentation of any nature before the end of its retention period.** Where the auditor finds it necessary to **modify existing** documentation or **add new** audit documentation after assembly is complete (e.g., because of a subsequently discovered fact), the auditor shall document, regardless of the nature of the modifications: (a) the specific reasons for making them; and (b) when and by whom they were made and reviewed.

**Conclusion:** The final audit file must be assembled ordinarily within 60 days of the audit report date, and thereafter only documented, justified changes are permitted — documentation may not be deleted before the retention period ends.

*(Full-marks tip: the 60-day limit and the "no deletion, only documented additions" rule after assembly are the two scoring points; students often confuse the 60-day assembly period with the retention period.)*

### Q63. Ch: Audit Documentation & Quality — Documentation of Significant Matters and the "Experienced Auditor" (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain what is meant by an "experienced auditor" for the purposes of SA 230. Also state the matters that the auditor should document in respect of significant matters and professional judgements, and give examples of significant matters.

**Answer:**
**Experienced auditor:** For the purposes of SA 230, an "experienced auditor" means an individual (whether internal or external to the firm) who has **practical audit experience, and a reasonable understanding of**: (a) audit processes; (b) the SAs and applicable legal and regulatory requirements; (c) the business environment in which the entity operates; and (d) auditing and financial reporting issues relevant to the entity's industry. The sufficiency of documentation is measured against whether such a person, having no previous connection with the audit, could understand the audit from the documentation alone.

**Documentation of significant matters and professional judgements:** The auditor shall prepare documentation that is sufficient to enable an experienced auditor to understand the significant matters arising during the audit, the conclusions reached thereon, and significant professional judgements made in reaching those conclusions. Judging the significance of a matter requires an objective analysis of the facts and circumstances.

**Examples of significant matters** include:
- Matters that give rise to **significant risks**.
- **Results of audit procedures indicating that the financial information could be materially misstated**, or a need to revise the auditor's previous assessment of risks and the responses to those risks.
- Circumstances that cause the auditor **significant difficulty in applying necessary audit procedures.**
- Findings that could result in a **modification to the audit opinion** or the inclusion of an Emphasis of Matter paragraph in the auditor's report.

Where the auditor identifies information that is **inconsistent** with the final conclusion on a significant matter, the auditor shall document how the inconsistency was addressed. Additionally, the auditor should document discussions of significant matters with management, those charged with governance and others, including **when and with whom** the discussions took place.

**Conclusion:** Documentation of significant matters, the judgements made and their resolution is central to SA 230, and its adequacy is always tested against the understanding of an experienced auditor with no prior connection to the engagement.

### Q64. Ch: Audit Documentation & Quality — Elements of a System of Quality Control (SQC 1) (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** SQC 1 requires a firm to establish a system of quality control designed to provide it with reasonable assurance that the firm and its personnel comply with professional standards and that reports issued are appropriate. Enumerate and briefly explain the elements of a system of quality control under SQC 1.

**Answer:**
SQC 1, "Quality Control for Firms that Perform Audits and Reviews of Historical Financial Information, and Other Assurance and Related Services Engagements", requires the firm to establish and maintain a system of quality control that includes **policies and procedures addressing each of the following six elements**:

1. **Leadership responsibilities for quality within the firm ("tone at the top"):** The firm's leadership (e.g., managing partner or board of partners) must assume ultimate responsibility for the firm's system of quality control and promote an internal culture that recognises that quality is essential in performing engagements. Commercial considerations must not override the quality of work.

2. **Ethical requirements:** The firm should establish policies and procedures to provide reasonable assurance that the firm and its personnel comply with relevant ethical requirements, in particular the fundamental principles of integrity, objectivity, professional competence and due care, confidentiality and professional behaviour, and requirements relating to **independence** (including obtaining written confirmations of compliance).

3. **Acceptance and continuance of client relationships and specific engagements:** The firm should establish policies to accept or continue engagements only where it: (a) is competent to perform the engagement and has the capabilities, time and resources to do so; (b) can comply with ethical requirements; and (c) has considered the **integrity** of the client and has no information that would lead it to conclude the client lacks integrity.

4. **Human resources:** The firm should establish policies to provide reasonable assurance that it has sufficient personnel with the competence, capabilities and commitment to ethical principles necessary to perform engagements in accordance with professional standards — covering recruitment, performance evaluation, capabilities, competence, career development, promotion, compensation, and the estimation of personnel needs. This includes the **assignment of engagement teams**.

5. **Engagement performance:** The firm should establish policies to provide reasonable assurance that engagements are performed in accordance with professional standards and that appropriate reports are issued. This encompasses **direction, supervision and review**, consultation on difficult or contentious matters, resolution of differences of opinion, and **engagement quality control review (EQCR)** for engagements where required.

6. **Monitoring:** The firm should establish policies to provide reasonable assurance that the policies and procedures relating to the system of quality control are relevant, adequate, operating effectively and complied with in practice — including ongoing consideration and evaluation of the firm's system and periodic **inspection** of completed engagements.

**Conclusion:** These six inter-related elements — leadership, ethics, acceptance/continuance, human resources, engagement performance and monitoring — together constitute the firm's system of quality control, and the firm must document them and communicate them to its personnel.

*(Full-marks tip: name all six elements with a one-line explanation each; missing "acceptance and continuance" or "monitoring" is the most common cause of lost marks. Mention documentation and communication of policies to personnel for the last mark.)*

### Q65. Ch: Audit Documentation & Quality — Application: Retention Period and Ownership (Marks: 4) [Application]
**Question:** During the peer review of "R & Co., Chartered Accountants", the following was observed: for a statutory audit whose auditor's report was dated 15 July 2024, the firm had discarded certain working papers on 30 June 2027 to save storage space, and the audit partner asserted that "since we are the owners of the working papers, we may deal with them as we please." Comment on the firm's actions with reference to SQC 1 and SA 230.

**Answer:**
The firm's two assertions must each be tested against SQC 1 and SA 230.

**1. Retention period — discarding working papers on 30 June 2027:** SQC 1 provides that the firm should establish policies and procedures for the retention of engagement documentation for a period sufficient to meet the needs of the firm and to comply with legal and regulatory requirements. The retention period for audit engagements ordinarily is **no shorter than seven years from the date of the auditor's report**, or, if later, the date of the group auditor's report.

Applying this to the facts: the auditor's report is dated **15 July 2024**; the minimum retention period therefore runs until **at least 14 July 2031**. By discarding the working papers on **30 June 2027** — less than three years later — the firm has violated the retention requirement of SQC 1. Further, SA 230 states that after the assembly of the final audit file has been completed, the auditor **shall not delete or discard audit documentation of any nature before the end of its retention period.** The firm's action is therefore improper.

**2. Ownership assertion — "we may deal with them as we please":** While it is correct that, unless otherwise specified by law or agreement, **audit working papers are the property of the auditor** (SQC 1 also notes the firm may, at its discretion, make portions available to clients provided such disclosure does not undermine the audit), ownership does **not** confer an unrestricted right to destroy them. The auditor's ownership is subject to the overriding professional obligations of confidentiality and, critically, the **retention requirements** of SQC 1 and SA 230. The partner's assertion is therefore not tenable.

**Conclusion:** R & Co. has contravened the retention requirements of SQC 1 and SA 230 by discarding the working papers before the expiry of the minimum seven-year retention period; the claim of ownership does not justify premature destruction. The action would be reported as a deficiency in the peer/quality review.

*(Full-marks tip: state the 7-year rule and compute the expiry date from the report date to show the breach; then separately rebut the ownership argument — addressing only one of the two points caps the marks.)*

### Q66. Ch: Audit Documentation & Quality — Engagement Quality Control Review (EQCR) under SQC 1 (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** With reference to SQC 1, explain the meaning of an "Engagement Quality Control Review (EQCR)" and state the matters that the engagement quality control reviewer should consider before the report is issued. Also state the qualities required of the reviewer.

**Answer:**
**Meaning:** Under SQC 1, an **Engagement Quality Control Review (EQCR)** is a process designed to provide an **objective evaluation, on or before the date of the report**, of the significant judgements the engagement team made and the conclusions it reached in formulating the report. The **engagement quality control reviewer** is a partner, other person in the firm, suitably qualified external person, or a team of such individuals — none of whom is part of the engagement team — with sufficient and appropriate experience and authority to objectively evaluate the significant judgements made.

The firm should establish policies requiring an EQCR for audits of financial statements of **listed entities** and for other engagements for which the firm has determined that such a review is required. For the engagements subject to review, the firm must set out criteria for evaluating whether an EQCR is warranted.

**Matters the reviewer should consider** in performing the review include:
- **Discussion of significant matters** with the engagement partner.
- **Review of the financial statements or other subject-matter information and the proposed report.**
- **Review of selected audit documentation** relating to the significant judgements the engagement team made and the conclusions it reached.
- **Evaluation of the conclusions reached** in formulating the report and consideration of whether the proposed report is appropriate.

For audits of listed entities the review should additionally consider the engagement team's **evaluation of the firm's independence** in relation to the engagement, whether **appropriate consultation** has taken place on matters involving differences of opinion or other difficult/contentious matters and the conclusions arising, and whether the documentation selected for review reflects the work performed in relation to significant judgements and supports the conclusions reached.

**Qualities of the reviewer:** The reviewer must be objective and **not part of the engagement team**; possess the necessary **technical qualifications, competence, experience and authority**; and carry out the review with due care, without impairing the engagement partner's responsibility. The engagement partner should **not date the report until the completion of the EQCR.**

**Conclusion:** The EQCR is a key element of "engagement performance" under SQC 1, providing an independent, objective safeguard over the significant judgements and conclusions before the report is issued.

*(Full-marks tip: emphasise "objective evaluation on or before the date of the report" and that the report cannot be dated until the EQCR is complete; listing the reviewer's qualities separately earns the last mark.)*

### Q67. Ch: Audit Documentation & Quality — Application: Departure from a Relevant Requirement (SA 230) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** During the audit of "Nova Textiles Ltd", CA Arjun judged, in the specific circumstances of the engagement, that it was not necessary to perform a particular audit procedure otherwise required by a relevant SA, because an alternative procedure achieved the aim of that requirement. The audit assistant told CA Arjun that "since the SA procedure was not performed, nothing at all needs to be documented about it." Additionally, after the audit report was signed, CA Arjun received information about a fact that existed at the report date and performed a new procedure. Advise CA Arjun on the documentation required in each situation under SA 230.

**Answer:**
Both situations are governed by specific documentation requirements of SA 230, and the audit assistant's view is incorrect.

**Situation 1 — Departure from a relevant requirement:** SA 230 recognises that, in exceptional circumstances, the auditor may judge it necessary to **depart from a relevant requirement in an SA**. In such circumstances, the auditor is required to document **how the alternative audit procedures performed achieve the aim of that requirement, and the reasons for the departure.** (This applies where the requirement is relevant and the circumstances of the audit exist; it does not apply where a requirement is not relevant, e.g., a requirement that is conditional and the condition does not exist.)

Applying this to the facts: CA Arjun has, on the basis of his professional judgement, substituted an alternative procedure that achieves the aim of the SA requirement. Contrary to the assistant's view, this is **not** a situation where "nothing needs to be documented"; rather, CA Arjun **must document** (a) the reasons for the departure, and (b) how the alternative procedures performed achieved the aim of the original requirement. The assistant's advice is therefore wrong.

**Situation 2 — New procedure performed after the date of the report:** SA 230 provides that if, in **exceptional circumstances**, the auditor performs **new or additional audit procedures or draws new conclusions after the date of the auditor's report**, the auditor shall document: (a) the **circumstances encountered**; (b) the **new or additional audit procedures performed, audit evidence obtained, and conclusions reached**, and their effect on the auditor's report; and (c) **when and by whom** the resulting changes to audit documentation were made and reviewed.

Applying this: on receiving information about a fact existing at the report date and performing a new procedure, CA Arjun must document all three of the above matters. (This documentation is in addition to any consideration required under SA 560 relating to subsequently discovered facts.)

**Conclusion:** In Situation 1 CA Arjun must document the reasons for departure and how the alternative achieved the requirement's aim; in Situation 2 he must document the circumstances, the new procedures/evidence/conclusions and their effect, and when and by whom the changes were made and reviewed. The assistant's assertion that no documentation is needed is contrary to SA 230.

*(Full-marks tip: correctly rebut the assistant and give the *specific* documentation content for each situation — "reasons + how alternative achieves the aim" for departure, and "circumstances + new procedures/evidence/conclusions + when/by whom" for post-report work.)*

### Q68. Ch: Audit Documentation & Quality — Documentation of Identifying Characteristics and Preparation on Timely Basis (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** SA 230 requires the auditor to document the identifying characteristics of items tested and to prepare documentation on a timely basis. Explain (i) the meaning and purpose of recording "identifying characteristics", with examples; and (ii) the importance of preparing audit documentation on a timely basis. Also state what the auditor should document regarding discussions of significant matters and how departures from documentation are handled where oral explanation is involved.

**Answer:**
**(i) Recording of identifying characteristics:** In documenting the nature, timing and extent of audit procedures performed, SA 230 requires the auditor to record the **identifying characteristics of the specific items or matters tested.** Recording identifying characteristics serves several purposes: it enables the engagement team to be **accountable** for its work, and it facilitates the **investigation of exceptions or inconsistencies** and the re-performance of procedures on the same items if necessary. Examples of how identifying characteristics may be recorded:

| Type of procedure | Illustrative identifying characteristic recorded |
|---|---|
| Test of detail on purchase orders | The dates and unique purchase-order numbers of the documents selected |
| Sample selected from a population by a set criterion | The source, starting point and sampling interval (e.g., every 125th voucher from a specified sequence) |
| Inquiry of specific personnel | The names and designations of persons inquired of, and the dates of inquiry |
| Observation procedure | The process/subject observed, the individuals involved and their responsibilities, and where and when the observation was carried out |

**(ii) Preparation on a timely basis:** SA 230 requires the auditor to prepare audit documentation **on a timely basis**. Preparing sufficient and appropriate documentation on a timely basis helps to **enhance the quality of the audit** and facilitates the **effective review and evaluation of the audit evidence obtained and conclusions reached before the auditor's report is finalised.** Documentation prepared after the audit work has been performed, or after the report has been issued, is likely to be **less accurate** than documentation prepared contemporaneously.

**Documentation of discussions of significant matters:** The auditor shall document discussions of significant matters with management, those charged with governance, and others, including the **nature of the significant matters discussed and when and with whom** the discussions took place. This documentation is not limited to records prepared by the auditor but may include other appropriate records such as agreed minutes of meetings.

Where documentation of a matter would otherwise be difficult, the auditor should record the substance of oral explanations in the working papers. Further, if the auditor considers it necessary to **depart from a relevant requirement**, this must be documented (reasons for departure and how alternative procedures achieved the aim), consistent with the requirement discussed above.

**Conclusion:** Recording identifying characteristics ensures accountability and traceability of items tested, while timely preparation ensures accuracy and enables effective review; documentation of significant discussions and the substance of oral explanations completes the auditor's record under SA 230.

### Q69. Ch: Company Audit — Remuneration of Auditor (Sec 142) (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the statutory provisions relating to fixation of remuneration of the auditor of a company under Section 142 of the Companies Act, 2013. State clearly what the remuneration includes and what it excludes.

**Answer:**
The remuneration of the auditor of a company is governed by **Section 142 of the Companies Act, 2013**, read with the manner of appointment under Section 139.

**Sub-section (1) — Fixation:** The remuneration of the auditor of a company shall be fixed **in its general meeting** or in such manner as may be determined therein. However, the Act carves out one exception — the **remuneration of the first auditor** may be fixed **by the Board of Directors**. This is a natural corollary of Section 139(6), under which the Board itself appoints the first auditor within 30 days of registration; consequently the Board that appoints may also fix the pay.

**What remuneration includes:** Section 142(2) clarifies that, in addition to the fee payable to the auditor, the remuneration shall **include the expenses, if any, incurred by the auditor in connection with the audit** of the company and **any facility extended to him** (for example, boarding, lodging or conveyance provided by the company).

**What remuneration excludes:** The remuneration **does not include** any remuneration paid to the auditor for **any other service rendered by him at the request of the company**. Thus, fees for services such as taxation representation (to the extent permissible) or certification are billed separately and do not form part of the audit remuneration governed by Section 142.

| Situation | Who fixes remuneration |
|---|---|
| First auditor | Board of Directors [Sec 139(6) + Sec 142(1) proviso] |
| Subsequent auditor | Company in general meeting (or as determined therein) |
| Casual vacancy filled by Board | Board fixes remuneration when it appoints |

**Conclusion:** Barring the first auditor whose remuneration the Board may fix, the auditor's remuneration is fixed by the members in general meeting, and it is deemed to include out-of-pocket audit expenses and facilities extended, but not fees for other services.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the express mention that remuneration **includes** audit expenses/facilities but **excludes** fees for other services — most students state only who fixes the pay and lose the Sec 142(2) marks.)*

### Q70. Ch: Company Audit — Auditor Not to Render Certain Services (Sec 144) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Section 144 of the Companies Act, 2013 prohibits an auditor from rendering certain services. Enumerate the services which an auditor of a company **shall not** provide, and explain the meaning of "directly or indirectly" as used in that section.

**Answer:**
**Section 144 of the Companies Act, 2013** provides that an auditor appointed under the Act shall provide to the company only such **other services as are approved by the Board of Directors or the audit committee**, as the case may be, but such services shall **not include** the following services (whether such services are rendered directly or indirectly to the company or its holding company or subsidiary company):

1. **accounting and book keeping services;**
2. **internal audit;**
3. **design and implementation of any financial information system;**
4. **actuarial services;**
5. **investment advisory services;**
6. **investment banking services;**
7. **rendering of outsourced financial services;**
8. **management services; and**
9. **any other kind of services as may be prescribed.**

The rationale is to preserve the **independence** of the auditor by preventing self-review, management and advocacy threats — an auditor cannot audit records or systems that he himself has prepared or designed.

**Meaning of "directly or indirectly" —** The Explanation to Section 144 clarifies that the expression "directly or indirectly" shall include rendering of services by the auditor —
- **(i) in case of an auditor being an individual** — either **himself** or **through his relative** or **any other person connected or associated** with such individual or **through any other entity** in which such individual has significant influence or control, or whose name or trade mark or brand is used by such individual;
- **(ii) in case of an auditor being a firm** — either **itself** or **through any of its partners** or **through its parent, subsidiary or associate entity** or **through any other entity** in which the firm or any partner of the firm has significant influence or control, or whose name or trade mark or brand is used by the firm or any of its partners.

**Transitional requirement:** An auditor or audit firm rendering any of the above services on or before commencement of the Act had to comply with Section 144 before the closure of the first financial year after such commencement.

**Conclusion:** An auditor may render only Board/audit-committee-approved permissible services and is absolutely barred from the nine categories of services above, whether provided directly or through relatives, partners or associated entities.

### Q71. Ch: Company Audit — Punishment for Contravention (Sec 147) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** State the penal consequences for contravention of the provisions of Sections 139 to 146 of the Companies Act, 2013, both for the **company/its officers** and for the **auditor**, as laid down in Section 147.

**Answer:**
**Section 147 of the Companies Act, 2013** prescribes punishment for contravention of any of the provisions of **Sections 139 to 146**.

**(A) Contravention by the company [Sec 147(1)] —** If any of the provisions of Sections 139 to 146 is contravened, the **company shall be punishable with fine which shall not be less than ₹25,000 but which may extend to ₹5,00,000**, and **every officer of the company who is in default** shall be punishable with **fine which shall not be less than ₹10,000 but which may extend to ₹1,00,000**. (Imprisonment of officers, earlier provided, now stands omitted, leaving fine only.)

**(B) Contravention by the auditor [Sec 147(2)] —** If an auditor contravenes any of the provisions of Section 139, 143, 144 or 145, the auditor shall be punishable with a **fine which shall not be less than ₹25,000 but which may extend to ₹5,00,000 or four times the remuneration of the auditor, whichever is less.**

**Aggravated contravention (wilful):** Where the auditor has contravened such provisions **knowingly or wilfully with the intention to deceive** the company or its shareholders or creditors or tax authorities, he shall be punishable with **imprisonment for a term which may extend to one year** and with **fine which shall not be less than ₹50,000 but which may extend to ₹25,00,000 or eight times the remuneration of the auditor, whichever is less.**

**(C) Consequences of conviction of the auditor [Sec 147(3)] —** Where an auditor has been convicted under sub-section (2), he shall be liable to —
- **(i)** **refund the remuneration** received by him to the company; and
- **(ii)** **pay for damages** to the company, statutory bodies or authorities or to any other persons for loss arising out of incorrect or misleading statements of particulars made in his audit report.

**(D) Firm liability [Sec 147(5)] —** Where the auditor is a firm and it is proved that the partner(s) acted in a fraudulent manner or abetted or colluded in any fraud, the **liability, whether civil or criminal, shall be of the partner(s) concerned and of the firm jointly and severally.**

| Party | Minimum | Maximum |
|---|---|---|
| Company | ₹25,000 | ₹5,00,000 |
| Officer in default | ₹10,000 | ₹1,00,000 |
| Auditor (non-wilful) | ₹25,000 | ₹5,00,000 or 4× remuneration, whichever is **less** |
| Auditor (wilful/deceit) | ₹50,000 + imprisonment up to 1 yr | ₹25,00,000 or 8× remuneration, whichever is **less** |

**Conclusion:** Section 147 imposes graded monetary penalties on the company and officers, and enhanced penalties (including imprisonment for wilful default) on the auditor, coupled with refund of remuneration and damages on conviction and joint-and-several liability of the firm for fraudulent partners.

*(Full-marks tip: State the "whichever is less" cap correctly for both non-wilful and wilful cases; many students write "whichever is higher" and lose marks. Also mention refund of remuneration under 147(3).)*

### Q72. Ch: Audit Report — Forming an Opinion & Basis for Opinion (SA 700) (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Under SA 700 "Forming an Opinion and Reporting on Financial Statements", on what basis does the auditor form an opinion, and what must the "Basis for Opinion" paragraph of the auditor's report contain?

**Answer:**
**SA 700 "Forming an Opinion and Reporting on Financial Statements"** deals with the auditor's responsibility to form an opinion on the financial statements and the form and content of the auditor's report.

**Forming an opinion:** The auditor shall form an opinion on **whether the financial statements are prepared, in all material respects, in accordance with the applicable financial reporting framework.** In order to form that opinion, the auditor shall conclude as to **whether he has obtained reasonable assurance about whether the financial statements as a whole are free from material misstatement, whether due to fraud or error.** That conclusion takes into account —
- whether **sufficient appropriate audit evidence** has been obtained (SA 330);
- whether **uncorrected misstatements** are material, individually or in aggregate (SA 450); and
- an evaluation of the qualitative aspects — whether the financial statements adequately disclose the significant accounting policies, whether those policies are consistent and appropriate, whether accounting estimates are reasonable, and whether the information is relevant, reliable, comparable, understandable and the terminology used is appropriate.

**Basis for Opinion paragraph:** The auditor's report shall include a section, directly following the Opinion section, with the heading **"Basis for Opinion"**, that —
- **(a)** states that the audit was conducted **in accordance with Standards on Auditing (SAs)**;
- **(b)** refers to the section of the report that describes the auditor's **responsibilities under the SAs**;
- **(c)** includes a statement that the auditor is **independent** of the entity in accordance with the relevant ethical requirements relating to the audit (the Code of Ethics issued by ICAI) and has fulfilled the auditor's **other ethical responsibilities** in accordance with these requirements; and
- **(d)** states whether the auditor believes that the **audit evidence obtained is sufficient and appropriate** to provide a basis for the opinion.

**Conclusion:** The auditor forms an unmodified opinion only when he concludes that the financial statements as a whole are free from material misstatement, and the mandatory "Basis for Opinion" section anchors that opinion to the SAs, the auditor's independence and the sufficiency of evidence obtained.

*(Full-marks tip: The four bullet contents of the "Basis for Opinion" section are the scoring points — especially the independence statement and the "sufficient and appropriate evidence" statement.)*

### Q73. Ch: Audit Report — Adverse Opinion vs Disclaimer of Opinion (SA 705) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between an **Adverse Opinion** and a **Disclaimer of Opinion** under SA 705 (Revised) "Modifications to the Opinion in the Independent Auditor's Report". When is each expressed, and how does the wording of the report differ?

**Answer:**
**SA 705 (Revised) "Modifications to the Opinion in the Independent Auditor's Report"** provides for three types of modified opinion — qualified, adverse and disclaimer — the choice depending on **(a) the nature of the matter** giving rise to the modification (whether the financial statements are materially misstated, or the auditor is unable to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence), and **(b) the auditor's judgement about the pervasiveness** of the effects or possible effects on the financial statements.

| Basis | Adverse Opinion | Disclaimer of Opinion |
|---|---|---|
| **Underlying reason** | Financial statements **are materially misstated** (a matter the auditor has evidence about) | Auditor is **unable to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence** (limitation on scope) |
| **Degree** | Misstatements are both **material and pervasive** | Possible effects of undetected misstatements are both **material and pervasive** |
| **When expressed** | When the auditor, having obtained evidence, concludes misstatements are so pervasive that a qualification is inadequate | When the auditor cannot obtain evidence and possible effects are so pervasive that a qualification is inadequate — or in rare cases of multiple uncertainties |
| **Opinion wording** | "…the accompanying financial statements **do not give a true and fair view**…" (Because of the significance of the matter, the FS do not present fairly) | "…**we do not express an opinion** on the accompanying financial statements…because we were **not able to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence**…" |
| **Heading of opinion section** | "Adverse Opinion" | "Disclaimer of Opinion" |
| **Basis section heading** | "Basis for Adverse Opinion" | "Basis for Disclaimer of Opinion" |

**Additional reporting effects of a Disclaimer:** When the auditor disclaims an opinion, the report does **not** include the reference that the audit was conducted in accordance with SAs in the ordinary way; instead the Basis section states that the auditor was **engaged** to audit the financial statements, and the auditor's responsibilities section is amended to state only that the auditor is independent and was unable to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence. Further, when the auditor disclaims an opinion, **Key Audit Matters (SA 701) are not reported** unless required by law, since to do so would suggest the FS are more credible than the disclaimer allows.

**Conclusion:** An **adverse opinion** arises from a **known, pervasive misstatement**, and the auditor positively states the financial statements do **not** give a true and fair view; a **disclaimer** arises from a **pervasive inability to obtain evidence**, and the auditor declines to express any opinion at all.

*(Full-marks tip: Anchor the distinction in the SA 705 two-factor matrix — nature (misstatement vs scope limitation) and pervasiveness — and add the special effects of a disclaimer on the SA-compliance statement and KAM.)*

### Q74. Ch: Audit Report — CARO 2020 Fraud Reporting (Clause 3(xi)) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** During the audit of Meridian Alloys Ltd for FY 2025-26, the auditor comes across an embezzlement of ₹42 lakh by an employee, and separately receives a copy of a report on suspected fraud filed by the company's cost auditor with the Central Government. State the reporting responsibilities of the statutory auditor under CARO 2020 (Clause 3(xi)), and clarify the relationship with the auditor's duty under Section 143(12).

**Answer:**
**Clause 3(xi) of the Companies (Auditor's Report) Order, 2020 (CARO 2020)** casts a three-part reporting duty on the statutory auditor regarding fraud:

**(a)** whether **any fraud by the company or any fraud on the company has been noticed or reported during the year**; if yes, the **nature and the amount involved** are to be indicated;

**(b)** whether any **report under sub-section (12) of Section 143** has been filed by the auditors in **Form ADT-4** as prescribed under Rule 13 of the Companies (Audit and Auditors) Rules, 2014 **with the Central Government**;

**(c)** whether the auditor has considered **whistle-blower complaints, if any, received during the year** by the company.

**Application to the facts —**

*Embezzlement of ₹42 lakh:* This is a **fraud on the company** noticed during the year. Under Clause 3(xi)(a) the auditor must report the fact of the fraud, its **nature** (misappropriation/embezzlement of funds by an employee) and the **amount involved (₹42 lakh).** The clause is not restricted to frauds committed by officers; it expressly covers "fraud on the company" of any amount, so materiality is not the test for reporting under CARO.

*Interplay with Section 143(12):* Section 143(12) requires the **statutory auditor** to report to the Central Government (Form ADT-4) only frauds **committed against the company by its officers or employees involving ₹1 crore or more.** The ₹42 lakh embezzlement is below the ₹1 crore threshold, so **no Section 143(12)/ADT-4 report is triggered**; the auditor instead reports it to the **Audit Committee or Board** under the second limb of Section 143(12) read with Rule 13, and discloses it in the **Board's Report**. Regardless of the ₹1 crore threshold, the fraud **must still be reported under CARO Clause 3(xi)(a).**

*Cost auditor's fraud report:* Clause 3(xi)(b) is confined to reports filed by the **auditors (statutory auditor)** under Section 143(12). A report filed by the **cost auditor** or the secretarial auditor is dealt with by them; however, the statutory auditor should **consider** such information as part of his risk assessment and evaluate whether it indicates a fraud requiring his own reporting under Section 143(12).

**Conclusion:** The ₹42 lakh embezzlement is reportable under CARO Clause 3(xi)(a) with its nature and amount, but being below ₹1 crore it does not attract Form ADT-4 under Section 143(12); the cost auditor's fraud report is not reportable under Clause 3(xi)(b) though it must inform the statutory auditor's own assessment.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the sharp distinction between the CARO reporting threshold (any amount) and the Section 143(12) ADT-4 threshold (₹1 crore, by officers/employees), and the point that Clause 3(xi)(b) covers only the statutory auditor's ADT-4, not the cost auditor's.)*

### Q75. Ch: Audit Report — CARO 2020 Resignation of Statutory Auditors (Clause 3(xviii)) (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the reporting requirement under Clause 3(xviii) of CARO 2020 relating to resignation of statutory auditors, and explain what the incoming auditor is expected to consider while reporting on this clause.

**Answer:**
**Clause 3(xviii) of CARO 2020** requires the auditor to report **"whether there has been any resignation of the statutory auditors during the year, if so, whether the auditor has taken into consideration the issues, objections or concerns raised by the outgoing auditors."**

**Purpose:** The clause is a governance safeguard. When a predecessor auditor resigns mid-tenure, there is a risk that the resignation was driven by disagreements with management on accounting, disclosure or the integrity of records. The clause obliges the **incoming (reporting) auditor** to satisfy the users that any red flags flagged by the outgoing auditor have not been ignored.

**What the incoming auditor should consider:**
- Whether there was in fact a **resignation** of the statutory auditor during the financial year under audit (as distinct from retirement on completion of term or non-reappointment).
- The **reasons for resignation** as stated by the outgoing auditor, including the statement of resignation filed by the outgoing auditor with the company and the Registrar in **Form ADT-3** under Section 140(2), and, for listed/specified companies, the detailed reasons required to be furnished to the Board/audit committee.
- Any **issues, objections or concerns** raised by the outgoing auditor — for example, denial of information, disagreement on a material accounting treatment, or doubts about management integrity — obtained through communication with the outgoing auditor (also required by the ICAI Code of Ethics before accepting the appointment).
- Whether the incoming auditor has **evaluated and addressed** those matters in planning and performing the current audit and in forming his opinion.

**Reporting:** If there has been a resignation, the auditor states the fact and reports whether he has considered the issues/objections/concerns raised. If there has been **no resignation** during the year, the auditor reports that the clause is **not applicable.**

**Conclusion:** Clause 3(xviii) ensures continuity of professional scrutiny by requiring the incoming auditor to confirm, on record, that concerns raised by a resigning predecessor were duly taken into account.

*(Full-marks tip: Link the clause to Form ADT-3 (Sec 140(2)) and to the mandatory communication with the previous auditor under the Code of Ethics — that connection distinguishes a top answer.)*

### Q76. Ch: Audit Report — CARO 2020 Financial Ratios & Material Uncertainty (Clause 3(xix)) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** From the following data of Corvus Textiles Ltd for FY 2025-26, examine the auditor's reporting responsibility under **Clause 3(xix)** of CARO 2020 and state the conclusion the auditor would reach.

| Particulars | ₹ in lakh |
|---|---|
| Current assets | 480 |
| Current liabilities | 600 |
| Financial liabilities maturing within next 12 months | 350 |
| Other information | Directors have disclosed doubt over meeting obligations; company negotiating fresh term loan |

**Answer:**
**Clause 3(xix) of CARO 2020** requires the auditor to report — "on the basis of the **financial ratios, ageing and expected dates of realisation of financial assets and payment of financial liabilities**, other information accompanying the financial statements, the auditor's knowledge of the Board of Directors and management plans, whether the auditor is of the opinion that **no material uncertainty exists** as on the date of the audit report that the company is **capable of meeting its liabilities existing at the date of balance sheet as and when they fall due within a period of one year** from the balance sheet date."

**WN-1 — Current (liquidity) ratio:**
Current Ratio = Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities = 480 ÷ 600 = **0.80 : 1**
A ratio below 1:1 indicates that current liabilities exceed current assets by ₹120 lakh, signalling a working-capital deficiency.

**WN-2 — Short-term obligation coverage:**
Financial liabilities falling due within 12 months = ₹350 lakh, whereas current assets available = ₹480 lakh. Although current assets nominally exceed the ₹350 lakh due, the overall current ratio is below unity and the directors have themselves expressed **doubt over meeting obligations**, with fresh financing only under **negotiation** (i.e., not yet secured).

The clause requires the auditor to form an opinion based on ratios, ageing, management plans and other information. Here:
- the current ratio of **0.80:1** is adverse;
- management has **disclosed doubt** over its ability to meet obligations — a clear indicator of stress; and

### Q77. Ch: Company Audit — Application of Section 147 (Marks: 4) [Application]

### Q78. Ch: Audit Report — Key Audit Matters (SA 701) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** What are "Key Audit Matters" under SA 701? Explain the process by which the auditor determines Key Audit Matters, and state to which audits the communication of Key Audit Matters is mandatory.

**Answer:**
**SA 701 "Communicating Key Audit Matters in the Independent Auditor's Report"** defines **Key Audit Matters (KAM)** as **"those matters that, in the auditor's professional judgement, were of most significance in the audit of the financial statements of the current period."** KAM are selected from matters communicated with **those charged with governance (TCWG).**

**Applicability:** Communication of KAM is **mandatory** for audits of the **complete sets of general purpose financial statements of listed entities** and where the auditor otherwise decides, or is required by law or regulation, to communicate KAM. For other entities, the auditor may voluntarily do so. KAM are **not** communicated when the auditor **disclaims an opinion**, unless required by law.

**Determination process (a funnel of professional judgement):** From the matters communicated with TCWG, the auditor determines KAM by narrowing down in two stages —

*Stage 1 — Matters requiring significant auditor attention:* The auditor takes into account —
- **(a)** areas of **higher assessed risk of material misstatement**, or **significant risks** identified in accordance with SA 315;
- **(b)** significant **auditor judgements** relating to areas in the financial statements that involved significant **management judgement**, including **accounting estimates** identified as having high estimation uncertainty; and
- **(c)** the effect on the audit of **significant events or transactions** that occurred during the period.

*Stage 2 — Matters of most significance:* From the above, the auditor determines which matters were of **most significance** in the audit — these are the Key Audit Matters.

**Reporting KAM:** Each KAM is described in a separate section titled **"Key Audit Matters"**, stating **why the matter was considered to be of most significance** (and thus a KAM) and **how the matter was addressed** in the audit, with a reference to the related disclosures, if any.

**KAM is not a substitute:** Communicating KAM is **not** a substitute for (i) disclosures required by the framework, (ii) the auditor expressing a **modified opinion** under SA 705 when warranted, (iii) reporting on **material uncertainty related to going concern** under SA 570, or (iv) a separate opinion on individual matters.

**Conclusion:** KAM are the matters of most significance selected, through a two-stage judgemental process from those communicated with TCWG; their communication is mandatory for listed entities' financial-statement audits and enhances the informational value of the auditor's report without diluting the opinion.

*(Full-marks tip: The three inputs of Stage 1 (higher risk/significant risks, significant judgement areas including estimates, significant events) plus the "not a substitute" caution are the marks — a bare definition scores poorly.)*

### Q79. Ch: Audit Report — Emphasis of Matter vs Other Matter Paragraph (SA 706) (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between an **Emphasis of Matter Paragraph** and an **Other Matter Paragraph** under SA 706 (Revised). Give one example of each.

**Answer:**
**SA 706 (Revised) "Emphasis of Matter Paragraphs and Other Matter Paragraphs in the Independent Auditor's Report"** provides additional communication mechanisms that do **not** modify the auditor's opinion.

An **Emphasis of Matter (EOM) paragraph** is a paragraph included in the auditor's report that refers to a matter **appropriately presented or disclosed in the financial statements** that, in the auditor's judgement, is of such **importance** that it is **fundamental to users' understanding** of the financial statements.

An **Other Matter (OM) paragraph** is a paragraph that refers to a matter **other than those presented or disclosed in the financial statements** that, in the auditor's judgement, is **relevant to users' understanding of the audit, the auditor's responsibilities or the auditor's report.**

| Basis | Emphasis of Matter Paragraph | Other Matter Paragraph |
|---|---|---|
| **Subject of the paragraph** | A matter **disclosed in** the financial statements | A matter **not disclosed in** the financial statements |
| **Purpose** | To draw attention to a matter **fundamental to users' understanding of the FS** | To draw attention to a matter relevant to understanding the **audit / auditor's responsibilities / report** |
| **Pre-condition** | Auditor must first have concluded the matter is not materially misstated; there must be sufficient appropriate evidence | Used where the matter is not required to be disclosed in the FS but is relevant to users |
| **Placement/heading** | Separate section headed **"Emphasis of Matter"** | Separate section headed **"Other Matter"** |
| **Example** | Drawing attention to a note disclosing an **uncertain outcome of exceptional litigation**, or the effects of a **major catastrophe/natural disaster** on the entity | Reporting that the **prior period financial statements were audited by a predecessor auditor**, or that the FS have been **prepared for a specific purpose/for one set of users** |

**Common condition:** For both, the matter should **not** be one that requires the opinion to be modified (SA 705) and, in the case of EOM, should **not** be a Key Audit Matter under SA 701.

**Conclusion:** An EOM paragraph highlights something **already disclosed in** and fundamental to the financial statements, whereas an OM paragraph highlights something **outside** the financial statements relevant to understanding the audit or the report — neither alters the audit opinion.

*(Full-marks tip: The pivot is "disclosed in the FS (EOM)" vs "not disclosed in the FS / relevant to the audit (OM)". Give a correct, distinct example for each; wrong examples (e.g., using going concern as an EOM example) lose marks.)*

### Q81. Ch: Audit Report — Qualified Opinion / Piecemeal Effect (SA 705) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** While auditing Halcyon Pharma Ltd for FY 2025-26 (profit before tax ₹9 crore), the auditor finds that closing inventory is overstated by ₹35 lakh due to inclusion of obsolete stock at cost, and management refuses to write it down. In all other respects the financial statements are properly prepared. Determine the type of opinion the auditor should express under SA 705, and draft the opening lines of the "Basis" paragraph.

**Answer:**
**Legal/standard basis:** Under **SA 705 (Revised)**, the auditor expresses a **Qualified Opinion** when either (a) the auditor, having obtained sufficient appropriate audit evidence, concludes that misstatements are **material but not pervasive**, or (b) the auditor is unable to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence, but the possible effects are material but not pervasive.

**WN-1 — Nature of the matter:** The auditor **has** obtained evidence — he knows the stock is obsolete and overstated by ₹35 lakh. This is therefore a case of a **known material misstatement**, not a scope limitation.

**WN-2 — Materiality and pervasiveness:**
Overstatement of inventory = ₹35 lakh, i.e. ₹0.35 crore.
As a proportion of profit before tax = 0.35 ÷ 9 = **3.9%** — this is **material.**
However, the misstatement is **confined to one line item** (inventory, and correspondingly profit); it does not represent a substantial proportion of the financial statements, is not confined only in the sense of being fundamental, and does not render the statements as a whole misleading. Hence it is **material but not pervasive.**

**Conclusion on opinion type:** Since the misstatement is material but not pervasive and arises from a **known misstatement**, the auditor should express a **Qualified Opinion** ("except for"). An adverse opinion would be inappropriate because the effect is not pervasive.

**Suggested drafting —**

*Heading:* **"Qualified Opinion"** — the opinion paragraph would read: "In our opinion and to the best of our information and according to the explanations given to us, **except for the effects of the matter described in the Basis for Qualified Opinion section**, the aforesaid financial statements give a true and fair view…"

*Basis paragraph* — **"Basis for Qualified Opinion":** "The company's closing inventory includes obsolete stock carried at cost which is overstated by ₹35 lakh, as the same has not been written down to its net realisable value as required by **AS 2 'Valuation of Inventories'.** Accordingly, closing inventory, profit before tax, and reserves and surplus are each overstated by ₹35 lakh. We conducted our audit in accordance with Standards on Auditing…"

**Conclusion:** The auditor should issue a **Qualified ("except for") Opinion**, with a "Basis for Qualified Opinion" paragraph quantifying the ₹35 lakh overstatement and citing the departure from AS 2.

*(Full-marks tip: Justify "material but not pervasive" with the ratio to PBT and quantify the effect on all affected line items (inventory, profit, reserves). Naming AS 2 as the breached standard and using "except for" wording clinches full marks.)*

### Q82. Ch: Company Audit — Application of Section 144 (Marks: 4) [Application]
**Question:** M/s Solace & Associates are the statutory auditors of Talon Logistics Ltd. Examine, with reasons, whether the firm can accept the following assignments from the company during the tenure of its statutory audit:
(i) designing and implementing a new financial information system for the company;
(ii) representing the company before the GST authorities in a tax matter;
(iii) rendering internal audit services **through its associate firm**, M/s Solace Advisory LLP.

**Answer:**
**Governing provision:** **Section 144** of the Companies Act, 2013 permits the auditor to render only such other services as are **approved by the Board or the audit committee**, but such services shall **not** include the nine specified categories — which include **internal audit** and **design and implementation of any financial information system.** The **Explanation** clarifies that "directly or indirectly" includes services rendered by a **firm through its parent, subsidiary or associate entity.**

| Assignment | Permissible? | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| (i) Design & implement financial information system | **No** | Expressly listed as a prohibited service under Sec 144; would create a self-review threat as the auditor would later audit a system he designed |
| (ii) Representation before GST authorities | **Yes**, subject to Board/audit-committee approval | Tax representation is not among the nine prohibited services; it is a permissible "other service" if approved; it must also be independent of "management services" and must not compromise independence |
| (iii) Internal audit through associate LLP | **No** | Internal audit is a prohibited service; and by the Explanation, rendering it "indirectly" through an **associate entity** (Solace Advisory LLP) is equally barred — routing a prohibited service through an associate does not cure the prohibition |

**Conclusion:** Assignments (i) and (iii) are **prohibited** under Section 144 — (i) directly and (iii) because the "directly or indirectly" Explanation catches services routed through an associate entity. Assignment (ii) — GST representation — is **permissible** provided it is approved by the Board or audit committee and does not otherwise impair independence.

*(Full-marks tip: The scoring point in (iii) is the "directly or indirectly / through associate entity" Explanation — candidates who allow it because a separate LLP renders it lose the mark.)*

### Q84. Ch: Audit Report — Elements of the Independent Auditor's Report (SA 700) (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** SA 700 (Revised) prescribes the form and content of the independent auditor's report. Enumerate, in the order in which they appear, the basic elements of an auditor's report on financial statements of a company, briefly describing the content of each.

**Answer:**
**SA 700 (Revised) "Forming an Opinion and Reporting on Financial Statements"** requires the auditor's report to be **in writing** and to contain the following basic elements, ordinarily in the sequence set out below:

1. **Title —** The report shall have a title that clearly indicates that it is the report of an **independent auditor** (e.g., "Independent Auditor's Report"), affirming that the auditor has met the ethical requirements as to independence.

2. **Addressee —** The report shall be **addressed** as required by the circumstances of the engagement — for a company, ordinarily **"To the Members of … Ltd."**

3. **Opinion section —** Placed **first**, it identifies the entity, states that the financial statements have been audited, identifies the title of each statement, refers to the notes/summary of significant accounting policies, specifies the date/period covered, and expresses the auditor's **opinion** on whether the financial statements give a true and fair view in accordance with the applicable financial reporting framework.

4. **Basis for Opinion —** States that the audit was conducted in accordance with **SAs**, refers to the auditor's responsibilities section, states that the auditor is **independent** and has fulfilled ethical responsibilities, and that the **audit evidence obtained is sufficient and appropriate.**

5. **Material Uncertainty Related to Going Concern (if applicable) —** A separate section under this heading where such uncertainty exists and is adequately disclosed (SA 570).

6. **Key Audit Matters —** For listed entities (and where otherwise required), a section describing the matters of most significance in the audit (SA 701).

7. **Other Information (SA 720), where applicable —** Reporting on the other information included in the annual report.

8. **Responsibilities of Management and Those Charged with Governance for the Financial Statements —** Describes management's responsibility for preparation and fair presentation of the FS, internal control, and assessment of **going concern.**

9. **Auditor's Responsibilities for the Audit of the Financial Statements —** States the objective of **reasonable assurance** and that the report is issued, describes the concept of **material misstatement**, and (for detailed reporting) the auditor's exercise of professional judgement/scepticism, evaluation of estimates, going concern conclusion, and communication with TCWG.

10. **Report on Other Legal and Regulatory Requirements —** For a company audit, the auditor's reporting under **Section 143(3)** of the Companies Act, 2013 and under the **CARO 2020**, presented as a separate section with an appropriate sub-title.

11. **Signature of the Auditor —** The report is **signed** in the personal name of the auditor and, where the firm is appointed, in the firm's name as well, mentioning the **membership number** and the **firm registration number (FRN).**

12. **Place of signature —** The city/location where the report is signed.

13. **Date of the report —** Dated **no earlier** than the date on which the auditor has obtained sufficient appropriate audit evidence, including evidence that all statements comprising the FS have been prepared and TCWG have taken responsibility for them.

Additionally, for company audits the auditor states in the Opinion section the reference to the **Unique Document Identification Number (UDIN)** as required by ICAI.

**Conclusion:** The auditor's report is a standardised document whose elements — from the "Independent Auditor's Report" title through the signature, place and date — together communicate the scope, basis and result of the audit; the **Opinion section appears first**, immediately followed by the **Basis for Opinion**.

*(Full-marks tip: List the elements in the SA 700 order with the Opinion section first and Basis for Opinion immediately after; mention the Sec 143(3)/CARO "Report on Other Legal and Regulatory Requirements" section and the signature details (membership no., FRN, UDIN) — these are the company-specific marks.)*

### Q85. Ch: Professional Ethics & Independence — Meaning of "Chartered Accountant in Practice" (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the meaning of a "member deemed to be in practice" under the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949. State whether a member who renders management consultancy services only, and never signs an audit report, is to be regarded as being "in practice".

**Answer:**
Section 2(2) of the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949 defines when a member of the Institute shall be **deemed "to be in practice"**. A member is deemed to be in practice when, individually or in partnership with chartered accountants in practice, he, in consideration of remuneration received or to be received:

(i) engages himself in the practice of accountancy; or
(ii) offers to perform or performs services involving the auditing or verification of financial transactions, books, accounts or records, or the preparation, verification or certification of financial accounting and related statements or holds himself out to the public as an accountant; or
(iii) renders professional services or assistance in matters of principle or detail relating to accounting procedure or the recording, presentation or certification of financial facts or data; or
(iv) renders such other services as, in the opinion of the Council, are or may be rendered by a chartered accountant in practice.

By virtue of the **Explanation to Section 2(2)**, an associate or fellow of the Institute who is a salaried employee of a chartered accountant in practice or of a firm of such chartered accountants shall, notwithstanding such employment, be deemed to be in practice for the limited purpose of the training of articled assistants.

The Council has, under clause (iv), resolved that the members rendering **management consultancy and other services** (as spelt out in the Council's statement) shall also be regarded as being in practice. Therefore, a member who renders only management consultancy services covered by the Council's list, for remuneration, **is deemed to be in practice** even though he never signs an audit report.

**Conclusion:** The expression "in practice" is not confined to attest/audit work; a member rendering permitted management consultancy services for remuneration is a member in practice under Section 2(2) read with the Council's resolution.

### Q86. Ch: Professional Ethics & Independence — Practice vs. Employment (Marks: 4) [Application]

The core distinction lies between a **member in practice** and a **member in employment (in service)**.

### Q87. Ch: Professional Ethics & Independence — Ceiling on Tax Audit Assignments (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** M/s Aakash & Associates is a firm of chartered accountants having **three** partners — Mr. A, Mr. B and Mr. C. Mr. C is also a partner in another firm, M/s Prime & Co., which has two partners. During the assessment year the firms accepted the following tax audits under section 44AB:

| Firm | Tax audits accepted (excluding sec. 44AD/44ADA/44AE audits) |
|---|---|
| M/s Aakash & Associates | 130 |
| M/s Prime & Co. (signed by Mr. C) | 30 |

Determine, with reference to the Council General Guidelines, 2008, the maximum number of tax audits M/s Aakash & Associates can accept, and whether any ceiling has been breached.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Specified number of tax audits (the ceiling):**
Under the Council General Guidelines, 2008 (Chapter VI — "Tax Audit Assignments under section 44AB of the Income-tax Act, 1961"), a member in practice shall not accept, in a financial year, **more than the "specified number" of tax audit assignments**. The specified number is **60** per member. Audits conducted under **sections 44AD, 44ADA and 44AE** (presumptive taxation) are **excluded** from this count.

**WN-2 — Ceiling for a firm:**
In the case of a firm of chartered accountants, the specified number of tax audits is **60 multiplied by the number of partners** of the firm. However, the ceiling of 60 also operates on **each individual partner** across all the firms in which he is a partner.

- Ceiling for M/s Aakash & Associates = 60 × 3 partners = **180 tax audits**.

**WN-3 — Individual ceiling of Mr. C (partner in two firms):**
Mr. C's personal ceiling of 60 is shared across both firms. Tax audits already signed by / attributable to Mr. C through Prime & Co. = 30. Hence Mr. C can still take up to 60 − 30 = 30 more in his own name, but this does **not** reduce the firm-level ceiling of Aakash & Associates as a distinct entity, provided the audits are distributed so that no single partner signs more than 60.

**Computation of maximum audits Aakash & Associates may accept:**

| Particulars | No. |
|---|---|
| Firm ceiling (60 × 3 partners) | 180 |
| Tax audits already accepted by the firm | 130 |
| **Balance the firm can still accept** | **50** |

**Answer:** M/s Aakash & Associates can accept a **maximum of 180** tax audits in the year; having accepted 130, it can accept **50 more**. There is **no breach** of the ceiling either at the firm level (130 < 180) or for Mr. C individually (30 < 60), so long as no single partner signs more than 60 reports.

*(Full-marks tip: state clearly that the specified number is 60, that 44AD/44ADA/44AE audits are excluded, and that the firm ceiling is 60 × number of partners; forgetting the exclusion or using an old figure loses marks.)*

- Company PAN No.

- Directors' names and addresses; Directors' Identification Number (DIN).

**D. If the engagement is authorised by an individual on behalf of the entity**
- Name of the person authorising the engagement.

**Conclusion:** KYC norms are mandatory; the member must obtain and retain the above corporate-client information before accepting the assignment, thereby guarding the profession against association with illicit dealings.

### Q89. Ch: Professional Ethics & Independence — Board of Discipline vs. Disciplinary Committee (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between the **Board of Discipline** and the **Disciplinary Committee** constituted under the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949, with regard to their constitution, jurisdiction and the punishments they can award.

**Answer:**
The disciplinary mechanism of the Institute operates through two bodies — the **Board of Discipline (Section 21A)** and the **Disciplinary Committee (Section 21B)** — depending on the Schedule under which the alleged misconduct falls. The **Director (Discipline)** first forms a *prima facie* opinion on receipt of a complaint or information under **Section 21** and refers the case to the appropriate body.

| Basis | Board of Discipline (Sec. 21A) | Disciplinary Committee (Sec. 21B) |
|---|---|---|
| Deals with misconduct falling under | The **First Schedule** | The **Second Schedule** or **both Schedules** |
| Constitution | A person of eminence with legal/professional background nominated by Central Government as **presiding officer**, and two members (one member of the Council, one nominee of the Central Government) | A person nominated by Central Government as **presiding officer** and members — the body comprises members of the Council and Government nominees as specified |
| Procedure trigger | Director (Discipline) opines member is *prima facie* **guilty of First Schedule misconduct** | Director (Discipline) opines member is *prima facie* **guilty of Second Schedule / both-Schedules misconduct** |
| Punishments it may award | (a) Reprimand the member; (b) Remove name from Register of Members up to a **maximum of 3 months**; (c) Impose fine up to **₹1,00,000** | (a) Reprimand the member; (b) Remove name from Register of Members **permanently or for any duration**; (c) Impose fine up to **₹5,00,000** |

Where the Director (Discipline) is of the opinion that the member is **not** guilty, he places the matter before the Board of Discipline; if the Board agrees, the complaint is closed, and if it disagrees it may advise the Director to further investigate.

**Conclusion:** First Schedule cases (generally less serious) go to the **Board of Discipline** with lighter powers (up to 3 months removal / ₹1,00,000 fine), whereas Second Schedule (and both-Schedule) cases go to the **Disciplinary Committee** with graver powers (permanent removal / ₹5,00,000 fine).

*(Full-marks tip: the marks lie in the exact monetary limits (₹1,00,000 vs ₹5,00,000), the 3-month cap for the Board, and the Schedule-wise jurisdiction — mixing these up is the standard error.)*

### Q90. Ch: Professional Ethics & Independence — Procedure on Prima Facie Opinion (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** A complaint is received by the Institute alleging that CA. Vikram, a member in practice, has committed professional misconduct. Explain the role of the **Director (Discipline)** on receipt of such a complaint, and the procedure to be followed where he forms a *prima facie* opinion of guilt under the **Second Schedule**.

**Answer:**
The disciplinary process is governed by **Sections 21, 21A and 21B** of the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949, read with the Chartered Accountants (Procedure of Investigations of Professional and Other Misconduct and Conduct of Cases) Rules, 2007.

**Role of the Director (Discipline) [Section 21]:**
On receipt of a complaint (in the prescribed Form) or on information relating to any misconduct of a member, the **Director (Discipline)** shall **arrive at a *prima facie* opinion** on the occurrence of the alleged misconduct. In forming this opinion he examines the complaint, the written statement of the respondent and the material on record.

**Where the misconduct falls under the First Schedule** — the Director places the matter before the **Board of Discipline (Section 21A)**.

**Where the misconduct falls under the Second Schedule or both the Schedules** — the Director places the matter before the **Disciplinary Committee (Section 21B)**.

Applying this to CA. Vikram, since the Director (Discipline) forms a *prima facie* opinion of guilt under the **Second Schedule**, the following procedure applies:

1. The matter is referred to the **Disciplinary Committee** under Section 21B.
2. The Disciplinary Committee affords the member an **opportunity of being heard** before making any order (principles of natural justice).
3. If the Committee, on hearing, is of the opinion that the member is **guilty**, it shall **afford an opportunity of being heard on the quantum of punishment** and thereafter pass an order.
4. The Committee may (a) reprimand the member; (b) remove his name from the Register **permanently or for such period as it thinks fit**; and/or (c) impose a fine up to **₹5,00,000**.

**Conclusion:** The Director (Discipline) is the gatekeeper who forms the *prima facie* opinion; a Second-Schedule *prima facie* finding routes the case to the **Disciplinary Committee**, which after a fair hearing may impose punishment up to permanent removal and a fine of ₹5,00,000.

*(Full-marks tip: emphasise the Director (Discipline)'s "prima facie opinion" as the pivot and that the guilty member gets a separate hearing on the quantum of punishment; skipping the natural-justice step is a common omission.)*

### Q91. Ch: Professional Ethics & Independence — Fees Based on Percentage / Contingent Fees (Marks: 4) [Application]
**Question:** CA. Naveen, in practice, accepts an engagement to recover an outstanding debt for his client and agrees that his professional fee will be **15% of the amount actually recovered**, payable only if the recovery succeeds. Examine whether he has committed any professional misconduct.

**Answer:**
The issue is governed by **Clause (10) of Part I of the First Schedule** to the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949, which provides that a chartered accountant in practice shall be deemed to be guilty of professional misconduct if he **charges or offers to charge, accepts or offers to accept, in respect of any professional employment, fees which are based on a percentage of profits or which are contingent upon the findings or results of such employment**.

The rationale is that fees contingent on the outcome compromise the objectivity and independence of the member.

However, **exceptions** are recognised by the Council under Regulation 192 of the Chartered Accountants Regulations, 1988, where fees may be based on percentage/results — for example:
- In the case of a **receiver or liquidator**, fees may be based on a percentage of the realisation or disbursement of assets;
- In the case of an **auditor of a cooperative society**, fees may be based on a percentage of paid-up capital / working capital / gross or net income / profits;
- Fees of a **valuer** for the purposes of direct taxes and duties may be based on a percentage of the value of property.

Applying the law: recovery of a debt for a fee of 15% **contingent on the amount recovered** does **not** fall within any of the permitted exceptions. It is a fee both **based on a percentage** and **contingent on the result** of the engagement.

**Conclusion:** CA. Naveen is **guilty of professional misconduct** under **Clause (10) of Part I of the First Schedule**, as the arrangement is a prohibited contingent/percentage-based fee not covered by the Regulation 192 exceptions.

### Q92. Ch: Professional Ethics & Independence — Misconduct of a Member in Service (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** CA. Suhana is a member of the Institute employed as an accountant with Orbit Textiles Ltd. She does not hold a Certificate of Practice. ### Q93. Ch: Audit of Different Entities — Audit of a Charitable Trust / NGO (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** You have been appointed as the auditor of a **charitable trust** running schools and hospitals, registered under section 12A of the Income-tax Act, 1961. Draft the important audit considerations you would keep in mind while auditing its **receipts** and **application of income**.

**Answer:**
A charitable trust (NGO) may be constituted under the **Indian Trusts Act, 1882**, or as a society under the **Societies Registration Act, 1860**, or as a **Section 8 company** under the Companies Act, 2013. Where its income is claimed as exempt, it is registered under **Section 12A / 12AB** of the Income-tax Act, 1961, and the audit report is furnished in **Form 10B**. The auditor must plan the audit around the twin tests of **genuine receipt** and **application of income for charitable purposes**.

**A. Verification of Receipts / Income**
1. **Grants and donations:** Verify grants/donations from the correspondence, grant sanction letters and counterfoils of receipts; ensure **corpus donations** (donations with a specific direction to form part of corpus) are separately recorded, as they enjoy distinct tax treatment.
2. **Foreign contributions:** Verify that any foreign contribution is received and utilised in accordance with the **Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act, 2010** and is credited to the designated FCRA bank account.
3. **Fees / user charges:** Vouch school fees and hospital charges with the fee registers, patient bills and admission records; test the completeness of collections.
4. **Investment income:** Verify interest and dividends with reference to the investment schedule; ensure investments are in the modes specified under **Section 11(5)** of the Income-tax Act.

**B. Verification of Application of Income**
5. **Charitable objects:** Ensure that income has been applied **only for the objects of the trust** as per the trust deed; expenditure outside the objects is not a valid application.
6. **85% application test:** Verify that at least **85% of the income** has been applied for charitable purposes during the year, or that the shortfall is validly accumulated / deemed applied under **Section 11(2)** with Form 10 filed.
7. **Capital expenditure and assets:** Verify additions to fixed assets (school buildings, hospital equipment) with title deeds, contracts and physical verification.
8. **Payments to related persons:** Scrutinise payments to trustees / specified persons under **Section 13** to ensure no part of income enures to their benefit, which would jeopardise exemption.
9. **Books and statutory records:** Confirm proper maintenance of books of account, minute book of the governing body, and register of investments; check compliance with the registering statute (e.g., filing with the Registrar of Societies).

**Conclusion:** The auditor must be satisfied both that receipts are genuinely brought to account and that income is applied to charitable objects to the extent required, and must report in Form 10B accordingly.

### Q94. Ch: Audit of Different Entities — Audit of a Cinema / Multiplex (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** State the special points to be examined by the auditor while conducting the audit of the **collections and expenses of a cinema theatre**.

**Answer:**
The audit of a cinema theatre focuses primarily on the **completeness of box-office collections** and the **genuineness of expenditure on exhibition of films**, since cash sales predominate and the risk of leakage is high.

**A. Collections (Box-office receipts)**
1. Verify that **entrance to the cinema hall is only through printed, pre-numbered tickets**, and that tickets are **serially numbered** and issued from bound books or through a computerised ticketing system.
2. Verify that there are **two persons** manning the entrance and that no person is allowed to enter without a ticket, except with a **free pass** issued under proper authority; ensure a proper record of free passes / complimentary tickets is maintained.

4. Verify that the **statement of daily collections** is checked and cash is deposited daily into the bank; confirm that advance booking collections are separately recorded and reconciled to the shows subsequently held.
5. Verify that the **Goods and Services Tax / entertainment tax / local body cess** collected has been correctly accounted for and deposited with the authorities.

**B. Expenditure**
6. Vouch **film hire / distributor's share** with the agreements entered into with the distributors; ensure the share is computed on the correct basis (percentage of net collections or minimum guarantee).
7. Vouch **advertisement, slide charges and publicity** expenditure with supporting bills.
8. Verify **salaries, electricity, air-conditioning and maintenance** costs with supporting records, and confirm capital expenditure on projection/sound equipment is capitalised.
9. Verify the collections from **canteen, parking and advertisement slides** exhibited before the show.

**Conclusion:** The auditor's principal assurance objective is to establish that **all admissions are through numbered tickets and every rupee of collection is accounted for**, and that distributors' share and other expenses are genuine and properly authorised.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner rewards the ticket-control chain — serially numbered tickets, two persons at the gate, free-pass register, and reconciliation of tickets to cash; a plain list of expenses without the collection-completeness angle scores low.)*

### Q95. Ch: Audit of Different Entities — Audit of a Club: Entrance Fees & Subscriptions (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** You are auditing "The Riverside Recreation Club". During the year the club admitted new members and collected entrance fees and annual subscriptions. Explain how you would verify (a) **entrance / admission fees** and (b) **members' subscriptions**, and how you would deal with **arrears and advance** subscriptions.

**Answer:**
A members' club is generally a **non-profit organisation**; its principal recurring receipts are **entrance (admission) fees** and **annual subscriptions**. The audit objective is **completeness of income** and correct **period cut-off**.

**(a) Entrance / Admission Fees**
1. Vouch the entrance fees received with the **counterfoils of receipts** issued and reconcile them with the **register of members** — every new member admitted during the year should be traceable to an entrance-fee receipt.
2. Verify the **rate of entrance fee** with the rules/bye-laws of the club and any resolution of the managing committee, and check that the correct amount has been collected from each new member.
3. Ensure that the accounting treatment (whether entrance fee is treated as **capital receipt** or **revenue income**) is consistent with the club's adopted policy and applied uniformly.

**(b) Members' Subscriptions**
4. Verify subscriptions received with the **counterfoils / receipts** and the **subscription register / members' ledger**; ensure that the amount collected agrees with the rate fixed by the bye-laws.
5. Reconcile the total subscriptions receivable (number of members × annual subscription) with the amount actually accounted for, so as to detect any omission.

**(c) Arrears and Advance Subscriptions (cut-off)**
6. Ensure that **subscriptions in arrears** (due but not received for the current year) are properly recorded as **income accrued** and shown as receivable, and that **provision for irrecoverable arrears** is made where recovery is doubtful.
7. Ensure that **subscriptions received in advance** for the next year are **not credited to the current year's income**; they must be carried forward as a **liability (subscription received in advance)** so that only the current year's subscription is recognised as income.
8. Cross-check the opening arrears/advance with the current year's collections to confirm correct roll-forward.

**Conclusion:** The auditor must ensure that the club has accounted for **all** entrance fees and subscriptions due, that the correct rates per the bye-laws are applied, and that **arrears are recognised as income and advances are deferred**, giving a true and fair view of the club's income.

*(Full-marks tip: the marks are earned by the cut-off treatment — arrears recognised as income due, advance subscriptions deferred as a liability — and reconciliation with the members' register; simply "vouch the receipts" is insufficient.)*

### Q96. Ch: Audit of Different Entities — Special Features in Audit of a Cooperative Society (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** State the **special features** to be considered by an auditor while auditing the accounts of a **cooperative society** under the Cooperative Societies Act, 1912 (or the relevant State Act).

**Answer:**
The audit of a cooperative society is conducted under **Section 17 of the Cooperative Societies Act, 1912** (or the corresponding State Cooperative Societies Act). In addition to the normal audit procedures, the auditor must examine certain matters peculiar to cooperative societies:

1. **Examination of overdue debts:** Overdue debts have a significant bearing on the working of a society. The auditor should **classify overdue debts** from the date they became due into (i) less than six months, (ii) six months to five years, and (iii) more than five years, and report on the possibility of their recovery, since overdue debts affect the recovery of loans and the valuation of assets.

2. **Overdue interest:** Overdue interest should be **excluded from interest outstanding and accrued due** while computing profit. The auditor should ensure that overdue interest (interest on debts overdue) is **not credited to profit** but is separately shown, as its realisation is doubtful.

3. **Certification of Bad Debts:** No bad debt can be written off unless the same is **certified as bad by the auditor**, where the State Act or Rules so require. The auditor should therefore examine the process of writing off bad debts and satisfy himself before certifying.

4. **Valuation of assets and liabilities:** The auditor should verify the existence, ownership and valuation of assets; although there are no specific provisions like the Companies Act, the general principles of correct valuation must be observed.

5. **Adherence to cooperative principles:** The auditor should ascertain, in general terms, how far the objects of the society are achieved — the **benefit to members** rather than the maximisation of profit is the touchstone; the auditor examines whether the society is working for the **mutual benefit** of members.

6. **Observance of provisions of the Act and Rules:** The auditor should point out **infringements** of the provisions of the Act, Rules and bye-laws — e.g., restrictions on shareholding (Sec. 5), loans to non-members (Sec. 29), borrowings (Sec. 30), investment of funds (Sec. 32), and appropriation of profits including transfer to Reserve Fund (Sec. 33) and contribution to charitable purposes (Sec. 34/43 as applicable).

7. **Discussion of draft audit report with managing committee:** Before finalising, the auditor should discuss the draft report with the managing committee to settle facts and obtain their comments.

**Conclusion:** Beyond an ordinary audit, the cooperative-society auditor must give particular attention to **overdue debts and interest, certification of bad debts, adherence to cooperative principles and statutory restrictions**, and finally award an **audit classification (rating)** to the society.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner looks for the society-specific features — overdue-debt classification, overdue interest exclusion, bad-debt certification and statutory restrictions — not a generic asset/liability verification list.)*

### Q97. Ch: Audit of Different Entities — Cooperative Society: Overdue Debts & Special Report to the Registrar (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** While auditing the "Sunrise Cooperative Credit Society", you notice that (i) a large portion of loans granted to members is long overdue, (ii) interest on such overdue loans has been credited to the Profit & Loss Account, and (iii) you have unearthed personal profiteering by two committee members. State how you would deal with each matter and whether a **special report** to the Registrar is warranted.

**Answer:**
The audit is governed by the Cooperative Societies Act (**Section 17**), and the treatment of overdue amounts and the auditor's special reporting obligation are settled features of a cooperative-society audit.

**(i) Overdue loans (overdue debts):**
The auditor should **classify the overdue debts** from the date they fell due into slabs — less than six months, six months to five years, and above five years — and report on the **prospects of recovery**. A large proportion of long-overdue loans indicates poor recovery and doubtful realisability; the auditor should assess whether adequate **provision** has been made and comment in the audit report, as overdue debts materially affect the valuation of assets and the society's financial position.

**(ii) Overdue interest credited to Profit & Loss Account:**
**Overdue interest** (interest accrued on debts which are themselves overdue) should be **excluded from profit** because its realisation is uncertain. Crediting it to the Profit & Loss Account **overstates the profit**. The auditor should require that the overdue interest be **reversed / excluded** while working out the divisible profit, and should qualify the report if the society does not do so.

**(iii) Personal profiteering by committee members — Special Report:**
Where, in the course of audit, the auditor notices matters such as **personal profiteering by members of the managing committee**, **fraud, mismanagement, or acts prejudicial to the interest of the society or its members**, he is duty-bound to make a **special report to the Registrar** so that the Registrar may take necessary action. Instances warranting a special report include:
- Personal profiteering by members of the managing committee in transactions of the society;
- Detection of fraud relating to expenses, sales, purchases or property of the society;
- Mismanagement causing financial loss;
- Any act prejudicial to the security of the society's funds.

**Conclusion:** The auditor should (i) classify and report on the overdue debts and provisioning, (ii) exclude overdue interest from profit and qualify if necessary, and (iii) since there is **personal profiteering by committee members**, submit a **Special Report to the Registrar** in addition to the regular audit report.

*(Full-marks tip: link the personal-profiteering fact directly to the auditor's duty to make a "Special Report to the Registrar", and treat overdue interest as an exclusion from profit — these two triggers are exactly what the examiner tests.)*

### Q98. Ch: Audit of Different Entities — Audit of a Local Body (Municipality) (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the **objectives** of audit of a **Local Body** (such as a Municipality), and the principal points to be examined by the auditor regarding its **expenditure**.

**Answer:**
A **Local Body** (Municipal Corporation, Municipality or Panchayat) is a self-governing institution at the local level, functioning under the relevant State Municipal / Panchayati Raj Act, and largely dependent on **grants, taxes and duties**. The audit is generally an **external audit** conducted by the Local Fund Audit wing of the State Government or, where entrusted, by the **Comptroller and Auditor General (C&AG)** or a chartered accountant.

**A. Objectives of audit of a Local Body**
1. **Reporting on fairness** of the contents and presentation of the financial statements.
2. **Detection and prevention of errors and frauds**.
3. **Verifying the existence and effectiveness of internal controls** and reporting on weaknesses.
4. Ensuring that the expenditure is incurred **with proper sanction** and out of the fund to which it is properly chargeable.
5. Ensuring **value for money / propriety** — that money is spent economically and for the purpose intended.

**B. Points to be examined regarding expenditure ("expenditure audit")**
1. **Authorisation and sanction:** The auditor should verify that every item of expenditure is **incurred under a proper sanction** of the competent authority — either a special sanction for the specific item or under a general delegation of powers.
2. **Provision in the budget / grant:** He should ensure that there exists a **provision of funds (budget grant)** for the expenditure, duly authorised by the competent authority, and that expenditure does not exceed the sanctioned grant.
3. **Chargeable to the correct fund/head:** Verify that the expenditure is charged to the **appropriate head of account** and is a legitimate charge on the fund.
4. **Propriety of expenditure:** Confirm that there is **no wastage** and that the amount spent is **not more than the occasion demands** — the expenditure should be such as a person of ordinary prudence would incur (the principle of propriety).
5. **Supporting documents:** Vouch the expenditure with bills, muster rolls, work orders, contracts and measurement books (for works expenditure), and confirm the receipt of goods/services.

**Conclusion:** The audit of a local body is directed at **fair presentation, prevention of fraud, and above all the regularity and propriety of expenditure** — that money is spent with sanction, within budget grants, and without waste.

*(Full-marks tip: bring in the "propriety / value-for-money" angle and the sanction-plus-budget-provision test for expenditure — these distinguish government/local-body audit from an ordinary commercial audit and are heavily rewarded.)*

### Q99. Ch: Audit of Different Entities — Appointment & Qualification of a Cooperative Society Auditor (Marks: 4) [Application]
**Question:** The managing committee of "Green Valley Cooperative Housing Society" proposes to appoint Mr. Deepak, a retired bank clerk who is **not** a chartered accountant, as its auditor, and to allow him to also handle the society's day-to-day accounting. Comment on the validity of this proposal with reference to the appointment and qualification of a cooperative society's auditor.

**Answer:**
The appointment and qualification of the auditor of a cooperative society are governed by **Section 17 of the Cooperative Societies Act, 1912** (or the corresponding State Act).

**Appointment:** Under the Act, the audit of every registered cooperative society is to be conducted by the **Registrar of Cooperative Societies** or by a **person authorised by the Registrar** by general or special order in this behalf. Thus, the power of appointing/authorising the auditor essentially vests in the **Registrar**, not solely in the managing committee; a society cannot appoint just any person of its own choice without such authorisation.

**Qualification:** The person conducting the audit must possess the **qualification prescribed** — a **chartered accountant** within the meaning of the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949, or a **person holding a Government Diploma in Cooperative Accounts / Cooperation and Accountancy**, or a person who has served in the Cooperative Department and is authorised by the Registrar. A mere retired bank clerk without any such prescribed qualification or authorisation is **not eligible**.

**Independence:** The auditor must be **independent** of the accounting function. A person who **maintains or writes the day-to-day books of account** of the society cannot audit the same accounts, as this destroys independence and objectivity.

Applying the law:
- Mr. Deepak is neither a chartered accountant nor shown to hold the prescribed cooperative-accounts qualification or the Registrar's authorisation — hence **not qualified** to be appointed.
- Allowing him to simultaneously maintain the accounts and audit them **violates the fundamental principle of independence**.

**Conclusion:** The proposal is **invalid**. The auditor must be appointed/authorised by the Registrar, must possess the prescribed qualification, and must not also keep the society's books.

*(Full-marks tip: cover all three limbs — appointment by/through the Registrar, prescribed qualification, and the independence bar on the accountant auditing his own books; missing the independence point is the usual gap.)*

### Q100. Ch: Audit of Different Entities — Charitable Trust u/s 12A: Audit & Reporting Considerations (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** "Helping Hands Trust", registered under section 12A of the Income-tax Act, 1961, received corpus donations, applied part of its income for objects, and accumulated the balance. As the auditor furnishing the report in **Form 10B**, state the specific matters you would verify before signing the report.

**Answer:**
Where a trust registered under **Section 12A / 12AB** claims exemption of its income, the auditor's report is required in **Form 10B** under **Section 12A(1)(b)** read with the Income-tax Rules. Before signing, the auditor must satisfy himself on the following trust-specific matters:

1. **Registration status:** Verify the **certificate of registration under Section 12A/12AB** (and, where donors seek deduction, under **Section 80G**) and confirm it is valid and subsisting for the year.

2. **Corpus donations:** Verify that donations received **with a specific written direction** that they shall form part of the corpus are so recorded and are **treated as capital receipts**; ensure they are separately identifiable and, where applicable, invested/deposited in the modes specified under **Section 11(5)**.

3. **Application of income (85% test):** Verify that at least **85% of the income** derived from property held under trust has been **applied for charitable/religious purposes** in India during the year. Confirm that "application" is to the objects of the trust as per the trust deed.

4. **Accumulation of income:** Where income is **accumulated / set apart** (up to 15% freely, and beyond that under **Section 11(2)**), verify that the amount accumulated under Section 11(2) is **invested in the specified modes** and that **Form 10** has been filed specifying the purpose and period (not exceeding five years) of accumulation.

5. **Section 13 — no benefit to specified persons:** Verify that **no part of the income or property** has been used or applied, directly or indirectly, for the benefit of the **author/trustee/specified persons** under Section 13, as this would forfeit the exemption.

6. **Investments (Section 11(5)):** Verify that the funds are invested only in the **modes/forms specified in Section 11(5)**; investment outside these modes jeopardises exemption.

7. **Books and vouchers:** Verify that proper books of account are maintained, receipts and payments are duly vouched, and the statement of income annexed to Form 10B agrees with the audited accounts.

**Conclusion:** Only after verifying valid 12A registration, correct treatment of corpus donations, satisfaction of the 85% application test, proper accumulation with Form 10 and Section 11(5) investment, and absence of any Section 13 benefit, should the auditor sign the **Form 10B** report.

*(Full-marks tip: tie every verification to a section — 11(5) investment modes, 11(2)/Form 10 accumulation, Section 13 benefit test — and mention Form 10B; a generic "verify income and expenditure" answer will not fetch full marks.)*
