### Q1. Ch: Nature, Objective & Scope — Reasonable vs Absolute Assurance (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** "An audit conducted in accordance with the Standards on Auditing provides *reasonable* assurance, but it can never provide *absolute* assurance." Explain the concept of reasonable assurance and give the reasons (inherent limitations) because of which absolute assurance is impossible to attain.

**Answer:**
As per **SA 200 "Overall Objectives of the Independent Auditor and the Conduct of an Audit in Accordance with Standards on Auditing"**, one of the overall objectives of the auditor is to obtain **reasonable assurance** about whether the financial statements as a whole are free from material misstatement, whether due to fraud or error, thereby enabling the auditor to express an opinion.

**Reasonable assurance** is a **high level of assurance**, but **not an absolute level of assurance**. It is obtained when the auditor has obtained **sufficient appropriate audit evidence** to reduce audit risk (the risk that the auditor expresses an inappropriate opinion when the financial statements are materially misstated) to an **acceptably low level**.

Reasonable assurance can never rise to the level of **absolute assurance** because of certain **inherent limitations of an audit**, which arise from the following:

| Source of limitation | Explanation |
|---|---|
| **Nature of financial reporting** | Preparation of financial statements involves **judgement** by management and the use of **accounting estimates** (e.g., provisions, fair values) which are subjective and depend on future outcomes that are inherently uncertain. |
| **Nature of audit procedures** | The auditor works on the basis of **evidence that is persuasive rather than conclusive**; management may not provide complete information; and fraud may involve **sophisticated and carefully organised schemes** (collusion, forgery, deliberate failure to record) designed to conceal it. |
| **Timeliness and cost–benefit** | Audit has to be completed within a **reasonable time and at a reasonable cost**; hence the auditor uses **test checking/sampling** and directs effort to areas expected to contain risks, rather than examining every item. |
| **Other matters** | Areas such as **related party relationships, non-compliance with laws and regulations, and going concern** carry limitations that may affect the persuasiveness of evidence. |

Because of these limitations, there is an **unavoidable risk** that some material misstatements may not be detected, even though the audit is properly planned and performed in accordance with the SAs. Absolute assurance would require the auditor to examine **every transaction** and eliminate every possibility of error/fraud, which is **not attainable**.

**Conclusion:** An audit is designed to provide **reasonable (high but not absolute) assurance**; the inherent limitations of the nature of financial reporting, the nature of audit procedures, and the need for timeliness at reasonable cost make absolute assurance impossible.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards naming the three heads of inherent limitations from SA 200 and linking "persuasive not conclusive" evidence to why absolute assurance fails; students lose marks for merely writing "audit is based on sampling" without the SA 200 framework.)*

### Q2. Ch: Nature, Objective & Scope — Scope of Audit & Interrelationship of Procedures (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Auditing is a **systematic and logical** process in which the individual audit procedures are **interrelated** rather than isolated. Explain what is meant by the interrelationship of audit procedures with a suitable illustration.

**Answer:**
As per the guidance underlying **SA 200**, an audit is a **systematic process** whereby the auditor obtains and evaluates evidence regarding assertions about economic actions and events. The procedures the auditor performs are **not isolated, independent steps**; each procedure and its result **feeds into and influences** the nature, timing and extent of the other procedures. This is the **interrelationship of audit procedures**.

The interrelationship operates in the following manner:

1. **Risk assessment drives further procedures.** The results of **risk assessment procedures** (understanding the entity and its internal control under **SA 315**) determine the **assessed risks of material misstatement**, which in turn govern the design of **further audit procedures** (tests of controls and substantive procedures) under **SA 330**.
2. **Result of one procedure affects the extent of another.** If **tests of controls** show that internal controls are **operating effectively**, the auditor may **reduce** the extent of substantive testing; conversely, weak controls require **more extensive substantive procedures**.
3. **Evidence is cumulative and corroborative.** Evidence obtained from one source (e.g., external confirmation of debtors) is **corroborated** by evidence from another (e.g., subsequent receipts, ageing analysis); consistency between them **strengthens** the conclusion, while inconsistency triggers **further investigation** (SA 500).
4. **Reassessment loop.** If a procedure detects a misstatement or a condition inconsistent with earlier evidence, the auditor must **revise** the earlier risk assessment and **modify** subsequent procedures accordingly.

**Illustration:** While auditing debtors, unfavourable/no replies to external confirmations lead the auditor to perform **alternative procedures** (examine subsequent collections, invoices, dispatch records). The outcome of these alternative procedures then influences the auditor's conclusion on the **valuation and existence** assertions and may affect the extent of testing of the **provision for doubtful debts**.

**Conclusion:** Because the procedures are **interrelated**, the auditor treats the audit as an **integrated whole** — the result of each step continuously shapes the direction and depth of the remaining work.

*(Full-marks tip: Anchor the answer in the "systematic process" idea and give a concrete example (confirmations → alternative procedures → valuation) rather than only listing procedure names.)*

### Q3. Ch: Audit Strategy & Planning — Contents & Flexibility of the Audit Programme (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** "An audit programme should be flexible and should not be a rigid, mechanical checklist." Discuss the **contents** of an audit programme and explain why **flexibility** is essential, along with the disadvantages that arise if the programme is treated as rigid.

**Answer:**
An **audit programme** is a **detailed written plan** of the audit procedures to be applied for accomplishing the audit objectives. It is prepared after developing the overall **audit strategy** and **audit plan** in terms of **SA 300 "Planning an Audit of Financial Statements"**, and it sets out the **nature, timing and extent** of procedures to be performed by the members of the engagement team.

**Contents of an audit programme:** A well-designed audit programme ordinarily contains —

1. The **audit procedures** to be performed for each area/account balance and class of transactions (e.g., cash, purchases, sales, debtors, inventory).
2. The **assertions/objectives** each procedure is directed at (existence, completeness, valuation, rights & obligations, presentation).
3. The **extent of checking** (test-check or 100%) and the **basis of selection** of items.
4. The **timing** of procedures (interim/year-end) and the **allocation of work** to assistants of appropriate competence.
5. **Space for initials of the person performing the work, dates, and cross-references** to working papers, and space for reviewer's remarks.
6. Instructions and points requiring **special attention**.

**Why flexibility is essential:**

- **No two audits are alike.** The programme must be **tailored** to the size, nature of business, and the **specific internal control** of the client; a standard checklist blindly applied cannot address entity-specific risks.
- **Conditions change.** Business, systems and risks change year on year; the programme must be **reviewed and revised** so that new risk areas are covered and redundant steps dropped.
- **Findings during the audit.** If procedures reveal **weaknesses or misstatements**, the auditor must be free to **extend or add** procedures; a rigid programme would prevent this responsiveness.

**Disadvantages if treated as rigid / mechanical:**

| Disadvantage | Effect |
|---|---|
| Work becomes **mechanical** | Assistants may follow steps without understanding, missing obvious errors outside the list. |
| **Initiative is killed** | Efficient assistants get no scope to use judgement; the programme becomes a routine to be "ticked off". |
| **Rigidity** | A programme drawn for one entity may be unsuitable for another; parts may become **redundant**. |
| **Concealment / collusion** | Once staff know the fixed pattern of checking, employees may **defraud** in areas known to be untested. |

These are countered by keeping the programme **flexible, periodically reviewed and revised**, and by encouraging staff to **report matters** not covered by it.

**Conclusion:** An audit programme is an indispensable planning tool under **SA 300**, but to remain effective it must be **flexible and dynamic**, tailored to the entity and revised as circumstances and findings demand — never a rigid mechanical checklist.

*(Full-marks tip: State the SA 300 link, list the actual contents (not just "list of procedures"), then present flexibility and the disadvantages of rigidity in a table; a common deduction is answering only the disadvantages and omitting the contents.)*

### Q4. Ch: Materiality — Aggregation of Uncorrected Misstatements (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** CA Nirav is completing the audit of **Zenith Traders Ltd.** for the year ended 31st March 2026. He has set **overall materiality at ₹8,00,000** and **performance materiality at ₹6,00,000**. During the audit the following **uncorrected misstatements** were identified. Evaluate the effect of these misstatements individually and in the aggregate and advise CA Nirav on the impact on his audit opinion.

| S.No. | Nature of misstatement | Amount (₹) | Effect on profit |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Closing inventory overvalued (obsolete goods not written down) | 2,50,000 | Overstated |
| 2 | Prepaid insurance not carried forward (charged fully to P&L) | 1,20,000 | Understated |
| 3 | Sales of April 2026 wrongly booked in March 2026 (cut-off) | 3,40,000 | Overstated |
| 4 | Repairs of ₹90,000 wrongly capitalised as machinery | 90,000 | Overstated |

**Answer:**
**Governing Standard:** **SA 450 "Evaluation of Misstatements Identified during the Audit"** read with **SA 320 "Materiality in Planning and Performing an Audit"**. The auditor must **accumulate** misstatements identified during the audit (other than those clearly trivial) and evaluate whether they are material **individually or in aggregate**.

**WN-1 — Individual evaluation against overall materiality (₹8,00,000):**

| Item | Amount (₹) | Individually material? |
|---|---|---|
| Inventory overvalued | 2,50,000 | No (< ₹8,00,000) |
| Prepaid insurance | 1,20,000 | No |
| Sales cut-off error | 3,40,000 | No |
| Repairs capitalised | 90,000 | No |

No single item exceeds overall materiality of ₹8,00,000; hence **none is individually material**.

**WN-2 — Aggregation of the effect on profit:**
The net effect on profit must be aggregated (overstatements are +, understatement of profit is −):

Aggregate overstatement of profit
= 2,50,000 (inventory) + 3,40,000 (sales) + 90,000 (repairs) − 1,20,000 (prepaid insurance)
= **₹5,60,000 (net overstatement of profit)**.

**WN-3 — Comparison with materiality:**
- Aggregate net misstatement **₹5,60,000** vs overall materiality **₹8,00,000** → below overall materiality.
- However, the **gross uncorrected misstatements** total **₹8,00,000** (2,50,000 + 1,20,000 + 3,40,000 + 90,000), which **equals** overall materiality, and the net figure of **₹5,60,000 is close to** materiality. As per **SA 450**, the auditor must consider whether misstatements are material in aggregate **and** re-assess whether **materiality remains appropriate** in light of the entity's actual results; offsetting items should be evaluated with caution (a debtor overstatement should not simply be netted against an unrelated understatement without care).

**Advice / conclusion (as per SA 450):**
1. CA Nirav must **communicate all accumulated misstatements** to the appropriate level of **management** on a timely basis and **request correction**.
2. If management **corrects** all the misstatements, the auditor can issue an **unmodified opinion**.
3. If management **refuses to correct**, since the aggregate net effect (₹5,60,000) is **below overall materiality** but the gross total (₹8,00,000) reaches materiality, the auditor must apply **professional judgement** on qualitative factors (the cut-off error affects a key line, capitalisation of repairs affects fixed assets). If, after considering the qualitative factors, the uncorrected misstatements **are not material** individually or in aggregate, an **unmodified opinion** is appropriate; the auditor should also obtain a **written representation** from management that they believe the effects of uncorrected misstatements are immaterial (with a summary attached).

**Conclusion:** No item is individually material; the aggregate **net** effect of ₹5,60,000 is below overall materiality of ₹8,00,000, so a **modification is not warranted on the numbers alone**, but CA Nirav must accumulate and communicate the misstatements, weigh qualitative factors, and obtain the written representation before concluding on an **unmodified opinion**.

*(Full-marks tip: Show both the net aggregation AND the SA 450 procedural duties — communicate, request correction, written representation. Students who only compute the net figure and stop miss half the marks.)*

### Q5. Ch: Materiality — Materiality, Performance Materiality & Revision (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between **Materiality**, **Performance Materiality** and the **Materiality level(s) for particular classes of transactions, account balances or disclosures** as used in SA 320. When is the auditor required to **revise** materiality during the audit?

**Answer:**
**SA 320 "Materiality in Planning and Performing an Audit"** requires the auditor to apply the concept of materiality both in **planning and performing** the audit. The three related concepts are distinguished below:

| Concept | Meaning (per SA 320) |
|---|---|
| **Materiality (for the financial statements as a whole)** | The amount(s) set by the auditor at the planning stage above which misstatements — individually or in aggregate — could reasonably be expected to **influence the economic decisions of users** taken on the basis of the financial statements. Judged in the surrounding circumstances, affected by size and/or nature of misstatement. |
| **Performance materiality** | The amount(s) set at **less than materiality for the financial statements as a whole**, to **reduce to an appropriately low level** the probability that the aggregate of **uncorrected and undetected** misstatements exceeds materiality for the financial statements as a whole. |
| **Specific (lower) materiality** | For particular classes of transactions, account balances or disclosures where misstatements of **lesser amounts** than overall materiality could reasonably influence users' decisions (e.g., related-party transactions, directors' remuneration), a **lower materiality level** is set for those items. |

**Determining materiality:** The auditor often applies a **percentage to a chosen benchmark** (such as profit before tax, total revenue, gross profit, total equity, or net asset value) as a **starting point**, chosen having regard to the elements of the financial statements, the items on which users focus, the nature of the entity and industry, and the entity's ownership/financing structure.

**Revision of materiality (SA 320):** The auditor must **revise** materiality for the financial statements as a whole (and, if applicable, the specific materiality levels) if the auditor **becomes aware of information during the audit** that would have caused a different amount to be determined initially — for example:
- a **change in circumstances** (e.g., a decision to dispose of a major part of the business);
- **new information** obtained; or
- a change in the auditor's understanding of the entity, such that **actual results differ significantly** from the anticipated results originally used to determine materiality.

If a **lower materiality** is determined on revision, the auditor must reconsider whether **performance materiality** needs revision and whether the **nature, timing and extent** of further audit procedures remain appropriate.

**Conclusion:** Materiality is the overall threshold for users' decisions, performance materiality is a buffer set below it to absorb undetected/uncorrected misstatements, and specific materiality applies to sensitive items — all of which must be **revised** when new information or changed circumstances warrant.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner looks for the precise SA 320 wording that performance materiality is set *below* overall materiality to cover *undetected + uncorrected* misstatements, plus the three revision triggers.)*

### Q6. Ch: Audit Strategy & Planning — Benefits of Planning & Involvement of Key Members (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Adequate planning benefits the audit of financial statements in several ways. Discuss the **benefits of audit planning** as recognised by SA 300 and explain the requirement regarding the **involvement of the engagement partner and other key members** in planning.

**Answer:**
**SA 300 "Planning an Audit of Financial Statements"** requires the auditor to plan the audit so that it is performed in an **effective manner**. Planning is not a discrete phase but a **continual and iterative** process that begins shortly after the completion of the previous audit and continues until completion of the current engagement.

**Benefits of adequate audit planning (SA 300):** Adequate planning benefits the audit in several ways, in particular it —

1. Helps the auditor to **devote appropriate attention to important areas** of the audit;
2. Helps the auditor **identify and resolve potential problems** on a timely basis;
3. Helps the auditor **properly organise and manage** the audit engagement so that it is performed in an effective and efficient manner;
4. Assists in the **selection of engagement team members** with appropriate levels of capabilities and competence to respond to anticipated risks, and the **proper assignment of work** to them;
5. Facilitates the **direction and supervision** of engagement team members and the **review** of their work; and
6. Assists, where applicable, in **coordination of work** done by auditors of components and experts.

**Involvement of key engagement team members (SA 300):** The **engagement partner and other key members** of the engagement team must be **involved in planning the audit**, including planning and participating in the **discussion among engagement team members**. This is beneficial because it draws on their **experience and insight**, thereby **enhancing the effectiveness and efficiency** of the planning process.

**Preliminary engagement activities:** At the beginning of the current engagement the auditor shall perform procedures regarding the **continuance of the client relationship**, evaluate **compliance with ethical requirements (including independence)**, and establish an understanding of the **terms of the engagement** (SA 210).

**Conclusion:** Planning is a continual, iterative process whose benefits centre on attention to key areas, timely resolution of problems, and effective organisation and supervision; and SA 300 mandates the **involvement of the engagement partner and key members** to strengthen the planning process.

*(Full-marks tip: Reproduce the six SA 300 benefits as complete sentences and do not forget the mandatory involvement of the engagement partner — a frequently omitted point.)*

### Q7. Ch: Audit Strategy & Planning — Overall Audit Strategy vs Audit Plan (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the **overall audit strategy** and the **audit plan** as required by SA 300, and clearly bring out the **relationship** between the two. What matters does the auditor consider in establishing the overall audit strategy?

**Answer:**
**SA 300 "Planning an Audit of Financial Statements"** distinguishes between the **overall audit strategy** and the more detailed **audit plan**.

**Overall audit strategy:** The auditor shall establish an overall audit strategy that sets the **scope, timing and direction** of the audit and that **guides the development** of the audit plan. In establishing the overall audit strategy, the auditor shall —

1. Identify the **characteristics of the engagement** that define its scope (e.g., reporting framework, industry-specific requirements, locations);
2. Ascertain the **reporting objectives** to plan the timing of the audit and the nature of communications required (deadlines for interim and final reporting, key communications with management/those charged with governance);
3. Consider the factors that, in the auditor's professional judgement, are **significant** in directing the engagement team's efforts (determination of **materiality**, preliminary identification of areas of higher risk, results of previous audits);
4. Consider the results of **preliminary engagement activities** and knowledge gained on other engagements; and
5. Ascertain the **nature, timing and extent of resources** necessary to perform the engagement (staffing, hours, use of experts).

**Audit plan:** The audit plan is **more detailed** than the overall audit strategy. The auditor shall develop an audit plan that includes a description of —

- the **nature, timing and extent of planned risk assessment procedures** (SA 315);
- the **nature, timing and extent of planned further audit procedures** at the assertion level (SA 330); and
- **other planned audit procedures** required to comply with the SAs.

**Relationship between the two:**

| Overall Audit Strategy | Audit Plan |
|---|---|
| Sets the **scope, timing and direction** of the audit (broad approach). | Sets out the **detailed procedures** to be performed by team members. |
| Guides the **development** of the audit plan. | Developed **after** and **in accordance with** the strategy. |
| Higher-level / summary. | Detailed / operational. |

The strategy and plan are **not fixed** — they are **updated and changed** as necessary during the course of the audit as a result of unexpected events, changes in conditions, or evidence obtained.

**Conclusion:** The overall audit strategy provides the **broad framework** (scope, timing, direction and resources), and the audit plan **converts that framework into detailed procedures**; both are dynamic and revised as the audit progresses.

*(Full-marks tip: Present strategy → plan sequence and the four/five strategy considerations; a table contrasting the two earns the distinction marks.)*

### Q8. Ch: Nature, Objective & Scope — "True and Fair" & Auditor's Opinion (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** The auditor expresses an opinion on whether the financial statements give a **true and fair view**. Explain what "true and fair" conveys and the auditor's responsibility in this regard as distinguished from that of management.

**Answer:**
The **objective of an audit** of financial statements, as stated in **SA 200**, is to enable the auditor to express an opinion whether the financial statements are prepared, in all material respects, in accordance with an **applicable financial reporting framework** and give a **true and fair view** (or are presented fairly, in all material respects).

**Meaning of "true and fair":** The expression is **not defined** in the Companies Act, 2013, but it conveys that —

1. The financial statements are **free from material misstatement** — i.e., the figures are **true** (correct and factual, not false) and the presentation is **fair** (unbiased, reflecting the real state of affairs);
2. The **assets, liabilities, income and expenses** are properly **classified, valued and disclosed** in accordance with the applicable framework and applicable Accounting Standards; and
3. There is **adequate disclosure** of all material matters, and the statements are **not misleading**.

Under **Section 143** of the Companies Act, 2013, the auditor reports whether the accounts give a true and fair view of the **state of affairs** (Balance Sheet) and of the **profit or loss** (Statement of Profit and Loss).

**Auditor's responsibility vs management's responsibility:**

| Management | Auditor |
|---|---|
| **Prepares** the financial statements giving a true and fair view; maintains internal control; makes estimates. | **Does not prepare** the accounts; **examines** them and forms an **independent opinion** on whether they give a true and fair view. |
| Responsible for **preventing and detecting** fraud and error. | Responsible for obtaining **reasonable assurance** that the statements are free from material misstatement. |

An audit does **not relieve management** of its responsibilities. The auditor's opinion **enhances the credibility** of the financial statements but is **not a guarantee** of the future viability of the entity or of the efficiency/effectiveness of management.

**Conclusion:** "True and fair" means the financial statements are factually correct, unbiased and adequately disclosed in accordance with the applicable framework; management is responsible for preparing them, while the auditor is responsible only for **independently opining** on them with reasonable assurance.

*(Full-marks tip: Link "true and fair" to Section 143 and to proper classification/valuation/disclosure, and clearly separate management's vs auditor's responsibility — a favourite examiner distinction.)*

### Q9. Ch: Materiality — Materiality and Audit Risk Interrelationship (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the **inverse relationship between materiality and audit risk** and how this relationship affects the auditor's determination of the **nature, timing and extent** of audit procedures.

**Answer:**
**SA 320** and **SA 200** together establish that the concepts of **materiality** and **audit risk** are considered **throughout the audit**, particularly when (a) identifying and assessing the risks of material misstatement, (b) determining the nature, timing and extent of further audit procedures, and (c) evaluating the effect of uncorrected misstatements.

**Audit risk** is the risk that the auditor expresses an **inappropriate audit opinion** when the financial statements are materially misstated; it is a function of the **risks of material misstatement** and **detection risk**.

**Inverse relationship:** There is an **inverse relationship** between materiality and the level of audit risk — the **higher** the materiality level (i.e., a larger threshold), the **lower** the audit risk, and the **lower** the materiality level, the **higher** the audit risk. In other words, when the auditor sets materiality at a **lower** amount (a stricter threshold), a greater number/size of misstatements become "material", and therefore the auditor faces a **higher risk** of failing to detect them.

**Effect on nature, timing and extent of procedures:** The auditor **compensates** for this by adjusting audit procedures:

| If materiality is set LOWER (audit risk higher) | Auditor's response |
|---|---|
| **Nature** | Use **more effective/reliable** procedures — e.g., external confirmation, procedures directed to independent third-party evidence rather than inquiry alone. |
| **Timing** | Perform more procedures **at or near the period-end** rather than at interim. |
| **Extent** | **Increase** the extent — larger sample sizes, greater number of items tested — to reduce **detection risk**. |

Conversely, where a **higher** materiality is appropriate, the auditor may accept a **higher detection risk** and thus perform **less extensive** procedures.

**Conclusion:** Materiality and audit risk are **inversely related**; the auditor keeps **overall audit risk acceptably low** by lowering **detection risk** — through more reliable, later-timed and more extensive procedures — whenever a lower materiality (or higher assessed risk) is determined.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the explicit inverse statement plus the mapping to nature/timing/extent via detection risk; merely stating "they are inversely related" without the procedural consequence loses marks.)*

### Q10. Ch: Audit Strategy & Planning — Direction, Supervision & Review (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** SA 300 requires the auditor to plan the **direction, supervision and review** of the engagement team's work. Explain this requirement and state the factors that influence the extent of direction and supervision needed.

**Answer:**
As per **SA 300 "Planning an Audit of Financial Statements"**, in developing the overall audit strategy and audit plan, the auditor shall include in the audit plan the **nature, timing and extent of direction and supervision** of engagement team members and the **review of their work**.

**Direction, supervision and review explained:**
- **Direction** means **informing** team members of their responsibilities, the objectives of the procedures they are to perform, and matters that could affect the nature, timing and extent of procedures.
- **Supervision** means **tracking the progress** of the engagement, considering the capabilities and competence of team members, and **addressing significant matters** as they arise.
- **Review** means considering whether the **work has been performed** in accordance with professional standards and whether the **conclusions reached are appropriate** and supported by the work documented.

**Factors influencing the extent of direction and supervision required (and the review of work):**

1. The **size and complexity** of the entity;
2. The **area of the audit** (higher-risk areas need closer direction/supervision);
3. The **assessed risks of material misstatement** (the greater the risk, the more direction and supervision); and
4. The **capabilities and competence** of the individual team members performing the work.

Where team members are **less experienced**, more **detailed direction, closer supervision and more extensive review** of their work is required; where members are **experienced and competent**, a lesser degree may suffice.

**Conclusion:** SA 300 requires the auditor to plan for **direction, supervision and review** proportionate to the entity's size and complexity, the assessed risks, and the competence of the team members, so that the audit is performed effectively.

*(Full-marks tip: Define all three terms and list the four influencing factors; connecting "less experienced staff → more supervision" earns the applied mark.)*

### Q11. Ch: Nature, Objective & Scope — Auditing vs Investigation & Scope Determination (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** (a) Distinguish between **Auditing** and **Investigation**. (b) How is the **scope of an audit** determined?

**Answer:**
**(a) Auditing vs Investigation:**
Both involve examination of records, but they differ fundamentally in objective and approach.

| Basis | Auditing | Investigation |
|---|---|---|
| **Objective** | To express an opinion on the **true and fair view** of the financial statements (SA 200). | To establish a **specific fact** or answer a **specific question** for a special purpose (e.g., suspected fraud, business acquisition). |
| **Scope** | **General** — covers the financial statements as a whole. | **Specific/limited** — restricted to the matter under enquiry. |
| **Period** | Usually **one accounting year**. | May extend over **several years** as required. |
| **Extent of evidence** | Evidence is **persuasive**; relies on test checking/sampling. | Evidence sought is **conclusive**; may involve exhaustive/100% examination. |
| **Conducted for** | On behalf of **shareholders/owners** (statutory in many cases). | For the person/party who **commissions** it (management, prospective buyer, etc.). |
| **Governed by** | Standards on Auditing and statute. | Terms of the **specific engagement**; not bound by SAs. |

**(b) Determination of the scope of an audit:** As per **SA 200**, the **scope of an audit** — i.e., the audit procedures deemed necessary to achieve the objective of the audit — is determined by the auditor having regard to:
1. The requirements of the **relevant Standards on Auditing**;
2. The **relevant professional bodies' pronouncements**;
3. The **relevant legislation** (e.g., the Companies Act, 2013); and
4. Where relevant, the **terms of the audit engagement** and reporting requirements.

The audit should be organised to cover **adequately all aspects** of the entity relevant to the financial statements. The auditor assesses the **reliability and sufficiency** of information in the accounting records and determines whether the relevant information is **properly disclosed**. The scope should **not be restricted** by any limitation that prevents the auditor from forming an opinion; where such a limitation exists, the auditor considers its effect on the report.

**Conclusion:** Auditing is a **general opinion-forming** exercise, whereas investigation is a **specific, purpose-driven enquiry**; the scope of an audit is fixed by the **SAs, professional pronouncements, legislation and the engagement terms**, and must adequately cover all relevant aspects.

*(Full-marks tip: A full comparison table plus the four determinants of scope from SA 200 is expected; students often forget "terms of engagement" as one determinant.)*

### Q12. Ch: Materiality — Application of Materiality (Benchmark & Percentage) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** CA Sneha is planning the audit of **Aarav Manufacturing Ltd.** for FY 2025-26 and must determine materiality. The following data is available. Advise CA Sneha, with reasoning, on an appropriate benchmark and compute a preliminary overall materiality and performance materiality. State what she should do if she later learns actual profit before tax is only ₹22,00,000.

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Total revenue | 9,50,00,000 |
| Profit before tax (budgeted) | 60,00,000 |
| Total assets | 7,20,00,000 |
| Total equity | 4,00,00,000 |

**Answer:**
**Governing Standard:** **SA 320 "Materiality in Planning and Performing an Audit"** — the auditor applies a **percentage to a chosen benchmark** as a starting point for determining materiality for the financial statements as a whole.

**WN-1 — Selection of benchmark:** For a **profit-oriented manufacturing company**, **profit before tax (PBT) from continuing operations** is generally the most appropriate benchmark, because it is the figure on which the **users (shareholders, lenders)** most commonly focus. (Where profits are volatile, revenue or total assets may be used instead.) Here PBT is a stable, meaningful figure, so **PBT is chosen** as the benchmark.

**WN-2 — Preliminary overall materiality:** Applying a customary percentage of **5% of PBT** (percentages of about **5%–10% of PBT** are commonly regarded as appropriate; a lower end is used for greater prudence):

Overall materiality = 5% × ₹60,00,000 = **₹3,00,000**.

**WN-3 — Performance materiality:** Set at a level **below overall materiality** to allow for undetected and uncorrected misstatements; taking **75%** of overall materiality:

Performance materiality = 75% × ₹3,00,000 = **₹2,25,000**.

**Statement of Materiality Determination:**

| Item | Basis | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| Benchmark | Profit before tax | 60,00,000 |
| Overall materiality | 5% of PBT | 3,00,000 |
| Performance materiality | 75% of overall materiality | 2,25,000 |

**Revision if actual PBT is only ₹22,00,000 (WN-4):**
Under **SA 320**, materiality must be **revised** where **actual results differ significantly** from the budgeted results used to determine materiality. The budgeted PBT of ₹60,00,000 was used, but actual PBT is only ₹22,00,000 — a significant fall. CA Sneha must **re-compute** materiality on the actual figure:

Revised overall materiality = 5% × ₹22,00,000 = **₹1,10,000**;
Revised performance materiality = 75% × ₹1,10,000 = **₹82,500**.

Because the revised materiality is **much lower**, CA Sneha must **reconsider whether the nature, timing and extent** of the further audit procedures already planned remain appropriate (larger samples / additional procedures may now be required), since misstatements previously considered immaterial may now be material.

**Answer:** Benchmark = PBT; preliminary overall materiality = **₹3,00,000** and performance materiality = **₹2,25,000**; on learning actual PBT is ₹22,00,000, revise to overall materiality **₹1,10,000** and performance materiality **₹82,500**, and re-evaluate the planned procedures.

### Q14. Ch: Nature, Objective & Scope — Professional Skepticism & Professional Judgement (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** SA 200 requires the auditor to plan and perform an audit with **professional skepticism** and to exercise **professional judgement**. Explain both concepts and give examples of where professional judgement is essential.

**Answer:**
**SA 200 "Overall Objectives of the Independent Auditor and the Conduct of an Audit in Accordance with Standards on Auditing"** requires the auditor to comply with relevant ethical requirements and to conduct the audit with **professional skepticism** and **professional judgement** in order to obtain reasonable assurance.

**Professional skepticism:** It is an **attitude** that includes a **questioning mind**, being **alert to conditions** which may indicate possible misstatement due to fraud or error, and a **critical assessment** of audit evidence. Maintaining professional skepticism means the auditor does **not accept audit evidence at face value** but remains alert to:
- audit evidence that **contradicts** other evidence obtained;
- information that brings into question the **reliability** of documents and responses to inquiries;
- conditions that may indicate **possible fraud**; and
- circumstances suggesting the need for **additional procedures**.

Professional skepticism is necessary because of the **inherent limitations** of an audit and the risk of **management override of controls**; the auditor must not assume management is dishonest, nor assume unquestioned honesty.

**Professional judgement:** It is the application of **relevant training, knowledge and experience**, within the context provided by auditing, accounting and ethical standards, in making **informed decisions** about the courses of action appropriate in the circumstances of the engagement. 1. **Materiality** and **audit risk**;
2. The **nature, timing and extent** of audit procedures used to gather evidence;
3. Evaluating whether **sufficient appropriate audit evidence** has been obtained;
4. The evaluation of **management's judgements** in applying the applicable financial reporting framework (e.g., accounting estimates); and
5. Drawing **conclusions** based on the audit evidence obtained (e.g., assessing the reasonableness of estimates).

**Conclusion:** Professional skepticism is a **questioning, alert attitude** toward evidence, while professional judgement is the **informed application of training and experience**; both run throughout the audit and are indispensable to obtaining reasonable assurance.

### Q16. Ch: Nature, Objective & Scope — Advantages of Independent Audit (Cross-topic) (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State any **eight advantages** of an independent audit of financial statements.

**Answer:**

1. **Protection of interests** — It safeguards the **financial interest** of persons **not associated** with the management, such as partners not managing the business, shareholders, and other stakeholders.

8. **Useful to management / decision-making** — Audited accounts and the observations arising from the audit are useful to **management** for controlling the business and for **negotiations**, and are relied upon by **tax authorities and courts** as authentic.

### Q18. Ch: Risk Assessment & Internal Control — Risk Assessment Procedures (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Risk assessment procedures by themselves do not provide sufficient appropriate audit evidence on which to base the audit opinion, yet they are mandatory. **Answer:**
The governing Standard is **SA 315, "Identifying and Assessing the Risks of Material Misstatement through Understanding the Entity and its Environment."** SA 315 requires the auditor to perform risk assessment procedures to provide a basis for the identification and assessment of the risks of material misstatement at the financial statement and assertion levels; these procedures address the risk of failing to plan an effective audit rather than any single assertion directly.

Per **SA 315**, the risk assessment procedures comprise the following:
- **Inquiries of management and of others within the entity** who, in the auditor's judgement, may have information likely to assist in identifying risks — for example, those charged with governance, internal audit, in-house legal counsel, and employees involved in initiating, processing or recording complex or unusual transactions.

### Q20. Ch: Risk Assessment & Internal Control — Audit Risk Model (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the components of the audit risk model under SA 315 — inherent risk, control risk and detection risk — with a suitable example of each, and state how their interrelationship governs the auditor's work.

**Answer:**
The governing framework is **SA 315** read with **SA 200**, which define audit risk as the risk that the auditor expresses an inappropriate opinion when the financial statements are materially misstated. Audit risk is a function of the **risks of material misstatement (RoMM)** and **detection risk**. RoMM at the assertion level consists of two components — inherent risk and control risk.

The components are:
- **Inherent risk** is the susceptibility of an assertion about a class of transactions, account balance or disclosure to a misstatement that could be material, before consideration of any related controls. *Example:* the valuation of complex derivative instruments, involving significant estimation, carries high inherent risk because of the complexity of the calculation.
- **Control risk** is the risk that a misstatement that could occur in an assertion and that could be material will not be prevented, or detected and corrected on a timely basis, by the entity's internal control. *Example:* where the same clerk records cash receipts and reconciles the bank account, weak segregation raises control risk for the existence/occurrence of cash.
- **Detection risk** is the risk that the procedures performed by the auditor to reduce audit risk to an acceptably low level will not detect a misstatement that exists. *Example:* an inappropriately small sample of receivables confirmations may fail to detect overstated debtors.

The interrelationship is inverse and central: for a given acceptable level of audit risk, the assessed level of RoMM (inherent × control risk) determines the acceptable detection risk. The **higher** the assessed RoMM, the **lower** the detection risk the auditor can accept, requiring more persuasive audit evidence — i.e., more extensive procedures, more experienced staff, or testing nearer the year-end. Inherent and control risk are the entity's risks and exist independently of the audit; detection risk is a function of the effectiveness of the auditor's procedures and is the only component the auditor can directly control.

**Conclusion:** Audit risk = RoMM (inherent × control) × detection risk; the auditor manages audit risk by setting detection risk inversely to the assessed RoMM through the design of further audit procedures.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards a distinct example for each component and the explicit inverse relationship between RoMM and detection risk; marks are lost for defining the components without linking them to the extent of evidence gathered.)*

### Q22. Ch: Risk Assessment & Internal Control — Responses to Assessed Risks (SA 330) (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain how the auditor designs and implements overall responses and further audit procedures to address the assessed risks of material misstatement under SA 330, and describe when tests of controls become necessary.

**Answer:**
The governing Standard is **SA 330, "The Auditor's Responses to Assessed Risks."** SA 330 requires the auditor to design and implement responses that are appropriate to the assessed risks of material misstatement (RoMM) at both the financial statement level and the assertion level.

Per **SA 330**, at the **financial statement level** the auditor determines **overall responses**, which may include: emphasising to the engagement team the need for professional scepticism, assigning more experienced staff or those with specialised skills, providing more supervision, and incorporating an element of unpredictability in the selection of further audit procedures.

At the **assertion level**, the auditor designs and performs **further audit procedures** whose nature, timing and extent are responsive to the assessed RoMM. These comprise:
- **Tests of controls** — performed when the auditor's assessment of RoMM at the assertion level includes an expectation that controls are operating effectively (i.e., the auditor intends to rely on them), or when substantive procedures alone cannot provide sufficient appropriate audit evidence at the assertion level.
- **Substantive procedures** — comprising tests of details and substantive analytical procedures; SA 330 makes it mandatory to design and perform substantive procedures for **each material class of transactions, account balance and disclosure**, irrespective of the assessed risks, because of the inherent limitations of internal control.

SA 330 further requires that where the RoMM at the assertion level is assessed as **higher, the auditor obtains more persuasive audit evidence** — by increasing the quantity of evidence or obtaining evidence that is more relevant or reliable, for example by performing procedures at period-end rather than at an interim date. For **significant risks**, if the auditor has not tested controls, substantive procedures must be specifically responsive, and tests of details are required.

**Conclusion:** The auditor responds at the overall level through team and supervision measures, and at the assertion level through a tailored mix of tests of controls and mandatory substantive procedures, obtaining more persuasive evidence as assessed risk rises.

### Q24. Ch: Audit Evidence — Sufficiency & Appropriateness (SA 500) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** "Sufficiency and appropriateness of audit evidence are interrelated." Explain the meaning of sufficient appropriate audit evidence under SA 500 and the factors that influence the reliability of audit evidence.

**Answer:**
The governing Standard is **SA 500, "Audit Evidence."** SA 500 requires the auditor to design and perform audit procedures that are appropriate in the circumstances for the purpose of obtaining sufficient appropriate audit evidence to be able to draw reasonable conclusions on which to base the audit opinion.

Per **SA 500**:
- **Sufficiency** is the measure of the **quantity** of audit evidence. The quantity needed is affected by the auditor's assessment of the risks of material misstatement (the higher the assessed risk, the more evidence likely required) and by the quality of the evidence (the higher the quality, the less may be required).
- **Appropriateness** is the measure of the **quality** of audit evidence — that is, its **relevance** and its **reliability** in providing support for the conclusions on which the auditor's opinion is based.

The two are interrelated: merely obtaining more of poor-quality evidence does not compensate for its low reliability, and high-quality evidence still requires an adequate quantity.

SA 500 provides generalisations about the **reliability** of evidence, subject to exceptions:
- Evidence is more reliable when obtained from **independent sources outside the entity**.
- Internally generated evidence is more reliable when the related **controls are effective**.
- Evidence obtained **directly by the auditor** (e.g., observation) is more reliable than evidence obtained indirectly or by inference.
- Evidence in **documentary form** (paper, electronic) is more reliable than that obtained orally.
- **Original documents** are more reliable than photocopies or facsimiles.

**Conclusion:** Sufficient appropriate audit evidence means an adequate quantity of relevant and reliable evidence; sufficiency and appropriateness move together, and reliability is judged by source, control environment, directness and form.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the crisp sufficiency = quantity / appropriateness = quality (relevance + reliability) split and all five reliability generalisations; marks are lost for reproducing only definitions without the reliability factors.)*

The governing Standard is **SA 500**, which lists the procedures the auditor may use to obtain audit evidence, either as risk assessment procedures, tests of controls or substantive procedures.

Per **SA 500**, the procedures are:
- **Inspection** involves examining records or documents, whether internal or external, in paper or electronic form, or physically examining an asset. Inspection of documents provides evidence of existence but not necessarily of ownership or value; physical inspection of an asset provides reliable evidence of existence.
- **Observation** consists of looking at a process or procedure being performed by others — for example, observing inventory counting. Its value is limited to the point in time at which the observation takes place, and the act of being observed may affect how the procedure is performed.
- **External confirmation** is audit evidence obtained as a direct written response to the auditor from a third party (the confirming party), in paper or electronic form; it is highly reliable as it comes directly from an independent source.
- **Recalculation** consists of checking the mathematical accuracy of documents or records, and may be performed manually or electronically.
- **Reperformance** is the auditor's independent execution of procedures or controls that were originally performed as part of the entity's internal control.
- **Analytical procedures** consist of evaluations of financial information through analysis of plausible relationships among both financial and non-financial data.
- **Inquiry** consists of seeking information from knowledgeable persons, financial or non-financial, within or outside the entity; responses may provide new information or corroborative evidence, but inquiry alone ordinarily does not provide sufficient audit evidence and must be corroborated.

**Conclusion:** The seven procedures offer differing reliability and purpose; the auditor selects an appropriate mix, corroborating inquiry with other procedures, to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards naming all seven procedures with a one-line distinctive value, especially the caveat that inquiry alone is insufficient; a common deduction is merely listing without differentiating inspection of documents versus assets.)*

### Q26. Ch: Audit Evidence — Selecting Items for Testing (SA 500) (Marks: 4) [Theory]

**Answer:**
The governing Standard is **SA 500**. When designing tests of controls and tests of details, SA 500 requires the auditor to **determine means of selecting items for testing** that are effective in meeting the purpose of the audit procedure.

Per **SA 500**, the means available are:
- **Selecting all items (100% examination)** — appropriate where the population constitutes a small number of large-value items, when there is a significant risk and other means do not provide sufficient appropriate evidence, or where automation makes it cost-effective.
- **Selecting specific items** — such as high-value or key items, all items over a certain amount, or items to obtain information about the entity; note that selective examination of specific items is not sampling and results cannot be projected to the whole population.

**Conclusion:** The auditor may select all items, specific items, or a sample depending on the procedure's purpose, and when relying on a management's expert must assess the expert's competence, objectivity and work.

### Q29. Ch: Audit Evidence — External Confirmation (SA 505) (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between positive and negative external confirmation requests under SA 505, and explain the auditor's responsibility when management requests him not to seek external confirmation from certain parties.

**Answer:**
The governing Standard is **SA 505, "External Confirmations."** External confirmation is audit evidence obtained as a direct written response from a third party, and is a highly reliable source addressing assertions such as the **existence and rights/obligations** of account balances (e.g., receivables, bank balances).

Per **SA 505**, the two forms of confirmation request are distinguished below:

| Basis | Positive Confirmation Request | Negative Confirmation Request |
|---|---|---|
| Nature of response sought | Requests the confirming party to respond in **all cases**, indicating agreement or disagreement, or providing the requested information | Requests the confirming party to respond **only if he disagrees** with the information stated |
| Reliability of evidence | Provides **more reliable** audit evidence, since a response is expected in every case | Provides **less persuasive** evidence; non-response gives no explicit evidence |
| When used | Ordinarily used, especially where risk of material misstatement is higher | Used only when conditions are met — low assessed RoMM with tested controls, a large number of small homogeneous balances, a low expected exception rate, and no reason to expect recipients to disregard the request |
| Non-response | Requires **follow-up** / alternative procedures | No response is treated as agreement (its key limitation) |

Regarding **management's refusal to allow confirmation**: per SA 505, if management requests the auditor not to send a confirmation request to a particular party, the auditor shall **inquire into management's reasons** and **seek audit evidence as to their validity and reasonableness**; **evaluate the implications** on the assessment of the relevant risks of material misstatement, including the risk of fraud, and on the nature, timing and extent of other audit procedures; and **perform alternative audit procedures** designed to obtain relevant and reliable evidence. If the auditor concludes that management's refusal is **unreasonable**, or he is unable to obtain relevant and reliable evidence from alternative procedures, he shall communicate with those charged with governance under SA 260 and consider the implications for the opinion under SA 705.

**Conclusion:** Positive requests demand a response in all cases and are more reliable; negative requests are used only under restrictive conditions; and management's refusal to permit confirmation obliges the auditor to test the reasons, evaluate risk, and perform alternative procedures.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards a full distinction table plus all four conditions for using negative confirmations and the three-step response to management refusal; a common deduction is missing that unreasonable refusal escalates to TCWG and SA 705.)*

### Q35. Ch: Audit Sampling & Analytical Procedures — Defining the Population and Sampling Unit (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** While planning the substantive testing of trade payables of Zephyr Manufacturing Ltd., CA Nandini instructs her team to "select 60 vouchers from the payment cash book." Her senior partner objects that the population and sampling unit have not been properly defined before drawing the sample. Explain, with reference to SA 530 "Audit Sampling", what is meant by "population" and "sampling unit", the characteristics a population must possess, and why a defective definition of the population undermines the audit conclusion.

**Answer:**
As per SA 530 "Audit Sampling", **"population"** means the entire set of data from which a sample is selected and about which the auditor wishes to draw a conclusion. The **"sampling unit"** means the individual items constituting a population — for example, a cheque, a credit entry, an invoice, or a customer balance.

Before selecting a sample, the auditor is required to ensure that the population possesses the following two essential characteristics:

**(i) Appropriateness (relevance to the audit objective):** The auditor must determine that the population is appropriate for the specific audit objective, because sample results can be projected only to the population from which the sample was selected. If the objective is to test *understatement* of trade payables (completeness), selecting only from *recorded* payables in the cash book is inappropriate, since unrecorded liabilities can never be caught — the auditor would instead sample subsequent payments, unpaid invoices and goods-received-not-invoiced. If the objective is *overstatement* (existence), the recorded payables ledger is the appropriate population.

**(ii) Completeness:** The population must be complete — it must include all relevant items for the entire period. If certain locations, months or a class of items are excluded, a conclusion drawn from the sample cannot validly be extended to the whole population.

The auditor must also **define the sampling unit** in a manner consistent with the assertion tested; the efficiency of the sample is improved by an appropriately defined sampling unit (e.g., individual credit balances versus individual invoices).

**Why a defective definition undermines the conclusion:** Since the auditor projects the results of the sample to the entire population, a sample drawn from a wrongly-defined or incomplete population supports a conclusion only about that flawed population — not about the account balance as recorded in the financial statements. In CA Nandini's case, "60 vouchers from the payment cash book" tests only amounts already paid and recorded; it provides no assurance on the *completeness* assertion for trade payables, which is the assertion at highest risk for a liability.

**Conclusion:** The senior partner is correct — the population must first be defined as appropriate to the assertion (here, completeness of trade payables) and complete for the period; only then may a valid sample be drawn and its results projected.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards linking the population to the specific assertion — understatement vs. overstatement direction of testing. A common deduction is merely reproducing the definitions without explaining "appropriateness" and "completeness" as the two required characteristics.)*

### Q36. Ch: Audit Sampling & Analytical Procedures — Projecting Misstatements to the Population (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** During the substantive testing of the sales invoices of Kaveri Textiles Ltd. for FY 2025-26, CA Rohit used monetary unit sampling on the recorded sales population. The relevant data is given below. Compute the projected misstatement, evaluate it against the tolerable misstatement, and state the auditor's conclusion under SA 530.

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Total recorded sales (population) | 2,40,00,000 |
| Value of items individually inspected (100% examined high-value items) | 40,00,000 |
| Sampling interval / value of sampling population (remaining) | 2,00,00,000 |
| Book value of the sample selected from remaining population | 25,00,000 |
| Aggregate misstatement (overstatement) found in the sample | 1,00,000 |
| Misstatement found in the 100%-examined items | 30,000 |
| Tolerable misstatement set for sales | 3,50,000 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Classify the population.** SA 530 requires the auditor to project misstatements found in the *sample* over the *sampling population*, but misstatements found in items that were **individually examined (100%)** are NOT projected — they are known misstatements and taken at actual. Hence:
- Sampling population (subject to projection) = ₹2,00,00,000
- Individually examined items (no projection) = ₹40,00,000

**WN-2 — Compute the sample misstatement ratio.**
Ratio = Misstatement in sample ÷ Book value of sample
= ₹1,00,000 ÷ ₹25,00,000 = **0.04 (i.e., 4%)**

**WN-3 — Project the sample misstatement over the sampling population.**
Projected misstatement = Misstatement ratio × Sampling population
= 0.04 × ₹2,00,00,000 = **₹8,00,000**

**WN-4 — Add the known (actual) misstatement from 100%-examined items.**
These are not projected; the actual amount is added.
Known misstatement = ₹30,000

**Statement Showing Total Likely Misstatement in Sales**

| Component | Basis | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| Projected misstatement (sampling population) | 4% × 2,00,00,000 | 8,00,000 |
| Known misstatement (100% examined items) | Actual | 30,000 |
| **Total likely misstatement** | | **8,30,000** |

**WN-5 — Compare with tolerable misstatement.**
Total likely misstatement ₹8,30,000 **>** Tolerable misstatement ₹3,50,000.

**Auditor's conclusion (SA 530):** Since the projected misstatement plus known misstatement (₹8,30,000) exceeds the tolerable misstatement (₹3,50,000), the sampling results indicate that the recorded sales population **may be materially misstated**. The auditor must (a) request management to investigate the misstatements and the possibility of further misstatement, and make any necessary corrections, and (b) if uncorrected, perform additional/extended audit procedures (e.g., increase the sample size or apply alternative procedures) before forming an opinion, and consider the effect on the audit opinion.

**Answer:** Projected misstatement = **₹8,00,000**; total likely misstatement = **₹8,30,000**, which exceeds tolerable misstatement of ₹3,50,000 — the population may be materially misstated.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner specifically checks that misstatements in individually-inspected items are added at actual and NOT projected. A common error is projecting the entire ₹2,40,00,000 or projecting the ₹30,000 too — both lose marks.)*

### Q37. Ch: Audit Sampling & Analytical Procedures — When Analytical Procedures Alone Suffice (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** CA Iqbal wishes to obtain substantive assurance over the "Interest on term loan" expense of Meridian Logistics Ltd. purely by a substantive analytical procedure, without testing any individual entry. Explain, under SA 520 "Analytical Procedures", the factors that determine whether a substantive analytical procedure can, by itself, provide the required assurance, and illustrate why interest on loan is a suitable candidate.

**Answer:**
As per SA 520 "Analytical Procedures", **substantive analytical procedures** are evaluations of financial information through analysis of plausible relationships among both financial and non-financial data. When designing and performing substantive analytical procedures, either alone or in combination with tests of details, the auditor must:

**(a) Determine the suitability of the procedure for given assertions:** Substantive analytical procedures are generally more suitable for large volumes of transactions that tend to be **predictable over time**. Their suitability depends on the auditor's assessment of how effective the procedure will be in detecting a misstatement that could be material.

**(b) Evaluate the reliability of the data** from which the expectation is developed, considering the source, comparability, nature and relevance of the information available, and the controls over its preparation.

**(c) Develop an expectation of recorded amounts or ratios** and evaluate whether the expectation is **sufficiently precise** to identify a material misstatement at the desired level of assurance.

**(d) Determine the amount of any difference** of recorded amounts from expected values that is acceptable without further investigation (having regard to materiality and the desired level of assurance).

A substantive analytical procedure can suffice **on its own** where: the relationship is plausible and stable, the account is predictable, the data used is reliable, and the expectation can be developed with sufficient precision so that an unacceptable difference would signal a material misstatement.

**Why interest on term loan is a suitable candidate:** Interest expense is highly predictable — it can be independently estimated as **Opening/average loan balance × applicable rate of interest**, adjusted for repayments and drawdowns during the year. The inputs (sanctioned amount, rate per the loan agreement, repayment schedule) are reliable and externally corroborated by the bank statement/loan confirmation. Because a plausible and precise expectation can be built, a material misstatement (e.g., unrecorded interest, wrong rate) would produce a difference beyond the threshold. Therefore, this account can be substantively tested by analytics alone.

**Conclusion:** CA Iqbal may rely on a substantive analytical procedure alone for interest on term loan, provided he documents a sufficiently precise independent expectation from reliable data and investigates any difference exceeding the pre-set threshold.

### Q39. Ch: Audit Sampling & Analytical Procedures — Evaluating Results of the Sample (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** After completing his sample testing of the purchases of Orion Chemicals Ltd., CA Vishal has computed the misstatements found. State the steps he must follow under SA 530 to "evaluate the results of audit sampling", including the treatment of an "anomaly".

**Answer:**
As per SA 530 "Audit Sampling", after performing audit procedures on each item selected, the auditor is required to **evaluate the results of the sample** through the following steps:

**(i) Evaluate results of sampling for tests of controls:** An unexpectedly high sample deviation rate may lead the auditor to increase the assessed risk of material misstatement, unless further evidence supporting the initial assessment is obtained.

**(ii) Project misstatements for tests of details:** For substantive procedures, the auditor must **project misstatements found in the sample to the population** so as to obtain a broad view of the scale of misstatement, though this projected amount may not fully reflect the actual misstatement.

**(iii) Treatment of an "anomaly":** An **anomaly** is a misstatement or deviation that is *demonstrably not representative* of misstatements or deviations in the population. In the extremely rare case where the auditor considers a misstatement/deviation to be an anomaly, he must obtain a **high degree of certainty** that it is not representative — by performing additional audit procedures to establish that it does not affect the remainder of the population. Such an anomalous misstatement is **excluded when projecting** sample misstatements to the population, but its actual amount is still added to the total.

**(iv) Evaluate whether the use of sampling has provided a reasonable basis for conclusions:** The auditor must compare the **projected misstatement (plus anomalous misstatement, if any) with the tolerable misstatement**. If the projected misstatement exceeds tolerable misstatement, the sample does not provide a reasonable basis; the population may be materially misstated, and the auditor should reassess sampling risk, request management to correct the misstatements, and/or extend procedures.

**Conclusion:** CA Vishal must project the sample misstatement, treat any anomaly separately with high certainty, and conclude by comparing the projected misstatement against the tolerable misstatement to decide whether the population may be materially misstated.

*(Full-marks tip: Correctly define "anomaly" and note it is excluded from projection but added at actual — this precise point is frequently examined and rewarded.)*

### Q40. Ch: Audit Sampling & Analytical Procedures — Statistical vs. Non-Statistical Sampling and Selection Methods (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** CA Priya is deciding between statistical and non-statistical sampling for the audit of Sentinel Retail Ltd. and is also unsure which sample-selection method to adopt. Distinguish between statistical and non-statistical sampling under SA 530, and briefly describe the principal methods of selecting a sample.

**Answer:**
As per SA 530 "Audit Sampling", **audit sampling** is the application of audit procedures to less than 100% of items within a population of audit relevance such that all sampling units have a chance of selection, in order to draw a conclusion about the entire population.

**Distinction — Statistical vs. Non-Statistical Sampling:**

| Basis | Statistical Sampling | Non-Statistical Sampling |
|---|---|---|
| Random selection | Uses random selection of the sample | Does not necessarily use random selection |
| Probability theory | Uses probability theory to evaluate sample results, including measurement of sampling risk | Does not use probability theory to measure sampling risk |
| Measurement of sampling risk | Sampling risk is measured quantitatively | Sampling risk is assessed only by judgement |
| Basis of conclusion | Objective/mathematical | Based on auditor's professional judgement |

A sampling approach that has either of the features of random selection or use of probability theory but not both, is a **non-statistical** approach.

**Methods of selecting a sample:** SA 530 recognises the following principal methods —
1. **Random selection** — using a random number generator/table, giving each item an equal chance of selection.
2. **Systematic selection** — the number of sampling units is divided by the sample size to give a sampling interval, and after a random start, every nth item is selected.
3. **Monetary Unit Sampling (MUS)** — a value-weighted selection where the sampling unit is an individual monetary unit, so higher-value items have a proportionately greater chance of selection.
4. **Haphazard selection** — the auditor selects the sample without following a structured technique, but avoiding any conscious bias or predictability (not appropriate for statistical sampling).
5. **Block selection** — selection of a block(s) of contiguous items; generally not appropriate because most populations are structured so that items in a sequence share similar characteristics.

**Conclusion:** CA Priya may choose either approach; whichever she selects, the sample size must be sufficient to reduce sampling risk to an acceptably low level, and the selection method must give all sampling units a chance of selection.

*(Full-marks tip: The tabular distinction (random selection + probability theory = statistical) and naming all five selection methods with a one-line description each secures full marks. Note that block selection is generally inappropriate — examiners look for this caveat.)*

5. **Evaluation and disclosure (AS 29):** Assess whether each item is correctly classified — where an outflow has become *probable* and estimable, a **provision** must be made rather than mere disclosure; where the possibility is *remote*, no disclosure is required. Ensure disclosure per Schedule III and AS 29 (nature, estimate of financial effect, uncertainties).

### Q47. Ch: Audit Sampling & Analytical Procedures — Tolerable Misstatement, Tolerable Rate & Sampling Risk (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the meaning of "sampling risk", "non-sampling risk", "tolerable misstatement" and "tolerable rate of deviation" as defined in SA 530, and briefly state how sampling risk affects the auditor's conclusions.

**Answer:**
As per SA 530 "Audit Sampling", the relevant terms are defined as follows:

1. **Sampling risk:** The risk that the auditor's conclusion based on a sample may be **different from the conclusion** if the entire population were subjected to the same audit procedure. Sampling risk can lead to two types of erroneous conclusions:
   - For **tests of controls** — that controls are more effective than they actually are; and for **tests of details** — that a material misstatement does not exist when in fact it does. This type affects **audit effectiveness** and is more likely to lead to an inappropriate audit opinion.
   - That controls are less effective than they actually are, or that a material misstatement exists when in fact it does not. This type affects **audit efficiency** as it usually leads to additional work.

2. **Non-sampling risk:** The risk that the auditor reaches an **erroneous conclusion for any reason not related to sampling risk** — e.g., use of inappropriate procedures, or misinterpretation of audit evidence and failing to recognise a misstatement.

3. **Tolerable misstatement:** A monetary amount set by the auditor in respect of which he seeks to obtain an appropriate level of assurance that the amount set is **not exceeded by the actual misstatement** in the population. It is the application of performance materiality to a particular sampling procedure.

4. **Tolerable rate of deviation:** A **rate of deviation** from prescribed internal control procedures set by the auditor in respect of which he seeks to obtain an appropriate level of assurance that the rate set is not exceeded by the actual rate of deviation in the population.

**Effect of sampling risk:** Since a sample is less than 100% of the population, the auditor's conclusion carries sampling risk. A larger sample size reduces sampling risk; hence the auditor determines a sample size sufficient to reduce sampling risk to an **acceptably low level**.

**Conclusion:** The auditor addresses sampling risk by adequate sample size and evaluation, and non-sampling risk through proper planning, supervision and review.

*(Full-marks tip: Distinguish the two directions of sampling-risk error — effectiveness vs. efficiency — and note that tolerable misstatement is the application of performance materiality to a sampling procedure.)*

1. **Examine the schedule** of debtors and agree the total with the general ledger control account and the Balance Sheet; check ageing analysis.
2. **External confirmation (SA 505):** Obtain **direct confirmation of balances** from a sample of debtors as at the balance-sheet date; the auditor should control the confirmation requests (selecting accounts, framing and dispatching, receiving replies directly). Reconcile differences reported by debtors.
3. **Alternative procedures** where confirmations are not received — verify **subsequent receipts** after the year-end, examine sales invoices, delivery challans and customer correspondence to establish existence and recoverability.
4. **Valuation/recoverability:** Review the ageing, scrutinise long-outstanding and disputed balances, and assess the adequacy of the **provision for doubtful debts**.
5. **Cut-off and rights:** Perform sales cut-off tests and ensure the debts have not been factored/assigned (rights assertion).

**Comment on management's refusal of confirmation (SA 505):** When management **refuses to allow the auditor to send a confirmation request**, the auditor must (a) enquire into management's reasons and seek audit evidence as to their validity and reasonableness; (b) evaluate the implications on the assessment of the relevant risks of material misstatement, including the risk of **fraud**, and on the nature, timing and extent of other audit procedures; and (c) perform **alternative audit procedures** to obtain relevant and reliable audit evidence. If management's refusal is **unreasonable**, or the auditor is unable to obtain relevant and reliable evidence from alternative procedures, he should communicate with those charged with governance and consider the implications for the audit opinion (possible scope limitation).

**Comment on the two debts:**
- **₹35 lakh from a customer under insolvency:** The commencement of insolvency proceedings is strong evidence that the debt is **doubtful/bad**. A provision (or write-off, to the extent irrecoverable) must be made; carrying it at full value overstates receivables and profit. This is an **adjusting event** if the condition existed at the year-end (SA 560 / AS 4).
- **₹12 lakh outstanding over three years with no provision:** A debt long overdue with no recovery indicates doubtful recoverability; absence of any provision violates the **prudence principle**. The auditor should require an appropriate provision unless management demonstrates recoverability with convincing evidence.

**Conclusion:** After performing confirmation and alternative procedures, CA must insist on adequate provisioning for the ₹35 lakh (insolvent debtor) and the ₹12 lakh (long-overdue) debts, and treat management's blanket refusal of confirmations as a matter requiring evaluation of fraud risk and possible reporting/scope-limitation implications.

The insolvency debt is an adjusting event needing provision — link to prudence. Missing the fraud-risk implication of a refusal is a common deduction.)*

### Q50. Ch: Audit Sampling & Analytical Procedures — Stratification and Projection of Misstatement (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** CA Farhan stratified the trade receivables population of Crestwood Ltd. into two strata for substantive testing. High-value balances above ₹5,00,000 were examined 100%; the remaining balances were sampled. From the data below, compute the total likely misstatement and compare it with a tolerable misstatement of ₹4,00,000.

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Stratum 1 — balances above ₹5,00,000 (examined 100%) | 60,00,000 |
| Misstatement actually found in Stratum 1 | 45,000 |
| Stratum 2 — remaining balances (sampling population) | 1,80,00,000 |
| Book value of sample drawn from Stratum 2 | 30,00,000 |
| Misstatement found in the Stratum 2 sample | 90,000 |
| Tolerable misstatement | 4,00,000 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Treatment of each stratum (SA 530).** Stratum 1 is examined **100%**, so its misstatement is a **known misstatement** taken at actual (₹45,000) and is **not** projected. Stratum 2 is sampled, so its sample misstatement must be **projected** over the Stratum 2 population only.

**WN-2 — Misstatement ratio for Stratum 2.**
Ratio = ₹90,000 ÷ ₹30,00,000 = **0.03 (3%)**

**WN-3 — Project over the Stratum 2 population.**
Projected misstatement (Stratum 2) = 0.03 × ₹1,80,00,000 = **₹5,40,000**

**Statement Showing Total Likely Misstatement in Trade Receivables**

| Stratum | Basis | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| Stratum 1 (100% examined) | Actual (known) | 45,000 |
| Stratum 2 (sampled) | 3% × 1,80,00,000 (projected) | 5,40,000 |
| **Total likely misstatement** | | **5,85,000** |

**WN-4 — Comparison.**
Total likely misstatement ₹5,85,000 **>** Tolerable misstatement ₹4,00,000.

**Auditor's conclusion (SA 530):** Because the aggregate of the projected misstatement in the sampled stratum and the known misstatement in the fully-examined stratum (₹5,85,000) exceeds the tolerable misstatement (₹4,00,000), the sampling results indicate that trade receivables **may be materially misstated**. CA Farhan should request management to investigate and correct the misstatements and, if they remain uncorrected, extend his procedures (larger sample/alternative tests) and consider the impact on his opinion.

**Answer:** Total likely misstatement = **₹5,85,000** (₹45,000 known + ₹5,40,000 projected), which exceeds the tolerable misstatement of ₹4,00,000.

*(Full-marks tip: Stratification means the 100%-examined stratum's misstatement is added at actual and NOT projected, while only the sampled stratum is projected — over its own stratum value, not the whole population. Mixing the two strata for projection is the standard error.)*

### Q51. Ch: Audit Sampling & Analytical Procedures + Audit of Items of Financial Statements — Integrated Case (Marks: 8) [Application]
**Question:** CA Rukmini is auditing Astra Consumer Products Ltd. for FY 2025-26. She encounters the following three situations. Address each with reference to the applicable SA/AS and state the appropriate audit response.

(a) To test the "existence and valuation" of the large, homogeneous trade-receivables population (over 8,000 accounts), she wants to know whether audit sampling is appropriate and how to select the sample to test both overstatement and completeness.

(b) The gross profit ratio has fallen from 38% to 29% though the sales mix is unchanged, and management attributes it "to competition". She has performed no tests of details on cost of sales, relying only on this ratio.

(c) A revaluation of plant during the year created a Revaluation Reserve of ₹50 lakh; the company transferred ₹50 lakh from this reserve to the Statement of Profit and Loss and treated it as profit available for distribution.

**Answer:**

**(a) Audit sampling for trade receivables (SA 530):**
Audit sampling is **appropriate** for a large, homogeneous population such as 8,000 receivable accounts, as it enables the auditor to draw a conclusion about the whole population by testing less than 100% of items, with all sampling units having a chance of selection. To test **overstatement (existence)**, she should sample from the **recorded receivables ledger** — for example, using **monetary unit sampling**, which weights selection towards higher-value balances (the direction of risk for overstatement) — and confirm/verify those balances. However, sampling the recorded ledger cannot detect **completeness/understatement** (unrecorded credit sales); for that assertion she must select from a **different population** — e.g., dispatch/delivery records and post-year-end collections — to trace forward into the ledger. She must define the population as appropriate and complete before selecting, and project any misstatement found to the population, comparing it with tolerable misstatement.

**(b) Reliance on analytical procedures alone for cost of sales (SA 520):**
Performing only a high-level GP-ratio comparison and accepting a vague explanation ("competition") is **not sufficient**. Under SA 520, when the analytical procedure identifies a fluctuation or relationship **inconsistent with other information or differing significantly from expected values** (here a 9-percentage-point fall in GP with an unchanged sales mix is a significant, unexplained variance), the auditor must (i) **enquire of management and obtain appropriate corroborative evidence** for the explanation, and (ii) **perform other audit procedures** (tests of details) as necessary. A generic explanation that cannot be corroborated is unacceptable; the sharp margin drop may indicate misstatement of sales, purchases, or closing inventory (e.g., inventory understatement, unrecorded sales, or fictitious purchases). CA Rukmini must therefore corroborate the explanation and perform substantive tests of details on cost of sales and inventory before drawing a conclusion.

**(c) Revaluation Reserve transferred to P&L as distributable profit (AS 10):**
Under **AS 10 "Property, Plant and Equipment"**, a revaluation surplus is credited to a **Revaluation Reserve** and represents an **unrealised gain**; it must **not be credited to the Statement of Profit and Loss** as income, and it is **not a free reserve available for distribution** of dividend. The company's transfer of ₹50 lakh from the Revaluation Reserve to the Statement of Profit and Loss overstates profit by ₹50 lakh and wrongly treats an unrealised capital reserve as distributable. The surplus may be transferred to **retained earnings** only when the asset is derecognised (or progressively, to the extent of the additional depreciation on the revalued portion) — but never routed through the P&L as profit for distribution. CA Rukmini must require the ₹50 lakh to be **reversed out of profit** and retained in the Revaluation Reserve (or transferred directly to retained earnings, not as distributable profit); if not corrected, she should consider a **modified opinion** for the ₹50 lakh overstatement of profit.

**Conclusion:** (a) Sampling is appropriate but the population must match the assertion (overstatement vs. completeness); (b) analytics alone do not suffice for a significant unexplained GP fall — corroborate and perform tests of details; (c) the ₹50 lakh revaluation surplus cannot be routed through P&L or distributed, and must be reversed, failing which the opinion is modified.

*(Full-marks tip: This integrated question rewards correctly pairing each situation with its Standard — SA 530 (population must fit the assertion), SA 520 (respond to significant unexplained fluctuations with enquiry + tests of details), and AS 10 (revaluation surplus is unrealised, not P&L income, not distributable). State the consequence for the opinion in (c).)*

### Q52. Ch: Audit in an Automated Environment — Understanding & Documenting an IT-dependent Control (Marks: 6) [Application]

**Question:** While auditing Nimbus Retail Ltd., which runs an integrated ERP, the audit team notes that the "customer credit-limit exceeded" exception report is generated by the system every night and reviewed by the credit controller before dispatch of goods is released. The engagement partner asks the team to treat this as an *IT-dependent manual control*. Explain what an IT-dependent control is, how the auditor should obtain an understanding of it, and what specific matters the auditor must document and test before placing reliance on the credit controller's review.

**Answer:**
An **automated environment** is one in which the entity's business processes and financial reporting are carried on through IT systems, and SA 315 (Revised), "Identifying and Assessing the Risks of Material Misstatement", requires the auditor to understand the IT environment relevant to the flow of transactions.

An **IT-dependent manual control** is a control where a person performs the control activity, but the effectiveness of that person's action depends critically on the completeness and accuracy of information produced by the IT system (an *IPE — Information Produced by the Entity*). Here, the credit controller's review is manual, but it is only as reliable as the system-generated "credit-limit exceeded" exception report; if the report is incomplete or inaccurate, the manual review is worthless. Hence the control is *dependent* on the IT system.

**Obtaining an understanding.** In accordance with SA 315, the auditor should perform a **walkthrough** of the control — tracing one exception from the underlying customer master and sales order, through the system logic that flags it, to the report and the controller's follow-up action — using the techniques of **inquiry, observation, inspection and re-performance**. The auditor should understand (i) how the system identifies an exceeded credit limit (the business rule/parameter), (ii) how the report is compiled and whether it captures *all* exceptions (completeness), and (iii) what action the controller takes and how it is evidenced.

**Matters to document and test (before placing reliance):**

| Matter to address | What the auditor documents / tests |
|---|---|
| Reliability of the IPE (the report) | Test the **completeness and accuracy** of the exception report — that the report parameters/logic correctly capture every order breaching the limit and nothing is omitted or duplicated. |
| General IT Controls (GITC) | Assess GITCs over the ERP — access security, change management and program development — because an ineffective GITC undermines the report's reliability and hence the automated portion of the control. |
| Design & implementation of the manual review | Document how the controller reviews the report, the criteria applied, evidence of review (sign-off/timestamp) and the escalation of unresolved items. |
| Operating effectiveness | Test a sample of exceptions to confirm the controller acted (blocked/released with approval) and that no flagged order was despatched without resolution. |

*(Full-marks tip: examiners reward explicitly splitting the control into its automated part [the report] and its manual part [the review], and stating that reliability of the IPE plus GITCs must be established first — candidates who test only the controller's sign-off and ignore report completeness lose marks.)*

**Conclusion:** The credit-review is an IT-dependent manual control; the auditor may rely on it only after testing the completeness and accuracy of the system-generated exception report, evaluating the relevant GITCs, and confirming the operating effectiveness of the controller's review.

### Q53. Ch: Audit in an Automated Environment — Testing Automated Controls Once vs. Each Time (Marks: 5) [Theory]

**Question:** "Once an automated application control has been tested and found to be operating effectively, the auditor need not test it afresh every year." Examine this statement and state the conditions the auditor must be satisfied about before adopting the "test once" approach, including the benchmarking of controls.

**Answer:**
The statement is **broadly correct but conditional**. A key characteristic of a purely **automated application control** is that, because it is performed consistently by the program, it processes transactions the *same way every time* unless the program itself is changed. This gives rise to the principle of **consistency (inherent consistency)** of automated controls, which permits the auditor to reduce the extent and frequency of testing compared with a manual control (where human performance may vary).

Under SA 330, "The Auditor's Responses to Assessed Risks", once the auditor has tested an automated control and established that it is operating effectively, the auditor may not need to test its operating effectiveness afresh in every subsequent audit — a technique known as **benchmarking of automated controls** — *provided* the auditor is satisfied about the supporting environment.

**Conditions to be satisfied before relying on "test once":**

| Condition | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Effective General IT Controls (GITC) | The GITCs — particularly **change management** and **access/security** — must be operating effectively so that unauthorised or undocumented changes to the program cannot occur. |
| The control has not changed | The auditor must confirm the application control and the related program logic have **not been modified** since it was last tested (obtain and inspect the change log). |
| No significant changes in the environment | No new versions, patches, migrations or configuration changes that could affect the control. |
| Periodic re-establishment | The auditor should **re-test the benchmark periodically** (not indefinitely rely on the earlier test), and re-test immediately if any change has occurred. |

If the GITCs are weak or a program change has occurred, the "consistency" assumption fails and the automated control must be **re-tested each time**.

*(Full-marks tip: the marks lie in linking the "test once" concession to effective GITCs and change management, and in naming the technique "benchmarking" — candidates who merely say automated controls are consistent, without the GITC condition, cap out at half marks.)*

**Conclusion:** The auditor may test an automated control once and benchmark it for future periods only where GITCs (especially change management) are effective and the control/program is confirmed unchanged; otherwise annual re-testing is necessary.

### Q54. Ch: Audit Documentation & Quality — Timely Preparation of Audit Documentation (Marks: 4) [Theory]

**Question:** State, with reference to SA 230, why audit documentation must be prepared on a *timely* basis, and the consequence of preparing documentation after the auditor's work is completed.

**Answer:**
SA 230, "Audit Documentation", deals with the auditor's responsibility to prepare audit documentation for an audit of financial statements. Paragraph 7 of SA 230 requires the auditor to prepare audit documentation on a **timely basis**.

**Why timely preparation matters:**
1. Preparing documentation on a timely basis helps to **enhance the quality of the audit** and facilitates the **effective review and evaluation** of the audit evidence obtained and conclusions reached *before the auditor's report is finalised*.
2. Documentation prepared **after the work has been performed** is likely to be **less accurate** than documentation prepared at the time the work is done, because memory fades and the connection between evidence and conclusion weakens.
3. Timely documentation supports the auditor if the work is later challenged, and enables real-time supervision and direction of the engagement team.

**Conclusion:** Timely preparation of audit documentation is a requirement of SA 230; documentation prepared contemporaneously is more reliable and improves audit quality, whereas documentation deferred to after completion tends to be less accurate.

### Q55. Ch: Audit Documentation & Quality — Assembly & Retention of the Final Audit File (Marks: 6) [Application]

**Question:** CA Meera signed her audit report on the financial statements of Orchid Foods Ltd. on **25 May 2025**. Owing to staff shortage, the office administrator asked whether the working papers can be organised "whenever convenient" and whether old files of a 2018 audit can now be shredded to save space. Advise CA Meera on (a) the time limit for assembly of the final audit file, (b) what may and may not be done to documentation after assembly, and (c) the minimum retention period, citing SA 230 and SQC 1.

**Answer:**
The matter is governed by **SA 230, "Audit Documentation"** (read with SQC 1, "Quality Control for Firms that Perform Audits and Reviews of Historical Financial Information, and Other Assurance and Related Services Engagements"), on the **assembly and retention** of the audit file.

**(a) Time limit for assembly of the final audit file.**
SA 230 requires the auditor to assemble the audit documentation in an audit file and complete the **administrative process of assembly on a timely basis after the date of the auditor's report**. SQC 1 states that an appropriate time limit within which to complete assembly of the final audit file is **ordinarily not more than 60 days after the date of the auditor's report**. Therefore, assembly cannot be done "whenever convenient" — for a report dated **25 May 2025**, the file must be assembled by **24 July 2025 (within 60 days)**.

**(b) Changes to documentation after assembly.**
After assembly of the final audit file has been completed, the auditor **shall not delete or discard** audit documentation of any nature before the end of its retention period. Where the auditor finds it necessary to **modify existing** documentation or **add new** documentation after assembly, the auditor must document, regardless of the nature of the modifications: (i) the **specific reasons** for making them, and (ii) **when and by whom** they were made and reviewed. Assembly is an administrative process that does **not** involve performing new audit procedures or drawing new conclusions.

**(c) Retention period.**
SQC 1 requires the firm to establish policies for retention of engagement documentation for a period sufficient to meet the needs of the firm, and in any case **not shorter than seven years from the date of the auditor's report** (or the date of the group auditor's report, if later). Therefore the **2018 audit files cannot yet be shredded** — the seven-year period from the 2018 report date has not elapsed; shredding now would breach SQC 1.

*(Full-marks tip: give the two numbers precisely — 60 days for assembly and 7 years for retention — and stress that post-assembly changes require documenting the reason, who and when; students who confuse the 60-day and 7-year periods lose easy marks.)*

**Conclusion:** CA Meera must assemble the file within 60 days of 25 May 2025, must not delete anything before the retention period ends, must record the reason/author/date of any later change, and must retain files for at least seven years — so the 2018 files cannot be destroyed.

### Q56. Ch: Audit in an Automated Environment — General IT Controls vs. Application Controls (Marks: 5) [Theory]

**Question:** Distinguish between **General IT Controls (GITC)** and **Application Controls** in an automated environment, and explain the relationship between the two for the purpose of an audit.

**Answer:**
In an automated environment, the auditor evaluates two broad categories of internal controls over IT — **General IT Controls** and **Application Controls** — both of which are relevant under SA 315 (Revised).

**General IT Controls** are policies and procedures that relate to *many applications* and support the effective functioning of application controls by ensuring the continued proper operation of information systems. They operate at an *entity/infrastructure* level.

**Application Controls** are *manual or automated* procedures that typically operate at a *business-process level* and apply to the processing of transactions by individual applications.

| Basis | General IT Controls (GITC) | Application Controls |
|---|---|---|
| Level | Pervasive — across the IT environment/many applications | Specific to a particular application/business process |
| Purpose | Ensure integrity of information and proper operation of systems | Ensure completeness, accuracy, validity and authorisation of transactions |
| Examples | Access security, change management, program development, IT operations/back-up | Input validation checks, edit checks, automated calculations, sequence checks, configurable approvals/tolerances |
| Nature | Mostly automated/procedural, IT-department level | Automated or IT-dependent manual, at process level |

**Relationship:** Application controls can be relied upon only if the **GITCs are effective**. Weak GITCs (e.g., poor change management or unrestricted access) undermine even a well-designed application control, because the program could be altered or overridden. Thus GITCs provide the **foundation** on which reliance on application controls is built.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner wants the "foundation" linkage — that effective application controls presuppose effective GITCs — plus at least two concrete examples of each; a table earns full comparison marks.)*

**Conclusion:** GITCs are pervasive controls that safeguard the IT environment, while application controls operate within specific business processes; the reliability of application controls depends on effective GITCs.

### Q57. Ch: Audit in an Automated Environment — CAATs & Data Analytics (Marks: 6) [Application]

**Question:** The audit team of Vertex Pharma Ltd. has been given a full download of the 4.2 lakh sales-invoice records for the year in a data file. The senior wants to move away from manual sample-checking and use **Computer Assisted Audit Techniques (CAATs)/Data Analytics**. Explain what CAATs and Automated Auditing / Data Analytics (CAAT-DA) are, list four specific analyses the team can run on this population, and state the precautions before relying on the results.

**Answer:**
**Computer Assisted Audit Techniques (CAATs)** are the application of auditing procedures **using computers as an audit tool** to test the reliability of an entity's data and controls, enabling the auditor to test a **100% population** rather than a sample. When such techniques are combined with analytical logic to obtain audit evidence, they are referred to as **Automated Auditing / Audit Data Analytics (ADA)**. Their use is consistent with SA 500, "Audit Evidence", (relevance and reliability of evidence) and SA 520, "Analytical Procedures".

**Four specific analyses on the 4.2 lakh sales invoices:**

| Understand data **structure and field definitions** | Wrong field mapping produces wrong analysis. |
| Evaluate **exceptions with professional judgement** | An exception is a *lead*, not automatically a misstatement; corroborate before concluding. |

*(Full-marks tip: candidates score by naming CAATs' ability to test 100% of the population AND by emphasising reconciliation of the data extract to the ledger before any analysis — skipping data-integrity precautions is the common deduction.)*

**Conclusion:** CAATs/Data Analytics let the team test the entire invoice population through completeness, cut-off, exception and duplicate analyses, but reliance is valid only after confirming the extracted data is complete, accurate and sourced from the production system.

### Q58. Ch: Audit Documentation & Quality — Form, Content & Extent of Documentation (Experienced Auditor Test) (Marks: 5) [Theory]

### Q59. Ch: Audit Documentation & Quality — Elements of a Quality Control System for the Firm (SQC 1) (Marks: 8) [Theory]

**Question:** SQC 1 requires every firm to establish and maintain a **system of quality control**. Enumerate and briefly explain the **six elements** of a firm's system of quality control, and state on whom the ultimate responsibility for the system rests.

**Answer:**
SQC 1, "Quality Control for Firms that Perform Audits and Reviews of Historical Financial Information, and Other Assurance and Related Services Engagements", requires a firm to establish a system of quality control designed to provide it with **reasonable assurance** that the firm and its personnel **comply with professional standards and regulatory/legal requirements**, and that reports issued are **appropriate in the circumstances**. The system comprises **six elements**:

| Element | Brief explanation |
|---|---|

| 2. **Ethical requirements** | Policies to provide reasonable assurance that the firm and personnel comply with relevant ethical requirements — integrity, objectivity, professional competence and due care, confidentiality and professional behaviour. |

### Q60. Ch: Audit Documentation & Quality — Engagement Quality Control Review (EQCR) (Marks: 6) [Application]

**Question:** Orion & Associates audits **Skyline Ltd., a listed company**. The engagement partner intends to sign the audit report the same day the review is completed and considers an Engagement Quality Control Review (EQCR) "optional for a routine audit". Advise the firm, under SQC 1, on (a) when an EQCR is mandatory, (b) what the reviewer must evaluate, and (c) the effect of the EQCR on the dating of the report.

**Answer:**
The issue is governed by **SQC 1** dealing with the **Engagement Quality Control Review (EQCR)**, a component of the "engagement performance" element of the firm's quality control system.

**(a) When EQCR is mandatory.**
SQC 1 requires the firm to establish policies setting out the criteria for an EQCR, and requires an EQCR to be carried out for **all audits of financial statements of listed entities**. As **Skyline Ltd. is a listed company**, an EQCR is **mandatory**, not optional. The firm should also apply EQCR to other engagements meeting its risk-based criteria.

**(b) What the reviewer evaluates.**
The engagement quality control reviewer performs an **objective evaluation of the significant judgements** made by the engagement team and the conclusions reached in formulating the report. This includes:
- discussion of significant matters with the engagement partner;
- review of the financial statements and the proposed report;
- review of **selected audit documentation** relating to significant judgements and the conclusions reached;
- evaluation of the **conclusions reached in formulating the report** and consideration of whether the proposed report is appropriate;
- for listed entities, consideration of the engagement team's evaluation of the firm's **independence**, whether appropriate **consultation** on contentious matters has taken place, and whether documentation reflects the resolution of significant matters.

**(c) Effect on dating of the report.**
The report must **not be dated earlier than the completion of the EQCR**. SQC 1 requires the firm's policies to require **completion of the engagement quality control review before the report is issued**. ### Q61. Ch: Audit in an Automated Environment — Risks in an Automated Environment & Auditor's Response (Marks: 6) [Theory]

**Question:** An automated environment brings distinctive risks that a manual environment does not. Discuss the specific **risks that arise in an automated environment** and the auditor's response to them.

**Answer:**
In an **automated environment**, the entity's reliance on IT systems for initiating, recording, processing and reporting transactions gives rise to risks that the auditor must consider under SA 315 (Revised). The specific **IT-related risks** include:

| Risk in an automated environment | Nature of the risk |
|---|---|
| Unauthorised **access** to systems/data | Reliance on systems/programs that are inaccurately processing data, or processing inaccurate data, or both. |
| Unauthorised **changes** to data in master files | Master data (prices, rates, customer limits) may be altered without authorisation, affecting many transactions. |
| Unauthorised **changes to systems or programs** | Program changes may be made without approval, causing systematic errors. |
| **Failure to make necessary changes** | Required updates (e.g., tax-rate changes) not implemented. |
| Inappropriate **manual intervention / override** | Automated controls bypassed by privileged users. |
| Potential **loss of data** or inability to access data | System failure or inadequate back-up. |

A distinctive feature is that an error in the program produces a **consistent, systematic misstatement** across all affected transactions (unlike random human error).

**Auditor's response:**
1. Obtain an understanding of the **IT environment and IT-related risks** as part of understanding internal control (SA 315).
2. Evaluate the design and implementation, and test operating effectiveness, of **General IT Controls** (access, change management, program development, IT operations).
3. Test relevant **application controls**, and where controls are relied upon, ensure GITCs support them.
4. Use **CAATs/Data Analytics** to test large volumes of data and identify exceptions.
5. Where controls cannot be relied upon, design **substantive procedures** responsive to the assessed risk (SA 330).

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner rewards naming the six access/change/master-data risks AND the point that program errors are systematic; a response section referencing GITCs, application controls and CAATs completes the answer.)*

**Conclusion:** An automated environment creates risks of unauthorised access, changes and data loss that can cause systematic misstatements; the auditor responds by understanding IT risks, testing GITCs and application controls, and applying CAATs and substantive procedures.

### Q62. Ch: Audit Documentation & Quality — Ownership & Confidentiality of Working Papers (Marks: 4) [Application]

**Question:** After completing the audit of Larkspur Textiles Ltd., the client's finance director demands copies of all audit working papers, claiming that "since the company paid the fees, the papers belong to the company." Advise on the **ownership and availability** of audit working papers with reference to SA 230 and SQC 1.

**Answer:**
The issue concerns **ownership, custody and confidentiality of audit documentation** under SA 230 and SQC 1.

4. **Incoming auditor.** The **incoming (new) auditor has no right** to access the retiring auditor's working papers. The predecessor is not bound to hand them over; the new auditor conducts the audit independently (the retiring auditor may, as a matter of professional courtesy and with the client's consent, allow limited access, but is not obliged to).

### Q63. Ch: Audit in an Automated Environment — Data Integrity & the CIA Triad / IPE Reliability (Marks: 5) [Application]

**Question:** In the audit of Cobalt Logistics Ltd., the audit team relies heavily on system-generated reports (freight-cost summaries, ageing analyses) as audit evidence. Explain the concept of **data integrity** in an automated environment and how the auditor should test the **completeness and accuracy of a system-generated report (IPE)** before using it as audit evidence.

**Answer:**
In an automated environment, **data integrity** refers to the accuracy, completeness and consistency of data maintained and processed by the IT system throughout its life-cycle, so that data remains reliable and unaltered by unauthorised action. SA 500, "Audit Evidence", requires the auditor, when using information produced by the entity, to evaluate whether the information is **sufficiently reliable**, including its **accuracy and completeness**, and to evaluate whether it is **sufficiently precise and detailed** for the auditor's purposes.

**Testing completeness and accuracy of the report (IPE):**

| Attribute | Auditor's procedure |
|---|---|
| **Completeness** | Reconcile the report's record count/control totals to the source system or general ledger; confirm the report parameters/filters capture the full intended population and exclude nothing. |
| **Accuracy** | Trace a sample of report items back to source documents/underlying data; re-perform key calculations in the report (e.g., ageing buckets, freight totals). |

*(Full-marks tip: link report reliance to SA 500's requirement to test accuracy AND completeness of IPE, and mention reconciling control totals plus checking the report parameters — candidates who test only sample accuracy and ignore completeness lose marks.)*

**Conclusion:** Data integrity underpins the reliability of system-generated reports; before using an IPE as audit evidence the auditor must, under SA 500, test both its completeness (reconcile control totals, verify parameters) and its accuracy (trace and re-perform), supported by relevant GITCs.

### Q64. Ch: Audit Documentation & Quality — Monitoring & Inspection of Completed Engagements (SQC 1) (Marks: 5) [Theory]

### Q65. Ch: Audit in an Automated Environment — Understanding & Documenting the IT Environment (Walkthrough) (Marks: 6) [Application]

**Question:** As part of planning the audit of Tesla-Mart Ltd., an e-commerce retailer whose entire order-to-cash cycle runs through a cloud ERP, you must document your **understanding of the IT environment** and how transactions flow. SA 315 (Revised), "Identifying and Assessing the Risks of Material Misstatement", requires the auditor to obtain an understanding of the entity's information system and the **IT environment** relevant to financial reporting, including the flow of transactions from initiation to recording in the general ledger.

### Q66. Ch: Audit Documentation & Quality — Documentation of Departure from a Relevant Requirement & Discussions of Significant Matters (Marks: 5) [Application]

**Question:** During the audit of Meridian Ltd., (i) the auditor judged it necessary, in the circumstances, to **depart from a relevant requirement of an SA** and perform an alternative procedure, and (ii) a **significant matter** (a disputed related-party transaction) was discussed with management. The audit manager is unsure what to record. Advise, under SA 230, on documenting a departure from a requirement and documenting discussions of significant matters.

**Answer:**
The matter is governed by the **specific documentation requirements of SA 230, "Audit Documentation"**.

**(i) Departure from a relevant requirement.**
SA 230 recognises that in **exceptional circumstances**, the auditor may judge it necessary to **depart from a relevant requirement in an SA**. In such a case, the auditor shall document:
- **how the alternative audit procedures performed achieve the aim** of that requirement, and
- **the reasons for the departure**.

- the **nature of the significant matters** discussed, and

**Additionally**, SA 230 requires that if the auditor identifies **information inconsistent with the final conclusion** on a significant matter, the auditor shall document **how the inconsistency was addressed**.

*(Full-marks tip: for a departure, state both limbs — how the alternative achieves the aim AND the reasons; for discussions, record nature, when and with whom. Omitting either limb of the departure requirement is the common deduction.)*

### Q67. Ch: Audit in an Automated Environment — Automated Controls in Revenue: Configurable vs. Fully Automated & "Test Once" Application (Marks: 8) [Application]

**Question:** In the audit of Quasar Telecom Ltd., the revenue system applies three controls: (A) a **fully automated three-way match** (PO–GRN–invoice) that blocks payment on mismatch; (B) a **configurable credit-limit tolerance** set in the customer master; and (C) an **IT-dependent manual review** of a system exception report by the billing supervisor. GITCs over change management and access were tested and found effective, and the change log shows **no changes** to the revenue programs since last year's test. (i) Classify each control, (ii) state whether each can be tested using the "test once / benchmarking" approach this year, and (iii) explain how you would test control (A) and control (B).

**Answer:**
The classification and testing strategy follow SA 315 (Revised) and SA 330, applying the principle that **automated controls operate consistently** and may be benchmarked where **GITCs are effective and no program change has occurred**.

**(i) Classification of the three controls:**

| Control | Classification | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| (A) Automated three-way match blocking payment | **Fully automated application control** | Performed entirely by the program with no human action; consistent every time. |
| (B) Configurable credit-limit tolerance in the master | **Configurable/automated control (parameter-driven)** | The control is automated but depends on a configuration/parameter set in master data. |
| (C) Manual review of exception report by supervisor | **IT-dependent manual control** | A person performs the review, relying on a system-generated report (IPE). |

**(ii) Applicability of the "test once / benchmarking" approach this year:**

| Control | Benchmarking available? | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| (A) Fully automated | **Yes** | Effective change-management and access GITCs plus a confirmed unchanged program mean the auditor may rely on last period's test (benchmarking), subject to periodic re-testing. |
| (B) Configurable | **Yes, but** | Benchmarking applies to the automated logic; however, the auditor must additionally **test that the configuration/parameter (the tolerance value) has not been changed** and remains appropriately set — configuration changes may occur without a program change. |
| (C) IT-dependent manual | **No** | It has a **manual element** performed by a person, so operating effectiveness must be **tested each period** (human performance is not inherently consistent); the reliability of the exception report (IPE) must also be re-established. |

**(iii) Testing controls (A) and (B):**
- **Control (A) — three-way match:** *Test of one/few transactions plus GITC reliance.* Process **test data** (or select actual transactions) with a deliberate mismatch (e.g., invoice quantity ≠ GRN quantity) and confirm the system **blocks** the payment; process a matching set and confirm it is **released**. Combined with effective GITCs, a single successful test establishes the control (benchmarking). Inspect the change log to confirm no change to the matching program.
- **Control (B) — configurable tolerance:** Inspect the **current configuration** of the credit-limit tolerance in the customer master and agree it to the approved policy; obtain the **configuration change history** to confirm the parameter was not altered during the year (or, if altered, was authorised via change management); and test one/few transactions at/above the limit to confirm the system responds as configured.

*(Full-marks tip: the discriminator is recognising that a configurable control needs its parameter re-verified even when benchmarking, and that any control with a manual element must be re-tested annually; a table for both classification and benchmarking earns full structure marks.)*

**Conclusion:** Controls A and B are automated and may be benchmarked given effective GITCs and an unchanged program — but B additionally requires verifying its configuration parameter — whereas the IT-dependent manual control C must be re-tested every period.

### Q69. Ch: Company Audit — Appointment of Subsequent Auditor & Term (Sec 139) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** At the first Annual General Meeting (AGM) of Vermillion Textiles Ltd (a company that is neither a Government company nor a company covered under rotation), the Board proposes that the subsequent auditor, M/s Rhea & Co., be "appointed to hold office till the conclusion of the next AGM." A shareholder objects, arguing that a subsequent auditor must be appointed for a fixed block of five years and that yearly ratification is compulsory. Examine, with reference to the Companies Act, 2013, the correct legal position as to (i) the term for which a subsequent auditor is to be appointed, and (ii) whether annual ratification at every AGM is mandatory.

**Answer:**
The appointment of a subsequent auditor is governed by Section 139(1) of the Companies Act, 2013 read with Rule 3 of the Companies (Audit and Auditors) Rules, 2014.

Under Section 139(1), every company shall, at the **first annual general meeting**, appoint an individual or a firm as auditor who shall **hold office from the conclusion of that meeting till the conclusion of its sixth annual general meeting** and thereafter till the conclusion of every sixth meeting. Thus, the correct term of a subsequent auditor is a **block of five years** (i.e., from the conclusion of the AGM at which he is appointed up to the conclusion of the sixth AGM counting the meeting of appointment as the first), and **not** a term expiring at the conclusion of the "next" AGM. The Board's proposal appointing Rhea & Co. only "till the conclusion of the next AGM" is therefore incorrect for a subsequent auditor; a one-year tenure is the pattern for a first auditor under Section 139(6)/139(7), not for a subsequent auditor.

On the point of ratification: the first proviso to Section 139(1) originally required that the appointment be **placed for ratification** by members at every AGM. However, this proviso was **omitted by the Companies (Amendment) Act, 2017 (w.e.f. 07.05.2018)**. Consequently, there is now **no requirement of annual ratification** of the auditor's appointment at each AGM. The shareholder's contention that yearly ratification is compulsory is therefore no longer valid.

Before such appointment, under Section 139(1) the company must obtain the auditor's **written consent** and a **certificate** that the appointment, if made, shall be in accordance with the conditions prescribed, and that the auditor satisfies the criteria under Section 141. The certificate must also confirm that the proposed appointment is within the limits laid down by the Act and that no proceedings against the auditor are pending. Notice of appointment must be filed with the Registrar in **Form ADT-1 within 15 days** of the meeting (Rule 4(2)).

| Aspect | Shareholder's claim | Correct legal position |
|---|---|---|
| Term of subsequent auditor | Fixed 5-year block, so far correct | 5 years (till conclusion of 6th AGM) — Sec 139(1) |
| Board's "till next AGM" proposal | — | Wrong for a subsequent auditor; that is a 1-year first-auditor pattern |
| Annual ratification | Mandatory every AGM | Proviso omitted by Amendment Act, 2017; **not** mandatory |

**Conclusion:** Rhea & Co. must be appointed to hold office from the conclusion of the first AGM till the conclusion of the sixth AGM (a five-year block); the Board's one-year proposal is legally incorrect, and annual ratification is no longer required after the 2017 amendment.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the specific citation of Section 139(1), the "sixth AGM" language, and awareness that the ratification proviso was omitted w.e.f. 07.05.2018. A common deduction is confusing the subsequent-auditor 5-year term with the first-auditor 1-year term, or still stating ratification is mandatory.)*

### Q70. Ch: Company Audit — Retiring Auditor's Eligibility for Reappointment (Sec 139(9) & 139(10)) (Marks: 4) [Application]
**Question:** M/s Falcon & Associates are the retiring auditors of Sunmica Boards Ltd. Advise the company on the status of the retiring auditor's reappointment.

The reappointment of a retiring auditor is governed by **Section 139(9) and Section 139(10)** of the Companies Act, 2013.

### Q71. Ch: Company Audit — Removal of Auditor Before Expiry of Term (Sec 140(1)) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** The Board of Directors of Meridian Alloys Ltd wishes to remove its statutory auditor, M/s Kohli & Co., before the expiry of their term because of a disagreement over an accounting treatment. A director claims that a simple Board resolution followed by an ordinary resolution in a general meeting is sufficient. Explain the correct procedure for removal of an auditor before the expiry of his term under the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:**
The removal of an auditor before the expiry of his term is governed by **Section 140(1)** of the Companies Act, 2013 read with **Rule 7** of the Companies (Audit and Auditors) Rules, 2014.

The section makes it clear that an auditor appointed under Section 139 **may be removed from his office before the expiry of his term only** by a **special resolution of the company**, after **obtaining the previous approval of the Central Government** (power delegated to the Regional Director) in the prescribed manner. The director's claim that a mere Board resolution and an ordinary resolution suffice is therefore incorrect on two counts — the resolution must be a **special** resolution, and prior **Central Government approval** is a condition precedent.

The correct step-by-step procedure under Rule 7 is as follows:

| Step | Action required |
|---|---|
| 1 | The Board passes a resolution proposing the removal and authorising an application. |
| 2 | An application is made to the **Central Government in Form ADT-2** within **30 days** of the Board resolution, along with the prescribed fee. |
| 3 | On receipt of the **approval** of the Central Government, the company must **hold the general meeting within 60 days** of receipt of such approval. |
| 4 | At that general meeting, a **special resolution** is passed to remove the auditor. |

This protects auditor independence and prevents removal merely because the auditor has taken a firm professional stance (as in this case, on an accounting disagreement).

**Conclusion:** Meridian Alloys Ltd cannot remove M/s Kohli & Co. by a Board resolution and an ordinary resolution. *(Full-marks tip: The examiner looks for all three time limits (ADT-2 within 30 days; GM within 60 days of approval), the special resolution + CG approval requirement, and the opportunity of being heard. ### Q72. Ch: Company Audit — Distinguish Removal (Sec 140(1)) vs Resignation (Sec 140(2)/(3)) (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between the "removal of an auditor before expiry of term" and the "resignation of an auditor" under the Companies Act, 2013, bringing out clearly the compliance obligations and the consequence of default in each case.

**Answer:**
Both situations lead to the auditor ceasing to hold office before the natural end of his tenure, but the governing provisions and obligations differ materially.

**Removal** is governed by **Section 140(1)**, and **resignation** by **Section 140(2) and 140(3)** of the Companies Act, 2013 read with Rule 8.

| Basis | Removal before expiry — Sec 140(1) | Resignation — Sec 140(2) & 140(3) |
|---|---|---|
| Initiated by | The **company** (management/members) | The **auditor** himself |
| Approval needed | **Special resolution** + prior **Central Government approval** (Form ADT-2 within 30 days) | No CG approval; auditor's own decision |
| Statement / filing | Company files ADT-2; auditor given opportunity of being heard | Resigning auditor must file a **statement in Form ADT-3** |
| Time limit for filing | GM within 60 days of CG approval | ADT-3 to be filed **within 30 days** from date of resignation |
| Filed with whom | Central Government / Registrar | **Registrar**, and the **company**; and additionally the **Comptroller and Auditor-General (C&AG)** in the case of a Government company |
| Contents | Grounds for removal | Facts and **reasons for the resignation** |
| Consequence of default | Removal is invalid if procedure not followed | Auditor is **punishable with penalty**: not less than ₹50,000 or the auditor's remuneration, whichever is **less**, and a **further ₹500 per day** during which the default continues, subject to a **maximum of ₹5,00,000** (Sec 140(3)) |

### Q78. Ch: Audit Report — Structure of an Unmodified Report (SA 700) (Marks: 6) [Theory]

2. **Addressee:** The report shall be addressed as required by the circumstances of the engagement, usually to the **members/shareholders** of the company.

11. **Place of signature** and **Date of the auditor's report**.

### Q79. Ch: Audit Report — Unmodified vs Modified Opinion (SA 700 vs SA 705) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between an **unmodified opinion** and a **modified opinion**. Also state the three types of modified opinion under SA 705 and the two circumstances that give rise to a modification.

**Answer:**
The concepts are governed by **SA 700 "Forming an Opinion and Reporting on Financial Statements"** and **SA 705 "Modifications to the Opinion in the Independent Auditor's Report."**

An **unmodified opinion** is the opinion expressed by the auditor when he concludes that the financial statements are prepared, in all material respects, **in accordance with the applicable financial reporting framework** (SA 700). It is often called a "clean" opinion.

A **modified opinion** is expressed under **SA 705** when either (a) the auditor concludes that, based on the audit evidence obtained, the financial statements as a whole are **not free from material misstatement**; or (b) the auditor is **unable to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence** to conclude that the financial statements as a whole are free from material misstatement.

**Circumstances giving rise to a modification (two):**
1. There is a **material misstatement** of the financial statements (e.g., inappropriate accounting policy, disagreement over facts, inadequate disclosure); or
2. There is an **inability to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence** (a limitation on scope).

**The three types of modified opinion under SA 705:**

| Nature of matter giving rise to modification | Material but NOT pervasive | Material AND pervasive |
|---|---|---|
| Financial statements are **materially misstated** | **Qualified opinion** | **Adverse opinion** |
| **Inability to obtain** sufficient appropriate audit evidence | **Qualified opinion** | **Disclaimer of opinion** |

Thus, a **Qualified opinion** is given when the effect is material but not pervasive; an **Adverse opinion** when misstatements are both material and pervasive; and a **Disclaimer of opinion** when the auditor cannot obtain evidence and the possible effects could be both material and pervasive.

**Conclusion:** An unmodified opinion signifies a true and fair view, while a modified opinion (qualified, adverse, or disclaimer) is driven by either a material misstatement or an inability to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence, with pervasiveness determining which of the three modifications applies.

**Conclusion:** Under Clause 3(x) of CARO 2020, the auditor of Orion Renewables Ltd must report — for the IPO under Clause 3(x)(a), whether the moneys were applied for the purposes raised (with details of any delay/default); and for the preferential allotment of convertible debentures under Clause 3(x)(b), whether the requirements of Sections 42 and 62 were complied with and whether the funds were used for the intended purpose, disclosing the amount and nature of any non-compliance.

### Q85. Ch: Professional Ethics & Independence — First vs Second Schedule Misconduct (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** "A Chartered Accountant in practice may render himself liable for professional misconduct under the First Schedule as well as under the Second Schedule to the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949, and the consequences differ." Distinguish between professional misconduct falling under the First Schedule and that falling under the Second Schedule, bringing out (a) the broad nature of clauses covered by each, (b) two illustrative clauses under each, and (c) the difference in the disciplinary consequences.

**Answer:**
The Chartered Accountants Act, 1949 codifies professional misconduct through Schedules. Section 21A read with Section 21B distinguishes the two Schedules on the basis of the gravity of the misconduct and the authority empowered to decide the matter.

The **First Schedule** contains clauses relating to comparatively less serious misconduct. Under Section 21A(3), the **Board of Discipline** is empowered to deal with cases falling under the First Schedule. The First Schedule is divided into four Parts — Part I (professional misconduct of members in practice, containing clauses (1) to (12)), Part II (members in service), Part III (members generally) and Part IV (other misconduct in relation to members generally).

The **Second Schedule** contains clauses relating to more serious professional misconduct. Under Section 21B(3), the **Disciplinary Committee** is empowered to deal with cases falling under the Second Schedule (and cases falling under both Schedules). The Second Schedule has three Parts — Part I (professional misconduct of members in practice, clauses (1) to (10)), Part II (members generally) and Part III (other misconduct).

The distinction may be tabulated as follows:

| Basis | First Schedule | Second Schedule |
|---|---|---|
| Nature/gravity | Less serious misconduct | More serious misconduct |
| Deciding authority | Board of Discipline (Sec. 21A) | Disciplinary Committee (Sec. 21B) |
| Illustrative clause 1 | Part I, Cl.(2) — paying/allowing share of fees or profits to a non-member | Part I, Cl.(5) — failing to disclose a material fact in a financial statement known to him |
| Illustrative clause 2 | Part I, Cl.(6) — soliciting clients/professional work by advertisement, circular or personal communication | Part I, Cl.(7) — gross negligence in the conduct of professional duties |
| Penalty on member (Sec. 21A(3)/21B(3)) | Reprimand; OR removal of name from Register up to a maximum of **3 months**; OR fine up to **₹1,00,000** | Reprimand; OR removal of name **permanently or for any duration** he thinks fit; OR fine up to **₹5,00,000** |

**Conclusion:** The First Schedule covers less serious misconduct decided by the Board of Discipline with a milder penalty, whereas the Second Schedule covers grave misconduct decided by the Disciplinary Committee with the power to remove the member's name permanently and impose a heavier fine.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner rewards citing Sections 21A and 21B and the exact monetary/time limits — ₹1 lakh / 3 months versus ₹5 lakh / permanent removal. Students commonly lose marks by naming the wrong deciding authority or by swapping the penalty ceilings.)*

### Q86. Ch: Professional Ethics & Independence — Fees Contingent on Findings (Clause 10, Part I, First Schedule) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** CA. Meera, a practising Chartered Accountant, accepts an engagement to conduct an investigation into the affairs of a partnership firm for the incoming partner. She agrees that her fee will be **10% of the additional profit she is able to unearth** during the investigation. Separately, she accepts an appointment as a receiver in a winding-up matter where her remuneration is fixed by the Court as a percentage of the assets realised. Examine both arrangements with reference to the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949.

**Answer:**
The relevant provision is **Clause (10) of Part I of the First Schedule** to the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949, which provides that a Chartered Accountant in practice is deemed guilty of professional misconduct if he **charges or offers to charge, accepts or offers to accept, in respect of any professional employment, fees which are based on a percentage of profits or which are contingent upon the findings, or results of such employment**.

The rationale is that contingent fees compromise the member's independence and objectivity, since his remuneration then depends on producing a particular result.

However, Clause (10) itself is subject to **exceptions specified in Regulation 192** of the Chartered Accountants Regulations, 1988. Regulation 192 permits fees to be based on a percentage in certain cases, including — in the case of a **receiver or liquidator**, fees based on a percentage of the realisation or disbursement of assets; in the case of an **auditor of a co-operative society**, fees based on a percentage of paid-up capital / working capital / gross/net income; and in the case of certain **valuation** work, fees based on a percentage of the value.

Applying the law:

**(a) Investigation fee at 10% of additional profit unearthed** — This is a fee contingent upon the "findings or results" of the professional employment. An investigation is **not** one of the exceptions carved out in Regulation 192. Therefore CA. Meera is **guilty of professional misconduct** under Clause (10) of Part I of the First Schedule.

**(b) Receiver's remuneration as a percentage of assets realised, fixed by the Court** — A receiver is an **express exception** under Regulation 192, and additionally the remuneration is fixed by the Court. Therefore this arrangement is **permissible** and does not amount to misconduct.

**Conclusion:** The contingent investigation fee renders CA. Meera guilty under Clause (10), Part I, First Schedule, whereas the receiver's percentage remuneration is permitted under the Regulation 192 exception.

### Q87. Ch: Professional Ethics & Independence — Solicitation and Advertisement (Clauses 6 & 7, Part I, First Schedule) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Examine the following independent situations with reference to the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949 and state whether they amount to professional misconduct:
(i) CA. Rohan issues a printed circular to the general public through a local newspaper announcing that his firm has opened a new branch office and inviting "all businessmen desirous of quality audit and tax services" to contact the firm.
(ii) CA. Sneha, on being requested by an existing client, gives a written response to a tender/enquiry for statutory audit floated by a public sector undertaking, quoting her fees.
(iii) CA. Vikas prints his visiting card mentioning his name, firm name, address, telephone number and the designation "Chartered Accountant" along with "Ex-President of a Rotary Club".

### Q88. Ch: Professional Ethics & Independence — Communication with Previous Auditor (Clause 8, Part I) (Marks: 4) [Application]

Deepa by ordinary post and, without waiting for a reply, commences the audit the next day. CA. Deepa later complains that she never received the letter. Comment on the professional propriety of CA. **Answer:**
The relevant provision is **Clause (8) of Part I of the First Schedule** to the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949, under which a Chartered Accountant in practice is deemed guilty of professional misconduct if he **accepts a position as auditor previously held by another Chartered Accountant without first communicating with him in writing**.

The object of the communication is to enable the incoming auditor to ascertain whether there are any circumstances (professional or otherwise) which ought to prevent him from accepting the appointment, and it is a mark of professional courtesy. The Council has clarified that "communicating" means the communication must reach the previous auditor, and therefore mere posting is not enough — the letter should be sent by a mode that provides **positive evidence of delivery**, such as registered post with acknowledgement due, speed post, or by hand against a written acknowledgement.

He therefore failed to ensure that the communication actually reached CA. Deepa before accepting/commencing the assignment.

### Q89. Ch: Professional Ethics & Independence — Ceiling on Number of Company Audits (Sec. 141(3)(g)) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** CA. Nikhil, a practising Chartered Accountant, is a partner in M/s Nikhil & Associates along with two other partners. Determine the maximum number of company audits the firm can accept and comment on the following proposed appointments taken together, indicating which fall within and which fall outside the ceiling:

| Category of company | Number proposed |
|---|---|
| Public companies with paid-up capital ≥ ₹100 crore | 8 |
| Public companies with paid-up capital < ₹100 crore | 40 |
| Private companies with paid-up capital ≥ ₹100 crore | 12 |
| Small companies | 15 |
| One person companies | 10 |
| Dormant companies | 6 |

**Answer:**
The ceiling on the number of company audits is governed by **Section 141(3)(g) of the Companies Act, 2013** read with **Clause (1) of Part I of the Second Schedule** of the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949 (holding more audits than permitted amounts to misconduct). Section 141(3)(g) provides that a person shall not be eligible for appointment as auditor if he is, at the date of appointment, holding appointment as auditor of **more than twenty companies**. In the case of a firm, the ceiling of 20 applies **per partner** who is not otherwise disqualified.

By virtue of the proviso/exclusions, the following companies are **excluded** from the count of 20:
- one person companies;
- dormant companies;
- small companies; and
- private companies having paid-up share capital of **less than ₹100 crore**.

**WN-1 — Ceiling for the firm:** The firm has 3 partners; ceiling = 20 × 3 = **60 company audits**.

**WN-2 — Which proposed audits count towards the ceiling of 20 per partner:**

| Category | Proposed | Counts? | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Public co. paid-up ≥ ₹100 cr | 8 | Yes (8) | Public company, always counted |
| Public co. paid-up < ₹100 cr | 40 | Yes (40) | Public company; ₹100 cr limit exclusion is only for **private** companies |
| Private co. paid-up ≥ ₹100 cr | 12 | Yes (12) | Private co. **≥ ₹100 cr** is counted (only < ₹100 cr excluded) |
| Small companies | 15 | No | Excluded |
| One person companies | 10 | No | Excluded |
| Dormant companies | 6 | No | Excluded |
| **Total counted** | | **60** | |

**Analysis:** The audits that count towards the ceiling total **60**, exactly equal to the firm's ceiling of 60 (20 × 3 partners). Hence, if the 60 counted audits are distributed as 20 per partner, all the proposed appointments — including the 31 excluded ones (15 + 10 + 6) — can be accepted.

**Conclusion:** The firm can accept **all the proposed audits**, since only 60 audits count towards the ceiling and the three-partner firm's limit is 60; the small companies, OPCs and dormant companies do not consume the ceiling.

### Q90. Ch: Professional Ethics & Independence — Disciplinary Mechanism & Powers (Sec. 21, 21A, 21B, 21C) (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Describe the disciplinary mechanism under the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949 in relation to (a) the role of the Director (Discipline), (b) the constitution and powers of the Board of Discipline, and (c) the constitution and powers of the Disciplinary Committee. Also state the appellate authority against their orders.

**Answer:**
The disciplinary mechanism is contained in **Sections 21, 21A, 21B, 21C and 22A** of the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949.

**(a) Director (Discipline) — Section 21:** On receipt of any information or complaint under Section 21, the **Director (Discipline)** arrives at a *prima facie* opinion on the occurrence of the alleged misconduct. Where he is of the opinion that the member is guilty of any professional or other misconduct mentioned in the **First Schedule**, he places the matter before the **Board of Discipline**; where the misconduct is mentioned in the **Second Schedule or in both Schedules**, he places it before the **Disciplinary Committee**.

**(b) Board of Discipline — Section 21A:** The Board of Discipline consists of a **presiding officer** (a person with experience in law and having knowledge of disciplinary matters and the profession, to be a person of eminence), **two members** — one being a member of the Council nominated by it, and the other a person nominated by the Central Government from amongst persons of eminence having experience in the field of law, economics, business, finance or accountancy. If the Board finds a member guilty of misconduct under the **First Schedule**, it may afford an opportunity of being heard and then (i) **reprimand** the member; (ii) **remove his name from the Register up to a maximum of 3 months**; or (iii) **impose a fine up to ₹1,00,000**.

**(c) Disciplinary Committee — Section 21B:** The Disciplinary Committee consists of the **President or Vice-President of the ICAI as its Presiding Officer**, **two members** to be elected from amongst the members of the Council, and **two members** nominated by the Central Government from amongst persons of eminence. Where the Committee finds a member guilty of misconduct under the **Second Schedule or both Schedules**, it may, after an opportunity of being heard, (i) **reprimand** the member; (ii) **remove his name permanently or for such period as it thinks fit**; or (iii) **impose a fine up to ₹5,00,000**.

**Appellate authority — Section 22A:** Any member aggrieved by an order of the Board of Discipline or the Disciplinary Committee may prefer an **appeal to the Appellate Authority** constituted under Section 22A within the prescribed time.

**Conclusion:** The Director (Discipline) screens complaints; First Schedule matters go to the Board of Discipline and Second Schedule/both-Schedule matters to the Disciplinary Committee, with appeals lying to the Appellate Authority.

*(Full-marks tip: the composition (numbers and who nominates whom) and the two sets of penalties are the marks-carrying content. Confusing the Board's ₹1 lakh/3-month powers with the Committee's ₹5 lakh/permanent powers is the most common deduction.)*

### Q92. Ch: Audit of Different Entities — Special Considerations in Audit of a Hospital (Marks: 6) [Theory]

### Q94. Ch: Audit of Different Entities — Special Considerations in Audit of a Co-operative Society (Marks: 8) [Theory]
**Question:** As the auditor of a Co-operative Society, discuss the special features of a co-operative society audit, covering (a) the qualification and appointment of the auditor, (b) the special matters he must examine (including the books and the Registrar's powers), and (c) the special report on infringements and the audit classification of the society.

**Answer:**
The audit of a co-operative society is governed by the **Co-operative Societies Act, 1912** (or the relevant State Co-operative Societies Act) and the Rules made thereunder. Its special features are as follows:

**(a) Qualification and appointment of auditor — Sections 17.** Under **Section 17** of the Co-operative Societies Act, 1912, the Registrar of Co-operative Societies shall audit or cause to be audited the accounts of every registered society. The audit is thus a **statutory audit conducted under the authority of the Registrar**, and the auditor is appointed by the Registrar (or from a panel maintained by the Registrar/Government), unlike a company where the members appoint the auditor. A person conducting the audit must possess the qualification prescribed under the relevant State Act (a Chartered Accountant, or a person holding a Government Diploma in Co-operative Accounts / Co-operation and Accountancy where the State so permits).

**(b) Special matters to be examined.**
- **Examination of overdue debts.** The auditor should classify overdue debts (those outstanding for periods, e.g., 6 months to 5 years and more than 5 years) and report them, since overdue debts affect the working capital position and the recoverability of dues.
- **Overdue interest.** Overdue interest should be **excluded from interest outstanding and accrued due** while calculating profit; the auditor verifies that a reserve for overdue interest has been created.
- **Certification of Bad Debts (write-off).** Bad debts and irrecoverable losses should be written off **only with the certification of the auditor and with the sanction of the managing committee** as required by the Act/Rules; the auditor should verify such write-offs.
- **Valuation of assets and liabilities.** Though no specific accounting standards apply as rigidly as to companies, the auditor should ensure that assets and liabilities are valued reasonably and that adequate provision for depreciation is made.
- **Adherence to co-operative principles.** The auditor should examine whether the society's working conforms to co-operative principles and whether the objects for which it was established are being achieved — e.g., how far the society has been able to serve its members' interest.
- **Observations of the provisions of the Act and Rules and Bye-laws.** He should verify that the transactions are in conformity with the Act, Rules and the registered bye-laws — for example, restrictions on **contributions to charitable purposes** (Section 34), on **investment of funds** (Section 32), on **loans to non-members** (Section 29), and the mandatory transfer of **at least 25% of profits to the Reserve Fund** (Section 33) before distribution of dividend.
- **Restriction on shareholding (Section 5)** and the maximum dividend/bonus limits should be checked.

**(c) Special report and audit classification.**
- **Special report to the Registrar.** During the course of audit, if the auditor notices irregularities such as personal profiteering by members of the managing committee, detrimental or dishonest transactions, mismanagement, or serious infringements of the Act/Rules/bye-laws, he should report these matters specially to the Registrar.
- **Audit classification of the society.** After completing the audit, the auditor has to **award a class ("A", "B", "C" or "D")** to the society on the basis of specified criteria reflecting its overall performance/soundness, and the auditor should be careful and just in awarding the class.
- **Audit report format.** The audit report is submitted in the form prescribed by the Registrar, covering the financial position and the special matters noted above.

**Conclusion:** A co-operative society audit is a statutory audit ordered by the Registrar, requiring the auditor to specially examine overdue debts/interest, adherence to the Act's restrictions (Reserve Fund, investment of funds, loans), report infringements to the Registrar, and award an audit classification to the society.

*(Full-marks tip: the marks lie in the co-operative-specific items — overdue debts/interest, 25% Reserve Fund transfer under Sec. 33, restrictions under Secs. 29/32/34, special report to Registrar, and the "A–D" audit classification. Cite the section numbers to lift the answer to full marks.)*

### Q95. Ch: Audit of Different Entities — Special Considerations in Audit of a Hotel (Marks: 6) [Theory]

- Hotels carry large stocks of **food and beverages, crockery, cutlery, linen and other consumables** which are highly susceptible to pilferage and wastage. The auditor should examine the internal control over receipt, storage, issue and consumption of these items and, where possible, attend or review the **physical stock-taking**.
- Verify the **valuation** of stocks — these should be valued at cost or net realisable value, whichever is lower, with proper adjustment for slow-moving/obsolete items. The consumption should be reasonable in relation to occupancy/covers served.

**(c) Fixed assets and general environment.**
- Hotels have **heavy investment in land, building, furniture, air-conditioning plant, kitchen equipment and interior décor**. The auditor should verify title deeds, the fixed assets register, and additions with authorisation; ensure adequate **depreciation** is provided given the wear and tear.
- Examine expenditure on **renovation/refurbishment** to distinguish capital from revenue expenditure.
- Since operations often run round the clock at many points of sale, the auditor should evaluate the overall control environment — segregation of duties between recording and custody, the **night audit** function, and reconciliation of the various point-of-sale systems with the general ledger.

**Conclusion:** The auditor of a hotel must place special reliance on internal control over room occupancy and other-service billing, on the physical control and valuation of highly pilferable inventories (food, beverages, linen), and on the verification and adequate depreciation of the large fixed-asset base.

*(Full-marks tip: the occupancy-register-to-room-sales reconciliation and the "night audit" concept are the marks that distinguish a hotel answer from a generic one. Mention the pilferage risk on linen/crockery and the tariff-authorisation point.)*

### Q96. Ch: Audit of Different Entities — Audit of a Local Body / Municipality (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Discuss the objectives of audit of a Local Self-Government body (such as a Municipality) and state the main aspects the auditor should examine while auditing its expenditure.

**Answer:**
A local body (Municipality, Municipal Corporation, Panchayat) is a self-governing institution constituted under the relevant State law and financed largely by taxes, grants and borrowings. Its audit is conducted to ensure accountability of public funds.

**Objectives of audit of a local body.** The audit seeks to ensure that —
- the **expenditure incurred conforms to the relevant provisions** of the law and is in accordance with the financial rules and regulations framed by the competent authority;
- **all types of sanctions**, either special or general, accorded by the competent authority, are attached to the expenditure;
- there is a **provision of funds** for the expenditure, duly authorised by a competent authority;
- the expenditure is incurred by an **authority competent** to sanction it (propriety of expenditure);
- there is adequate control over the **collection and proper accounting of revenues** (taxes, rates, fees, rents and other receipts); and
- the different schemes, programmes and projects are running **economically** and the purpose for which they were taken up is being achieved (performance/value-for-money aspect).

**Main aspects of expenditure to be examined.** The auditor should verify that —
- every payment is supported by a **valid voucher** and sanctioned by the competent authority;
- the expenditure is charged to the **correct head of account** and does not exceed the budget grant, and that reappropriation between heads has proper sanction;
- purchases and works are made through the prescribed **tender/quotation procedure** and payments to contractors are as per the measured work and agreed rates;
- **stores and stocks** are properly recorded and no excess/obsolete stores are held; and
- the principles of **propriety** are observed — i.e., the expenditure is such as a person of ordinary prudence would incur, and public money is not spent for the benefit of a particular person or section.

**Conclusion:** The audit of a local body aims to ensure legality, sanction, budgetary provision, competence and propriety of expenditure, together with proper accounting of revenues and economical running of schemes.

*(Full-marks tip: link the answer to the four canons — legality, sanction, provision of funds, and propriety. Adding the value-for-money/economy objective lifts a routine answer to full marks.)*

### Q97. Ch: Professional Ethics & Independence — Fundamental Principles & Threats to Independence (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** The Code of Ethics requires a Chartered Accountant to comply with the fundamental principles and to remain independent. (a) Enumerate the five fundamental principles of professional ethics. (b) Explain the five categories of "threats" to compliance with the fundamental principles, giving one example of each.

**Answer:**
The ICAI Code of Ethics is built on a conceptual framework requiring members to comply with the fundamental principles and, for assurance engagements, to be independent.

**(a) Five fundamental principles.** A professional accountant is required to comply with the following fundamental principles:

| Principle | Requirement |
|---|---|
| **Integrity** | To be straightforward and honest in all professional and business relationships. |
| **Objectivity** | Not to allow bias, conflict of interest or undue influence of others to override professional or business judgements. |
| **Professional competence and due care** | To maintain professional knowledge and skill at the level required to render competent service, and to act diligently in accordance with applicable technical and professional standards. |
| **Confidentiality** | To respect the confidentiality of information acquired as a result of professional and business relationships and not to disclose or use it without proper and specific authority (unless there is a legal or professional right/duty to disclose). |
| **Professional behaviour** | To comply with relevant laws and regulations and avoid any conduct that discredits the profession. |

**(b) Five categories of threats.** Compliance with the fundamental principles may be threatened by the following, illustrated below:

| Threat | Nature | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Self-interest threat** | Threat that a financial or other interest will inappropriately influence judgement/behaviour | Auditor having a direct financial interest / a fee dependence on the client; undue concern about losing a client |
| **Self-review threat** | Threat that the member will not appropriately evaluate the results of a previous judgement made by himself | Auditing financial statements prepared/accounting done by the same firm |
| **Advocacy threat** | Threat that a member will promote a client's position to the point that objectivity is compromised | Promoting shares of an audit client, or acting as an advocate on the client's behalf in litigation |
| **Familiarity threat** | Threat that, due to a long or close relationship, a member becomes too sympathetic to the client's interests | A close relative of the client being in a key position, or a long association of senior personnel with the client |
| **Intimidation threat** | Threat that a member is deterred from acting objectively by actual or perceived pressures/threats | Threat of replacement over a disagreement, or pressure to reduce work to lower fees |

Where such threats are not at an acceptable level, the member must apply **safeguards** to eliminate them or reduce them to an acceptable level; if that is not possible, he should decline or discontinue the engagement.

**Conclusion:** The five fundamental principles — integrity, objectivity, professional competence and due care, confidentiality and professional behaviour — must be protected against the self-interest, self-review, advocacy, familiarity and intimidation threats through appropriate safeguards.

*(Full-marks tip: give the correct example for each threat — students routinely confuse self-review with self-interest. Naming "safeguards" as the response to threats secures the concluding mark.)*

### Q98. Ch: Professional Ethics & Independence — Disclosure of Client Information (Clause 1, Part I, Second Schedule) (Marks: 4) [Application]
**Question:** CA. Prerna, during the audit of Orbit Ltd., comes to know of certain price-sensitive information. At a social gathering she discloses this information to a friend who is a stock broker. Separately, in response to a summons issued by the Income-tax authorities under law, she furnishes certain information about another client, Comet Ltd. Examine both actions under the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949.

**Answer:**
The relevant provision is **Clause (1) of Part I of the Second Schedule** to the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949, under which a Chartered Accountant in practice is deemed guilty of professional misconduct if he **discloses information acquired in the course of his professional engagement to any person other than his client, without the consent of his client or otherwise than as required by any law for the time being in force**. This gives statutory force to the fundamental principle of **confidentiality**.

Two situations arise:

**(a) Disclosure of price-sensitive information to a stock-broker friend.** This is a disclosure of information acquired during the professional engagement to a person **other than the client, without the client's consent and not required by any law**. It is, in fact, an aggravated breach (price-sensitive information disclosed socially). CA. Prerna is **guilty of professional misconduct** under Clause (1), Part I, Second Schedule.

**(b) Furnishing information in response to a summons from Income-tax authorities.** Clause (1) itself carves out an exception — disclosure **"as required by any law for the time being in force"**. Since CA. Prerna furnished the information in compliance with a valid summons issued under the Income-tax law, the disclosure is protected by law and she is **not guilty** of misconduct.

**Conclusion:** The social disclosure of client information renders CA. Prerna guilty under Clause (1), Part I of the Second Schedule, whereas furnishing information under a legal summons is expressly permitted and does not amount to misconduct.

*(Full-marks tip: the whole answer turns on the "otherwise than as required by law" exception — clearly separate the voluntary disclosure (guilty) from the legally-compelled one (not guilty). Correctly placing this under the Second Schedule (not the First) matters.)*

### Q99. Ch: Professional Ethics & Independence — Gross Negligence vs Lack of Due Diligence (Clause 7, Part I, Second Schedule) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** CA. Rishi, the statutory auditor of Meridian Ltd., certifies the financial statements without physically verifying the existence of the closing inventory and without obtaining any confirmation of trade receivables, and issues a clean report. It later transpires that a substantial part of both the inventory and the receivables was fictitious, resulting in an overstatement of profit. A complaint of professional misconduct is filed against CA. Rishi. Examine his liability under the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949.

**Answer:**
The relevant provisions are found in **Part I of the Second Schedule** to the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949. **Clause (7)** provides that a member in practice is guilty of misconduct if he **does not exercise due diligence, or is grossly negligent in the conduct of his professional duties**. **Clause (8)** provides that he is guilty if he **fails to obtain sufficient information which is necessary for the expression of an opinion, or its exceptions are sufficiently material to negate the expression of an opinion**. **Clause (9)** covers failure to invite attention to a material departure from generally accepted audit procedure.

Applying these to the facts: the physical verification of inventory and the confirmation/verification of trade receivables are basic, well-established audit procedures. By certifying the financial statements **without verifying the existence of inventory and without any confirmation of receivables**, CA. Rishi failed to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence regarding the **existence** assertion for two material items, and did not follow generally accepted audit procedures. His omission of such fundamental procedures, resulting in a clean report on financial statements containing fictitious assets and overstated profit, constitutes **gross negligence and a failure to exercise due diligence** and a **failure to obtain sufficient information** necessary for expressing his opinion.

**Conclusion:** CA. Rishi is **guilty of professional misconduct** under Clause (7) (gross negligence / failure to exercise due diligence) and Clause (8) (failure to obtain sufficient information) of Part I of the Second Schedule to the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949.

*(Full-marks tip: link the failure to the "existence" assertion and to specific omitted procedures (physical stock verification, receivables confirmation). Citing both Clause (7) and Clause (8) — not just "negligence" — earns full marks.)*

### Q100. Ch: Audit of Different Entities — Cross-Topic: Audit of an NGO with an Ethics Dimension (Marks: 8) [Application]
**Question:** M/s Karan & Co., Chartered Accountants, is offered the statutory audit of "Prayaas Foundation", a Non-Governmental Organisation (NGO) registered as a society and receiving foreign contributions. The proprietor, CA. Karan, is a member of the governing council of a similar NGO and has been offered an audit fee of "15% of the foreign grants that the Foundation ultimately succeeds in getting released". You are required to:
(a) Advise CA. Karan on the ethical acceptability of the proposed fee arrangement; and
(b) State the special audit steps for the receipts and application of funds you would undertake as the auditor of such an NGO.

**Answer:**
This problem integrates the ethics of contingent fees with the special considerations in the audit of an NGO.

**(a) Ethical acceptability of the proposed fee.**
The proposed fee is **15% of the foreign grants ultimately released** to the Foundation — i.e., a fee **contingent upon the results/findings** of the professional engagement. **Clause (10) of Part I of the First Schedule** to the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949 prohibits a Chartered Accountant in practice from charging or accepting fees which are **based on a percentage of profits or which are contingent upon the findings or results** of the employment, save in the cases specified in **Regulation 192** of the Chartered Accountants Regulations, 1988. The audit of an NGO is **not** among the Regulation 192 exceptions. Therefore, a fee linked to the amount of grants released would render CA. Karan **guilty of professional misconduct** under Clause (10), Part I, First Schedule, and would also impair his **objectivity/independence** (a self-interest threat under the Code of Ethics). CA. Karan should decline the contingent fee and instead accept a **fixed/agreed audit fee**. (His membership of the governing council of a *similar* NGO does not by itself bar the appointment, but he must guard against any conflict of interest and, if it impairs independence, decline.)

**(b) Special audit steps for an NGO.**

*Governing documents and control:* Examine the **Memorandum/rules and regulations, the certificate of registration** (as a society/trust/Section 8 company) and the **registration/approval under the FCRA** (Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Act) for receipt of foreign contribution; study the minutes of the Governing Body for resolutions on grants, budgets and expenditure.

*Receipts side:*
- **Contributions and grants.** Verify grants and donations with the counterfoils of receipts, correspondence with donors, and the grant sanction letters; ascertain whether any grant is **tied/restricted to a specific project** and confirm it is credited to the correct fund.
- **Foreign contributions.** Verify that foreign contributions are received **only in the designated FCRA bank account** and are separately accounted for, and that the utilisation and reporting requirements of the FCRA are complied with.
- **Membership fees, subscriptions and other income** are checked with the members' register and receipts, and interest/dividend on investments with the investment schedule.

*Application/payments side:*
- **Project expenditure.** Vouch expenditure incurred on projects/programmes with supporting vouchers and see that it is within the **sanctioned budget** and applied for the purpose for which the grant was given; verify utilisation certificates submitted to donors.
- **Establishment and administrative expenses** are vouched with supporting evidence and checked for reasonableness; ensure that restricted funds are not diverted to general administration.
- **Capital expenditure/assets** acquired out of grants are verified with the asset register and the sanction of the Governing Body, and title is confirmed in the name of the NGO.

*Funds and reporting:* Ensure that **restricted, unrestricted and corpus funds** are separately maintained and that the closing balances of each are correctly carried forward; verify compliance with income-tax exemption conditions and FCRA reporting.

**Conclusion:** CA. Karan must decline the contingent fee (guilty under Clause (10), Part I, First Schedule if accepted) and charge a fixed fee; as auditor he must specially verify FCRA-designated receipt and separate accounting of foreign contributions, the purpose-wise application of restricted grants against sanctioned budgets, and proper maintenance of restricted/corpus funds.

*(Full-marks tip: this cross-topic question needs BOTH halves — the Clause (10)/Regulation 192 conclusion on the contingent fee AND the NGO-specific FCRA and restricted-fund audit steps. Missing the FCRA "designated account" point or treating the fee as acceptable loses substantial marks.)*
