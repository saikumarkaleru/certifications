### Q1. Ch: Nature, Objective & Scope — Inherent Limitations vs Expectation Gap (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** The management of Zephyr Traders Ltd., after receiving an unmodified audit opinion, was aggrieved when a large embezzlement by a cashier surfaced three months later. It contended that "an audit is a guarantee that the financial statements are free from every misstatement." Distinguish between the *inherent limitations of an audit* and the *audit expectation gap*, and explain why the management's contention is not tenable.

**Answer:**
As per **SA 200 "Overall Objectives of the Independent Auditor and the Conduct of an Audit in Accordance with Standards on Auditing"**, the auditor is not, and cannot be held, responsible for the prevention of fraud or error; an audit provides only **reasonable assurance**, not absolute assurance, because of certain **inherent limitations** that are built into the very nature of an audit.

These inherent limitations arise from the following factors:
1. **The nature of financial reporting** — the preparation of financial statements involves judgement by management and the making of accounting estimates (e.g., provisions, fair values) that are inherently subjective.
2. **The nature of audit procedures** — the auditor works on the basis of evidence that is **persuasive rather than conclusive**, and management or third parties may not provide, intentionally or unintentionally, complete information. Fraud may involve sophisticated and carefully organised schemes such as collusion, forgery, or deliberate misrepresentation designed to conceal it.
3. **Timeliness and cost-benefit** — an audit has to be completed within a reasonable time and at a reasonable cost; hence the auditor tests **on a sample basis** (SA 530) and cannot examine every transaction.
4. **Other limitations** — areas such as related-party relationships, non-compliance with laws, and the possibility of management override of controls carry a higher, unavoidable risk.

The **audit expectation gap**, on the other hand, is the difference between what **users of financial statements believe** the auditor's responsibilities are, and what the auditor's responsibilities **actually are** under the law and the Standards on Auditing. It is a gap in *perception*, not a gap in the *audit process*.

| Basis | Inherent Limitations of Audit | Audit Expectation Gap |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | An intrinsic feature of the audit process itself | A difference in perception among users |
| Cause | Nature of reporting, evidence, sampling, time & cost | Public/user misunderstanding of the auditor's role |
| Consequence | Auditor gives reasonable, not absolute, assurance | Public expects a "guarantee", which auditor never offers |
| Remedy | Cannot be eliminated; only reduced by good procedures | Reduced through education, clear reporting & communication |

The management's contention is **not tenable** because an audit conducted under the SAs is designed to obtain reasonable assurance about whether the financial statements as a whole are free from material misstatement; it is neither designed nor represented to detect **every** misstatement, particularly one concealed through collusion. The very belief that an audit is a "guarantee" is an illustration of the expectation gap.

**Conclusion:** The subsequent discovery of the embezzlement does not, by itself, imply that the audit was deficient; the auditor's assurance is reasonable, tempered by inherent limitations, and the management's expectation of a guarantee reflects the expectation gap.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner rewards a clear head-wise distinction table plus the SA 200 phrase "reasonable assurance, not absolute". Students lose marks by only listing inherent limitations and never defining the expectation gap, which is the pivot of the question.)*

### Q2. Ch: Nature, Objective & Scope — Reasonable Assurance & Professional Scepticism (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** "Reasonable assurance is a high level of assurance, but it is not absolute assurance." Explain how the concept of *reasonable assurance* connects with the auditor's obligation to plan and perform the audit with *professional scepticism* under SA 200.

**Answer:**
As defined in **SA 200**, **reasonable assurance** is a **high** but **not absolute** level of assurance. It is obtained when the auditor has gathered **sufficient appropriate audit evidence** to reduce audit risk (the risk that the auditor expresses an inappropriate opinion when the financial statements are materially misstated) to an **acceptably low level**.

To attain this high level of assurance, SA 200 requires the auditor to:
1. **Comply with all SAs relevant to the audit** and comply with relevant ethical requirements, including independence.
2. **Plan and perform the audit with professional scepticism**, recognising that circumstances may exist that cause the financial statements to be materially misstated.
3. **Exercise professional judgement** throughout the audit.

**Professional scepticism** means an attitude that includes a **questioning mind**, being **alert to conditions** that may indicate possible misstatement due to fraud or error, and a **critical assessment of audit evidence**. It requires the auditor **not** to accept management's representations and records at face value.

The connection is direct: because assurance can only ever be *reasonable* (owing to inherent limitations), the auditor must **not assume management is honest or dishonest**, but must remain alert to contradictory evidence, to the reliability of documents, and to conditions indicating fraud. Scepticism is thus the **behavioural safeguard** that enables the auditor to actually achieve the "high level" of assurance the framework demands.

**Conclusion:** Reasonable assurance is the *level* of assurance targeted; professional scepticism is the *attitude* through which that level is honestly attained. Neither dispenses with the other.

*(Full-marks tip: link the two concepts explicitly — many students define both separately but never state that scepticism is the means to reasonable assurance. Include "questioning mind" and "critical assessment of evidence" verbatim.)*

### Q3. Ch: Nature, Objective & Scope — Premise of an Audit / Management Responsibilities (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Every audit is conducted on the basis of a "premise" relating to the responsibilities of management and, where appropriate, those charged with governance. State the elements of this premise as contemplated by SA 200, and its significance for the auditor.

**Answer:**
**SA 200** defines the **"premise, relating to the responsibilities of management and, where appropriate, those charged with governance, on which an audit is conducted."** An audit under the SAs is conducted on the premise that management and, where appropriate, those charged with governance have **acknowledged and understand** that they have the following responsibilities:

1. **Preparation of the financial statements** in accordance with the applicable financial reporting framework, including, where relevant, their fair presentation.
2. **Internal control** — for such internal control as management determines is necessary to enable the preparation of financial statements that are free from material misstatement, whether due to fraud or error.
3. **Provision of access and information** to the auditor, namely:
   (a) **Access** to all information (records, documentation and other matters) relevant to the preparation of the financial statements;
   (b) **Additional information** that the auditor may request from management for the purpose of the audit; and
   (c) **Unrestricted access** to persons within the entity from whom the auditor determines it necessary to obtain audit evidence.

**Significance:** This premise is the **foundation** on which the auditor's own responsibilities rest. If management does not accept these responsibilities, or refuses to acknowledge them (for example, refuses unrestricted access), it is a **precondition for an audit** that is absent, and under **SA 210** the auditor should **not accept** the engagement (unless required by law to do so).

**Conclusion:** The premise fixes the boundary between management's responsibility (to prepare the statements and maintain controls) and the auditor's responsibility (to form an opinion on them); it is the bedrock of the entire engagement.

*(Full-marks tip: reproduce all three limbs, especially the three-part "access & information" clause. Linking the premise to SA 210 preconditions earns the application mark.)*

### Q4. Ch: Nature, Objective & Scope — Ethical Principles Governing an Audit (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** SA 200 states that the auditor shall comply with relevant ethical requirements. Enumerate the **fundamental principles** of professional ethics on which those requirements are founded, and briefly explain each with an example of a threat that may compromise it.

**Answer:**
**SA 200** requires the auditor to comply with **relevant ethical requirements**, including those pertaining to independence, relating to financial statement audit engagements. The **Code of Ethics** issued by ICAI, on which these requirements rest, is founded on **five fundamental principles**:

1. **Integrity** — to be straightforward and honest in all professional and business relationships. *Threat:* being associated with a report containing materially false information.
2. **Objectivity** — not to allow bias, conflict of interest, or undue influence of others to override professional judgement. *Threat:* auditing an entity in which the auditor's spouse holds a substantial interest.
3. **Professional Competence and Due Care** — to maintain professional knowledge and skill at the level required, and to act diligently in accordance with applicable technical and professional standards. *Threat:* accepting an engagement in a specialised industry without the requisite expertise.
4. **Confidentiality** — to respect the confidentiality of information acquired and not to disclose it to third parties without proper authority, nor use it for personal advantage. *Threat:* using client price data to trade in the client's shares.
5. **Professional Behaviour** — to comply with relevant laws and regulations and to avoid any conduct that discredits the profession. *Threat:* advertising or soliciting clients in a manner prohibited by the Code.

Compliance with these principles enables the auditor to act in the **public interest**, which is the hallmark of a profession.

**Conclusion:** Adherence to these five fundamental principles, and independence in particular, is a statutory prerequisite under SA 200 for conducting an audit that carries credibility.

*(Full-marks tip: name all five principles precisely and pair each with a one-line threat; the examiner deducts marks where "objectivity" and "independence" are confused, or where fewer than five principles are listed.)*

### Q5. Ch: Nature, Objective & Scope — Scope Limitation & Consequential Action (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** During the audit of Marigold Foods Ltd., the auditor concludes that he cannot achieve the overall objective of obtaining reasonable assurance because management refuses to allow confirmation of a material balance of trade receivables (₹2.10 crore) and no alternative procedure is available. Advise the auditor of the course of action under SA 200, and the implication for his opinion.

**Answer:**
**SA 200** provides that the **overall objectives of the auditor** are (a) to obtain **reasonable assurance** about whether the financial statements as a whole are free from material misstatement, and (b) to **report** on the financial statements and communicate as required by the SAs, in accordance with the auditor's findings.

Where the auditor is **unable to obtain reasonable assurance**, SA 200 requires that the auditor's objective is nonetheless met if he **modifies the opinion** in the report or **disclaims** the opinion, or, where required by law or regulation, **withdraws** from the engagement.

**Application to facts:**
- The trade receivables balance of **₹2.10 crore is material**.
- Management's refusal to permit confirmation, coupled with the **absence of any alternative audit procedure**, results in an **inability to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence** — i.e., a **limitation on scope imposed by management**.
- The auditor must first request management to remove the limitation. If management refuses, the auditor should **communicate the matter to those charged with governance** and determine whether alternative procedures are possible.
- Since no alternative procedure exists and the item is material, the auditor must consider whether the possible effects are **material** or **both material and pervasive**:
  - If material but **not pervasive** → **Qualified opinion** ("except for").
  - If both material **and pervasive** → **Disclaimer of opinion** (and, per SA 705, the auditor should consider withdrawing from the audit where practicable).

Given that a single receivables balance is involved (material but ordinarily not pervasive), a **qualified opinion** would be appropriate; had the limitation affected many balances so as to be fundamental to the statements as a whole, a **disclaimer** would follow.

**Conclusion:** The auditor should not issue an unmodified opinion; he must modify the opinion (qualified if material, disclaimer if material and pervasive), thereby still discharging his SA 200 reporting objective.

*(Full-marks tip: the marks turn on the material-vs-pervasive test driving the choice between qualified and disclaimer; also state the intermediate step of escalating to those charged with governance before concluding.)*

### Q6. Ch: Audit Strategy & Planning — Preconditions for an Audit (SA 210) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** CA Nandini is approached by Ironwood Rubber Ltd. to be its statutory auditor. The company informs her that it prepares its financial statements on a **cash basis** (a basis it has "always followed") and that the management will decide, after seeing her draft observations, whether to grant her access to certain subsidiary ledgers. Explain the **preconditions for an audit** under SA 210, and advise CA Nandini whether she should accept the engagement.

**Answer:**
**SA 210 "Agreeing the Terms of Audit Engagements"** requires the auditor, before accepting an audit engagement, to establish whether the **preconditions for an audit are present**. In order to establish this, the auditor shall:

1. **Determine whether the financial reporting framework** to be applied in the preparation of the financial statements is **acceptable**; and
2. **Obtain the agreement of management** that it acknowledges and understands its responsibility:
   (a) for the preparation of the financial statements in accordance with the applicable framework;
   (b) for such internal control as is necessary to enable the preparation of financial statements free from material misstatement; and
   (c) to provide the auditor with **access** to all relevant information, **additional information** requested, and **unrestricted access** to persons within the entity.

**Application to facts:**
- **Acceptability of the framework** — A cash basis may be an acceptable framework for certain entities, but for a **company**, the financial statements must be prepared under the **accrual basis** as mandated by **Section 128 of the Companies Act, 2013** (books to be kept on accrual basis and double entry), read with Schedule III and the applicable Accounting Standards. Therefore the cash-basis framework proposed is **not acceptable** for Ironwood Rubber Ltd.
- **Unrestricted access** — The management's stance that access to subsidiary ledgers is **conditional** on seeing her draft observations is a **limitation on scope** imposed before the audit even begins. This defeats the third limb of the premise; the auditor must have **unrestricted access**.

Under SA 210, **if the preconditions are not present**, the auditor shall **discuss the matter with management**; and unless required by law to accept, the auditor **shall not accept** the engagement if (a) the framework is unacceptable, or (b) management does not agree to the premise.

**Conclusion:** Both preconditions are absent — the framework is unacceptable and access is restricted. CA Nandini should **decline** the engagement unless the company agrees to prepare accrual-basis statements and grant unrestricted access.

*(Full-marks tip: cite Section 128 to prove the cash-basis framework is unacceptable for a company; many candidates only object to the access limitation and miss the framework point, forfeiting half the marks.)*

### Q7. Ch: Audit Strategy & Planning — Contents of the Engagement Letter (SA 210) (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** State the principal contents that an **audit engagement letter** should ordinarily include under SA 210. Also state the circumstances in which the auditor of a **recurring audit** may decide **not** to send a fresh engagement letter each year, and the factors that may make it appropriate to send one.

**Answer:**
**SA 210** requires the agreed terms of the audit engagement to be recorded in an **audit engagement letter** or other suitable form of written agreement, which shall include:
1. The **objective and scope** of the audit of the financial statements;
2. The **responsibilities of the auditor**;
3. The **responsibilities of management**;
4. Identification of the **applicable financial reporting framework** for the preparation of the financial statements; and
5. Reference to the **expected form and content** of any reports to be issued by the auditor and a statement that there may be circumstances in which a report may differ from its expected form and content.

In addition, the letter may include matters such as: elaboration of the scope, the fact that owing to inherent limitations an unavoidable risk that some material misstatements may not be detected exists, arrangements regarding the **planning and performance** of the audit, the expectation of **written representations**, agreement of management to provide **draft financial statements** in time, the basis of **fees**, and arrangements concerning the **involvement of other auditors, experts, internal auditors and predecessor auditor**.

**Recurring audits:** On **recurring audits**, the auditor shall assess whether circumstances require the **terms of the engagement to be revised** and whether there is a need to **remind the entity of the existing terms**. The auditor may decide **not to send a new engagement letter each period**. However, the following factors may make it **appropriate to revise the terms** or **to remind the entity** of the existing terms:
- Any indication that the entity **misunderstands** the objective and scope of the audit;
- Any **revised or special terms** of the engagement;
- A **recent change** of senior management;
- A **significant change in ownership**;
- A **significant change in nature or size** of the entity's business;
- A **change in legal or regulatory requirements**;
- A **change in the financial reporting framework** adopted; and
- A **change in other reporting requirements**.

**Conclusion:** The engagement letter documents the agreed terms; for recurring audits a fresh letter is not mandatory each year, but the above triggers may require its revision or a reminder.

*(Full-marks tip: split the answer cleanly into "contents" and "recurring-audit factors" — the second half carries substantial marks and is frequently omitted. Reproduce at least six recurring-audit factors.)*

### Q8. Ch: Audit Strategy & Planning — Overall Audit Strategy vs Audit Plan (SA 300) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between the **overall audit strategy** and the **audit plan** under SA 300 "Planning an Audit of Financial Statements", and explain the relationship between the two.

**Answer:**
**SA 300 "Planning an Audit of Financial Statements"** requires the auditor to establish an **overall audit strategy** that sets the scope, timing and direction of the audit, and that guides the development of the more detailed **audit plan**.

**Overall audit strategy:** In establishing it, the auditor shall (a) identify the characteristics of the engagement that define its **scope**; (b) ascertain the **reporting objectives** to plan the timing of the audit and the nature of communications required; (c) consider the factors that, in the auditor's professional judgement, are **significant** in directing the engagement team's efforts (e.g., materiality, high-risk areas); (d) consider the results of **preliminary engagement activities**; and (e) ascertain the **nature, timing and extent of resources** necessary to perform the engagement.

**Audit plan:** The audit plan is more **detailed** than the overall strategy. It shall include a description of:
(a) the **nature, timing and extent of planned risk assessment procedures** (as per SA 315);
(b) the **nature, timing and extent of planned further audit procedures** at the assertion level (as per SA 330); and
(c) **other planned audit procedures** required to be carried out to comply with the SAs.

| Basis | Overall Audit Strategy | Audit Plan |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | Broad — sets scope, timing & direction | Detailed — specific procedures |
| Level | Higher, overall level | Lower, operational level |
| Content | Resources, reporting objectives, significant factors | Nature, timing & extent of risk-assessment & further procedures |
| Sequence | Established first | Developed after the strategy |

**Relationship:** The two are **not discrete or sequential** in a rigid sense; they are **closely inter-related**, since a change in one may result in a consequential change to the other. The strategy provides the framework within which the detailed plan is developed; both are **updated and changed** as necessary during the course of the audit.

**Conclusion:** The overall audit strategy is the "macro" blueprint and the audit plan is the "micro" set of procedures flowing from it; together they constitute the planning required by SA 300.

*(Full-marks tip: the distinguishing table plus the "they are inter-related; a change in one changes the other" line is what the examiner looks for; do not treat the plan as being made strictly before the strategy.)*

### Q9. Ch: Audit Strategy & Planning — Planning a First-Year (Initial) Audit (SA 300) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** CA Rehan has been appointed as the statutory auditor of Copperfield Textiles Ltd. for the financial year 2025-26. This is an **initial audit engagement** — the company was audited by another firm in earlier years. State the **additional matters** the auditor should consider in establishing the overall audit strategy and audit plan for such an **initial audit engagement** under SA 300, and the related requirement regarding opening balances.

**Answer:**
**SA 300** recognises that the purpose and objective of planning are the same whether the audit is an initial or a recurring engagement. However, for an **initial audit engagement**, the auditor **may need to expand the planning activities** because he does not ordinarily have the previous experience with the entity that is considered in planning recurring engagements.

For an **initial audit engagement**, the auditor may consider the following **additional matters** in establishing the overall audit strategy and audit plan:
1. Unless prohibited by law or regulation, arrangements to be made with the **predecessor auditor**, for example, to **review the predecessor auditor's working papers**;
2. Any **major issues** (including the application of accounting principles or of auditing and reporting standards) **discussed with management** in connection with the initial selection as auditor, and the communication of these matters to those charged with governance and how they affect the overall audit strategy and audit plan;
3. The **audit procedures necessary to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence regarding opening balances** (as required by **SA 510 "Initial Audit Engagements — Opening Balances"**); and
4. The **assignment of firm personnel** with appropriate levels of capabilities and competence to respond to anticipated significant risks, and other procedures required by the firm's system of quality management for initial audit engagements (e.g., the **engagement quality review** under SA 220 / SQC 1).

**Regarding opening balances (SA 510):** As it is an initial engagement, the opening balances have not been audited by CA Rehan. He must obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence about whether the **opening balances contain misstatements** that materially affect the current period's financial statements, and whether appropriate **accounting policies** reflected in the opening balances have been **consistently applied**. This may be done by reviewing the **predecessor auditor's working papers** or by performing **specific audit procedures** in the current period (e.g., verifying subsequent realisation of opening receivables, physical verification of opening inventory levels through roll-back).

**Conclusion:** For Copperfield Textiles Ltd., CA Rehan should expand his planning to include predecessor-auditor liaison, opening-balance procedures under SA 510, and quality-management assignments, in addition to the normal SA 300 planning steps.

*(Full-marks tip: this is the assigned "first-year audit" sub-topic — the four SA 300 initial-engagement matters plus the SA 510 opening-balances requirement together earn full marks; mentioning the predecessor working-paper review twice (planning and opening balances) is legitimate and rewarded.)*

### Q10. Ch: Audit Strategy & Planning — Preliminary Engagement Activities (SA 300) (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Before beginning an initial or continuing audit, SA 300 requires the auditor to undertake certain **preliminary engagement activities**. State these activities and explain their purpose.

**Answer:**
**SA 300** requires the auditor to undertake the following **preliminary engagement activities** at the beginning of the current audit engagement:
1. Performing procedures required by **SA 220 "Quality Control for an Audit of Financial Statements"** regarding the **continuance of the client relationship and the specific audit engagement**;
2. Evaluating **compliance with relevant ethical requirements**, including **independence**, in accordance with SA 220; and
3. Establishing an **understanding of the terms of the engagement**, as required by **SA 210**.

**Purpose:** The performance of these preliminary engagement activities at the beginning of the current audit engagement **assists the auditor** in identifying and evaluating events or circumstances that may **adversely affect** the auditor's ability to plan and perform the audit engagement. In particular, performing these activities enables the auditor to plan an audit engagement for which, for example:
- The auditor **maintains the necessary independence and ability** to perform the engagement;
- There are **no issues with management integrity** that may affect the auditor's willingness to continue the engagement; and
- There is **no misunderstanding with the client** as to the terms of the engagement.

**Conclusion:** Preliminary engagement activities (client continuance, ethics/independence, and engagement terms) are the gateway that ensures the auditor can properly plan and perform the audit; they precede the establishment of the overall audit strategy.

*(Full-marks tip: name the three activities with their governing standards (SA 220, ethics, SA 210) and then the three "purpose" bullets — students who list the activities but omit the purpose lose the explanatory marks.)*

### Q11. Ch: Audit Strategy & Planning — Benefits of Planning & Direction/Supervision/Review (SA 300) (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** "Adequate planning benefits the audit of financial statements in several ways." Enumerate the benefits of planning as stated in SA 300, and state what the auditor must document regarding planning.

**Answer:**
**SA 300** states that **adequate planning benefits** the audit of financial statements in the following ways:
1. It helps the auditor to **devote appropriate attention to important areas** of the audit.
2. It helps the auditor **identify and resolve potential problems on a timely basis**.
3. It helps the auditor to **properly organise and manage** the audit engagement so that it is performed in an **effective and efficient manner**.
4. It assists in the **selection of engagement team members** with appropriate levels of capabilities and competence to respond to anticipated risks, and in the **proper assignment of work** to them.
5. It facilitates the **direction and supervision** of engagement team members and the **review** of their work.
6. It assists, where applicable, in the **coordination of work** done by auditors of components and experts.

**Documentation:** As required by SA 300 (read with SA 230), the auditor shall include in the audit documentation:
(a) The **overall audit strategy**;
(b) The **audit plan**; and
(c) Any **significant changes** made during the audit engagement to the overall audit strategy or the audit plan, and the **reasons** for such changes.

**Conclusion:** Planning is not a discrete phase but a **continual and iterative process**; its documented benefits enhance the effectiveness, efficiency and quality of the audit.

*(Full-marks tip: list all six benefits and the three-part documentation requirement; the documentation limb is often forgotten and carries a mark on its own.)*

### Q12. Ch: Audit Strategy & Planning — Direction, Supervision and Review Factors (SA 300) (Marks: 4) [Application]
**Question:** The engagement partner of a mid-sized audit firm is deciding how much **direction, supervision and review** the assistants on the audit of Silverline Ceramics Ltd. will require. State the factors, as contemplated by SA 300, that influence the **nature, timing and extent** of direction and supervision of engagement team members and the review of their work.

**Answer:**
**SA 300** provides that the **nature, timing and extent** of the direction and supervision of engagement team members and the review of their work **vary** depending on many factors, including:
1. The **size and complexity** of the entity;
2. The **area of the audit**;
3. The **assessed risks of material misstatement** (for example, an increase in the assessed risk of material misstatement for a given area of the audit ordinarily requires a corresponding increase in the **extent and timeliness** of direction and supervision of engagement team members, and a more detailed review of their work); and
4. The **capabilities and competence** of the individual team members performing the audit work.

**Application:** For Silverline Ceramics Ltd., where an area carries **higher assessed risk** (say, revenue recognition or inventory valuation), the partner should assign **more experienced staff**, provide **greater direction**, supervise **more closely and contemporaneously**, and review their work in **greater detail**. Conversely, low-risk, routine areas handled by competent seniors need comparatively **less** direction and supervision. The auditor also plans the **nature, timing and extent of direction and supervision of engagement team members and review of their work** as part of the overall audit strategy.

**Conclusion:** Direction, supervision and review are **risk- and competence-sensitive**: higher risk and less-experienced staff demand more; lower risk and more-experienced staff demand less.

*(Full-marks tip: the "inverse relationship" — higher assessed risk / lower competence ⇒ more supervision — is the key sentence the examiner wants; support it with all four factors.)*

### Q13. Ch: Materiality — Meaning and Determination of Materiality (SA 320) (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the concept of **materiality** in the context of an audit as per SA 320 "Materiality in Planning and Performing an Audit", and state how misstatements are regarded as material.

**Answer:**
**SA 320 "Materiality in Planning and Performing an Audit"** deals with the auditor's responsibility to apply the concept of materiality in planning and performing an audit. The applicable financial reporting frameworks generally discuss materiality in the following terms:
1. Misstatements, including omissions, are considered to be **material** if they, **individually or in the aggregate**, could reasonably be expected to **influence the economic decisions** of users taken on the basis of the financial statements;
2. Judgements about materiality are made in the **light of surrounding circumstances**, and are affected by the **size or nature** of a misstatement, or a combination of both; and
3. Judgements about matters that are material to users of the financial statements are based on a consideration of the **common financial information needs of users as a group**. The possible effect of misstatements on **specific individual users**, whose needs may vary widely, is **not** considered.

This concept provides a **frame of reference** to the auditor in (a) planning and performing the audit, and (b) evaluating the effect of identified misstatements on the audit and of uncorrected misstatements, if any, on the financial statements.

The determination of materiality is a matter of the **auditor's professional judgement**, and is affected by the auditor's perception of the financial information needs of users of the financial statements.

**Conclusion:** Materiality is a threshold, judged by both **size and nature**, above which misstatements could reasonably be expected to influence users' economic decisions; it is fundamental to both planning and evaluation.

*(Full-marks tip: the phrase "individually or in the aggregate could reasonably be expected to influence the economic decisions of users" is the definitional core; also state that specific individual users are not considered — a frequently missed point.)*

### Q14. Ch: Materiality — Benchmarks for Determining Materiality (SA 320) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Materiality for the financial statements as a whole is often determined by applying a **percentage to a chosen benchmark**. Discuss the factors relevant to the **selection of an appropriate benchmark** under SA 320, and comment on the suitability of the benchmark in each of the following independent situations:

| Case | Entity | Situation |
|---|---|---|
| (a) | Aurora Power Ltd. | Profit before tax is highly **volatile** year to year; capital-intensive utility |
| (b) | Helios Startup Pvt. Ltd. | An owner-managed entity where the owner **takes out much of the PBT as remuneration** |
| (c) | Trinity Charitable Trust | A **not-for-profit** entity |

**Answer:**
**SA 320** provides that determining materiality involves the exercise of **professional judgement**. A **percentage is often applied to a chosen benchmark** as a starting point in determining materiality for the financial statements as a whole. **Factors that may affect the identification of an appropriate benchmark** include:
1. The **elements** of the financial statements (e.g., assets, liabilities, equity, revenue, expenses);
2. Whether there are items on which the attention of the users of the particular entity's financial statements **tends to be focused** (for example, for the purpose of evaluating financial performance users may tend to focus on **profit, revenue or net assets**);
3. The **nature of the entity**, where the entity is in its **life cycle**, and the **industry and economic environment** in which the entity operates;
4. The entity's **ownership structure** and the way it is **financed** (for example, if an entity is financed solely by debt rather than equity, users may put more emphasis on **assets and claims on them** than on the entity's earnings); and
5. The **relative volatility** of the benchmark.

Examples of benchmarks include categories of reported income such as **profit before tax, total revenue, gross profit and total expenses, total equity or net asset value**. Profit before tax from continuing operations is often used for profit-oriented entities; when it is volatile, other benchmarks such as gross profit or total revenues may be more appropriate.

**Comment on each case:**

| Case | Suitable Benchmark & Reasoning |
|---|---|

### Q15. Ch: Materiality — Performance Materiality (SA 320) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Define **performance materiality** as per SA 320 and explain its purpose. Why is performance materiality set at an amount lower than materiality for the financial statements as a whole?

**Answer:**
As defined in **SA 320**, **"performance materiality"** means the amount or amounts set by the auditor at **less than materiality for the financial statements as a whole** to reduce to an **appropriately low level** the probability that the **aggregate of uncorrected and undetected misstatements** exceeds materiality for the financial statements as a whole. Where applicable, performance materiality also refers to the amount or amounts set by the auditor at less than the materiality level or levels for **particular classes of transactions, account balances or disclosures**.

**Requirement:** SA 320 requires that the auditor shall determine performance materiality for purposes of **assessing the risks of material misstatement** and **determining the nature, timing and extent of further audit procedures**.

**Purpose / Why lower than overall materiality:** Performance materiality is **not** a simple mechanical calculation and involves the exercise of professional judgement. It is set **below** materiality for the financial statements as a whole in order to build in a **margin or buffer** for the following reasons:
1. An audit is performed on a **test basis**; hence there is a risk that **some misstatements remain undetected** (undetected misstatements).
2. Misstatements that are identified but **left uncorrected** by management (uncorrected misstatements) may, **in the aggregate**, together with undetected misstatements, exceed the overall materiality level.

By setting performance materiality lower, the auditor reduces to an appropriately low level the probability that the total of such uncorrected **and** undetected misstatements exceeds the materiality for the financial statements as a whole.

It is affected by the auditor's **understanding of the entity** (updated during risk assessment) and the **nature and extent of misstatements identified in previous audits** and thereby the auditor's expectations in relation to misstatements in the current period.

**Conclusion:** Performance materiality is a deliberately lower operational threshold that provides a cushion against the aggregation of undetected and uncorrected misstatements, ensuring the audit remains effective.

*(Full-marks tip: the definition must contain both "uncorrected AND undetected misstatements" and "aggregate"; the buffer rationale is the crux — omitting either the undetected or uncorrected leg loses a mark.)*

### Q17. Ch: Materiality — Materiality and Audit Risk Relationship (SA 320 / SA 200) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the **relationship between materiality and audit risk**, and describe how the auditor uses materiality in **evaluating the effect of uncorrected misstatements** at the conclusion of the audit (link to SA 450).

**Answer:**
**SA 320**, read with **SA 200**, explains that in planning the audit, the auditor makes judgements about the **size of misstatements that will be considered material**. These judgements provide a basis for (a) determining the **nature, timing and extent of risk assessment procedures**; (b) **identifying and assessing the risks of material misstatement**; and (c) determining the **nature, timing and extent of further audit procedures**.

**Inverse relationship:** There is an **inverse relationship** between materiality and the level of audit risk — that is, the **higher** the materiality level, the **lower** the audit risk, and vice versa. The auditor takes this inverse relationship into account when determining the nature, timing and extent of audit procedures. For example, if, after planning specific audit procedures, the auditor determines that the **acceptable materiality level is lower**, audit risk is increased; the auditor compensates for this by either (a) **reducing the assessed risk of material misstatement**, where feasible, by carrying out **extended or additional tests of control**; or (b) **reducing detection risk** by modifying the **nature, timing and extent** of planned **substantive procedures**.

**Use in evaluating uncorrected misstatements (SA 450):** At the **conclusion** of the audit, materiality is used to **evaluate the effect of identified misstatements** on the audit and of **uncorrected misstatements**, if any, on the financial statements. Under **SA 450 "Evaluation of Misstatements Identified during the Audit"**, the auditor shall determine whether **uncorrected misstatements**, **individually or in aggregate**, are **material**, by comparing them against materiality for the financial statements as a whole (and materiality for particular classes/balances/disclosures where relevant). The auditor also considers the **nature** of the misstatements and the particular circumstances, not merely their size, and requests management to correct them; if uncorrected misstatements are material, the auditor **modifies the opinion** under SA 705.

**Conclusion:** Materiality and audit risk are **inversely related** and jointly drive the extent of audit work; at conclusion, materiality is the yardstick against which uncorrected misstatements are judged material, thereby determining the audit opinion.

*(Full-marks tip: the "inverse relationship" and the two compensating responses (lower assessed RoMM via tests of control, or lower detection risk via substantive procedures) are the theory core; linking to SA 450/705 at the conclusion stage earns the evaluation marks.)*

### Q18. Ch: Risk Assessment & Internal Control — Significant risk vs. risk of material misstatement at assertion level (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** SA 315 (Revised) requires the auditor to identify and assess the risks of material misstatement at the financial statement level and at the assertion level, and to determine which of the assessed risks are "significant risks". A newly-qualified assistant argues that every risk assessed at the assertion level is automatically a significant risk. Examine whether this view is correct and explain (a) how a risk of material misstatement at the assertion level is distinguished from a significant risk, and (b) the factors the auditor considers in deciding that a risk is a significant risk.

**Answer:**
The assistant's view is **not correct**. Under SA 315 (Revised) "Identifying and Assessing the Risks of Material Misstatement", not every risk assessed at the assertion level is a significant risk; a significant risk is a sub-set of the risks of material misstatement (RoMM) that lies on the higher end of the spectrum of inherent risk.

**(a) RoMM at the assertion level vs. significant risk**

The risk of material misstatement at the assertion level for classes of transactions, account balances and disclosures comprises two components — **inherent risk** and **control risk** — which the auditor is required to assess *separately*. Inherent risk is the susceptibility of an assertion to a misstatement, before consideration of controls; control risk is the risk that a misstatement will not be prevented, or detected and corrected, on a timely basis by the entity's internal control.

A **significant risk** is defined by SA 315 as an identified risk of material misstatement for which the assessment of **inherent risk is close to the upper end of the spectrum of inherent risk** due to the degree to which inherent risk factors affect the combination of the likelihood and magnitude of a possible misstatement; or a risk that is to be treated as a significant risk in accordance with the requirements of other SAs. Thus significant risk is judged by reference to **inherent risk alone**, before considering controls.

| Basis | RoMM at assertion level | Significant risk |
|---|---|---|
| Components considered | Inherent risk *and* control risk | Inherent risk only |
| Coverage | All identified assertion-level risks | Only those near the upper end of the inherent-risk spectrum |
| Auditor's response | Design further audit procedures (SA 330) | Additional requirements: understand related controls (SA 315) and, under SA 330, obtain audit evidence from **substantive procedures** specifically responsive to that risk; when the approach includes only substantive procedures they must include tests of details |

**(b) Factors indicating a significant risk**

In exercising judgement the auditor considers, among others: whether the risk is a **risk of fraud**; whether it relates to recent significant economic, accounting or other developments; the **complexity** of transactions; whether the risk involves significant **related-party transactions**; the degree of **subjectivity in measurement**, especially measurement involving a wide range of estimation uncertainty; and whether the risk involves **significant transactions outside the normal course of business** or that otherwise appear to be unusual. These are the inherent risk factors (subjectivity, complexity, uncertainty, change, and susceptibility to management bias or fraud).

**Conclusion:** A risk assessed at the assertion level becomes a significant risk only when its inherent risk lies close to the upper end of the spectrum; therefore the assistant is wrong to equate the two, and significant risks attract the additional requirements of SA 315 and SA 330.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards stating that significant risk is judged on **inherent risk alone** and that SA 330 mandates **tests of details** where the response is substantive-only. Marks are lost for listing risk factors without first defining the spectrum of inherent risk.)*

### Q19. Ch: Risk Assessment & Internal Control — Walkthrough vs. test of controls (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** During the audit of Meghna Textiles Ltd., the audit senior performed a "walkthrough" of the sales-to-cash cycle by tracing one sales order through order acceptance, dispatch, invoicing and recording, and on the strength of that single walkthrough concluded that controls over sales were operating effectively and reduced substantive testing accordingly. The engagement partner objected. Analyse whether a walkthrough can substitute for tests of controls, and explain the purpose each serves under SA 315 and SA 330.

**Answer:**
The engagement partner is right; a walkthrough **cannot** substitute for a test of controls, and reducing substantive procedures on the basis of a single walkthrough is not supported by the Standards.

**Nature and purpose of a walkthrough (SA 315):** A walkthrough involves tracing a **single (or a few) transaction(s)** from its origination through the entity's processes until it is reflected in the financial records. Its purpose is to help the auditor **understand and confirm** that the auditor's understanding of the flow of transactions and the design of controls is complete and accurate, and to determine whether the controls have been **implemented** (i.e., that they exist and the entity is using them). It is a **risk assessment procedure** performed to obtain an understanding of internal control; it addresses the **design and implementation** of controls, not their operating effectiveness over the period.

**Nature and purpose of tests of controls (SA 330):** A test of controls is designed to evaluate the **operating effectiveness** of controls in preventing, or detecting and correcting, material misstatements at the assertion level **throughout the period of intended reliance**. Because a single transaction says nothing about whether the control operated consistently over the whole period, the auditor must test a **sample** of transactions, and also test how the control was applied, the consistency of its application, and by whom.

| Basis | Walkthrough | Test of controls |
|---|---|---|
| SA | SA 315 (risk assessment procedure) | SA 330 (further audit procedure) |
| Objective | Confirm understanding; check **design & implementation** | Evaluate **operating effectiveness** over the period |
| Extent | Usually one/few transactions | A representative **sample** across the period |
| Effect on substantive work | None on its own | Permits reduced substantive testing only if controls are found effective |

When does the auditor need tests of controls? Only when (i) the auditor **intends to rely** on the operating effectiveness of controls in determining the nature, timing and extent of substantive procedures, or (ii) substantive procedures **alone cannot** provide sufficient appropriate audit evidence at the assertion level (SA 330). Merely confirming that a control is implemented via a walkthrough does not entitle the auditor to reduce substantive procedures.

**Conclusion:** The senior confused a risk assessment walkthrough with a test of controls. The reliance and the consequent reduction in substantive testing are not justified; the senior must perform proper tests of controls over a sample for the period before any reliance is placed.

*(Full-marks tip: State clearly that a walkthrough tests **design & implementation** while tests of controls test **operating effectiveness over the period** — the one-line distinction that carries most marks. Deductions occur where students treat "implementation" and "operating effectiveness" as synonyms.)*

### Q20. Ch: Risk Assessment & Internal Control — Communicating deficiencies (SA 265) (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** While auditing Kaveri Foods Ltd., the auditor noted that a bank reconciliation had not been prepared for four months and that no one independently reviews journal entries posted by the chief accountant. State how the auditor should determine whether a deficiency in internal control is a "significant deficiency" under SA 265, and to whom and by when such deficiencies must be communicated.

**Answer:**
SA 265 "Communicating Deficiencies in Internal Control to Those Charged with Governance and Management" governs this situation.

A **deficiency in internal control** exists when (a) a control is designed, implemented or operated in such a way that it is unable to prevent, or detect and correct, misstatements in the financial statements on a timely basis; or (b) a control necessary to prevent, or detect and correct, such misstatements is **missing**.

A **significant deficiency** is a deficiency, or combination of deficiencies, in internal control that, in the auditor's **professional judgement**, is of **sufficient importance to merit the attention of those charged with governance**. In evaluating significance, the auditor considers, among others: the likelihood of the deficiencies leading to material misstatements in future; the susceptibility of the related asset or liability to loss or fraud; the subjectivity/complexity of the amounts; the amounts exposed; the **volume of activity**; the importance of the controls to the financial reporting process; and the interaction of the deficiency with other deficiencies. The absence of an independent review of journal entries and the non-preparation of bank reconciliations for four months are strong indicators of significant deficiencies.

- The auditor shall communicate **in writing** the **significant deficiencies** identified during the audit to **those charged with governance** on a **timely basis**.
- The auditor shall also communicate to **management** at an appropriate level of responsibility, on a timely basis, (i) in writing, the significant deficiencies communicated or intended to be communicated to those charged with governance (unless it would be inappropriate to communicate directly to management), and (ii) other deficiencies of sufficient importance not already communicated by others.

The written communication must include a **description** of the deficiencies and their **potential effects**, and **sufficient information** to enable the reader to understand the context (that the purpose of the audit was to express an opinion, that the audit considered internal control relevant to the financial statements, and that the matters reported are limited to those identified during the audit).

**Conclusion:** Both matters appear to be significant deficiencies; they must be communicated in writing to those charged with governance on a timely basis, with a description and potential effects, and separately drawn to management's attention.

### Q22. Ch: Risk Assessment & Internal Control — Overall responses & controls-reliance approach (SA 330) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** For Godavari Pharma Ltd., the auditor assessed control risk over the revenue cycle as low based on strong, automated application controls, and decided to place reliance on those controls to reduce year-end substantive testing. The controls were last tested two years ago and have not changed. Advise the auditor, under SA 330, on (a) whether audit evidence about operating effectiveness from prior audits can be used, and (b) the timing considerations when tests of controls are performed at an interim date.

**Answer:**
SA 330 "The Auditor's Responses to Assessed Risks" applies.

**(a) Using audit evidence about operating effectiveness obtained in prior audits:**
The auditor may use such evidence, but only after establishing its **continuing relevance** by obtaining audit evidence about whether **changes** in those specific controls have occurred since they were last tested. The auditor obtains this by inquiry combined with observation or inspection.
- If the control has **changed** since last tested, the auditor must **test its operating effectiveness in the current audit**.
- If the control has **not changed**, the auditor must test its operating effectiveness **at least once in every third audit**, and must test **some** controls in each audit (to avoid testing all controls being relied upon in a single period and none in the intervening two periods).
- **Important exception — controls over significant risks:** If the controls mitigate a **significant risk**, the auditor **cannot** rely on prior-period evidence; those controls must be tested in the **current period**.

Here the controls were last tested two years ago and have not changed. If the revenue cycle does **not** involve a significant risk, reliance on prior evidence is permissible provided this is the third audit — the auditor must therefore **test them in the current audit**. If revenue involves a significant risk, the controls must be tested **every year**.

**(b) Timing — tests of controls at an interim date:**
When the auditor obtains evidence about operating effectiveness during an interim period, the auditor must obtain evidence about the **remaining period** by determining what additional evidence is needed for the period from the interim date to the year-end. This is done by obtaining evidence about **significant changes** to those controls after the interim period and determining the **additional audit evidence** to be obtained for the remaining period (which may involve extending interim tests or testing the monitoring of controls).

**Conclusion:** The auditor may not automatically rely on the two-year-old test; unchanged non-significant-risk controls must be tested at least once in three years (and some each year), and controls over significant risks must be tested every year, with interim testing supplemented for the remaining period.

### Q23. Ch: Risk Assessment & Internal Control — Inherent risk factors and the spectrum of inherent risk (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** SA 315 (Revised) introduces the concept of a "spectrum of inherent risk" and lists inherent risk factors. Explain what is meant by the spectrum of inherent risk and describe the inherent risk factors the auditor considers when assessing inherent risk at the assertion level.

**Answer:**
**Spectrum of inherent risk:** SA 315 (Revised) requires the auditor to assess **inherent risk** by assessing the **likelihood** and **magnitude** of misstatement. The degree to which inherent risk varies is referred to as the **spectrum of inherent risk**. Assertions to which inherent risk factors give rise to a higher combination of likelihood and magnitude sit towards the **higher end** of the spectrum; those with a lower combination sit towards the **lower end**. Risks assessed close to the **upper end** are candidates for classification as **significant risks**. Assessing inherent risk on a spectrum, rather than as merely "high/low", enables the auditor to direct greater effort and more persuasive evidence to the assertions most susceptible to material misstatement.

**Inherent risk factors:** These are characteristics of events or conditions that affect the susceptibility of an assertion to misstatement, before consideration of controls. They are:
- **Complexity** — arising from the nature of the information or the manner in which it is prepared (e.g., complex financial-instrument valuations).
- **Subjectivity** — arising from limitations in the knowledge or data reasonably available, such that management must make a subjective choice among an acceptable range (e.g., estimates).
- **Change** — resulting from economic, accounting, regulatory, industry or other developments (e.g., a new revenue model).
- **Uncertainty** — arising when information cannot be corroborated by direct, objective evidence (e.g., estimation uncertainty in provisions).
- **Susceptibility to misstatement due to management bias or fraud** — the extent to which the assertion is susceptible to intentional or unintentional bias.

Other factors, such as the transaction being **non-routine or outside the normal course of business**, also affect susceptibility.

**Conclusion:** The spectrum of inherent risk positions each assertion by the combined likelihood and magnitude of possible misstatement; the five inherent risk factors — complexity, subjectivity, change, uncertainty and susceptibility to bias/fraud — are the lens through which the auditor places an assertion on that spectrum.

*(Full-marks tip: Name the **five inherent risk factors** and tie the "upper end of the spectrum" to significant risks. Students who only define inherent risk without the factors and the likelihood/magnitude framing lose marks.)*

### Q24. Ch: Risk Assessment & Internal Control — Limitations of internal control and management override (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** The Managing Director of Chambal Steels Ltd. tells the auditor that because the company has installed a robust, well-documented system of internal control certified by consultants, the auditor may safely dispense with substantive testing of material balances. Critically examine this statement with reference to the **inherent limitations** of any internal control system.

**Answer:**
The Managing Director's statement is **not tenable**. However well-designed and operated, **no internal control system can provide absolute assurance** of achieving the entity's financial reporting objectives; it can, at best, provide **reasonable assurance**. This is because every system of internal control has **inherent limitations**, recognised by SA 315.

The inherent limitations are:
1. **Human judgement in decision-making** can be faulty, and **breakdowns** in internal control can occur because of **human error** (e.g., a mistake in the design or operation of a control, or misunderstanding of instructions).
2. **Management override** of controls — controls can be circumvented by the collusion of management with employees or third parties, and management may override controls it has itself established (e.g., to overstate results). This is a pervasive limitation and is one reason SA 240 requires the auditor to always treat management override of controls as a significant risk of fraud.
3. **Collusion** among two or more people can defeat controls (e.g., segregation of duties circumvented by conspiracy).
4. **Cost-benefit consideration** — the entity designs controls only where the expected benefit exceeds the cost; consequently some risks are deliberately left uncontrolled.
5. **Controls tend to be directed at routine transactions** rather than **non-routine or unusual** transactions, which therefore remain more susceptible.
6. Controls may become **inadequate due to changes in conditions** and compliance may deteriorate over time.

Consequently, an assessment that control risk is low **reduces but never eliminates** the need for substantive procedures. Under SA 330, **irrespective** of the assessed risks, the auditor shall design and perform substantive procedures for **each material class of transactions, account balance and disclosure**; and substantive procedures must be responsive to any **significant risk**. Reliance on controls affects only the **nature, timing and extent** of substantive procedures, not their complete elimination.

**Conclusion:** Because of the inherent limitations of internal control — particularly management override — the auditor cannot dispense with substantive procedures on material balances; the MD's suggestion must be declined.

### Q25. Ch: Audit Evidence — Inventory attendance where count is not on the balance-sheet date (SA 501) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Yamuna Agro Ltd. closes its accounts on 31 March. Owing to labour unavailability, physical verification of inventory was conducted on **10 April** (10 days after year-end). Inventory is material to the financial statements. State the auditor's responsibilities under SA 501 for attending the count and for the timing difference, and, using the data below, indicate how the auditor should reconcile the counted quantity to the year-end balance for one product.

| Particulars — Product P (units) | Qty |
|---|---|
| Physical quantity counted on 10 April | 4,200 |
| Purchases received 1 April – 10 April | 900 |
| Sales dispatched 1 April – 10 April | 1,500 |
| Sales returns received 1 April – 10 April | 120 |
| Goods returned to supplier 1 April – 10 April | 200 |

**Solution:**
When inventory is material, SA 501 "Audit Evidence — Specific Considerations for Selected Items" requires the auditor to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence regarding the existence and condition of inventory by:
1. **Attendance at physical inventory counting** (unless impracticable), during which the auditor shall — (a) **evaluate management's instructions and procedures** for recording and controlling the results of the count; (b) **observe** the performance of management's count procedures; (c) **inspect** the inventory; and (d) perform **test counts** (tracing from the count records to the inventory and from the inventory to the count records, i.e., two-directional testing to test completeness and existence).
2. Perform audit procedures over the **final inventory records** to determine whether they accurately reflect actual count results.

**Count on a date other than the date of the financial statements:** SA 501 provides that when physical counting is conducted at a date **other than** the date of the financial statements, the auditor shall, in addition to the above, perform audit procedures to obtain audit evidence about whether **changes in inventory between the count date and the date of the financial statements are properly recorded**. This requires "rolling back" the transactions in the intervening period. Because the count here (10 April) is **after** the year-end (31 March), movements between 1 April and 10 April must be **reversed** to arrive at the 31 March quantity.

**WN-1 — Reconciliation of counted quantity to year-end quantity (roll-back):**
To go *back* from the 10-April count to the 31-March balance, add back what was dispatched/removed after year-end and deduct what was received/added after year-end.

| Particulars | Units | Effect on 31-Mar balance |
|---|---|---|
| Physical quantity counted on 10 April | 4,200 | Base |
| *Add:* Sales dispatched after year-end (were in stock on 31 Mar) | 1,500 | + |
| *Add:* Goods returned to supplier after year-end (were in stock on 31 Mar) | 200 | + |
| *Less:* Purchases received after year-end (not in stock on 31 Mar) | (900) | − |
| *Less:* Sales returns received after year-end (not in stock on 31 Mar) | (120) | − |
| **Inventory quantity as at 31 March (Product P)** | **4,880** | |

Formula: Year-end qty = Count qty + Outward movements after year-end − Inward movements after year-end = 4,200 + (1,500 + 200) − (900 + 120) = **4,880 units**.

**Cross-check (roll-forward logic):** Starting from 31 March 4,880, add inwards (900 + 120 = 1,020) and deduct outwards (1,500 + 200 = 1,700): 4,880 + 1,020 − 1,700 = **4,200 units** = counted quantity. Reconciles.

The auditor must also verify that the intervening movements are supported by proper cut-off documentation (GRNs, dispatch notes, sales-return notes, debit notes) and that internal controls over movements in the intervening period are adequate; where controls are deficient the reliability of the roll-back is impaired.

**Answer:** Physical inventory of Product P as at 31 March is **4,880 units**; the auditor discharges SA 501 by attending the 10-April count (test counts, observation, inspection) and by verifying the roll-back of the 1–10 April movements to the year-end date with supporting cut-off evidence.

### Q26. Ch: Audit Evidence — Litigation, claims and segment information (SA 501) (Marks: 5) [Theory]

### Q27. Ch: Audit Evidence — Negative vs. positive confirmation requests (SA 505) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** SA 505 recognises two forms of external confirmation request — positive and negative. Distinguish between them, and state the conditions that must **all** be present before the auditor may use **negative** confirmations as the sole substantive procedure to address an assessed risk of material misstatement at the assertion level.

**Answer:**
SA 505 "External Confirmations" defines an external confirmation as audit evidence obtained as a **direct written response** to the auditor from a third party (the confirming party), in paper or electronic form. It contemplates two forms of request.

**Distinction:**

| Basis | Positive confirmation request | Negative confirmation request |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | A request that the confirming party **respond directly** to the auditor indicating whether it **agrees or disagrees** with the information, or providing the requested information | A request that the confirming party respond **only if** it **disagrees** with the information provided |
| Evidence from non-response | A non-response yields **no audit evidence**; alternative procedures are needed | A non-response is treated as agreement, but provides **less persuasive** evidence (silence may mean the request was never received or read) |
| Reliability | More reliable | Less reliable |
| When used | Default form; used where risk is higher | Only where the strict SA 505 conditions are met |

**Conditions for using negative confirmations as the sole substantive procedure:** The auditor shall **not** use negative confirmation requests as the sole substantive procedure to address an assessed risk of material misstatement at the assertion level **unless all** of the following are present:
1. The auditor has assessed the risk of material misstatement as **low** and has obtained sufficient appropriate audit evidence regarding the **operating effectiveness of controls** relevant to the assertion;
2. The population of items subject to negative confirmation comprises a **large number of small, homogeneous** account balances, transactions or conditions;
3. A **very low exception rate** is expected; and
4. The auditor is **not aware of circumstances or conditions** that would cause recipients of negative confirmation requests to **disregard** such requests.

**Conclusion:** Positive confirmations are the more reliable, default form; negative confirmations, being less persuasive, may serve as the sole substantive procedure only when all four SA 505 conditions — low risk with tested controls, large homogeneous population, very low expected exception rate, and no reason to expect requests to be ignored — are simultaneously satisfied.

*(Full-marks tip: Full marks require **all four** conditions listed conjunctively, and the point that a **positive non-response yields no evidence** whereas a negative non-response is weak evidence. Missing even one of the four negative-confirmation conditions is a partial-mark answer.)*

### Q28. Ch: Audit Evidence — Management refuses permission to send confirmations; non-responses (SA 505) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** During the audit of Narmada Distributors Ltd., the auditor decided to confirm trade receivables. (i) Management refused to allow the auditor to send confirmation requests to three large customers, saying the relationships were "commercially sensitive". (ii) For another set of positive confirmations, no reply was received despite two follow-ups. (iii) One reply came back directly to the auditor but through the client's accounts department. Advise the auditor's course of action in each case under SA 505.

**Answer:**
SA 505 "External Confirmations" addresses each situation.

**(i) Management's refusal to permit the auditor to send confirmation requests:**
When management refuses to allow the auditor to send a confirmation request, the auditor shall:
- **Inquire** as to management's reasons for the refusal, and seek audit evidence about their **validity and reasonableness**;
- **Evaluate the implications** of management's refusal on the auditor's assessment of the relevant **risks of material misstatement**, including the risk of **fraud**, and on the nature, timing and extent of other audit procedures; and
- Perform **alternative audit procedures** designed to obtain relevant and reliable audit evidence (e.g., examining subsequent cash receipts, shipping documentation, and sales near the period-end).

If the auditor concludes that management's refusal is **unreasonable**, or the auditor is **unable to obtain** relevant and reliable audit evidence from alternative procedures, the auditor shall **communicate with those charged with governance** under SA 260 and determine its implications for the audit and the auditor's opinion under SA 705 (a possible **modification**). "Commercial sensitivity" would need to be a genuinely valid reason; a blanket refusal for three large (material) customers is a red flag warranting sceptical evaluation.

**(ii) Non-responses to positive confirmation requests:**
In the case of each **non-response**, the auditor shall perform **alternative audit procedures** to obtain relevant and reliable audit evidence — for example, examining specific **subsequent cash receipts**, shipping documentation, and sales near the period-end. A positive-confirmation non-response provides **no audit evidence**, so alternative procedures are mandatory. However, where a **response to a positive confirmation request is necessary** to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence (e.g., the risk is such that only external confirmation will suffice), and no such confirmation is obtained even after alternative procedures, the auditor shall determine the implications for the audit and the opinion under SA 705.

**(iii) Reply routed through the client's accounts department:**
The reliability of external confirmations is impaired if the response does **not come directly** to the auditor. The auditor is required to maintain **control over external confirmation requests**, including sending the requests and receiving the responses **directly**. A reply that passes through the client's accounts department raises doubt about its **reliability and authenticity** (risk of interception/alteration). The auditor should treat the response with professional scepticism, investigate its reliability, and, if doubts about reliability remain, may need to obtain **further evidence** to resolve them; if the auditor determines the response is not reliable, the auditor shall evaluate the implications on the risk assessment (including fraud) and on the related audit procedures.

**Conclusion:** (i) Inquire into and evaluate the refusal, perform alternative procedures, and if unreasonable/insufficient, involve TCWG and consider modifying the opinion; (ii) perform alternative procedures for each non-response and consider a modification if a confirmation was essential; (iii) treat the indirectly-routed reply with scepticism as its reliability is compromised and obtain further evidence.

*(Full-marks tip: For the refusal, examiners want the three-step response — inquire, evaluate RoMM/fraud implications, alternative procedures — plus TCWG/SA 705 escalation. For non-responses, "no audit evidence, hence alternative procedures". The "maintain control / direct response" principle scores the third part.)*

### Q29. Ch: Audit Evidence — Sufficiency, appropriateness, relevance and reliability (SA 500) (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** SA 500 requires the auditor to obtain "sufficient appropriate audit evidence". Explain the terms **sufficiency** and **appropriateness**, and state the generalisations about the **reliability** of audit evidence that the auditor applies.

**Answer:**
Under SA 500 "Audit Evidence", the objective of the auditor is to design and perform audit procedures in such a way as to enable the auditor to obtain **sufficient appropriate audit evidence** to be able to draw reasonable conclusions on which to base the opinion.

- **Sufficiency** is the measure of the **quantity** of audit evidence. The quantity needed is affected by the auditor's **assessment of the risks of material misstatement** (the higher the assessed risk, the more evidence is likely to be required) and also by the **quality** of the evidence (the higher the quality, the less may be required).
- **Appropriateness** is the measure of the **quality** of audit evidence — that is, its **relevance** and its **reliability** in providing support for the conclusions on which the auditor's opinion is based.
  - **Relevance** deals with the **logical connection** with, or bearing upon, the purpose of the audit procedure and, where appropriate, the assertion under consideration. A given set of audit procedures may provide evidence relevant to certain assertions but not others (e.g., inspecting inventory tests existence, not necessarily rights or valuation).

**Generalisations about reliability (SA 500):** While subject to exceptions, the reliability of audit evidence is generally higher when:
1. It is obtained from **independent sources outside** the entity;
2. Internally-generated evidence is more reliable when the **related controls** (including over its preparation and maintenance) are **effective**;
3. It is obtained **directly by the auditor** (e.g., observation of a control) than indirectly or by inference (e.g., inquiry about its application);
4. It exists in **documentary form** (paper, electronic or other medium) than obtained **orally**; and
5. It is provided by **original documents** than by photocopies, facsimiles, or documents that have been filmed, digitised or otherwise transformed.

**Conclusion:** Sufficiency (quantity) and appropriateness (quality = relevance + reliability) are interrelated — evidence that is more appropriate reduces the quantity required, but merely increasing quantity cannot compensate for poor quality; the five reliability generalisations guide the auditor in weighing the evidence.

*(Full-marks tip: State clearly that sufficiency = quantity and appropriateness = quality (relevance + reliability), and that more quantity does **not** compensate for poor quality. The five reliability generalisations are individually markable.)*

### Q31. Ch: Audit Evidence — Subsequent events (SA 560) after the auditor's report date (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** The financial statements of Bhima Motors Ltd. for the year ended 31 March 2026 were approved and the auditor's report signed on **20 May 2026**. The financial statements were issued to shareholders on **10 June 2026**. On **28 May 2026**, before issue, the auditor learned that a major debtor (₹80 lakh, already provided at 10%) had been declared insolvent on 15 May 2026, indicating the debt was largely irrecoverable at the balance-sheet date. Explain the auditor's obligations under SA 560 for facts that become known **after** the date of the auditor's report but **before** the financial statements are issued, and advise the specific action here.

**Answer:**
SA 560 "Subsequent Events" distinguishes three periods. The event here falls in the period **after the date of the auditor's report (20 May) but before the financial statements are issued (10 June)**.

**Auditor's obligations in this period:** The auditor has **no obligation to perform any audit procedures** regarding the financial statements after the date of the auditor's report. However, if, **after** the date of the auditor's report but **before** the financial statements are issued, a fact becomes known to the auditor that, had it been known at the date of the report, may have caused the auditor to **amend** the report, the auditor shall:
1. **Discuss** the matter with management and, where appropriate, those charged with governance;
2. **Determine whether the financial statements need amendment**; and, if so,
3. **Inquire how management intends to address the matter** in the financial statements.

**If management amends the financial statements:** The auditor shall (a) carry out the audit procedures **necessary in the circumstances** on the amendment; and (b) **extend** the audit procedures for subsequent events up to the **date of the new auditor's report** (i.e., "dual dating" is permitted — the auditor may either date the new report as of a later date, or dual-date the report for the amendment so as to indicate that the procedures on subsequent events are restricted to the amendment). The auditor shall then provide a **new auditor's report** on the amended financial statements, dated no earlier than the date of approval of the amended financial statements.

**If management does not amend where the auditor believes amendment is necessary:** (i) If the auditor's report has **not yet been provided** to the entity, the auditor shall **modify the opinion** (SA 705) and then provide the report; (ii) if the report **has already been provided** to the entity, the auditor shall **notify** management and TCWG **not to issue** the financial statements before the amendments are made; if they are nonetheless issued, the auditor shall take **appropriate action to seek to prevent reliance** on the report (after considering legal rights and obligations, on the basis of legal advice).

**Application to Bhima Motors Ltd.:** The insolvency arose from conditions **existing at the balance-sheet date** (the debtor's financial deterioration), so it is an **adjusting event** under AS 4 "Contingencies and Events Occurring After the Balance Sheet Date". The provision of only 10% on ₹80 lakh (₹8 lakh) is inadequate; the debt is now known to be largely irrecoverable and the receivable is materially overstated. The auditor must **discuss** with management and require them to **amend** the financial statements to write down the debtor appropriately (raise the provision towards the irrecoverable amount). If management amends, the auditor performs procedures on the amendment, extends subsequent-events procedures to the new report date, and issues a **new (or dual-dated) report**. If management **refuses** to amend, the auditor must **modify the opinion** (qualified/adverse) since the report has effectively been provided, and take steps to prevent issuance/reliance.

**Conclusion:** The insolvency is an adjusting subsequent event; the auditor must have the financial statements amended and issue a new/dual-dated report, or, on refusal, modify the opinion and act to prevent reliance on the original report.

*(Full-marks tip: Correctly classify the period (after report, before issue) and the AS 4 **adjusting event** treatment, then give the amend/refuse fork with **dual-dating** and the "prevent reliance" step. Treating it as a non-adjusting event or omitting the AS 4 linkage loses marks.)*

### Q32. Ch: Audit Evidence — Going concern with mitigating factors (SA 570) (Marks: 6) [Application]

Explain the auditor's procedures and reporting under SA 570 where events or conditions cast doubt on going concern but a **material uncertainty** is adequately disclosed, and distinguish that reporting from the case where the disclosure is **inadequate**.

**Answer:**
SA 570 "Going Concern" governs the auditor's responsibilities relating to management's use of the going concern basis of accounting.

**Events/conditions and additional procedures:** A net current liability position and a **default on a loan** are events or conditions that may cast **significant doubt** on the entity's ability to continue as a going concern. When such events/conditions are identified, the auditor shall obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence to determine whether or not a **material uncertainty** exists, by performing additional audit procedures, including:
- Requesting management to make its **assessment** of going concern where it has not yet done so;
- **Evaluating management's plans** for future actions and whether the outcome of these plans is likely to improve the situation and is feasible;
- Where a **cash flow forecast** is a significant factor, evaluating the reliability of the underlying data and the support for the assumptions;
- Considering whether any **additional facts or information** have become available since the assessment date; and
- Requesting **written representations** from management and, where appropriate, those charged with governance regarding their plans for future action and the feasibility of those plans.

Here, the auditor would evaluate the **holding company's support letter** (its authenticity, the supporter's ability and intention to provide support, and whether it is legally/practically enforceable) and the **post-year-end loan renegotiation**.

| Situation | Going concern basis | Reporting under SA 570 |
|---|---|---|
| Material uncertainty exists **and is adequately disclosed** | Appropriate | Express an **unmodified opinion** and include a separate section headed **"Material Uncertainty Related to Going Concern"**, drawing attention to the note and stating that these events indicate a material uncertainty and that the opinion is not modified in this respect |

| Use of going concern basis **inappropriate** | Inappropriate | Express an **adverse opinion** |
| Management **unwilling** to make/extend its assessment | — | Consider the implications for the report (possible qualified/disclaimer) |

**Application to Kosi Infra Ltd.:** If, after evaluating the support letter and renegotiation, the auditor concludes that a **material uncertainty exists** but the accounts adequately disclose the facts (nature of the uncertainty, and that the entity may be unable to realise assets/discharge liabilities in the normal course), the auditor issues an **unmodified opinion with a "Material Uncertainty Related to Going Concern" section**. If the disclosure is **inadequate** — e.g., the note fails to state that a material uncertainty exists — the auditor expresses a **qualified or adverse opinion** and explains the missing disclosure in the Basis for Opinion.

### Q33. Ch: Audit Evidence — Limits and reliability of written representations (SA 580) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** The management of Wardha Chemicals Ltd. offered to give the auditor a written representation confirming the completeness and valuation of all inventories and receivables, and suggested that in view of this representation the auditor need not perform detailed substantive testing on these balances. Examine, under SA 580, the **purpose and limitations** of written representations, and the auditor's course of action where a representation is **inconsistent** with other audit evidence or where management **refuses** to provide a requested representation.

**Answer:**
SA 580 "Written Representations" governs written representations as a form of audit evidence.

**Nature and purpose:** A written representation is a **written statement by management** provided to the auditor to confirm certain matters or support other audit evidence. Written representations are **necessary information** that the auditor requires in connection with the audit; accordingly, similar to responses to inquiries, they are **audit evidence**. The auditor obtains representations that management has fulfilled its responsibility for the **preparation of the financial statements** and for the **completeness of information provided**, and representations to support other evidence relevant to the financial statements or specific assertions where determined necessary.

**Limitations:** Although written representations provide necessary audit evidence, they do **not** provide sufficient appropriate audit evidence **on their own** about any of the matters with which they deal. Furthermore, the fact that management has provided reliable written representations does **not** affect the **nature or extent of other audit evidence** that the auditor obtains about the fulfilment of management's responsibilities or about specific assertions. Written representations **cannot substitute** for the audit evidence the auditor could reasonably expect to be available. Therefore management's suggestion — that a representation on inventory and receivables removes the need for substantive testing — is **incorrect**; the auditor must still perform appropriate substantive procedures (e.g., attendance at inventory count under SA 501, confirmation of receivables under SA 505).

**Doubt over reliability / inconsistency with other evidence:** If the auditor has concerns about the **competence, integrity, ethical values or diligence** of management, or about its commitment to or enforcement of these, the auditor shall determine the effect that such concerns may have on the reliability of representations and audit evidence in general. In particular, if written representations are **inconsistent with other audit evidence**, the auditor shall perform audit procedures to attempt to **resolve the matter**; if the matter remains unresolved, the auditor shall **reconsider the assessment of the competence, integrity, ethical values or diligence of management**, and determine the effect on the reliability of representations (oral or written) and audit evidence in general, and take appropriate action.

**Management refuses to provide a requested representation:** If management does not provide one or more of the requested written representations, the auditor shall (a) **discuss** the matter with management; (b) **re-evaluate** the integrity of management and evaluate the effect that this may have on the reliability of representations and audit evidence in general; and (c) **take appropriate actions**, including determining the possible effect on the opinion under SA 705.

**Disclaimer situations:** If management does not provide the written representations regarding its **responsibilities** (for the preparation of the financial statements and for the completeness of information), or if the auditor concludes there is sufficient doubt about the integrity of management such that those representations are not reliable, the auditor shall **disclaim an opinion** on the financial statements.

**Conclusion:** Written representations are necessary audit evidence but never a substitute for other substantive procedures; an inconsistency must be investigated and may cause the auditor to doubt management's integrity, and a refusal to provide required representations can lead to a modified opinion or a **disclaimer**.

*(Full-marks tip: The scoring lines are that representations are **not sufficient on their own** and **do not reduce other evidence**, plus the **disclaimer** consequence when representations about management's responsibilities are withheld or unreliable. Saying the representation lets the auditor skip testing would itself be marked wrong.)*

### Q34. Ch: Audit Evidence — Reconciliation of a debtor confirmation reply (SA 505) (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** In the audit of Sabarmati Traders Ltd. (year-end 31 March 2026), the auditor sent a **positive** confirmation to a customer, M/s Vindhya Retail. The ledger of Sabarmati shows Vindhya as a debtor of **₹6,45,000** at 31 March 2026. Vindhya's reply confirms a balance **due to Sabarmati of ₹4,90,000**. Investigation reveals the following reconciling items. Prepare a statement reconciling the two balances and state the audit conclusion.

| Ref | Reconciling item | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Goods dispatched by Sabarmati on 30 Mar, in transit and received by Vindhya on 3 Apr | 1,20,000 |
| 2 | Payment sent by Vindhya on 29 Mar, received & recorded by Sabarmati on 2 Apr | 60,000 |
| 3 | Sales return by Vindhya on 28 Mar recorded by Vindhya but debit note not yet processed by Sabarmati | 25,000 |
| 4 | Invoice wrongly posted twice by Sabarmati in its ledger | 40,000 |
| 5 | Discount allowed by Sabarmati but not recorded by Vindhya | 10,000 |

**Solution:**
The objective is to reconcile Sabarmati's book balance (₹6,45,000) with the balance confirmed by the customer (₹4,90,000), a difference of **₹1,55,000**, by classifying each item as a genuine timing/recording difference or an **error requiring adjustment** in Sabarmati's books.

**WN-1 — Analysis of each reconciling item:**
- **Item 1 (Goods in transit ₹1,20,000):** Correctly a debtor in Sabarmati's books (sale/dispatch on 30 Mar); Vindhya records the purchase only on receipt (3 Apr). **Timing difference** — no adjustment in Sabarmati's books; add to reconcile Vindhya's balance up.
- **Item 2 (Cash in transit ₹60,000):** Vindhya has reduced its balance for a payment made on 29 Mar; Sabarmati records receipt on 2 Apr. **Timing difference** — no adjustment; add to Vindhya's balance to reconcile.
- **Item 3 (Sales return ₹25,000):** A genuine return; Sabarmati has **failed to record** the debit note. **Error in Sabarmati's books** — its debtor is **overstated**; requires adjustment (reduce debtor by ₹25,000).
- **Item 4 (Double-posted invoice ₹40,000):** Sabarmati has posted the same invoice twice. **Error in Sabarmati's books** — debtor **overstated**; requires adjustment (reduce debtor by ₹40,000).
- **Item 5 (Discount ₹10,000):** Discount allowed by Sabarmati but not recorded by Vindhya. If Sabarmati has correctly recorded it, this is a difference on **Vindhya's side**; to reconcile, Vindhya's balance is understated by ₹10,000. **No adjustment** in Sabarmati's books.

**WN-2 — Statement of Reconciliation of Balances (₹):**

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Balance as per Vindhya's confirmation (credit balance due to Sabarmati) | 4,90,000 |
| *Add:* Item 1 — Goods in transit (not yet recorded by Vindhya) | 1,20,000 |
| *Add:* Item 2 — Cash in transit (already deducted by Vindhya) | 60,000 |
| *Add:* Item 5 — Discount not recorded by Vindhya | 10,000 |
| **Sub-total (Vindhya's balance adjusted to Sabarmati's basis)** | **6,80,000** |
| *Less:* Item 3 — Sales return not recorded by Sabarmati | (25,000) |
| *Less:* Item 4 — Invoice double-posted by Sabarmati | (40,000) |
| **Corrected balance = Balance as per Sabarmati's ledger after adjusting its errors** | **6,15,000** |

**Cross-check with Sabarmati's books:** Sabarmati's ledger balance ₹6,45,000 − Item 3 (₹25,000) − Item 4 (₹40,000) = **₹6,15,000**, which agrees with the reconciled figure above. **Reconciled.**

**Audit conclusion:** The confirmation has revealed **two errors in Sabarmati's books** aggregating **₹65,000** (₹25,000 + ₹40,000) by which trade receivables (and correspondingly income/discount) are **overstated**. The auditor should propose an **adjusting entry** to reduce the debtor and record the sales return, and reassess whether similar unposted debit notes/double postings exist elsewhere (i.e., extend testing). Items 1, 2 and 5 are valid **timing/other-side differences** requiring no adjustment but confirming correct cut-off.

### Q35. Ch: Audit Sampling & Analytical Procedures — Statistical vs Non-statistical Sampling (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** CA Meera is planning the substantive testing of a large volume of sales invoices. Her articled assistant argues that "statistical sampling is always superior because it is scientific, so we should never use judgemental sampling." Evaluate this statement by distinguishing the two approaches under SA 530, and state whether a non-statistical sample can ever be an "audit sample".

**Answer:** SA 530 "Audit Sampling" defines audit sampling as the application of audit procedures to less than 100% of items within a population of audit relevance such that all sampling units have a chance of selection, in order to draw a conclusion about the entire population. The Standard deliberately permits **both** statistical and non-statistical approaches, so the assistant's absolute statement is incorrect.

Under SA 530, "statistical sampling" is an approach having **two** characteristics: (a) random (or otherwise probability-based) selection of the sample; **and** (b) use of probability theory to evaluate sample results, including measurement of sampling risk. A sampling approach that does **not** have both of these characteristics is a **non-statistical** approach. Both are valid; the choice is a matter of the auditor's judgement about which is more efficient in the circumstances.

| Basis | Statistical sampling | Non-statistical (judgemental) sampling |
|---|---|---|
| Selection | Random / probability-based (every unit has a known chance) | Auditor's judgement (e.g. haphazard, block, or judgemental selection) |
| Evaluation of results | Uses probability theory; sampling risk is **measured** | Sampling risk is **not** measured, only controlled by judgement |
| Sample size | Determined by statistical formula | Determined by auditor's judgement using the same relevant factors |
| Objectivity / defensibility | More objective; results are quantifiable | More dependent on the auditor's competence and judgement |
| Cost / training | Often costlier; needs statistical skill | Simpler and often cheaper to apply |

A non-statistical sample, provided every sampling unit had a chance of selection and a conclusion is projected to the whole population, **is** an audit sample within SA 530 and is a perfectly acceptable method. What matters is that, whichever approach is used, the sample must be representative and the auditor must project the misstatements found to the population.

**Conclusion:** The assistant is wrong. Both statistical and non-statistical sampling are permitted by SA 530; statistical sampling is not inherently "superior", and a properly designed non-statistical sample is a valid audit sample.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the precise two-limb definition of "statistical sampling" (random selection **and** probability-theory evaluation) and the clear statement that SA 530 permits both. Marks are lost for merely listing "advantages/disadvantages" without citing SA 530 or without the definition.)*

### Q36. Ch: Audit Sampling & Analytical Procedures — Tolerable vs Expected Misstatement (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** While planning a substantive test of details on the "trade payables" balance, the audit senior sets a "tolerable misstatement" of ₹4,20,000 and, based on prior years, an "expected misstatement" of ₹90,000. Explain, under SA 530, what each of these two terms means and how the relationship between them influences the sample size.

**Answer:** SA 530 requires the auditor, when designing a sample, to consider the tolerable misstatement in order to address sampling risk, and to take account of the level of misstatement expected in the population.

**Tolerable misstatement** is defined by SA 530 as a monetary amount set by the auditor in respect of which the auditor seeks to obtain an appropriate level of assurance that the monetary amount set by the auditor is **not exceeded** by the actual misstatement in the population. It is the application of **performance materiality** (as defined in SA 320) to a particular sampling procedure — i.e. the maximum error the auditor is willing to accept in the population and still conclude that the audit objective has been met. Here it is ₹4,20,000.

**Expected misstatement** is the amount of misstatement the auditor **expects to find** in the population, generally estimated from prior-period results, changes in the entity's processes, and evidence from other procedures. Here it is ₹90,000.

The relationship drives the sample size as follows:

| Factor | Effect on sample size |
|---|---|
| Higher **tolerable** misstatement (auditor accepts more error) | **Smaller** sample |
| Higher **expected** misstatement (more error anticipated) | **Larger** sample |
| **Narrower gap** between tolerable and expected (₹4,20,000 vs ₹90,000 narrowing) | **Larger** sample, because less "room" is left for sampling risk |

When the expected misstatement is high, the auditor needs a larger sample so that the projected misstatement can be estimated with sufficient precision relative to the tolerable misstatement; when expected misstatement approaches tolerable misstatement, sampling may become an inefficient approach and the auditor may test 100% or focus on specific high-value items instead.

**Conclusion:** Tolerable misstatement is the *maximum error the auditor will accept* (performance materiality applied to the procedure); expected misstatement is the *error the auditor anticipates finding*. A larger expected misstatement and a smaller gap between the two both increase the required sample size.

*(Full-marks tip: State explicitly that tolerable misstatement = application of performance materiality (SA 320) to the sampling procedure — that link is what the examiner looks for. Do not confuse "tolerable misstatement" with "tolerable rate of deviation", which relates to tests of controls, not substantive tests.)*

### Q37. Ch: Audit Sampling & Analytical Procedures — Anomaly (SA 530) (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** During substantive testing of a sample of 120 purchase vouchers, CA Rohan finds a single misstatement of ₹48,000 caused by a one-time software glitch in a legacy system that was permanently decommissioned three months before the year-end. He wishes to treat it as an "anomaly" and exclude it while projecting misstatements. State the meaning of an "anomaly" under SA 530 and the conditions and consequences of such treatment.

**Answer:** SA 530 defines an **anomaly** as a misstatement or deviation that is **demonstrably not representative** of misstatements or deviations in a population. Because an anomaly, by definition, is not representative of the rest of the population, the auditor is permitted **not** to project it over the population.

However, SA 530 sets a **high threshold** before a misstatement may be treated as an anomaly. The auditor must obtain a **high degree of certainty** that the misstatement is not representative of the population. To obtain that certainty, the auditor must perform **additional audit procedures** to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence that the misstatement (a) does not affect the remainder of the population, and (b) arose from an event or condition that is genuinely isolated and one-off. Here, that means Rohan must gather evidence that the software glitch (i) affected only that specific transaction stream, (ii) related solely to the now-decommissioned legacy system, and (iii) cannot have affected transactions processed on the replacement system.

The **consequences** of anomaly treatment under SA 530 are:
1. The anomalous misstatement is **excluded when projecting** sample misstatements to the population; but
2. It is **still added back** to the projected misstatement of the rest of the population when the auditor evaluates whether the **total** misstatement in the population may exceed tolerable misstatement. In other words, an anomaly is not simply ignored — its own amount (₹48,000) still counts in the aggregate; only the *projection* of it is avoided.

**Conclusion:** Rohan may treat the ₹48,000 as an anomaly **only if** he first obtains a high degree of certainty, through additional procedures, that it is genuinely non-representative. If so, he does not project it, but he must still include the ₹48,000 itself in the total misstatement he compares against tolerable misstatement.

*(Full-marks tip: The two markers of a top answer are (i) the "high degree of certainty + additional procedures" threshold and (ii) the point that the anomalous amount is still added to projected misstatement for the overall evaluation. Weak answers wrongly say the anomaly is dropped entirely.)*

### Q38. Ch: Audit Sampling & Analytical Procedures — Factors Affecting Sample Size & Stratification (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** CA Anand is designing a substantive sample over a trade receivables population of 6,000 balances totalling ₹9 crore, in which 40 customers account for ₹5.4 crore. List the principal factors under SA 530 that increase or decrease the sample size for tests of details, and explain how "stratification" and "value-weighted selection" would improve the efficiency of his sample here.

**Answer:** SA 530 (Appendix 3) identifies the factors that influence the sample size for **tests of details**. The direction of each effect is as follows:

| Factor | Movement | Effect on sample size |
|---|---|---|
| Assessed risk of material misstatement (higher RoMM) | Increase | **Increase** |
| Extent of other substantive procedures directed at the same assertion | Increase | **Decrease** |
| Auditor's required confidence that tolerable misstatement is not exceeded | Increase | **Increase** |
| Tolerable misstatement | Increase | **Decrease** |
| Expected misstatement in the population | Increase | **Increase** |
| Stratification of the population (where appropriate) | Applied | **Decrease** |
| Number of sampling units in the population | Virtually no effect | Negligible |

**Stratification:** SA 530 states that audit efficiency may be improved if the auditor **stratifies** a population by dividing it into discrete sub-populations that have an identifying characteristic — commonly **monetary value**. This reduces the variability of items within each stratum, allowing a smaller sample size for a given level of sampling risk. Anand's population is highly skewed: 40 customers (0.67% of the count) represent ₹5.4 crore (60% of the value). He should therefore **stratify** — for example, examine the top 40 high-value balances **100%** (they are individually significant and warrant full examination) and apply audit sampling only to the residual 5,960 balances totalling ₹3.6 crore.

**Value-weighted (monetary-unit) selection:** For the residual population, SA 530 permits the auditor to select the sample so that each **rupee** in the population has a proportionate chance of selection, which automatically directs audit effort toward larger balances (where more misstatement value lies) and produces a smaller sample size than an equal-probability approach over balances.

**Conclusion:** By examining the 40 large balances in full and applying value-weighted sampling to the remainder, Anand covers 60% of the value with certainty and tests the rest efficiently, reducing overall sample size while keeping sampling risk within acceptable limits.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner wants the direction (increase/decrease) tied to each factor, plus the specific point that stratification works by reducing variability within strata and that individually significant items are usually examined 100% and excluded from the sampling population. Merely listing factors without direction earns partial marks only.)*

### Q39. Ch: Audit Sampling & Analytical Procedures — Projecting Misstatements to the Population (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** CA Kavya performs a substantive test of details on a homogeneous population of 1,000 sales invoices with a total book value of ₹50,00,000, using non-statistical sampling. She examines a sample of 100 invoices with a total book value of ₹8,00,000. The misstatements found are given below. Tolerable misstatement for the population is ₹1,80,000.

| Item | Book value (₹) | Audited value (₹) | Misstatement (₹) | Nature |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Invoice A | 25,000 | 19,000 | 6,000 | Recurring pricing error |
| Invoice B | 40,000 | 22,000 | 18,000 | Recurring pricing error |
| Invoice C | 30,000 | 24,000 | 6,000 | One-off (duplicate posting, since reversed) — anomaly |
| **Sample total misstatement** | | | **30,000** | |

Project the misstatement to the population and advise CA Kavya on the conclusion, following SA 530.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Identify and separate the anomalous misstatement.**
Invoice C (₹6,000) has been established, through additional procedures, to be a one-off duplicate posting that was reversed and is not representative of the population. Under SA 530 an **anomaly is excluded from projection** but is added back for the overall evaluation.
- Non-anomalous misstatement in the sample = ₹30,000 − ₹6,000 = **₹24,000**
- Anomalous misstatement = **₹6,000**

**WN-2 — Project the non-anomalous misstatement using the ratio method.**
Projected misstatement = (Non-anomalous sample misstatement ÷ Sample book value) × Population book value
= (₹24,000 ÷ ₹8,00,000) × ₹50,00,000
= 0.03 × ₹50,00,000 = **₹1,50,000**

**WN-3 — Compute total expected misstatement in the population.**
Total misstatement = Projected misstatement + Anomalous misstatement
= ₹1,50,000 + ₹6,000 = **₹1,56,000**

**Statement Showing Projection and Evaluation of Misstatement (SA 530)**

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| (a) Sample book value | 8,00,000 |
| (b) Total misstatement found in sample | 30,000 |
| (c) Less: Anomalous misstatement (excluded from projection) | (6,000) |
| (d) Non-anomalous misstatement to be projected | 24,000 |
| (e) Projection factor = Population BV ÷ Sample BV = 50,00,000 ÷ 8,00,000 | 6.25 |
| (f) Projected misstatement = (d) × (e) | 1,50,000 |
| (g) Add back: Anomalous misstatement | 6,000 |
| (h) **Total misstatement in population = (f) + (g)** | **1,56,000** |
| (i) Tolerable misstatement | 1,80,000 |

**OR (cross-check by average method):** Average non-anomalous misstatement per sampled invoice = ₹24,000 ÷ 100 = ₹240. Projected over 1,000 invoices = ₹240 × 1,000 = ₹2,40,000. *(Note: the two methods differ because the population value per invoice (₹5,000 avg) differs from the sample value per invoice (₹8,000 avg); the ratio method is preferred where a reliable book value exists for each item, so ₹1,50,000 is used.)*

**Evaluation (SA 530):** The total misstatement of ₹1,56,000 is **below** the tolerable misstatement of ₹1,80,000. However, because ₹1,56,000 is **close to** ₹1,80,000, the auditor cannot be reasonably confident that the actual misstatement does not exceed tolerable misstatement (sampling risk is high). SA 530 requires her, in this situation, to **reassess sampling risk** — she should extend the sample and re-perform the procedure, or perform alternative substantive procedures, before concluding.

**Answer:** Projected misstatement = ₹1,50,000; total misstatement (incl. anomaly) = **₹1,56,000**, which is below tolerable misstatement of ₹1,80,000 but uncomfortably close to it. CA Kavya should extend testing/perform additional procedures rather than conclude directly.

*(Full-marks tip: Full marks require (i) excluding the anomaly before projecting, (ii) adding it back for the overall evaluation, and (iii) the SA 530 judgement that when projected misstatement is close to tolerable misstatement the auditor must reassess sampling risk. Stopping at "1,56,000 < 1,80,000, so accept" loses the concluding mark.)*

### Q40. Ch: Audit Sampling & Analytical Procedures — Monetary Unit Sampling: Interval & Sample Size (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** CA Suresh decides to use monetary unit sampling (MUS, a value-weighted statistical method) to test the "sundry debtors" population, whose total book value is ₹75,00,000. He works at a 95% confidence level, for which the reliability factor (assuming zero expected errors) is 3.0. Tolerable misstatement is set at ₹3,75,000.

Required:
(i) Compute the sampling interval and the sample size.
(ii) The first randomly selected monetary unit falls at ₹40,000. List the first four "logical units" (debtor balances) that will be selected, given the cumulative balances below.
(iii) Recompute the sample size if the auditor now allows for an expected misstatement of ₹50,000 (expansion factor 1.6).

| Debtor | Balance (₹) | Cumulative (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| A | 60,000 | 60,000 |
| B | 30,000 | 90,000 |
| C | 1,50,000 | 2,40,000 |
| D | 90,000 | 3,30,000 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Sampling interval (zero expected error).**
Sampling interval = Tolerable misstatement ÷ Reliability factor
= ₹3,75,000 ÷ 3.0 = **₹1,25,000**

**WN-2 — Sample size (zero expected error).**
Sample size = Population book value ÷ Sampling interval
= ₹75,00,000 ÷ ₹1,25,000 = **60 sampling units (logical units)**

**WN-3 — Selection of logical units (systematic value-weighted selection).**
Starting monetary unit = ₹40,000; thereafter add the interval ₹1,25,000 each time. The debtor whose **cumulative** balance first equals or exceeds the selected monetary unit is picked.

| Selection no. | Monetary unit hit (₹) | Falls within cumulative of | Debtor selected |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 40,000 | ≤ 60,000 | **A** |
| 2 | 40,000 + 1,25,000 = 1,65,000 | 90,001 – 2,40,000 | **C** |
| 3 | 1,65,000 + 1,25,000 = 2,90,000 | 2,40,001 – 3,30,000 | **D** |
| 4 | 2,90,000 + 1,25,000 = 4,15,000 | next stratum (> 3,30,000) | (next debtor beyond D) |

Note that debtor **B** (₹30,000) is **not** selected because no monetary unit fell in its cumulative band 60,001–90,000 — this is the hallmark of MUS: **larger balances have a proportionately higher chance of selection**, and any single balance equal to or larger than the interval (e.g. debtor C at ₹1,50,000 > ₹1,25,000) is **certain** to be selected.

**WN-4 — Sample size allowing for expected misstatement.**
When expected misstatement is anticipated, the interval is reduced:
Adjusted interval = (Tolerable misstatement − (Expected misstatement × Expansion factor)) ÷ Reliability factor
= (₹3,75,000 − (₹50,000 × 1.6)) ÷ 3.0
= (₹3,75,000 − ₹80,000) ÷ 3.0
= ₹2,95,000 ÷ 3.0 = ₹98,333 (interval)
Sample size = ₹75,00,000 ÷ ₹98,333 = 76.27 → **77 logical units** (round **up**).

**Statement Showing MUS Sample Design**

| Particulars | Zero expected error | With expected misstatement |
|---|---|---|
| Population book value (₹) | 75,00,000 | 75,00,000 |
| Tolerable misstatement (₹) | 3,75,000 | 3,75,000 |
| Less: Expected misstatement × expansion factor (₹) | — | (80,000) |
| Reliability factor (95%) | 3.0 | 3.0 |
| Sampling interval (₹) | 1,25,000 | 98,333 |
| **Sample size (units)** | **60** | **77** |

**Answer:** (i) Sampling interval = ₹1,25,000; sample size = **60** units. (ii) The first three logical units selected are debtors **A, C and D** (B is skipped; C is a certainty item). (iii) Allowing ₹50,000 expected misstatement, the sample size rises to **77** units — anticipating error always increases the required sample.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the correct formulae (interval = TM ÷ reliability factor; sample size = BV ÷ interval) and the insight that MUS is value-weighted so any item ≥ interval is a certainty selection and small balances may be skipped. Always round the sample size UP. Forgetting the expansion factor in part (iii) is the common deduction.)*

### Q41. Ch: Audit Sampling & Analytical Procedures — Reliability of Substantive Analytical Procedures (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** CA Nikhil wishes to substantiate the "power and fuel expense" figure of a manufacturing company largely through a substantive analytical procedure rather than tests of details. Under SA 520, what must he do before he can rely on that analytical procedure, and what factors determine whether the procedure gives sufficient assurance?

**Answer:** SA 520 "Analytical Procedures" governs the use of substantive analytical procedures. When designing and performing such a procedure, either alone or in combination with tests of details, as a substantive procedure under SA 330, the auditor is required to do the following four things:

1. **Determine suitability** — Determine the suitability of the particular substantive analytical procedure for given assertions, taking account of the assessed risk of material misstatement and any tests of details for those assertions. Analytical procedures are generally more suitable for large volumes of **predictable** transactions over time (power and fuel, which correlates with production volume, is a good candidate).

### Q43. Ch: Audit Sampling & Analytical Procedures — Analytical Procedures: Risk Assessment vs Overall Review (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** The audit manager tells a trainee that "analytical procedures are used only near the end of the audit as a substantive test." Correct this statement by explaining the **three** distinct purposes for which analytical procedures are used across the audit, and identify which of these is **mandatory**.

**Answer:** The manager's statement is incorrect. Analytical procedures — evaluations of financial information through analysis of plausible relationships among both financial and non-financial data — are used at **three** stages of the audit, governed by different Standards.

| Stage | Standard | Purpose | Mandatory? |
|---|---|---|---|
| (1) Planning / risk assessment | SA 315 | As **risk assessment procedures** to obtain an understanding of the entity and its environment and to identify areas of potential risk of material misstatement (e.g. unusual ratios/trends flag risk) | **Yes** — SA 315 requires them |
| (2) Substantive testing | SA 520 | As **substantive analytical procedures** to obtain audit evidence about particular assertions, alone or with tests of details | Optional — at the auditor's discretion |
| (3) Overall / final review | SA 520 | Near the end of the audit, to form an **overall conclusion** on whether the financial statements are consistent with the auditor's understanding of the entity | **Yes** — SA 520 requires them |

Thus analytical procedures are **compulsory at two points**: (a) at the **risk-assessment stage** under SA 315, and (b) at the **overall-review stage** under SA 520 near the end of the audit. Their use as a **substantive** procedure in the middle of the audit is **optional**, depending on the auditor's judgement about efficiency and the nature of the assertion.

At the overall-review stage, if analytical procedures identify a previously unrecognised risk of material misstatement, SA 520 requires the auditor to **revise** the assessment of the risks of material misstatement and modify the further planned audit procedures accordingly.

**Conclusion:** The manager is wrong. Analytical procedures serve three purposes — risk assessment (SA 315, mandatory), substantive testing (SA 520, optional), and overall final review (SA 520, mandatory). They are far more than an end-of-audit substantive tool.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner looks for the SA 315 vs SA 520 split and, above all, that analytical procedures are **mandatory** at both the risk-assessment and the final-review stages but **optional** as a substantive test. Note the SA 520 rule to revise risk assessment if the final review throws up new risks.)*

### Q44. Ch: Audit of Items of Financial Statements — Auditing a Provision (AS 29) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** A company is defending a customer's damages suit of ₹60,00,000 filed during the year. Its legal counsel opines it is "probable" the company will have to pay, and the best estimate of the outflow is ₹22,00,000. The company has recognised a provision of ₹22,00,000 in the balance sheet. As the auditor, state the recognition criteria you would verify under AS 29 and the specific audit procedures you would perform to substantiate this provision.

**Answer:** A provision is a liability of uncertain timing or amount, and its recognition is governed by **AS 29 "Provisions, Contingent Liabilities and Contingent Assets"**. The auditor's task is to verify that all three recognition conditions of AS 29 are satisfied and that the amount is a proper best estimate.

**Recognition criteria to verify (AS 29):** A provision must be recognised when, and only when:
1. the entity has a **present obligation** (legal or constructive) as a result of a **past event** — here, the past event is the act giving rise to the customer's suit, and the obligation is the legal claim against the company;
2. it is **probable** (i.e. more likely than not) that an **outflow of resources** embodying economic benefits will be required to settle the obligation — confirmed by counsel's opinion that payment is "probable"; and
3. a **reliable estimate** can be made of the amount of the obligation — here ₹22,00,000, being the best estimate of the expenditure required to settle the present obligation at the balance-sheet date.

Since all three conditions are met, recognition of the ₹22,00,000 provision (not the ₹60,00,000 claimed) is **correct**; AS 29 requires the provision to be the **best estimate**, not the maximum possible amount.

**Audit procedures to substantiate the provision:**
- Obtain the entity's **schedule of litigation** and read the plaint, the company's reply, and correspondence to understand the nature and stage of the suit (**valuation and completeness** assertions).
- Send an **external confirmation / letter of enquiry to the entity's legal counsel** (in line with SA 505 and SA 501 on litigation and claims), corroborating the "probable" assessment and the ₹22,00,000 estimate.
- Verify that the **best estimate** reflects the risks and uncertainties and, where the effect of the time value of money is material, whether **discounting** is appropriate.
- Obtain a **written representation** from management (SA 580) that all known actual or possible litigation and claims have been disclosed and accounted for.
- Perform **subsequent-events procedures** (SA 560) up to the date of the audit report — e.g. a court order or settlement after year-end is an **adjusting event** confirming the condition existing at the balance-sheet date and may require the estimate to be revised.
- Verify the **disclosure** required by AS 29 (nature, movement in the provision, and uncertainties) in the notes.

**Conclusion:** As all three AS 29 conditions (present obligation from a past event, probable outflow, reliable estimate) are met, the ₹22,00,000 provision is correctly recognised; the auditor substantiates it primarily through legal confirmation, review of the best estimate, subsequent-events review, and management representation.

*(Full-marks tip: Recite the three AS 29 conditions precisely, and make the point that a provision is the **best estimate**, not the amount claimed (₹22 lakh, not ₹60 lakh). Linking to SA 501/SA 505 (legal confirmation), SA 560 (subsequent events) and SA 580 (representations) earns the procedure marks.)*

### Q45. Ch: Audit of Items of Financial Statements — Contingent Liabilities: Recognition vs Disclosure (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** During the audit of Zenith Ltd for FY 2025-26, you come across three matters:
(a) A ₹35,00,000 income-tax demand under appeal; the company's counsel assesses the chance of losing as "possible but not probable".
(b) A guarantee of ₹80,00,000 given to a bank for a subsidiary's loan; the subsidiary is currently servicing the loan normally.
(c) A supplier's ₹12,00,000 claim which the company's counsel says has "only a remote chance" of succeeding.

For each, state whether the item is a provision, a contingent liability requiring disclosure, or ignored, under AS 29, and how you as auditor would deal with it.

**Answer:** Under **AS 29**, the treatment of a possible obligation depends on the probability of the outflow of economic benefits, as summarised below:

| Likelihood of outflow | AS 29 treatment |
|---|---|
| **Probable** (more likely than not) and reliably estimable | **Recognise a provision** in the balance sheet |
| **Possible**, or present obligation but outflow not probable / not reliably estimable | **Disclose** as a **contingent liability** in the notes |
| **Remote** | **No provision and no disclosure** (ignore) |

**Item (a) — Income-tax demand of ₹35,00,000, outflow "possible but not probable":** Because the outflow is only **possible** and not probable, AS 29 prohibits recognition of a provision. It is a **contingent liability** to be **disclosed** in the notes (nature of the demand, amount, and the uncertainty). *Auditor's action:* verify the demand order and appeal papers, obtain legal confirmation of the "possible" assessment, and check that the disclosure is complete and accurate.

**Item (b) — Corporate guarantee of ₹80,00,000, subsidiary servicing normally:** A guarantee creates a **possible obligation** that will crystallise only if the subsidiary defaults — presently not probable, as the loan is being serviced. It is therefore a **contingent liability** requiring **disclosure** of ₹80,00,000 in the notes. *Auditor's action:* inspect the guarantee deed, confirm the outstanding loan balance with the bank, assess the subsidiary's ability to service the loan, and verify disclosure. If, on evidence, the subsidiary's default became **probable**, the item would be reassessed for a **provision**.

**Item (c) — Supplier's claim of ₹12,00,000, "remote" chance:** As the possibility of an outflow is **remote**, AS 29 requires **neither a provision nor a disclosure** — the item is ignored in the financial statements. *Auditor's action:* obtain the legal opinion supporting the "remote" assessment and document it, and ensure the assessment is not being used to avoid legitimate disclosure.

**Conclusion:** (a) disclose as contingent liability ₹35,00,000; (b) disclose as contingent liability ₹80,00,000; (c) no provision and no disclosure (remote). The auditor corroborates each probability assessment with legal evidence, tests completeness (that no contingency has been omitted), and verifies note disclosures.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner wants the clean AS 29 three-way test — probable → provide; possible → disclose; remote → ignore — applied item-by-item with a reason. Emphasise the auditor's completeness concern (undisclosed contingencies) and the need for legal confirmation of each assessment.)*

### Q50. Ch: Audit of Items of Financial Statements — Revenue Recognition & Cut-off (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** During the audit of Polaris Ltd (year-end 31 March 2026), you notice the following near the year-end. For each, state the correct treatment for revenue and the audit assertion involved, citing AS 9 and the relevant SA.
(a) Goods costing ₹6,00,000 (invoiced at ₹8,00,000) were dispatched on 30 March 2026 but the sale invoice was raised and recorded on 2 April 2026.
(b) Goods invoiced and recorded as a sale of ₹15,00,000 on 31 March 2026 were dispatched only on 4 April 2026; the customer had not yet approved delivery.
(c) An advance of ₹5,00,000 received on 28 March 2026 against goods to be manufactured and delivered in May 2026 was credited to "Sales".

**Answer:** Revenue recognition is governed by **AS 9 "Revenue Recognition"**, under which revenue from the sale of goods is recognised when the seller has **transferred the property in the goods** (significant risks and rewards of ownership) to the buyer for a consideration, **no significant uncertainty** exists regarding the amount of consideration, and it is reasonable to expect **ultimate collection**. The relevant assertions are **occurrence** and **cut-off**, tested through **cut-off procedures** (part of substantive testing under SA 330; sales cut-off links dispatch/GRN dates to the recording period).

**(a) Dispatched 30 March, invoice recorded 2 April:** The risks and rewards passed on **dispatch (30 March 2026)**, which falls in the year under audit. Under AS 9 the sale should be recognised in **FY 2025-26**, irrespective of the invoice date. Recording it in the next year **understates** current-year revenue and **overstates** closing inventory (the goods would wrongly remain in stock). *Assertion: completeness / cut-off.* The auditor should require the ₹8,00,000 sale to be recorded in FY 2025-26 and the goods excluded from closing inventory.

**(b) Invoiced 31 March, dispatched 4 April, delivery not approved:** The risks and rewards had **not** been transferred at year-end — the goods were neither dispatched nor approved. Under AS 9 revenue must **not** be recognised in FY 2025-26; recording ₹15,00,000 as a sale **overstates** revenue and receivables and **understates** closing inventory. *Assertion: occurrence / cut-off.* The auditor should require the ₹15,00,000 to be **reversed** and the goods (at cost) **included in closing inventory** as at 31 March 2026. This is a classic **cut-off overstatement / window dressing** concern (also alert to fraud risk, SA 240).

**(c) Advance of ₹5,00,000 credited to Sales:** No goods have been delivered and the property has not passed; under AS 9 this is an **advance from a customer**, a **liability**, not revenue. *Assertion: occurrence.* The auditor should require the ₹5,00,000 to be **de-recognised from Sales** and shown as an **advance received / current liability** until the goods are delivered in May 2026.

**Conclusion:** (a) Recognise ₹8,00,000 as sale in FY 2025-26 (do not defer); (b) reverse ₹15,00,000 — not a sale at year-end — and include the goods in closing stock; (c) reclassify ₹5,00,000 from Sales to advances (liability). Net effect: current-year sales should be **decreased by ₹12,00,000** (−15,00,000 − 5,00,000 + 8,00,000) after all three corrections, with corresponding inventory and liability adjustments.

### Q52. Ch: Audit in an Automated Environment — Data Integrity in an ERP (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** "Nirvana Textiles Ltd." migrated from a manual bookkeeping system to an integrated ERP during the year. The engagement partner is concerned that, in an automated environment, the *integrity of data* residing in the system cannot simply be presumed. Explain what is meant by "data integrity" in the context of an automated environment, list the principal risks that threaten it, and state how these risks bear upon the auditor's overall audit approach.

**Answer:**
Data integrity, in the context of an automated environment, refers to the accuracy, completeness, consistency and reliability of data throughout its life-cycle — from the point of input, through processing and storage, to output and reporting — such that the information produced by the system faithfully reflects the underlying business transactions. Because in an automated environment the financial statements are the end-product of data that flows through the IT system, the auditor obtains audit evidence about the financial information only to the extent that the underlying data possesses integrity.

The principal risks that threaten data integrity are the following. First, there is the risk of **unauthorised access**, where a person without proper authority views, initiates or alters data, defeating segregation of duties. Second, there is the risk of **unauthorised changes to data or to master data** (such as vendor bank accounts, price masters or credit limits), which can systematically distort every transaction that references that master. Third, there is the risk of **data loss or incomplete processing**, where transactions are dropped, duplicated or only partially posted during interfaces between systems. Fourth, there is the risk of **systematic error** — because a computer processes every transaction using the same programmed logic, a single erroneous configuration or formula produces a consistent, repeated misstatement across the whole population, unlike a manual error which is usually random. Fifth, there is the risk of an **inadequate or absent audit trail**, so that changes cannot be traced back to a source and an authoriser.

These risks bear directly on the auditor's approach. Since automated systems process transactions uniformly, the auditor is able, where controls are found to be effective, to place greater reliance on **automated controls and a controls-based (test of controls) strategy**, and to use computer-assisted audit techniques on the entire population rather than on a sample. Conversely, if the general IT controls that underpin data integrity — access security, change management, and IT operations — are found to be weak, the auditor cannot rely on the automated application controls, must treat the data as unreliable, and must respond with more extensive **substantive procedures** and, where appropriate, re-evaluate the assessed risk of material misstatement.

**Conclusion:** In an automated environment the reliability of the financial statements is only as strong as the integrity of the underlying data; the auditor must therefore understand the risks to data integrity and calibrate the audit strategy — reliance on controls versus extended substantive testing — accordingly.

### Q53. Ch: Audit in an Automated Environment — Audit Trail (Rule 3, proviso) (Marks: 4) [Application]
**Question:** For the financial year 2024-25, "Sunrise Ceramics Pvt. Ltd." maintained its books of account using accounting software. During the audit it emerged that (i) the audit trail (edit log) feature existed in the software but had been **kept switched off** for the first four months of the year, and (ii) even where the log was on, the log itself could be edited by the IT administrator. As the statutory auditor, state your reporting responsibility regarding the audit trail and how you would deal with each of the two facts.

**Answer:**
The statutory basis is the proviso to Rule 3(1) of the Companies (Accounts) Rules, 2014, which requires every company that uses accounting software for maintaining its books of account to use software that has a feature of recording an **audit trail (edit log)** of each and every transaction, creating an edit log of each change made in the books, together with the date on which such changes were made, and that ensures the audit trail **cannot be disabled**. Correspondingly, Rule 11(g) of the Companies (Audit and Auditors) Rules, 2014 casts a duty on the auditor to report, in the auditor's report, whether the company has used such software and whether the audit trail feature was operated throughout the year for all transactions recorded in the software, whether it was tampered with, and whether it was preserved as per the statutory retention period.

Applying this to the two facts:

**Fact (i) — audit trail switched off for four months.** The requirement is that the audit trail operate *throughout* the year for *all* transactions. Because the feature was disabled for the first four months, the condition is not met for that period. The auditor must state this fact in the report under Rule 11(g), specifying that the audit trail feature was not enabled/operated for the said period. The auditor should also consider whether the absence of the audit trail for that period gives rise to a limitation on the audit evidence available for those transactions and respond with additional substantive procedures.

**Fact (ii) — the log itself is editable by the administrator.** The Rule requires that the audit trail *cannot be disabled or tampered with*. Where the edit log can itself be altered by a user, the integrity of the audit trail is compromised and the auditor is unable to conclude that the audit trail was not tampered with. This too must be reported under Rule 11(g), and the auditor should evaluate the effect of this deficiency on the reliability of the records as a whole.

**Conclusion:** The auditor must report both exceptions under Rule 11(g) — that the audit trail was not operated for four months and that the log was capable of being altered — and factor the resulting limitation into the design of substantive procedures.

### Q54. Ch: Audit in an Automated Environment — Continuous Auditing (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** The audit committee of "Metro Retail Ltd.", a company with more than 900 point-of-sale terminals feeding a central system in real time, has asked whether the concept of "continuous auditing" could be adopted. Explain the concept of continuous auditing, distinguish it from a traditional periodic audit, and state the pre-conditions that must exist in the entity's environment for continuous auditing to be effective.

**Answer:**
Continuous auditing is an approach that enables the auditor to perform audit-related activities — the collection of audit evidence and the testing of controls and transactions — on a **continuous or near-real-time basis**, rather than at discrete points after the period has closed. It relies on automated routines, embedded audit modules and analytics that examine transactions as, or shortly after, they occur, and that raise exceptions when a transaction or a control breaches a pre-defined rule.

The distinction from a traditional periodic audit may be drawn as follows.

| Basis | Traditional periodic audit | Continuous auditing |
|---|---|---|
| Timing | Performed after the period-end on historical data | Performed continuously / in near real time on current data |
| Coverage | Sample-based testing of selected transactions | Automated testing of the full population |
| Detection of exceptions | Detected long after the event, when correction is costly | Detected promptly, allowing timely correction |
| Nature of effort | Largely manual, point-in-time | Automated, technology-driven, recurring |
| Dependence on IT | Moderate | High — depends on system integration and reliable data feeds |

For continuous auditing to be effective, several pre-conditions must exist in the entity's environment. There must be a **high degree of automation and system integration**, so that transaction data is available electronically and in a consistent, structured form. The **general IT controls must be strong**, particularly access security and change management, because continuous auditing draws directly on system data and the auditor must be able to trust that data. There must be **reliable, well-defined rules and parameters** against which transactions can be tested, and a mechanism to capture and follow up exceptions. Finally, adequate **infrastructure and tools** (embedded audit modules, analytics, dashboards) and the requisite skills must be available to run and interpret the routines.

**Conclusion:** Continuous auditing shifts the audit from periodic, sample-based, backward-looking testing to automated, population-wide, near-real-time assurance; it is well-suited to a high-volume, fully automated environment such as Metro Retail Ltd., but only where system integration and general IT controls are sufficiently robust.

### Q56. Ch: Audit in an Automated Environment — CAATs / Data Analytics (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** As auditor of "Vertex Pharma Ltd.", you decide to use Computer-Assisted Audit Techniques (CAATs) and data analytics on the complete population of the year's sales and receivables data (over 4,00,000 invoices). Also state two pre-requisites you must satisfy before relying on the results.

**Answer:**
Because a computer processes the entire population uniformly, CAATs allow the auditor to test 100% of the transactions rather than a sample, thereby directly examining the population for anomalies. Five specific tests over the sales and receivables data, with the assertion or risk each addresses, are as follows.

First, a **gap and duplicate test on invoice numbers** — identifying missing serial numbers and duplicated invoice numbers — which addresses the *completeness* of recorded sales and the risk of duplicate billing or omitted invoices.

Second, the auditor must be satisfied that the **general IT controls (access and change management) over the source system are effective**, so that the data drawn from it has integrity, and must ensure the extraction is done in a controlled, repeatable manner.

**Conclusion:** CAATs enable population-wide, assertion-focused testing of the sales and receivables data; however, their results are dependable only where the completeness and accuracy of the data and the integrity of the source system have first been established.

### Q57. Ch: Audit in an Automated Environment — Impact of IT on the Audit (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** "The use of an automated environment by an entity does not change the fundamental objective of an audit, but it does change *how* the auditor obtains assurance." In the light of this statement, explain how the existence of an automated environment affects the auditor's understanding of the entity and the assessment of risk of material misstatement.

**Answer:**
It is correct that the overall objective of the audit — to obtain reasonable assurance about whether the financial statements as a whole are free from material misstatement and to express an opinion thereon — remains unchanged whether the entity operates manually or in an automated environment. What changes is the *manner* in which the auditor obtains that assurance, because in an automated environment transactions are initiated, recorded, processed and reported by IT systems.

First, the auditor's **understanding of the entity and its environment** must extend to the IT environment. The auditor must understand the applications, databases, operating systems and networks that are relevant to financial reporting, the flow of transactions through those systems, the automated and IT-dependent controls in place, and the general IT controls that support them. Without this understanding the auditor cannot identify where misstatements might arise.

Second, the **assessment of the risk of material misstatement** is affected because the automated environment introduces its own risks — unauthorised access, unauthorised changes to data and programs, over-reliance on systems that may process erroneous data, potential loss of data and the possibility of **systematic (pervasive) error**, whereby a single flaw in programmed logic misstates the entire population consistently. These are risks that would not arise, or would arise differently, in a manual system. The auditor must factor them into the identification and assessment of risks at both the financial-statement level and the assertion level.

Third, the **audit strategy and response** are shaped accordingly: where controls (including general IT controls) are strong, the auditor may adopt a controls-reliance approach and use CAATs on the full population; where they are weak, the auditor must extend substantive procedures.

**Conclusion:** The automated environment leaves the objective of the audit intact but reshapes the auditor's risk assessment and evidence-gathering — the auditor must understand the IT systems, respond to IT-specific risks such as systematic error, and calibrate reliance on controls accordingly.

### Q58. Ch: Audit in an Automated Environment — Testing Automated Controls / Benchmarking (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** In the audit of "Zephyr Appliances Ltd." you have concluded that a particular automated application control — the system's three-way match of purchase order, goods receipt and invoice before an invoice can be posted — operated effectively when tested. The audit manager proposes that, because it is a fully automated control, it need not be re-tested in detail every year. Explain the concept the manager is relying on, the conditions that must be satisfied before it can be applied, and what the auditor must still do each year.

**Answer:**
The concept the manager is relying on is **"benchmarking" of automated application controls**. The logic is that an automated control, being performed entirely by the system using programmed logic, will operate **consistently** — it processes every transaction the same way — until the underlying program or its configuration is changed. Consequently, once the auditor has tested that an automated control is operating effectively, the auditor may, in subsequent periods, place reliance on that earlier test result without re-performing a detailed test of the control every year, provided certain conditions are met.

The conditions that must be satisfied before benchmarking can be applied are the following. The **general IT controls, particularly change management and access security, must be effective**, so that the auditor can be satisfied that the control (the program) has not been altered without authorisation since it was last tested. The control must be one that is **not subject to significant change** and does not depend on volatile parameters. The auditor must have **evidence of the effective operation of the general IT controls** throughout the intervening period. The auditor must also consider the risk associated with the control — the greater the risk, the shorter the period for which reliance on the earlier test can be extended.

Even where benchmarking is applied, the auditor must each year: (i) confirm and test that the **relevant general IT controls (change management and access) remained effective** throughout the period; (ii) enquire into and confirm that **no changes were made** to the automated control since it was last tested, and inspect the change logs to corroborate this; and (iii) periodically **re-establish the benchmark by re-testing the automated control in detail**, and re-test immediately if any change to the control is identified.

**Conclusion:** The manager is correctly relying on benchmarking, which permits reduced annual testing of a stable, fully automated control — but only where general IT controls are effective; each year the auditor must still verify that change-management controls held and that the control was not altered, and must periodically re-baseline the control.

*(Full-marks tip: The pivotal condition is effective change management — reliance on benchmarking collapses if program changes are not controlled. Candidates who present benchmarking as "test once, never test again" lose marks for omitting the annual ITGC verification and periodic re-baselining.)*

### Q59. Ch: Audit Documentation & Quality — Nature & Purpose of Documentation (SA 230) (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the meaning of "audit documentation" as defined in SA 230 and explain any four purposes that audit documentation serves, other than supporting the auditor's opinion.

**Answer:**
As defined in SA 230, "Audit Documentation", audit documentation means the record of audit procedures performed, relevant audit evidence obtained, and conclusions the auditor reached (terms such as "working papers" or "work papers" are also used). It is the tangible record that demonstrates the basis for the auditor's conclusions and the compliance of the audit with the Standards on Auditing and with applicable legal and regulatory requirements.

While the primary purpose of audit documentation is to provide evidence of the basis for the auditor's conclusion about the achievement of the overall objectives and evidence that the audit was planned and performed in accordance with the SAs, SA 230 recognises that documentation serves several **additional purposes**, four of which are as follows.

First, it **assists the engagement team to plan and perform the audit** — the record of prior and current work directs and coordinates the team's efforts. Second, it enables **members of the team responsible for supervision to direct and supervise the audit work and to discharge their review responsibilities** under SA 220. Third, it enables the team to be **accountable for its work**, providing a record that can be referred to when the quality of the work is questioned. Fourth, it enables the conduct of **external inspections and peer/quality reviews and regulatory inspections**, and retains a record of matters of continuing significance to future audits of the same entity. (Documentation also assists in the conduct of quality control reviews and engagement quality control reviews under SQC 1.)

**Conclusion:** Audit documentation is the auditor's record of procedures, evidence and conclusions; beyond supporting the opinion, it serves to plan and perform the audit, enable direction, supervision and review, establish accountability, and permit external inspections and quality/peer reviews.

### Q60. Ch: Audit Documentation & Quality — Sufficiency (Experienced Auditor Test) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** SA 230 requires the auditor to prepare documentation that is sufficient to enable an "experienced auditor, having no previous connection with the audit," to understand the work done. Explain this requirement, state the three matters such an experienced auditor should be able to understand from the documentation, and explain why documentation prepared "after the event" or purely from memory is not acceptable.

**Answer:**
SA 230 requires the auditor to prepare audit documentation that is sufficient to enable an **experienced auditor, having no previous connection with the audit, to understand** the work performed. An "experienced auditor" for this purpose is an individual (whether internal or external to the firm) who has practical audit experience and a reasonable understanding of audit processes, the Standards on Auditing, the business environment in which the entity operates, and the auditing and financial reporting issues relevant to the entity's industry. This is an **objective test** of sufficiency — the documentation must stand on its own and communicate the work to a competent professional who was not part of the engagement.

The three matters such an experienced auditor should be able to understand from the documentation are:
(a) the **nature, timing and extent** of the audit procedures performed to comply with the SAs and applicable legal and regulatory requirements;
(b) the **results of the audit procedures performed and the audit evidence obtained**; and
(c) **significant matters** arising during the audit, the conclusions reached thereon, and significant professional judgements made in reaching those conclusions.

Documentation prepared "after the event" or purely from memory is not acceptable for two related reasons. First, SA 230 requires documentation to be prepared on a **timely basis**, because preparing it after the audit work has been performed reduces its reliability — the record is more likely to be accurate and to reflect the actual work when it is compiled contemporaneously, and delay increases the risk of matters being forgotten or reconstructed. Second, documentation compiled from memory may not faithfully record what was actually done and the judgements actually made at the time; it therefore fails to provide reliable evidence that the audit was properly planned and performed, defeating the very purpose of documentation.

**Conclusion:** Sufficiency under SA 230 is measured against the objective standard of an experienced auditor with no prior connection to the engagement, who must be able to understand the procedures, the results and the significant judgements; contemporaneous preparation is essential, and after-the-fact or memory-based documentation does not meet the Standard.

*(Full-marks tip: State that the experienced-auditor test is *objective* and list all three matters (a)–(c). Link the rejection of "after-the-event" documentation to the *timely basis* requirement — that connection is what the examiner looks for.)*

### Q61. Ch: Audit Documentation & Quality — Assembly of the Final Audit File & Retention (SA 230) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** The audit of "Highland Foods Ltd." for FY 2024-25 is complete; the auditor's report is dated 20 May 2025. During assembly of the audit file, the audit senior does the following. State, with reference to SA 230 and SQC 1, whether each action is permissible and the relevant time limits.

| No. | Action taken by the audit senior |
|---|---|
| 1 | Completes the administrative assembly of the final audit file by 30 August 2025 |
| 2 | On 10 September 2025, deletes some superseded draft working papers and inserts cross-references |
| 3 | On 12 September 2025, after learning of a new fact, performs a new audit procedure and adds a working paper, recording who did it, when and why |
| 4 | Decides the file will be retained for 5 years from the date of the auditor's report |

**Answer:**
The relevant framework is SA 230 read with SQC 1. SQC 1 requires the firm to establish policies and procedures for the **timely completion of the assembly of final audit files**, and SA 230 states that an appropriate time limit within which to complete the assembly of the final audit file is **ordinarily not more than 60 days after the date of the auditor's report**. The completion of the assembly of the final audit file after the date of the auditor's report is an **administrative process that does not involve the performance of new audit procedures or the drawing of new conclusions**. After the assembly of the final audit file has been completed, the auditor **shall not delete or discard audit documentation of any nature before the end of its retention period**. SQC 1 requires the retention period for audit engagements to be **ordinarily no shorter than seven years** from the date of the auditor's report (or the group auditor's report, if later).

Applying this to each action:

**No. 1 — assembly completed by 30 August 2025.** The auditor's report is dated 20 May 2025; 60 days therefrom is **19 July 2025**. Assembly completed on 30 August 2025 is **beyond the 60-day limit** and is therefore **not in compliance** with the ordinary time limit contemplated by SA 230/SQC 1.

**No. 2 — deleting superseded drafts and inserting cross-references on 10 September 2025.** During the assembly period, changes that are administrative in nature — such as deleting or discarding superseded documentation, sorting, collating and cross-referencing working papers, and signing off on completion checklists — are **permitted**. However, this must be done *during* the assembly period. Since assembly on these facts ought to have been completed by 19 July 2025, deletion on 10 September 2025 (after the assembly should have been completed) is **not permissible**, because after completion of assembly documentation may not be deleted before the end of the retention period.

**No. 3 — new procedure and new working paper on 12 September 2025, with who/when/why recorded.** SA 230 permits the auditor, in circumstances where it becomes necessary to **modify existing or add new audit documentation after assembly** (for example, because of a new fact), to do so provided the auditor documents (a) the specific reasons for making the changes, and (b) when and by whom they were made and reviewed. As the senior has recorded who did it, when and why, this action is **permissible**.

**No. 4 — retention for 5 years.** SQC 1 requires a retention period of **ordinarily not less than seven years** from the date of the auditor's report. A retention period of only 5 years is **not compliant**; the file must be retained for at least seven years.

**Conclusion:** Actions 1, 2 and 4 do **not** comply (assembly exceeded 60 days; deletion occurred after the assembly window; retention period is short of seven years), while Action 3 **is** permissible because the post-assembly addition was properly documented as to reason, timing and identity.

*(Full-marks tip: Quote the two numbers precisely — 60 days for assembly (SA 230) and 7 years for retention (SQC 1) — and compute the actual 19 July 2025 deadline from the report date. The subtle trap is Action 2: deletion is fine *during* assembly but not after it; earn the mark by making that distinction.)*

### Q62. Ch: Audit Documentation & Quality — Documenting Departure from a Relevant Requirement (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** In rare circumstances an auditor may judge it necessary to depart from a relevant requirement of a Standard on Auditing. Explain the documentation obligation cast by SA 230 in such a case, and separately explain the documentation required where the auditor performs new or additional audit procedures, or draws new conclusions, *after the date of the auditor's report*.

**Answer:**
**Departure from a relevant requirement.** SA 230 recognises that the Standards on Auditing contain, in exceptional and rare circumstances, situations in which the auditor may judge it necessary to depart from a relevant *requirement* of a Standard. In such a case, SA 230 requires the auditor to document **how the alternative audit procedures performed achieve the aim of that requirement, and the reasons for the departure**. The obligation is available only where the relevant requirement is conditional and the condition does not exist, or where the requirement, in the specific circumstances of the audit, would not be effective in achieving its aim; it is not a licence to disregard requirements at will. The documentation must make clear both *why* the departure was necessary and *how* the aim of the requirement was nonetheless met by the alternative procedures.

**Procedures or conclusions after the date of the auditor's report.** SA 230 deals separately with the situation where, after the date of the auditor's report, the auditor performs new or additional audit procedures or draws new conclusions — for example, on becoming aware of facts that existed at the date of the report which might have affected it (as contemplated by SA 560, Subsequent Events). In such circumstances the auditor is required to document:
(a) the **circumstances encountered** — that is, what gave rise to the new work;
(b) the **new or additional audit procedures performed, audit evidence obtained, and conclusions reached**, and their effect on the auditor's report; and
(c) **when and by whom** the resulting changes to audit documentation were made and reviewed.

**Conclusion:** SA 230 imposes a heightened documentation discipline in both situations — for a departure, the auditor must record the reasons and how the aim of the requirement was met by alternative procedures; and for post-report work, the auditor must record the circumstances, the new procedures/evidence/conclusions and their effect, and the who/when of the changes.

*(Full-marks tip: Keep the two obligations distinct — the "departure" documentation (reasons + how the aim was achieved) is different from the "after-report" documentation (circumstances + new procedures + who/when). Merging them is the common error.)*

### Q63. Ch: Audit Documentation & Quality — Ownership & Confidentiality of Working Papers (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** A client company, on termination of the audit engagement, demands that the retiring auditor hand over all the audit working papers relating to the audit, contending that since the client paid the audit fee, the papers belong to it. Discuss the correct position regarding ownership and confidentiality of audit working papers, and state the auditor's obligation as to retention.

**Answer:**
The position on ownership is well settled. Under SA 230 and the guidance of the Institute of Chartered Accountants of India, **audit working papers are the property of the auditor**. Although the client may request access to portions of the documentation, the auditor is under no obligation to surrender the working papers to the client, and may, at the auditor's discretion, make portions of, or extracts from, the documentation available to the client, provided such disclosure does not undermine the independence or validity of the audit process. The fact that the client has paid the audit fee does not make the client the owner of the auditor's records; the fee is consideration for the professional service, not for the working papers, which are the auditor's evidence of the work performed.

As to **confidentiality**, the auditor is bound by the duty of confidentiality (reflected in the Code of Ethics and in SQC 1 / SA 230) not to disclose the information in the working papers to third parties without the client's consent, except where there is a legal or professional right or duty to disclose. The firm must establish controls designed to maintain the **confidentiality, safe custody, integrity, accessibility and retrievability** of documentation, and, where the documentation is in electronic form, appropriate controls to prevent unauthorised access or alteration.

As to **retention**, the auditor must retain the working papers for the period required by SQC 1 — a retention period **ordinarily not shorter than seven years** from the date of the auditor's report — and must not part with or destroy them before the end of that period, since they may be needed for peer review, quality review, regulatory inspection or the auditor's own defence.

**Conclusion:** The working papers belong to the auditor, not the client; the auditor need not surrender them merely because the fee was paid, must preserve their confidentiality and safe custody, and must retain them for not less than seven years.

### Q64. Ch: Audit Documentation & Quality — Leadership Responsibilities for Quality (SQC 1) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** SQC 1 requires that a firm's leadership assume ultimate responsibility for the firm's system of quality control, and warns against a culture in which commercial considerations override quality. Explain the leadership responsibilities for quality within the firm under SQC 1, and describe the "tone at the top" that the Standard seeks to establish.

SQC 1, "Quality Control for Firms that Perform Audits and Reviews of Historical Financial Information, and Other Assurance and Related Services Engagements," identifies **leadership responsibilities for quality within the firm** as one of the six elements of a system of quality control. The Standard requires the firm to establish policies and procedures designed to **promote an internal culture based on the recognition that quality is essential in performing engagements**, and it requires such policies and procedures to require the firm's **chief executive officer (or equivalent) or the managing board of partners (or equivalent) to assume ultimate responsibility** for the firm's system of quality control.

The **"tone at the top"** that the Standard seeks to establish is therefore a clear, consistent and frequently communicated message from leadership — through its own actions and its allocation of resources — that quality is paramount and that commercial success must never be pursued at the expense of the quality of professional work. This message must be reinforced through the firm's training, its documentation, and above all through leadership's own conduct, so that it permeates the entire firm.

**Conclusion:** Under SQC 1, ultimate responsibility for the system of quality control rests with the firm's CEO or managing board of partners, who must foster a culture — a "tone at the top" — in which quality is recognised as essential and is never subordinated to commercial considerations, backed by evaluation, compensation and promotion policies that reward quality.

*(Full-marks tip: Name the responsible office precisely — "CEO or managing board of partners" — and stress the phrase that commercial considerations must not override quality. Linking the culture to compensation/promotion incentives distinguishes a full-marks answer.)*

### Q65. Ch: Audit Documentation & Quality — Monitoring Element of SQC 1 (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the "Monitoring" element of a system of quality control under SQC 1. Your answer should cover the objective of monitoring, what an ongoing consideration and evaluation (including cyclical inspection of completed engagements) involves, and how the firm should deal with deficiencies noted, including where a report may have been inappropriate or procedures were omitted.

**Answer:**
**Objective and meaning.** Monitoring is one of the six elements of a system of quality control under SQC 1. The firm is required to establish policies and procedures designed to provide it with reasonable assurance that the policies and procedures relating to the system of quality control are **relevant, adequate and operating effectively**. Monitoring is the process of obtaining that assurance — it involves an **ongoing consideration and evaluation of the firm's system of quality control**, including a periodic inspection of a selection of completed engagements.

**What monitoring involves.** The firm assigns responsibility for the monitoring process to a partner or partners or other persons with sufficient and appropriate experience and authority in the firm. Ongoing consideration and evaluation includes matters such as the analysis of new developments in professional standards and regulatory requirements, confirmation of compliance with policies on independence, continuing professional development, and decisions on acceptance and continuance of clients. It also includes **cyclical inspection**: selecting, on a cyclical basis, at least **one completed engagement for each engagement partner** and inspecting it to evaluate whether the firm's quality control policies and procedures were complied with. Those performing the inspection should not be involved in performing the engagement or the engagement quality control review of that engagement.

**Communication and dealing with deficiencies.** The firm must evaluate the effect of deficiencies noted as a result of monitoring and determine whether they are either (i) instances that do not necessarily indicate that the firm's system is insufficient to provide reasonable assurance of compliance, or (ii) **systemic, repetitive or other significant deficiencies** requiring prompt corrective action. The firm must communicate the deficiencies noted, and recommendations for appropriate remedial action, to the relevant engagement partners and other appropriate personnel. Appropriate remedial actions may include taking remedial action in relation to an individual engagement or a member of personnel, communicating findings to those responsible for training and professional development, changes to the quality control policies and procedures, and disciplinary action where necessary.

Where the results of monitoring indicate that **a report may be inappropriate, or that procedures were omitted** during the performance of the engagement, the firm must determine what further action is appropriate to comply with relevant professional standards and legal and regulatory requirements, and should consider obtaining **legal advice**. Where deficiencies are systemic or significant, prompt corrective action is required.

**Conclusion:** The monitoring element gives the firm reasonable assurance that its quality control system is relevant, adequate and operating effectively, through ongoing evaluation and cyclical inspection of at least one completed engagement per engagement partner; noted deficiencies must be communicated and remedied, and where a report may be inappropriate or procedures were omitted, the firm must take further action and consider legal advice.

*(Full-marks tip: The specific, markable facts are "at least one completed engagement per engagement partner", the inspector's independence from the engagement, and the "report may be inappropriate / procedures omitted → consider legal advice" response. Include these to secure the last two marks.)*

### Q66. Ch: Audit Documentation & Quality — Engagement Quality Control Review / EQCR (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** "Cornerstone & Associates" is the statutory auditor of "Bluewave Ltd.", a listed company. The engagement partner is uncertain whether an Engagement Quality Control Review (EQCR) is required and, if so, what the reviewer must do and what the effect is on the dating of the auditor's report. Advise, with reference to SQC 1 and SA 220.

**Answer:**
**When an EQCR is required.** Under SQC 1, the firm must establish policies and procedures requiring an **engagement quality control review (EQCR)** for **all audits of financial statements of listed entities**, and setting out criteria against which all other audits and reviews are evaluated to determine whether an EQCR should be performed. Since Bluewave Ltd. is a **listed company**, an EQCR is **mandatory** for this engagement.

**Nature of the review and the reviewer.** An EQCR is an **objective evaluation, by a partner or other person not part of the engagement team (the engagement quality control reviewer), of the significant judgements made by the engagement team and the conclusions reached in formulating the report**, performed on or before the date of the report. The reviewer must have **sufficient and appropriate technical expertise and experience and the necessary authority**, and must be **objective** — the reviewer should not be selected by the engagement partner, and (for listed entities) rotation requirements and a cooling-off period apply so that the reviewer's objectivity is not impaired.

**What the reviewer does.** The engagement quality control review shall include (a) **discussion of significant matters** with the engagement partner; (b) **review of the financial statements and the proposed auditor's report**; (c) review of **selected audit documentation relating to the significant judgements** the engagement team made and the conclusions it reached; and (d) evaluation of the **conclusions reached in formulating the report** and consideration of whether the proposed report is appropriate. For listed entities the review also considers the engagement team's evaluation of the firm's independence, whether appropriate consultation has taken place on contentious or difficult matters, and whether the documentation reflects the work performed in relation to significant judgements.

**Effect on dating of the report.** SA 220 and SQC 1 require that, where an EQCR is required, the auditor **shall not date the auditor's report until the completion of the engagement quality control review**. In other words, the report cannot be dated until the reviewer has completed the EQCR.

**Conclusion:** Because Bluewave Ltd. is listed, an EQCR is mandatory; it must be performed by an objective, suitably experienced reviewer who is not on the engagement team, who evaluates the significant judgements and conclusions and the appropriateness of the report; and the auditor's report cannot be dated until the EQCR is complete.

*(Full-marks tip: State unequivocally that EQCR is compulsory for listed entities, and finish with the dating rule — "no dating of the report before EQCR completion" — which candidates often forget. Mentioning the reviewer's objectivity/rotation for listed clients earns the extra mark.)*

### Q69. Ch: Company Audit — Ceiling on Number of Audits u/s 141(3)(g) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** CA. Vasant is a partner in M/s VBR & Associates, a firm of three partners — CA. Vasant, CA. Bharti and CA. Ravi. As on the date of the AGMs for FY 2025-26, the audit engagements held by each partner (in their individual name) are as follows:

| Partner | Audits already held (as auditor) |
|---|---|
| CA. Vasant | 12 public companies, 3 private companies with paid-up capital of ₹120 crore each, 4 dormant companies, 2 one-person companies (OPCs) |
| CA. Bharti | 18 public companies, 5 small companies, 1 private company with paid-up capital of ₹90 crore |
| CA. Ravi | 21 companies (all public, none exceeding the limit individually) |

The firm is now offered 6 fresh company audits, to be signed one each by the partners as available. Advise how many of the 6 new audits the firm can accept and by whom, citing the relevant provisions.

**Solution:**
**WN-1 — The statutory ceiling.** Under **Section 141(3)(g)** of the Companies Act, 2013, a person is **disqualified** from being appointed auditor if he holds appointment as auditor of **more than twenty companies** at the date of appointment. Where the auditor is a firm, the ceiling of 20 is applied to **each partner** who is not in full-time employment elsewhere.

**WN-2 — Companies excluded from the count of 20.** By virtue of the proviso/Explanation and the relaxation given effect via the Act and the MCA, the following are **NOT** counted in the ceiling of 20:
- **One Person Companies (OPCs)**;
- **Dormant companies**;
- **Small companies**; and
- **Private companies having paid-up share capital less than ₹100 crore**.

A private company with paid-up capital of **₹100 crore or more** is counted.

**WN-3 — Count for CA. Vasant.**

| Item | Held | Counted? | In ceiling |
|---|---|---|---|
| Public companies | 12 | Yes | 12 |
| Private co. — paid-up ₹120 crore each (3) | 3 | Yes (≥ ₹100 cr) | 3 |
| Dormant companies | 4 | Excluded | 0 |
| OPCs | 2 | Excluded | 0 |
| **Total counted** | | | **15** |

Vacancy available to Vasant = 20 − 15 = **5**.

**WN-4 — Count for CA. Bharti.**

| Item | Held | Counted? | In ceiling |
|---|---|---|---|
| Public companies | 18 | Yes | 18 |
| Small companies | 5 | Excluded | 0 |
| Private co. — paid-up ₹90 crore | 1 | Excluded (< ₹100 cr) | 0 |
| **Total counted** | | | **18** |

Vacancy available to Bharti = 20 − 18 = **2**.

**WN-5 — Count for CA. Ravi.** 21 public companies are counted → **21**, which **already exceeds** the ceiling of 20. Ravi is **disqualified** u/s 141(3)(g) and cannot accept **any** fresh audit; in fact he is holding one in excess of the permissible limit.

**Statement Showing Capacity of the Firm to Accept New Audits**

| Partner | Counted audits | Vacancy (20 − counted) | New audits acceptable |
|---|---|---|---|
| CA. Vasant | 15 | 5 | up to 5 |
| CA. Bharti | 18 | 2 | up to 2 |
| CA. Ravi | 21 | Nil (already in excess) | 0 |
| **Firm total capacity** | | | **7** |

**Conclusion / Answer:** The firm's combined spare capacity is **5 + 2 + 0 = 7 audits**, which exceeds the **6** offered. Therefore, the firm **can accept all 6 new audits** — signed by CA. Vasant and CA. Bharti between them (e.g., 4 by Vasant + 2 by Bharti, or 5 by Vasant + 1 by Bharti). **None** may be signed by CA. Ravi, who is already at 21 and disqualified.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards correctly EXCLUDING OPCs, dormant, small companies and sub-₹100-crore private companies, and the ₹100-crore test on private companies — and applying the ceiling partner-wise, not firm-wise. Common deduction: counting all private companies, or applying "20 × 3 partners = 60" to the firm as a block.)*

### Q70. Ch: Company Audit — Casual Vacancy u/s 139(8) (Marks: 4) [Application]
**Question:** During FY 2025-26, XYZ Ltd. (a company not covered by Section 139(5)) faced two situations: (a) its statutory auditor, CA. Neel, **resigned** on 30 June 2025 owing to ill-health; and (b) its other joint auditor, CA. Priya, **died** on 12 August 2025. In each case, state who is competent to fill the casual vacancy, within what time, and for how long the auditor so appointed will hold office. Also state the compliance an auditor who resigns must observe.

**Answer:** A **casual vacancy** is a vacancy that arises otherwise than by expiry of the auditor's normal term — for example, by **death, disqualification or resignation**. Its filling in a company (other than a Government company u/s 139(5)) is governed by **Section 139(8)(i)** read with the Companies (Audit and Auditors) Rules, 2014.

**Rule — casual vacancy in general:** Under Section 139(8)(i), a casual vacancy in the office of an auditor shall be filled by the **Board of Directors within thirty days**.

**Rule — where the vacancy is by resignation:** Where the casual vacancy results from the **resignation** of an auditor, the Board's appointment must in addition be **approved by the company in general meeting** convened **within three months** of the recommendation of the Board, and the auditor so appointed shall hold office **till the conclusion of the next Annual General Meeting**.

**Application to XYZ Ltd.**

| Situation | Cause of vacancy | Who fills it | Time limit / approval | Tenure |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| (a) CA. Neel | Resignation | Board fills; must be approved by members in general meeting | Board within 30 days; GM within 3 months of Board's recommendation | Till conclusion of next AGM |
| (b) CA. Priya | Death | Board fills | Within 30 days | Till conclusion of next AGM |

**Compliance by a resigning auditor:** Under **Section 140(2)** read with **Rule 8**, an auditor who resigns must file a statement in **Form ADT-3** with the **company** and the **Registrar** (and, for a Section 139(5)/(7) company, also with the **C&AG**) **within thirty days** of resignation, indicating the reasons and other facts relevant to the resignation. Default attracts penalty u/s 140(3).

**Conclusion:** The vacancy caused by CA. Neel's resignation is filled by the Board **and ratified by members in general meeting within 3 months**, whereas the vacancy caused by CA. Priya's death is filled by the **Board alone within 30 days**; in both cases the appointee holds office only **until the conclusion of the next AGM**. CA. Neel must additionally file **Form ADT-3** within 30 days.

*(Full-marks tip: The distinction that resignation ALSO needs members' approval in general meeting whereas death does not, plus the ADT-3 filing, are the mark-earning points. Common deduction: writing "till the next AGM" but omitting that only resignation requires GM approval.)*

### Q71. Ch: Company Audit — Branch Audit u/s 143(8) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Sunrise Ltd. has its head office at Mumbai and three branches — one at Chennai, one at a SEЗ unit in Kolkata, and one **foreign branch** at Dubai. For FY 2025-26 the company proposes: (i) that the Chennai and Kolkata branches be audited by CA. Menon, a branch auditor other than the company's auditor; and (ii) that the Dubai branch accounts be audited by a person qualified to audit accounts under the law of the UAE. The company's auditor, M/s HB & Co., asks whether it can rely on these branch audits and what its duty is regarding the branch audit reports. Advise, citing Section 143(8).

**Answer:** The audit of branch office accounts of a company is governed by **Section 143(8)** of the Companies Act, 2013, read with **Rule 12** of the Companies (Audit and Auditors) Rules, 2014.

**Who may audit a branch:** Under Section 143(8), where a company has a branch office, the accounts of that office shall be audited **either** by the company's (statutory) auditor **or** by **any other person qualified for appointment as auditor of the company under Section 139** and appointed as branch auditor under Section 139.

**Foreign branch:** In the case of a **branch office situated outside India** (the Dubai branch), the accounts may be audited **either** by the company's auditor **or** by an **accountant or by any other person duly qualified to act as an auditor of the accounts of the branch office in accordance with the laws of that foreign country**. Thus a UAE-qualified auditor may validly audit the Dubai branch.

**Duty of the branch auditor:** The branch auditor shall **prepare a report on the accounts of the branch** examined by him and **send it to the auditor of the company** (M/s HB & Co.), who shall **deal with it in his report in such manner as he considers necessary**.

**Powers and duties:** By virtue of Section 143(8), the branch auditor has the **same powers and duties** in respect of the branch audit as the company's auditor has under Section 143 generally.

**Application to Sunrise Ltd.**

| Branch | Permissible auditor | Valid? |
|---|---|---|
| Chennai (domestic) | Company auditor OR another qualified person (CA. Menon) | Valid |
| Kolkata SEZ (domestic) | Same as above | Valid |
| Dubai (foreign) | Company auditor OR person qualified under UAE law | Valid |

**Conclusion:** M/s HB & Co. is entitled to have the branches audited by CA. Menon (domestic branches) and by a UAE-qualified auditor (Dubai branch). Each branch auditor must send his report to M/s HB & Co., who must **deal with it in the main audit report in such manner as considered necessary** — the principal auditor is not absolved of responsibility merely because a branch auditor was appointed; it must consider whether the branch report is adequate for its own opinion (SA 600, "Using the Work of Another Auditor").

### Q72. Ch: Audit Report — Reporting on Internal Financial Controls u/s 143(3)(i) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the auditor's reporting responsibility regarding **Internal Financial Controls with reference to Financial Statements (IFCoFR)** under Section 143(3)(i). Which class of companies is **exempted** from this reporting, and what is the difference between "internal financial controls" as understood for the Board's responsibility and "internal financial controls over financial reporting" that the auditor reports upon?

**Answer:** Under **Section 143(3)(i)** of the Companies Act, 2013, the auditor's report must state **whether the company has adequate internal financial controls with reference to financial statements in place and the operating effectiveness of such controls**. This is a **separate reporting responsibility** and is discharged through an **Annexure to the main audit report** (a separate report on IFCoFR), prepared in accordance with the Guidance Note on Audit of Internal Financial Controls Over Financial Reporting issued by ICAI.

**Meaning of the two terms:**

| Aspect | Internal Financial Controls (IFC) — Sec 134(5)(e) / Board's responsibility | Internal Financial Controls over/with reference to Financial Statements (IFCoFR) — Sec 143(3)(i) / Auditor's responsibility |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | Broader — policies and procedures for **orderly and efficient conduct of business**, safeguarding of assets, prevention/detection of frauds and errors, accuracy and completeness of accounting records, and **timely preparation of reliable financial information** | Narrower — controls specifically relevant to the **preparation and fair presentation of financial statements** |
| Whose duty | Directors of a **listed** company (Board's Report) | The **auditor**, for every company on which the clause applies |
| Reported in | Directors' Responsibility Statement | Annexure to auditor's report |

**Exemption from auditor's IFCoFR reporting:** By MCA Notification dated **13 June 2017**, reporting under Section 143(3)(i) on the **adequacy and operating effectiveness** of IFCoFR is **not applicable to certain private companies** — namely a private company which:
(a) is a **one-person company** or a **small company**; **or**
(b) has **turnover less than ₹50 crore** as per the latest audited financial statements **and** has aggregate **borrowings** from banks/financial institutions/any body corporate **less than ₹25 crore** at any point of time during the financial year,
provided the company has **not committed a default** in filing its financial statements u/s 137 or annual return u/s 92 with the Registrar.

**Auditor's approach:** The auditor evaluates the **design** and **operating effectiveness** of IFCoFR by identifying material accounts/disclosures, understanding the flow of transactions, identifying and testing entity-level and process-level controls, and evaluating deficiencies. A **material weakness** would lead to an **adverse opinion** on IFCoFR.

**Conclusion:** The auditor must express a separate opinion on whether the company has **adequate IFCoFR and whether they were operating effectively**, except for the exempted class of small/OPC/threshold private companies; the auditor's reporting is confined to controls **over financial reporting**, which is narrower than the Board's broader IFC responsibility.

*(Full-marks tip: The two-limb ₹50 crore turnover AND ₹25 crore borrowings exemption test for private companies, and the design-vs-operating-effectiveness point, are the differentiators. Common deduction: confusing IFC (broad) with IFCoFR (narrow) or stating a single threshold.)*

### Q73. Ch: Audit Report — CARO 2020 Clause 3(i): Immovable Property (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** While auditing Meridian Ltd. for FY 2025-26 (a company on which CARO 2020 applies), you note: (a) the title deeds of a factory land held by the company (carrying value ₹4.2 crore) are **not held in the company's name** but in the name of a director's HUF; (b) during the year the company **revalued** its plant and buildings resulting in an upward revision of ₹6 crore; and (c) a search/proceeding under the **Benami Transactions (Prohibition) Act, 1988** is pending against the company in respect of a warehouse. State your reporting responsibilities under the relevant sub-clauses of **Clause 3(i)** of CARO 2020.

The auditor must report on each of the following sub-clauses:

**(a) Title deeds not in the company's name — Clause 3(i)(c):** Where the company holds any immovable property (other than properties where the company is the lessee and the lease agreements are duly executed in favour of the lessee) **whose title deeds are not held in the name of the company**, the auditor must report the details in the **prescribed format**, giving: description of property, gross carrying value, whether it is held in the name of a promoter/director/relative/employee, period held, and the reason for not being held in the company's name (including whether any proceeding is pending).

*Application:* The factory land of carrying value **₹4.2 crore** is held in the name of a **director's HUF**, not the company. **(c) Benami proceedings — Clause 3(i)(e):** The auditor must report whether **any proceedings have been initiated or are pending** against the company for holding any **benami property** under the **Benami Transactions (Prohibition) Act, 1988** and rules made thereunder, and if so, whether the company has **appropriately disclosed the details** in its financial statements.

*Application:* Since a benami proceeding regarding the warehouse is **pending**, the auditor must report the fact and state **whether the company has disclosed** the details of the proceeding in its financial statements.

**Statement of Reporting Obligations under Clause 3(i)**

| Fact | Sub-clause | Reporting requirement |
|---|---|---|
| Land ₹4.2 cr in director's HUF name | 3(i)(c) | Report in prescribed format |
| Revaluation ₹6 cr | 3(i)(d) | Report; whether by Registered Valuer; amount if change ≥ 10% |
| Benami proceeding on warehouse | 3(i)(e) | Report; and whether disclosed in FS |

**Conclusion:** The auditor must make an **adverse/affirmative report** on all three sub-clauses — disclosing the title-deed discrepancy in the prescribed format under 3(i)(c), the revaluation and Registered-Valuer status under 3(i)(d), and the pendency and disclosure of the benami proceeding under 3(i)(e).

Naming each sub-clause correctly earns full marks. Common deduction: giving one generic answer on "fixed assets" without the sub-clause split.)*

### Q74. Ch: Audit Report — CARO 2020 Clause 3(iii): Loans, Guarantees & Investments (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Kalpataru Ltd., on which CARO 2020 applies, has during FY 2025-26 granted a loan of ₹80 lakh to a related party. The terms stipulate repayment "on demand", no schedule of repayment or interest is specified in any agreement, and as at year-end interest of ₹6 lakh remains overdue for more than 120 days. The company is not registered as an NBFC. State the auditor's reporting responsibilities under **Clause 3(iii)** of CARO 2020 in respect of these facts.

**Answer:** **Clause 3(iii)** of CARO 2020 requires reporting where the company has **made investments in, provided guarantee or security, or granted loans or advances in the nature of loans**, whether secured or unsecured, to companies, firms, LLPs or any other parties. The relevant sub-clauses engaged here are:

**Clause 3(iii)(c) — schedule of repayment:** The auditor must report whether the **schedule of repayment of principal and payment of interest has been stipulated** and whether the repayments or receipts are **regular**.
*Application:* Since **no repayment schedule** and **no interest schedule** are stipulated in any agreement, the auditor must report that a schedule of repayment of principal and payment of interest has **not been stipulated**.

**Clause 3(iii)(d) — overdue amount beyond 90 days:** Where the amount is **overdue**, the auditor must state the **total amount overdue for more than ninety days**, and whether **reasonable steps** have been taken by the company for recovery of the principal and interest.
*Application:* Interest of **₹6 lakh** is overdue for **more than 120 days (> 90 days)**. The auditor must report this ₹6 lakh as overdue beyond 90 days and comment on the recovery steps taken.

*Application:* The **₹80 lakh** loan to a **related party** is **repayable on demand** with **no terms specified**. The auditor must report the aggregate amount (₹80 lakh), the percentage it bears to total loans, and that it is granted to a related party.

**Statement of Reporting Obligations under Clause 3(iii)**

| Fact | Sub-clause | Reporting requirement |
|---|---|---|
| No repayment/interest schedule | 3(iii)(c) | Report that schedule not stipulated; regularity |

### Q76. Ch: Audit Report — SA 705: Types of Modified Opinion (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** For three separate audit clients (FY 2025-26), the auditor faces the following situations. State, with reasons under **SA 705 (Revised)**, the type of opinion the auditor should express in each case:
(a) Inventory is materially misstated because it is valued at cost instead of the lower of cost and NRV; the effect is **material but not pervasive**.
(b) The company did not allow the auditor to attend the physical verification of inventory constituting 65% of total assets and no alternative procedures were possible; the possible effects are **material and pervasive**.
(c) Trade receivables are overstated and, on account of a chain of accounting errors across multiple heads, the financial statements as a whole are **materially misstated and the misstatement is pervasive**.

**Answer:** **SA 705 (Revised), "Modifications to the Opinion in the Independent Auditor's Report"**, requires the auditor to modify the opinion when either (i) the financial statements are **materially misstated**, or (ii) the auditor is **unable to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence** (limitation on scope). The **type** of modification depends on (a) the **nature** of the matter and (b) the **pervasiveness** of the effect (or possible effect) on the financial statements.

**Decision matrix under SA 705:**

| Nature of matter | Material but NOT pervasive | Material AND pervasive |
|---|---|---|
| Financial statements are materially misstated | **Qualified opinion** | **Adverse opinion** |
| Inability to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence | **Qualified opinion** | **Disclaimer of opinion** |

**Application:**

| Case | Nature | Pervasiveness | Opinion |
|---|---|---|---|
| (a) Inventory at cost, not lower of cost/NRV | Misstatement | Material, not pervasive | **Qualified opinion** ("except for") |
| (b) Could not attend verification of 65% of assets; no alternatives | Inability to obtain evidence | Material and pervasive | **Disclaimer of opinion** |
| (c) Chain of errors, FS materially misstated as a whole | Misstatement | Material and pervasive | **Adverse opinion** |

**Reasoning:** In (a), a **known misstatement** confined to inventory that is material but not pervasive warrants a **qualified** ("except for") opinion. In (b), a **scope limitation** whose possible effects are pervasive — no alternative procedures being possible on 65% of assets — warrants a **disclaimer**. In (c), a misstatement so pervasive that the financial statements as a whole are unreliable warrants an **adverse** opinion.

**Conclusion:** (a) **Qualified opinion**; (b) **Disclaimer of opinion**; (c) **Adverse opinion**.

### Q77. Ch: Audit Report — SA 706: Emphasis of Matter vs Other Matter Paragraph (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between an **Emphasis of Matter (EOM) paragraph** and an **Other Matter paragraph** under **SA 706 (Revised)**. Give one appropriate example of each and state whether including such paragraphs is a modification of the auditor's opinion.

**Answer:** **SA 706 (Revised), "Emphasis of Matter Paragraphs and Other Matter Paragraphs in the Independent Auditor's Report"**, provides for two types of additional communication in the auditor's report **without modifying the opinion**.

**Definitions:**
- An **Emphasis of Matter (EOM) paragraph** is a paragraph included in the auditor's report that refers to a matter **appropriately presented or disclosed in the financial statements** that, in the auditor's judgement, is of such importance that it is **fundamental to users' understanding** of the financial statements.
- An **Other Matter paragraph** is a paragraph included in the auditor's report that refers to a matter **other than those presented or disclosed in the financial statements** that, in the auditor's judgement, is relevant to users' understanding of the audit, the auditor's responsibilities or the auditor's report.

**Distinction:**

| Basis | Emphasis of Matter | Other Matter |
|---|---|---|
| Subject matter | A matter **disclosed IN** the financial statements | A matter **NOT disclosed** in the financial statements |
| Purpose | Draw users' attention to something fundamental to understanding the FS | Draw attention to something relevant to understanding the audit/report/responsibilities |
| Placement | Separate paragraph headed "Emphasis of Matter" | Separate paragraph headed "Other Matter" |
| Example | An uncertainty relating to the outcome of exceptional litigation, adequately disclosed in the notes | The prior-period financial statements were audited by a predecessor auditor |
| Effect on opinion | **Not a modification** — auditor's opinion is not modified | **Not a modification** — auditor's opinion is not modified |

**Conclusion:** Both paragraphs are used **without modifying the opinion**; the key difference is that an **EOM** relates to a matter **already disclosed in the financial statements** that is fundamental to users' understanding, whereas an **Other Matter** paragraph relates to a matter **not disclosed in the financial statements** but relevant to understanding the audit or the auditor's responsibilities.

*(Full-marks tip: The pivotal line — EOM = matter disclosed IN the FS; Other Matter = matter NOT in the FS — plus the fact that NEITHER modifies the opinion. Common deduction: treating EOM as a form of qualification.)*

### Q78. Ch: Audit Report — SA 701: Key Audit Matters (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the concept of **Key Audit Matters (KAM)** under **SA 701**. To which audits does SA 701 mandatorily apply, how does the auditor determine KAM, and what is the relationship between KAM and a modified opinion / an Emphasis of Matter paragraph?

**Answer:** **SA 701, "Communicating Key Audit Matters in the Independent Auditor's Report"**, deals with the auditor's responsibility to communicate key audit matters in the auditor's report. **Key Audit Matters** are defined as **those matters that, in the auditor's professional judgement, were of most significance in the audit of the financial statements of the current period**; they are **selected from matters communicated with those charged with governance (TCWG)**.

**Applicability:** SA 701 applies **mandatorily** to audits of **complete sets of general purpose financial statements of listed entities**, and when the auditor otherwise decides to communicate KAM, or is required by law/regulation to do so.

**Determination of KAM (a "narrowing-down" process):** From the matters communicated with TCWG, the auditor first identifies those that **required significant auditor attention**, having regard especially to:
(a) areas assessed as having **higher risk of material misstatement** or significant risks under SA 315;
(b) **significant auditor judgements** relating to areas involving significant **management judgement**, including accounting estimates with high estimation uncertainty; and
(c) the effect on the audit of **significant events or transactions** during the period.
From these, the auditor then determines those that were of **most significance** — these are the KAM.

**Description in the report:** Each KAM is described under a separate section headed "**Key Audit Matters**", stating **why** the matter was determined to be a KAM and **how** it was addressed in the audit, with a reference to the related disclosure(s).

**Relationship with modified opinion / EOM:**

| Aspect | Position under SA 701 |
|---|---|
| KAM vs opinion | Communicating KAM is **not a substitute** for expressing a modified opinion under SA 705 when required |
| KAM vs EOM | KAM is **not a substitute** for an Emphasis of Matter paragraph under SA 706 |
| Matter giving rise to modification | A matter giving rise to a **qualified/adverse opinion**, or a **material uncertainty on going concern**, is **by its nature a KAM** but is **NOT described in the KAM section**; instead the auditor reports it in the Basis for Qualified/Adverse Opinion or Material Uncertainty section and **refers to it** in the KAM section |

**Conclusion:** KAM are the matters of **most significance** in the current-period audit, selected from matters discussed with TCWG; SA 701 applies mandatorily to **listed entities**; and communicating KAM **neither replaces a modified opinion nor an EOM/Other Matter paragraph** — matters underlying a modification are reported in the relevant Basis section, not in the KAM section.

*(Full-marks tip: The three-factor determination (higher-risk areas, significant judgements, significant events) and the rule that KAM does NOT substitute a modified opinion or EOM are the scoring points. Common deduction: listing KAM without the "communicated with TCWG → required significant attention → most significant" funnel.)*

### Q79. Ch: Company Audit — Rotation of Auditors u/s 139(2) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Zenith Ltd. is an **unlisted public company** with paid-up share capital of ₹12 crore and outstanding public borrowings from a bank of ₹60 crore as at 31 March 2026. It has been audited by CA. Kiran (an individual) continuously since FY 2015-16. The company also wishes to know whether an audit firm, on completing its term, can be re-appointed and after what period. Advise on the applicability and mechanics of **mandatory rotation of auditors** under **Section 139(2)**.

**Answer:** **Section 139(2)** of the Companies Act, 2013, read with **Rule 5** of the Companies (Audit and Auditors) Rules, 2014, provides for **mandatory rotation of auditors**.

**Classes of companies to which Section 139(2) applies (Rule 5):** Besides every **listed company**, rotation applies to the following classes of companies (excluding OPCs and small companies):
(a) all **unlisted public companies** having paid-up share capital of **₹10 crore or more**;
(b) all **private companies** having paid-up share capital of **₹50 crore or more**; and
(c) all companies (other than the above) having **public borrowings** from financial institutions, banks or public deposits of **₹50 crore or more**.

**Maximum term under Section 139(2):** Such companies shall not appoint or re-appoint:
- an **individual** as auditor for **more than one term of five consecutive years**; and
- an **audit firm** as auditor for **more than two terms of five consecutive years** (i.e., 10 years).

**Cooling-off period:** An individual auditor who has completed his term (5 years) and a firm that has completed its two terms (10 years) shall **not be re-appointed** as auditor in the **same company for five years** from the completion of the term.

**Application to Zenith Ltd.:** Zenith is an **unlisted public company** with paid-up capital of **₹12 crore (≥ ₹10 crore)** — hence rotation **applies** [Rule 5(a)]. (It independently also crosses the ₹50 crore public-borrowing test.) CA. Kiran, an **individual**, has audited the company **continuously since FY 2015-16**, i.e., beyond one term of five consecutive years. Therefore CA. Kiran **cannot be re-appointed**; the company must **rotate** the auditor, and CA. Kiran cannot be re-appointed in Zenith Ltd. for **five years**.

**Re-appointment of a firm:** An audit firm may serve **two terms of five years (10 years)** and, on completion, is subject to the **same 5-year cooling-off**. Further, the incoming and outgoing auditors having **common partners** (of the outgoing firm) cannot be appointed during the cooling-off period.

**Conclusion:** Rotation under Section 139(2) applies to Zenith Ltd.; **CA. Kiran must be replaced** and cannot be re-appointed for 5 years, whereas an audit firm may serve a maximum of **two 5-year terms** followed by a **5-year cooling-off**.

*(Full-marks tip: State Rule 5's three thresholds (₹10 cr public / ₹50 cr private paid-up, or ₹50 cr borrowings), the 5-year (individual) vs 10-year (firm) terms, and the 5-year cooling-off. Common deduction: giving a firm "5 years" instead of "two terms of 5 years", or forgetting the borrowings-based class.)*

### Q83. Ch: Audit Report — SA 700: Forming an Opinion & Elements of the Report (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Under **SA 700 (Revised)**, on what basis does the auditor **form an opinion** on the financial statements, and what are the essential **elements** of an unmodified (independent) auditor's report? Where should the "Opinion" section and "Basis for Opinion" section be placed?

**Answer:** **SA 700 (Revised), "Forming an Opinion and Reporting on Financial Statements"**, deals with the auditor's responsibility to form an opinion on the financial statements and prescribes the form and content of the auditor's report.

**Basis for forming an opinion:** The auditor shall form an opinion on whether the financial statements are prepared, **in all material respects, in accordance with the applicable financial reporting framework**. To form that opinion the auditor shall conclude whether he has obtained **reasonable assurance** about whether the financial statements as a whole are **free from material misstatement, whether due to fraud or error**. That conclusion takes into account:
(a) whether **sufficient appropriate audit evidence** has been obtained (SA 330);
(b) whether **uncorrected misstatements** are material, individually or in aggregate (SA 450); and
(c) qualitative evaluations — whether the financial statements adequately disclose the significant accounting policies, whether accounting estimates are reasonable, and whether the financial statements achieve **fair presentation**.

**When the opinion is unmodified:** The auditor shall express an **unmodified (clean) opinion** when he concludes that the financial statements are prepared, in all material respects, in accordance with the applicable framework.

**Essential elements of the auditor's report (in order):**
1. **Title** — "Independent Auditor's Report".
2. **Addressee** — usually the members/shareholders.
3. **Opinion** section — placed **first**, identifying the entity, the statements audited, and expressing the opinion.
4. **Basis for Opinion** section — placed **immediately after** the Opinion section, stating that the audit was conducted in accordance with SAs, the auditor's independence and ethical responsibilities, and that the evidence obtained is sufficient and appropriate.
5. **Key Audit Matters** (where SA 701 applies).
6. **Responsibilities of Management/TCWG** for the financial statements.
7. **Auditor's Responsibilities** for the audit of the financial statements.
8. Other reporting responsibilities, **Signature** (with membership number and firm registration number), **Place** and **Date**.

**Conclusion:** The auditor forms his opinion by concluding whether he has obtained **reasonable assurance that the financial statements as a whole are free from material misstatement**; the report is titled "Independent Auditor's Report" and, under SA 700 (Revised), the **Opinion section comes first, immediately followed by the Basis for Opinion section**.

### Q84. Ch: Company Audit — Appointment of First Auditor u/s 139(6) & 139(7) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Advise on the appointment of the **first auditor** in each of the following independent cases for FY 2025-26:
(a) Comet Ltd., a **non-Government company**, whose Board failed to appoint the first auditor within the stipulated time;
(b) Nova Ltd., a **Government company** (majority held by the Central Government), where neither the C&AG nor the Board has yet acted;
(c) In case (a), suppose the first auditor once appointed is proposed to hold office "until the conclusion of the sixth AGM" — is this valid?

|---|---|---|---|

### Q85. Ch: Professional Ethics & Independence — Contingent / Percentage-based Fees (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** CA. Kiran, a practising Chartered Accountant, accepts the following four assignments in the same year and proposes to be remunerated as shown. Examine, with reference to the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949 and the Regulations, whether each fee arrangement is permissible:
(i) Appointed liquidator of a company under winding-up; fee agreed at **2% of the assets realised** (assets realised during the year ₹2,00,00,000).
(ii) Appointed to audit the accounts of a **co-operative society**; fee agreed at **1% of the working capital** of the society.
(iii) Appointed statutory auditor of a private limited company; fee agreed at **5% of the net profit** disclosed by the audited accounts.
(iv) Rendering a management-consultancy fund-raising service to a client; fee agreed at **1.5% of the term loan actually sanctioned and disbursed** by the bank.

The permitted exceptions are contained in **Regulation 192 of the Chartered Accountants Regulations, 1988**, which lists the specific situations in which percentage/contingent fees are allowed.

Applying the law to each fee arrangement:

| Case | Fee basis | Permissible? | Reason (Regulation 192) |
|---|---|---|---|
| (i) Liquidator | 2% of assets realised | **Yes** | Reg 192 expressly permits a receiver or a liquidator to charge fees on a percentage of the **realisation or disbursement of assets**. |

| (iii) Statutory auditor of company | 5% of net profit | **No** | Fee based on a percentage of profit is squarely hit by Clause 10; this is **not** among the Regulation 192 exceptions — professional misconduct. |
| (iv) Fund-raising service | 1.5% of loan raised | **Yes** | Reg 192 permits, in the case of **certain fund-raising services**, fees to be based on a percentage of the amount raised/disbursed. |

### Q86. Ch: Professional Ethics & Independence — Advertisement, Solicitation & Website (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** CA. Meera, a practising Chartered Accountant, does the following to develop her practice. Comment on each act with reference to the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949 and the Council's guidelines:
(i) She launches a firm website that automatically **pops up** (through paid online advertisements) when a user searches "GST consultant near me", displaying the words "Best GST experts in the city — lowest fees guaranteed".
(ii) On the same website, in a static "Our Team" page accessible only when a visitor visits the site, she lists the **names, qualifications, membership number and areas of practice** of her partners.
(iii) She sends a **personalised email** to fifty proprietors of nearby businesses who are **not** her clients, offering to take up their audit and tax work.

**Answer:**
The relevant provisions are **Clause 6 and Clause 7 of Part I of the First Schedule to the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949**, read with the Council's guidelines on the posting of particulars on a website. **Clause 6** treats as misconduct the solicitation of clients or professional work "**by circular, advertisement, personal communication or interview or by any other means**." **Clause 7** treats as misconduct the **advertising of professional attainments or services**, including anything of a self-laudatory character.

Applying these to each act:

**(i)** A website is permitted only on a "**pull**" model — that is, the information should be available only when a visitor voluntarily accesses the site, and the site must **not be pushed** on prospective clients. A **pop-up / paid advertisement** that thrusts the site before searchers is a "push" and amounts to solicitation, and the self-laudatory phrase "Best GST experts — lowest fees guaranteed" independently violates Clause 7 (advertising of professional attainments in a laudatory manner). **This is professional misconduct** under Clauses 6 and 7.

**(ii)** Listing the **name, qualification, membership number and areas of practice** of members on a website that a visitor accesses on his own (a "pull" entry) is **permitted** by the Council's website guidelines, provided the information is presented in a non-self-laudatory manner and without any solicitation. **This is not misconduct.**

**(iii)** Sending a **personalised email offering services to persons who are not clients** is direct solicitation by "personal communication / any other means" and is squarely hit by **Clause 6**. **This is professional misconduct.**

**Conclusion:** Acts (i) and (iii) render CA. Meera guilty of professional misconduct under Clauses 6/7 of Part I of the First Schedule; act (ii), being a permissible "pull-model" website listing, is not misconduct.

*(Full-marks tip: The distinction the examiner looks for is "pull vs push" — a passively accessed website listing is allowed, but a pop-up/paid ad and self-laudatory or "lowest fee" claims are not. Do not blanket-condemn all websites.)*

### Q87. Ch: Professional Ethics & Independence — Communication with Previous Auditor (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** CA. Rohan is appointed as the auditor of Zenith Traders Pvt. Ltd. in place of the retiring auditor CA. Sen. Rohan telephones CA. Sen, who orally says he has "no objection", and Rohan thereupon accepts the appointment and commences the audit. It later emerges that CA. Sen had, in fact, **not been paid his audit fee** for the previous year and had refused audit only because of the dispute. Examine whether CA. Rohan has acted properly, and state the correct procedure and its purpose.

**Answer:**
The governing provision is **Clause 8 of Part I of the First Schedule to the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949**, under which a Chartered Accountant in practice is deemed guilty of professional misconduct if he "**accepts a position as auditor previously held by another Chartered Accountant … without first communicating with him in writing**."

The requirement is of a **communication in writing**; a **mere oral / telephonic** enquiry does not satisfy Clause 8. Moreover, the Council recommends that the communication be sent by a mode that provides **positive evidence of delivery** — i.e., by **registered post with acknowledgement due (RPAD)**, or delivered by hand against a written acknowledgement — so that the incoming auditor has proof both of despatch and of receipt. It is also prudent to **wait a reasonable time for the retiring auditor's reply** before accepting, so that any professional reasons for non-acceptance can come to light.

The **purpose** of the mandatory prior written communication is two-fold: (a) as a matter of **professional courtesy** to the retiring auditor, and (b) more importantly, to enable the incoming auditor to **ascertain whether there are any circumstances** — such as unpaid audit fees, differences on accounting or auditing matters, or any other professional reason — that ought to be weighed before accepting the appointment. Undisputed **audit fees remaining unpaid** is, under the Council's guidance, ordinarily a valid professional reason for the incoming auditor to be cautious about accepting.

**Conclusion:** CA. Rohan acted **improperly**. By relying on an **oral** "no objection" and failing to communicate **in writing** with CA. Sen before accepting, he has contravened Clause 8 of Part I of the First Schedule and is guilty of professional misconduct; had he communicated in writing, the fact of the unpaid fees — a material professional consideration — would have come to his notice.

*(Full-marks tip: Stress that the communication must be IN WRITING and by a mode giving positive proof of delivery — oral consent is fatal. ### Q88. Ch: Professional Ethics & Independence — Self-Review Threat & Safeguards (Marks: 4) [Application]
**Question:** CA. Nandini's firm was engaged to **design and implement the computerised accounting system** and to **prepare the financial statements** of Orbit Ltd. during the year. After the year-end, the company offers the same firm the **statutory audit** of those very financial statements. Identify the threat to independence involved, explain it with reference to the Code of Ethics, and state whether and how the engagement may be accepted.

**Answer:**
The **Code of Ethics** issued by ICAI identifies five categories of threats to a member's compliance with the fundamental principles: **self-interest, self-review, advocacy, familiarity and intimidation**. The situation described gives rise to a **self-review threat**.

A **self-review threat** is the threat that a member will **not appropriately evaluate the results of a previous judgement made, or a service performed by the member (or his firm), on which the member will rely** when forming a conclusion as part of the current engagement. Here the firm both **prepared** the financial statements (and designed the very system that generated them) and is now asked to **audit** those same statements. In auditing, the firm would in effect be **reviewing its own work**; any error in its book-keeping/system work would be difficult to detect objectively because it is the firm's own judgement that is under examination. The threat is therefore self-review.

**Whether the engagement may be accepted:** For an audit client, providing accounting/book-keeping services and then auditing the same statements creates a self-review threat that, for a listed/public-interest entity, is generally so significant that **no safeguards can reduce it to an acceptable level**, and the audit should be **declined**. For other entities, the firm may proceed only if adequate **safeguards** are applied — for example, ensuring that the persons who did the accounting/system work are **not** members of the audit team, having the audit work reviewed by a **separate professional** not involved in the earlier service, or having the client take responsibility for the financial statements — and only if the threat is thereby reduced to an acceptable level.

**Conclusion:** The engagement gives rise to a **self-review threat**; CA. Nandini's firm may accept the statutory audit only if effective safeguards can reduce the threat to an acceptable level (and must decline where, as for a public-interest entity, no safeguard suffices).

*(Full-marks tip: Correctly name the threat as SELF-REVIEW — not self-interest — and give the Code's definition ("reviewing own previous work"). Marks are earned for both the identification AND at least two concrete safeguards.)*

### Q89. Ch: Professional Ethics & Independence — Advocacy Threat (Marks: 4) [Application]
**Question:** CA. Vikram is the statutory auditor of Sunrise Ltd. During the year the company becomes involved in a **tax litigation** before the Tribunal. The management requests CA. Vikram to **appear before the Tribunal and actively argue the company's case**, promoting the company's disputed position, while continuing as its statutory auditor. Identify the threat to independence and advise CA. Vikram.

**Answer:**
Under the **Code of Ethics** issued by ICAI, the situation gives rise to an **advocacy threat**.

An **advocacy threat** is the threat that a member will **promote a client's (or employer's) position to the point that the member's objectivity is compromised**. It arises where the member acts as an advocate for, or champion of, the client's cause. Here, if CA. Vikram were to **actively argue and promote Sunrise Ltd.'s disputed tax position** before the Tribunal, he would be espousing the client's position; when he subsequently forms his audit opinion — including on the adequacy of the **provision/disclosure for that very tax dispute** in the financial statements — his objectivity would be impaired, because he would be reluctant to take an audit view inconsistent with the position he publicly advocated.

**Advice / safeguards:** Representing a client before tax or other authorities is a normal professional service, but where it shades into **actively promoting/championing** the client's contested position while the member is also the auditor, the advocacy threat may be too significant. CA. Vikram should evaluate the significance of the threat and apply safeguards — for example, ensuring that the person who represents/advocates before the Tribunal is **not** a member of the audit engagement team, and subjecting the related audit judgements to an **independent review**. Where the threat cannot be reduced to an acceptable level, he should **decline the advocacy role** (while a merely explanatory appearance, not amounting to championing a contested position, may be acceptable).

**Conclusion:** Actively arguing and promoting the company's disputed case before the Tribunal creates an **advocacy threat** to CA. Vikram's independence; he should apply safeguards or decline the advocacy engagement so that his objectivity as statutory auditor is preserved.

*(Full-marks tip: Distinguish routine representation from ACTIVELY promoting a contested position — the latter is the advocacy threat. Name the threat correctly and tie it to the audit judgement on the tax provision.)*

### Q90. Ch: Professional Ethics & Independence — Ceiling on Number of Company Audits (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** M/s PQR & Associates is a firm of Chartered Accountants having three partners — P, Q and R. Partner **R is in whole-time employment** elsewhere. In their individual capacity, partner P holds **8** company audits and partner Q holds **6** company audits. The firm is now **offered the statutory audit of 30 companies**, the composition of which is given below. Determine, with reference to Section 141(3)(g) of the Companies Act, 2013, the maximum number of these audits the firm can accept.

| Type of company offered | Number |
|---|---|
| One-Person Companies (OPCs) | 6 |
| Dormant companies | 5 |
| Small companies | 4 |
| Private companies with paid-up capital of ₹80 crore each | 3 |
| Other companies (counting for the ceiling) | 12 |
| **Total offered** | **30** |

**Solution:**

**WN-1: The statutory ceiling — Section 141(3)(g)**
A person is disqualified for appointment as auditor if he is **holding appointment as auditor of more than twenty companies** (other than the exempted categories). For a **firm**, the ceiling of twenty applies **per partner who is not in full-time employment elsewhere**.

**WN-2: Number of partners available for computing the firm's ceiling**
Partners P and Q are available; partner **R is in whole-time employment elsewhere** and is therefore **excluded**.
Eligible partners = **2** (P and Q).
Firm's specified number (ceiling) = 20 × 2 = **40 company audits**.

**WN-3: Audits to be EXCLUDED from the count (proviso to Sec 141(3)(g))**
The following are **not counted** towards the ceiling of twenty:
- One-Person Companies (6),
- Dormant companies (5),
- Small companies (4), and
- Private companies having paid-up share capital **less than ₹100 crore** — the ₹80-crore companies (3) qualify.
Total exempt = 6 + 5 + 4 + 3 = **18 companies**.
Offered audits that **count** = 30 − 18 = **12 companies**.

**WN-4: Audits already held (counting) by the partners**
P (8) + Q (6) = **14 company audits** already held in individual capacity (these count against the firm's ceiling).

**Statement Showing Availability of Ceiling**

| Particulars | No. of audits |
|---|---|
| Firm's total ceiling (20 × 2 eligible partners) | 40 |
| Less: Audits already held — P (8) + Q (6) | (14) |
| **Balance ceiling available to the firm** | **26** |
| New offered audits that count towards the ceiling | 12 |
| Exempt offered audits (OPC/dormant/small/pvt < ₹100 cr) | 18 |

Since the **12 counting** audits are well within the **balance ceiling of 26**, and the **18 exempt** audits do not consume the ceiling at all, the firm can accept **all 30** offered audits.

**Answer:** The firm M/s PQR & Associates can accept **all 30 company audits** — the 12 that count are within the available balance ceiling of 26 (out of the firm's total ceiling of 40), and the remaining 18 (OPCs, dormant, small and private companies below ₹100 crore paid-up capital) are exempt from the ceiling under Section 141(3)(g).

*(Full-marks tip: Two things earn the marks — (1) excluding the partner in full-time employment when computing 20 × partners, and (2) correctly excluding OPC/dormant/small/private-below-₹100-crore companies from the count. Losing either the "per non-employed partner" rule or the exempt list is the usual deduction.)*

### Q93. Ch: Audit of Different Entities — Audit of a Hotel (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** State the **special considerations** that an auditor should bear in mind while conducting the audit of a **hotel**.

**Answer:**
A hotel is a cash/near-cash-intensive business with numerous revenue streams and significant risk of **pilferage** of stocks and leakage of revenue. The auditor should give special attention to the following:

**1. Internal control over revenue.** Because collections arise at many points (rooms, restaurant, bar, banquets, laundry, business centre), the auditor should evaluate the **internal control** over the recording of sales and the collection of cash, and satisfy himself that all services rendered are billed.

**2. Room sales / occupancy.** Verify that **room sales** are recorded at the correct **tariff** as per the approved tariff card, by reconciling the **housekeeping occupancy report / guest register** with the bills raised and the **night-audit reports**; test that the room revenue for a sample of nights agrees with occupancy. Ensure discounts/complimentary rooms are properly authorised.

**3. Other operating revenue.** Vouch revenue from the **restaurant, bar, banquet functions, laundry, telephone and other services** against the supporting records (e.g., **Kitchen Order Tickets** for food, banquet function orders), and check that it is correctly billed to guests and reflected in the guest ledger.

**4. Stocks — high pilferage risk.** Attend/verify the **physical stock-taking** of provisions, wines and beverages, and stores; given the high risk of **pilferage**, examine the internal controls over stores. Ensure stocks are **valued** appropriately (generally at cost or net realisable value, whichever is lower).

**5. Consumables — crockery, cutlery, linen, glassware.** Verify the treatment and physical control of **crockery, cutlery, linen and glassware**; these are subject to heavy breakage and pilferage, and the auditor should check that periodic physical counts are taken and that the charge to revenue for consumption/breakage is reasonable.

**6. Fixed assets and depreciation.** Verify **additions to fixed assets** (building, furniture, plant) against the fixed-asset register and check that appropriate **depreciation** has been provided.

**7. Advances and deposits.** Confirm that **advance deposits / advance room booking receipts** from guests are shown as a **liability** until the service is rendered, and that **credit-card and online collections** are duly reconciled with the operator/bank.

**8. Statutory compliance.** Verify compliance with applicable statutes, particularly **GST** on the various supplies and any other applicable local levies.

**Conclusion:** The auditor of a hotel must focus on the completeness of **multi-stream revenue** (especially room revenue reconciled to occupancy), tight control over **easily pilfered stocks and consumables** (provisions, liquor, crockery, linen), the correct treatment of **advance deposits** as liabilities, and compliance with GST.

### Q95. Ch: Audit of Different Entities — Audit of a Partnership Firm (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** The audit of a partnership firm is not compulsory under any statute. Nevertheless, partners often get the accounts audited. State the **special points/matters** an auditor should consider while auditing the accounts of a **partnership firm**, and briefly state the **advantages** of such an audit.

**Answer:**
The audit of a partnership firm is **not statutorily required** merely by virtue of its being a partnership (it becomes compulsory only where, e.g., a tax audit under the Income-tax Act is attracted); it is generally carried out **by agreement between the partners**. The auditor's authority and the scope of his work therefore flow from that agreement, and he should give attention to the following special matters:

**1. Terms of engagement.** Ascertain, from the **letter of appointment / the partners' resolution**, the exact **scope and objective** of the audit, since this is contractual and not statutory.

**2. Partnership Deed.** Study the **Partnership Deed** to ascertain the terms governing the accounts — the **capital contributed** by each partner, the **profit-sharing ratio**, **interest on capital and on drawings**, any **salary/commission payable to partners**, borrowing powers, and the provisions relating to **admission, retirement and death** of partners.

**3. Authorised business.** Verify that the business carried on is **within the scope authorised** by the Partnership Deed and that transactions have been entered into with proper authority.

**4. Division of profit.** Verify that the **profit or loss has been divided among the partners in the agreed ratio**, and that **interest on capital / drawings, and partners' salary or commission**, have been provided strictly in accordance with the Deed.

**5. Partners' accounts and assets.** Verify **loans and advances by/to partners** and the interest thereon, examine the partners' **capital and current accounts**, and ensure that the **assets of the firm have not been applied for the private purposes** of any partner.

**6. Changes in constitution.** Where there has been an **admission, retirement or death** of a partner during the year, verify the related adjustments — **revaluation of assets and liabilities, treatment of goodwill, and the adjustment of capital accounts** — as per the Deed/agreement.

**Advantages of audit of a partnership firm:** an audited account provides a **reliable basis for settlement of accounts** on the admission, retirement or death of a partner; it acts as a **moral check** and helps in the **detection and prevention of errors and fraud** by employees and partners; it provides a **convincing basis for the firm's tax assessment** and for dealings with banks and outsiders; and it is **useful in resolving disputes** among the partners.

**Conclusion:** Since a partnership audit is contractual, the auditor must first fix his scope from the engagement terms and then verify, against the **Partnership Deed**, the division of profit, partners' entitlements and any changes in constitution; such an audit, though not compulsory, offers real advantages in settlement, fraud-deterrence, taxation and dispute resolution.

*(Full-marks tip: The engagement-defines-scope point (audit is contractual, not statutory) plus specific verification against the Partnership Deed (profit ratio, interest, salary, admission/retirement adjustments) are the scoring points, along with at least three concrete advantages.)*

### Q96. Ch: Audit of Different Entities — Educational Institution: Grants & Fee Concessions (Marks: 4) [Application]
**Question:** During the audit of "Gyan Deep Vidyalaya", a government-aided school, you notice the following. State how you, as auditor, would deal with each:
(i) A government maintenance grant of ₹10,00,000 was received "for the construction of a science laboratory", but ₹3,00,000 of it was spent on **paying teachers' salaries**.
(ii) Free studentships and fee concessions totalling ₹1,50,000 were granted during the year, but the register does **not show any sanction** by the Managing Committee.

**Answer:**
**(i) Utilisation of a specific-purpose grant.** A grant received for a **specified purpose** must be utilised **only for that purpose**, and the auditor must verify, against the **sanction letter and the conditions attached to the grant**, that this has been done. Here the grant of ₹10,00,000 was sanctioned specifically "**for the construction of a science laboratory**", but ₹3,00,000 has been **diverted to teachers' salaries** — a revenue expense unconnected with the sanctioned capital purpose. This is a **breach of the conditions of the grant**, which may render that portion of the grant **refundable** or liable to be withheld in future. The auditor should verify the sanction terms, ensure the diversion is **disclosed**, obtain the management's explanation, and, if the breach is not rectified/regularised, **report** the improper utilisation.

**(ii) Fee concessions without sanction.** The auditor must satisfy himself that **free studentships and fee concessions have been sanctioned by the proper authority** (the Managing Committee / Governing Body). Here concessions of ₹1,50,000 have been allowed **without any recorded sanction**, meaning revenue of the institution has been forgone without authority. The auditor should examine whether the concessions were in fact approved (e.g., in the minutes), obtain the **management's explanation**, insist that the sanction be **regularised/recorded**, and, failing that, treat the amount as an **unauthorised loss of fee income** and report it accordingly.

**Conclusion:** In (i) the auditor should treat the ₹3,00,000 as a **diversion in breach of grant conditions** and report it; in (ii) the ₹1,50,000 of unsanctioned concessions represents an **unauthorised forgoing of fee income** which should be regularised or reported.

*(Full-marks tip: The two entity-specific principles — specific-purpose grants must be applied only to that purpose, and concessions need proper authority — are the crux. State the consequence (refundable grant / unauthorised income loss) and the reporting action, not just "verify".)*

### Q97. Ch: Audit of Different Entities — Hospital: Pharmacy & Patient Revenue (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** "Care & Cure Hospital" runs an **in-house pharmacy** that sells medicines both to admitted (in-patients) and to walk-in customers. As auditor, describe the audit procedures you would perform to satisfy yourself as to the **completeness and correctness of pharmacy revenue and the pharmacy stock**.

**Answer:**
The pharmacy of a hospital is a stock-and-cash-intensive activity with a real risk that **sales go unrecorded** and that **medicines are pilfered or lost to expiry**. The auditor should perform the following procedures:

**1. Understand and evaluate internal control.** Study the system by which medicines move from purchase to sale — receipt into the pharmacy store, issue against **in-patient indents / prescriptions** and **cash sales to walk-in customers** — and evaluate the internal control over recording of sales and receipt of cash.

**2. Test the completeness of sales.**
- For **in-patients**, trace a sample of medicines issued (per the ward indents / patients' treatment records) to the **charge posted in the patient's bill**, ensuring medicines supplied are billed and not omitted.
- For **cash/walk-in sales**, verify that every sale is supported by a **serially numbered cash memo/invoice**, test the numerical continuity of the invoices, and reconcile daily sales with the **cash/UPI collections** deposited.

**3. Verify pricing.** Check that medicines have been sold at the **approved price/MRP list**, and that any **discount or free/concessional supply** is duly authorised.

**4. Reconcile stock movement (analytical check).** Perform a **quantitative reconciliation**: Opening stock + Purchases − Closing stock = Quantity that should have been sold/issued; compare this with the **recorded sales/issues** and investigate any material **shortage** (which may indicate unrecorded sales or pilferage).

**5. Verify closing stock.** Attend/verify the **physical stock-take** of medicines, agree it to the stock records, and ensure that **expired, damaged or slow-moving** medicines are identified and appropriately **written down / provided for**.

**6. Purchases.** Vouch **purchases of medicines** with suppliers' invoices and goods-received records to ensure recorded purchases are genuine and complete (so that the reconciliation in step 4 is reliable).

**Conclusion:** By evaluating internal control, tracing in-patient issues to bills and cash sales to serially numbered memos, verifying pricing, and performing a purchases-to-sales **quantitative stock reconciliation** supported by physical verification (including expiry checks), the auditor can satisfy himself as to the completeness and correctness of the hospital's pharmacy revenue and stock.

*(Full-marks tip: The quantitative reconciliation (opening + purchases − closing = sold) and the in-patient-issue-to-bill trace are the completeness procedures examiners reward. Mention expired-stock write-down — specific to a pharmacy.)*

### Q98. Ch: Audit of Different Entities — Hotel: Room Occupancy vs Revenue (Marks: 4) [Application]
**Question:** As the auditor of "Grand Palace Hotel", you wish to verify that **room revenue** for the year is **complete**. The hotel has 100 rooms and a published tariff card. Describe the specific audit procedures you would carry out to verify the completeness of room revenue.

**Answer:**
Room revenue is the principal revenue stream of a hotel and is exposed to the risk that occupied rooms are **not billed** or are billed at a **lower-than-tariff** rate. To verify its completeness, the auditor should carry out the following procedures:

**1. Obtain the occupancy records.** Obtain the **housekeeping occupancy reports** and the **guest register / arrival-and-departure records**, which independently record which rooms were physically occupied on each night.

**2. Reconcile occupancy to billing.** For a sample of dates, **reconcile the rooms shown as occupied** in the housekeeping report with the **rooms billed** in the guest ledger / night-audit report, and investigate any room shown occupied but **not billed** (a possible revenue leakage) or any room billed as vacant.

**3. Verify the tariff applied.** Check the **room rate charged** on a sample of guest bills against the **approved tariff card**, ensuring guests are charged the correct rate and that any **discount, upgrade or complimentary stay** is supported by proper authorisation.

**4. Re-perform the night-audit reconciliation.** Test the hotel's **night-audit** procedure — the daily reconciliation of rooms available, occupied and vacant, and of revenue posted — and agree the day's room revenue to the amounts posted to guest accounts and to the collections/receivables.

**5. Analytical review.** Compute the **occupancy percentage and average room rate (ARR)** for the period and compare with the prior period / budget; investigate significant unexplained variations that could indicate unrecorded revenue.

*(OR — a useful cross-check: Room revenue for a period ≈ No. of rooms × No. of nights × Occupancy % × Average room rate; a large gap between this expected figure and the recorded room revenue signals possible leakage to be investigated.)*

**Conclusion:** By reconciling **independent occupancy records to the rooms billed**, verifying the **tariff** charged, re-performing the **night-audit** reconciliation, and applying an **occupancy/ARR analytical review**, the auditor obtains assurance that room revenue is complete.

*(Full-marks tip: The reconciliation of housekeeping occupancy to the billing records is the key completeness procedure — an auditor who only "vouches a few bills" misses under-recorded rooms. The occupancy% × ARR analytical cross-check earns the extra mark.)*

### Q99. Ch: Audit of Different Entities — Partnership Firm: Admission of a Partner (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** You are auditing the accounts of M/s Sharma & Associates, a partnership firm, for a year in which a **new partner was admitted** on 1st October. State the special matters you would verify in relation to this **admission of a partner** and the related adjustments in the firm's accounts.

**Answer:**
Where a partner is admitted during the year, the auditor — whose scope is fixed by the terms of engagement — must verify that the admission and the consequential accounting adjustments have been carried out in accordance with the **Partnership Deed / the agreement of admission** and the general principles governing a change in the firm's constitution. The special matters to verify are:

**1. Authority and terms of admission.** Examine the **new/reconstituted Partnership Deed or the admission agreement** to ascertain the terms on which the new partner was admitted — the **capital to be brought in**, the **new profit-sharing ratio**, the **effective date**, and any premium (goodwill) payable — and confirm that the existing partners consented to the admission.

**2. Capital introduced.** Verify that the **capital agreed to be contributed** by the incoming partner has actually been **brought into the firm** (cash/asset) and correctly credited to his capital account.

**3. Goodwill.** Verify the treatment of **goodwill** — whether the incoming partner brought in a premium for goodwill, and that it has been **credited to the old partners in their sacrificing ratio** as per the agreement, and accounted for in the manner agreed (e.g., not raised in the books unless agreed).

**4. Revaluation of assets and liabilities.** Verify that, as at the date of admission, the **assets and liabilities were revalued** (if so agreed) and that the resulting **profit or loss on revaluation was shared among the OLD partners in their old ratio** — the incoming partner not being entitled to it.

**5. Reserves and accumulated profits.** Verify that any **undistributed reserves / accumulated profits (or losses)** existing at the date of admission have been **distributed among the old partners in the old ratio** before the new ratio takes effect.

**6. Apportionment of the year's profit.** Verify that the profit for the year has been **apportioned between the pre-admission and post-admission periods** and divided in the **old ratio up to 30th September** and in the **new ratio thereafter**, in accordance with the Deed.

**Conclusion:** The auditor should verify, against the admission agreement/reconstituted Deed, that the incoming partner's **capital and goodwill** were duly brought in, that **revaluation profit/loss and accumulated reserves** were adjusted among the **old partners in the old ratio**, and that the year's profit was **apportioned pre- and post-admission** in the correct ratios.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the "old partners in old ratio" adjustments (revaluation, reserves, goodwill in sacrificing ratio) and the pre-/post-admission apportionment of profit — merely saying "verify capital" is not enough.)*

### Q100. Ch: Professional Ethics & Independence — Familiarity & Intimidation Threats (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Analyse each of the following independent situations, identify the **specific threat** to the auditor's independence involved (from the categories recognised in the Code of Ethics), and suggest a suitable **safeguard**:
(i) CA. Suresh has been the statutory auditor of a company **continuously for the last fifteen years**, and the finance director of the company is his **close personal friend** since college.
(ii) The management of an audit client threatens CA. Suresh that if he **modifies his audit opinion**, the firm will be **removed and its long-standing consultancy engagement withdrawn**.
(iii) The audit fee of one client constitutes **almost 45% of the total fees** of CA. Suresh's firm.

**Answer:**
The **Code of Ethics** issued by ICAI recognises five categories of threats to independence — **self-interest, self-review, advocacy, familiarity and intimidation** — and requires the member to identify the threat, evaluate its significance, and apply **safeguards** to reduce it to an acceptable level.

| Situation | Threat identified | Explanation | Suggested safeguard |
|---|---|---|---|
| (i) 15-year tenure + close personal friendship with the finance director | **Familiarity threat** | A long association and a close personal relationship may make the auditor too **sympathetic to the client's interests / too accepting of its work**, impairing professional scepticism. | **Rotate** the engagement/senior personnel, and provide for an **independent (engagement quality) review** of the audit by a professional not involved in the engagement. |
| (ii) Management threatens removal / withdrawal of work if the opinion is modified | **Intimidation threat** | The auditor may be **deterred from acting objectively** by actual or perceived pressures (threat of dismissal / loss of the consultancy engagement). | **Do not yield** to the pressure; refer the matter to those charged with governance, seek legal/Institute guidance, and, if the threat cannot be managed, **withdraw** from the engagement. |
| (iii) One client's fee ≈ 45% of the firm's total fees | **Self-interest threat** | **Undue dependence** on the fees of a single client (a financial interest in retaining it) may make the auditor reluctant to take a position adverse to the client. | Reduce dependence by **broadening the client base**, arrange an **external/independent review** of the engagement, and, for public-interest entities, monitor the fee dependency and take remedial steps where it is excessive. |

**Conclusion:** Situation (i) involves a **familiarity threat**, situation (ii) an **intimidation threat**, and situation (iii) a **self-interest threat**; in each case CA. Suresh must evaluate the significance of the threat and apply appropriate safeguards (rotation/independent review, resisting pressure and escalating/withdrawing, and reducing fee dependence, respectively) so that his independence is not compromised.

*(Full-marks tip: Precisely label each threat — long tenure/friendship = familiarity, pressure/threat of removal = intimidation, fee dependence = self-interest — and pair each with a matching safeguard. Mislabelling the fee-dependence case as anything other than self-interest is the usual error.)*
