### Q1. Ch: Nature, Objective & Scope of Audit (SA 200) — True and Fair vs True and Correct (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** An auditor of a manufacturing company remarked that his duty is only to report whether the financial statements are "true and fair" and not whether they are "true and correct." A director disputes this, arguing both expressions mean the same thing. Distinguish between "true and fair" and "true and correct," and explain why the auditor uses the former expression.

**Answer:** The objective of an audit of financial statements, as stated in SA 200 "Overall Objectives of the Independent Auditor and the Conduct of an Audit in Accordance with Standards on Auditing," is to enable the auditor to express an opinion on whether the financial statements are prepared, in all material respects, in accordance with an applicable financial reporting framework. Under Section 143(2) of the Companies Act, 2013 the auditor reports whether the accounts give a "true and fair view," and the two expressions must be carefully distinguished.

The expression "true and correct" implies arithmetical accuracy and exact correspondence of the accounts with the books, records and vouchers to the last rupee. It suggests that every figure is precisely accurate and free from any error whatsoever. In contrast, the expression "true and fair" is wider and more realistic: it means that the financial statements are free from material misstatement and present a fair and honest overall picture of the state of affairs and the results of operations of the entity.

The reasons why the auditor is required to report on "true and fair" and not "true and correct" are as follows. First, financial statements are not a matter of exactitude alone; they contain numerous estimates and matters of judgement — such as provision for depreciation, provision for doubtful debts, valuation of inventory at cost or net realisable value, and provisions for contingencies — which by their very nature cannot be "correct" to the rupee but can only be fair and reasonable. Secondly, an audit is carried out on the basis of test checks and sampling (SA 530), and it provides reasonable assurance and not absolute assurance; hence the auditor cannot vouch for arithmetical exactness. Thirdly, the objective of financial statements is to convey a fair and true reflection of the financial position, which is served by the concept of materiality (SA 320) — immaterial errors do not distort the overall view.

The following table distinguishes the two expressions:

| Basis | True and Correct | True and Fair |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Arithmetical accuracy to the last rupee | Free from material misstatement; fair overall picture |
| Scope | Narrow, focuses on exactness | Wider, focuses on substance and fairness |
| Role of estimates/judgement | Not accommodated | Accommodated, provided reasonable |
| Basis of assurance | Would require 100% verification | Consistent with reasonable assurance and test checks |
| Statutory usage | Not used | Used under Sec. 143 of Companies Act, 2013 |

**Conclusion:** The auditor rightly reports on whether the financial statements show a "true and fair view," because that expression realistically reflects the nature of financial statements, the use of estimates and the concept of materiality; the director's contention that the two are identical is incorrect.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the four distinct reasons (estimates/judgement, test-check basis, materiality, reasonable assurance) plus the comparison table; a common deduction is treating this only as a definition without explaining WHY "correct" is impractical.)*

### Q2. Ch: Nature, Objective & Scope of Audit (SA 200) — Advantages of Independent Audit to Stakeholders (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** "An independent audit, though not compulsory for many entities, is of great advantage to a wide range of persons connected with the entity." Explain the advantages of an independent audit to (i) the government and tax authorities, (ii) lenders/financial institutions, (iii) prospective investors, and (iv) settlement of disputes and claims.

**Answer:** An audit is the independent examination of financial information of an entity, whether profit-oriented or not, and irrespective of its size or legal form, with a view to expressing an opinion thereon. Even where audit is not statutorily compulsory, the independent, unbiased opinion of a professional auditor lends credibility to the financial statements and is therefore of great advantage to numerous stakeholders, as explained below.

(i) To the Government and tax authorities — Audited accounts are generally accepted by the income-tax, GST and other tax authorities as a reliable basis for the computation and assessment of tax liability, because they have been verified by an independent professional. This reduces disputes and expedites assessments. Audited financial statements are also relied upon by the Government while granting subsidies, licences, sanctioning grants and framing economic policy.

(ii) To lenders and financial institutions — Banks and financial institutions rely on audited financial statements to assess the financial position, solvency, profitability and repaying capacity of a borrower before sanctioning or renewing loans, cash-credit limits and other facilities. Audited accounts give the lender confidence that the figures of assets, liabilities and profits are free from material misstatement.

(iii) To prospective investors — A person intending to invest in, or purchase, a business can rely on audited accounts to judge the financial health, earning capacity and net worth of the entity. The independent audit gives the intending investor an assurance that the accounts fairly reflect the position, enabling a more informed decision and a fair negotiation of price.

(iv) For settlement of disputes and claims — Audited accounts serve as reliable evidence for the settlement of accounts on the admission, retirement or death of a partner, for the valuation of shares, for the settlement of insurance claims arising out of fire, loss of stock or loss of profit, and for the settlement of trade and tax disputes. The independent verification lends authority to the figures put forward.

Additional advantages accrue to the owners (assurance that accounts are properly maintained), to management (detection and deterrence of errors and fraud, and expert advice on internal controls), and to employees (for settlement of bonus and wage claims linked to profits).

**Conclusion:** Because an audit provides an independent, credible and unbiased opinion on the financial statements, it confers significant advantages on the Government, lenders, investors, and parties to disputes and claims, quite apart from any statutory compulsion.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards naming a distinct advantage for EACH stakeholder asked with a one-line justification; a common deduction is listing generic "detection of fraud/errors" only and ignoring the specific stakeholders named in the question.)*

### Q3. Ch: Nature, Objective & Scope of Audit (SA 200) — Relationship of Auditing with Other Disciplines (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Auditing is not a self-contained subject; it draws upon and interacts with several other disciplines. Explain the relationship of auditing with (i) accounting, (ii) law, and (iii) behavioural science, bringing out why knowledge of these disciplines is essential for an auditor.

**Answer:** Auditing, as a discipline, is inter-related with and dependent upon various other fields of knowledge, since the auditor is required to examine transactions and events that are recorded, governed and influenced by those disciplines. SA 200 requires the auditor to exercise professional judgement and to possess the competence and capabilities to perform the audit, which necessarily involves knowledge drawn from allied disciplines.

(i) Relationship with Accounting — Auditing and accounting are very closely related. Accounting is the recording, classification and summarisation of transactions and the preparation of financial statements, whereas auditing is the critical examination of those very statements to express an opinion on them. Auditing begins where accounting ends. An auditor must have a thorough knowledge of accounting principles, the accounting standards (AS) and generally accepted accounting practices, because unless he understands how the accounts have been prepared he cannot judge whether they present a true and fair view. Thus, accounting is the language in which the subject-matter of audit is expressed.

(ii) Relationship with Law — There is a very close relationship between auditing and law. An audit of financial statements requires the auditor to have a good working knowledge of the various laws affecting the entity — the Companies Act, 2013, tax laws, the laws relating to contracts, negotiable instruments, and other applicable statutes. Many transactions and their proper accounting are governed by legal provisions, and the auditor must ascertain whether the entity has complied with the requirements of the relevant law and whether the financial statements are drawn up in accordance with the applicable statutory framework (for example, Schedule III of the Companies Act, 2013).

(iii) Relationship with Behavioural Science — The auditor, in the course of the audit, deals extensively with human beings — the directors, management, accounting personnel and others. Knowledge of human behaviour is essential for the effective discharge of audit duties, because the auditor has to obtain information through enquiry, evaluate responses, and appreciate the internal control environment which is heavily dependent on the attitude and integrity of people. Understanding behavioural science helps the auditor in effective communication, in evaluating the reliability of representations, and in exercising professional scepticism.

The auditor's work is also related to other disciplines such as economics (understanding the entity and its environment), statistics and mathematics (audit sampling, analytical procedures), data processing and information technology (audit in an automated environment), and financial management (interpretation of ratios and financial health).

**Conclusion:** Auditing is intimately connected with accounting, law and behavioural science, and a sound knowledge of these allied disciplines is indispensable for the auditor to plan and perform the audit effectively and to form a sound opinion.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards a clear "auditing begins where accounting ends" point plus one sound reason each for law and behavioural science; a common deduction is confusing accounting with auditing or omitting the "why the auditor needs it" justification.)*

### Q4. Ch: Nature, Objective & Scope of Audit (SA 200) — Inherent Limitations of an Audit (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** An audit conducted in accordance with the Standards on Auditing cannot provide absolute assurance that the financial statements are free from material misstatement. Explain the inherent limitations of an audit as recognised by SA 200 that prevent the auditor from obtaining such absolute assurance.

**Answer:** SA 200 "Overall Objectives of the Independent Auditor and the Conduct of an Audit in Accordance with Standards on Auditing" clarifies that the auditor is not expected to, and cannot, reduce audit risk to zero and therefore cannot obtain absolute assurance. Consequently, an audit provides only reasonable assurance — a high but not absolute level of assurance. This is because there are inherent limitations in an audit which arise from the following factors.

1. The nature of financial reporting — The preparation of financial statements involves the exercise of judgement by management and involves subjective decisions and assessments, together with a degree of inherent uncertainty. Many items involve estimates (such as accounting estimates for fair value, provisions) about which reasonable differences of opinion may exist, so the accounts are not capable of being "certified" as absolutely accurate.

2. The nature of audit procedures — There are practical and legal limitations on the auditor's ability to obtain audit evidence. Management or others may not provide, intentionally or unintentionally, the complete information required. Fraud may involve sophisticated and carefully organised schemes designed to conceal it, such as collusion, forgery, deliberate failure to record transactions, or intentional misrepresentations made to the auditor; the auditor is neither trained as, nor expected to be, an expert in the authentication of documents. Further, an audit is not an official investigation into alleged wrongdoing and the auditor is not given specific legal powers of search.

3. Timeliness of financial reporting and the balance between benefit and cost — There is an expectation that the auditor will form an opinion within a reasonable period of time and at a reasonable cost. It is neither practicable to address all information that may exist nor to pursue every matter exhaustively on the assumption that information is in error or fraudulent. Accordingly, the auditor uses test checking and audit sampling (SA 530) rather than examining 100% of transactions.

4. Other matters affecting inherent limitations — In respect of certain assertions or subject matters, the potential effects of the limitations are particularly significant, for example matters involving fraud (especially management fraud), the existence and completeness of related party relationships and transactions, the occurrence of non-compliance with laws and regulations, and future events or conditions that may cause the entity to cease to continue as a going concern.

Because of these inherent limitations, there is an unavoidable risk that some material misstatements may not be detected, even though the audit is properly planned and performed in accordance with the Standards on Auditing.

**Conclusion:** Owing to the inherent limitations flowing from the nature of financial reporting, the nature of audit procedures, and the constraints of time and cost, an audit can furnish only reasonable — and not absolute — assurance that the financial statements are free from material misstatement.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards linking each limitation back to "reasonable and not absolute assurance" and the specific SA 200 heads; a common deduction is listing only "sampling" and "estimates" while omitting the fraud/collusion and cost-benefit dimensions.)*

### Q5. Ch: Nature, Objective & Scope of Audit (SA 200) — Scope of Audit / Aspects to be Covered (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** The scope of an audit of financial statements is determined by the auditor having regard to certain matters. Explain the factors that determine the scope of an audit and the principal aspects that the auditor should cover in an audit of financial statements.

**Answer:** The scope of an audit refers to the extent and the boundaries of the audit work that the auditor undertakes in order to form an opinion on the financial statements. As explained in the framework governing the conduct of an audit, the scope of an audit of financial statements is determined by the auditor having regard to (a) the requirements of the relevant legislation (such as the Companies Act, 2013), (b) the pronouncements of the Institute of Chartered Accountants of India, including the Standards on Auditing, and (c) the terms of the engagement. However, the terms of engagement cannot restrict the scope of an audit in relation to matters which are prescribed by legislation or by the pronouncements of the Institute.

In determining the scope, the audit should be organised to cover adequately all aspects of the entity relevant to the financial statements. The auditor should be reasonably satisfied that the information contained in the underlying accounting records and other source data is reliable and sufficient as the basis for the preparation of the financial statements. In forming his opinion, the auditor should cover the following principal aspects:

1. Examination of the accounting system and internal controls — to assess their adequacy and to determine the nature, timing and extent of audit procedures.
2. Verification that the information in the underlying records is complete, arithmetically accurate and properly posted and balanced.
3. Verification of assets and liabilities — as to their existence, ownership, valuation and disclosure.
4. Verification that transactions are properly authorised and correctly recorded, and that there is no unauthorised or improper transaction.
5. Comparison of the financial statements with the underlying accounting records to see that they are in agreement.
6. Determination that the financial statements are drawn up in accordance with the applicable financial reporting framework and comply with the relevant statutory requirements.
7. Verification that the accounting policies are appropriate, consistently applied and adequately disclosed, and that there is proper disclosure of all material matters.

The auditor assesses the reliability and sufficiency of the information by (i) making a study and evaluation of the accounting system and internal controls and (ii) carrying out such other tests, enquiries and verification procedures of the transactions and the balances as he considers appropriate.

**Conclusion:** The scope of an audit is governed by legislation, the pronouncements of the ICAI and the engagement terms (which cannot curtail the statutory or professional scope), and it requires the auditor to cover comprehensively the reliability of records, the verification of transactions and balances, and the fair presentation and disclosure in the financial statements.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards stating the three determinants of scope AND the "engagement terms cannot restrict statutory scope" rider, plus the principal aspects covered; a common deduction is answering only the determinants without the aspects, or vice versa.)*

### Q6. Ch: Nature, Objective & Scope of Audit (SA 200) — Ethical Requirements & Professional Scepticism (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** SA 200 requires the auditor to comply with relevant ethical requirements and to plan and perform an audit with professional scepticism. Explain the meaning of "professional scepticism" and the situations in which its maintenance is particularly necessary.

**Answer:** SA 200 "Overall Objectives of the Independent Auditor and the Conduct of an Audit in Accordance with Standards on Auditing" requires the auditor to plan and perform an audit with an attitude of professional scepticism, recognising that circumstances may exist that cause the financial statements to be materially misstated.

Professional scepticism is an attitude that includes a questioning mind, being alert to conditions which may indicate possible misstatement due to error or fraud, and a critical assessment of audit evidence. It means that the auditor does not simply accept the representations of management at face value; instead, he critically evaluates the reliability and sufficiency of the evidence obtained and remains alert to evidence that contradicts or brings into question the reliability of documents and responses to enquiries.

Maintaining professional scepticism is particularly necessary in the following situations:
1. Audit evidence that contradicts other audit evidence obtained.
2. Information that brings into question the reliability of documents and responses to enquiries to be used as audit evidence.
3. Conditions that may indicate possible fraud.
4. Circumstances that suggest the need for audit procedures in addition to those required by the Standards on Auditing.

Professional scepticism reduces the risks of overlooking unusual circumstances, of over-generalising when drawing conclusions from audit observations, and of using inappropriate assumptions in determining the nature, timing and extent of audit procedures. It is necessary throughout the audit, for example when assessing management's estimates and judgements, and it helps guard against the danger of the auditor being too easily satisfied.

**Conclusion:** Professional scepticism is a questioning, alert and critical attitude of mind that the auditor must maintain throughout the audit, and it is especially vital where evidence is contradictory, where the reliability of documents is in doubt, or where conditions indicative of fraud exist.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards a precise definition ("questioning mind... critical assessment of evidence") plus the four SA 200 situations; a common deduction is confusing scepticism with professional judgement, which is a separate concept.)*

### Q7. Ch: Nature, Objective & Scope of Audit (SA 200) — Application: Detection of Fraud vs Objective of Audit (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** A material fraud perpetrated by the finance manager through collusion with a supplier remained undetected in the statutory audit of ABC Ltd. On discovery a year later, the management alleges that the auditor was negligent because "detection of fraud is the primary object of an audit." Examine the correctness of the management's contention with reference to SA 200 and the auditor's responsibility relating to fraud.

**Answer:** The objective of an audit of financial statements, as laid down in SA 200 "Overall Objectives of the Independent Auditor and the Conduct of an Audit in Accordance with Standards on Auditing," is to obtain reasonable assurance about whether the financial statements as a whole are free from material misstatement, whether caused by fraud or error, and thereby to express an opinion on whether the financial statements are prepared, in all material respects, in accordance with the applicable financial reporting framework.

It follows that the detection of fraud is not the primary object of an audit; the primary object is the expression of an opinion on the true and fair view of the financial statements. Detection of material misstatement (whether arising from fraud or error) is incidental to, and a by-product of, that primary objective. The management's contention that "detection of fraud is the primary object of an audit" is therefore not correct as a statement of the auditor's fundamental objective.

Regarding the auditor's responsibility for fraud, SA 240 "The Auditor's Responsibilities Relating to Fraud in an Audit of Financial Statements" provides that the primary responsibility for the prevention and detection of fraud rests with those charged with governance and the management of the entity. The auditor is responsible for obtaining reasonable — not absolute — assurance that the financial statements taken as a whole are free from material misstatement, whether caused by fraud or error, and he must maintain professional scepticism throughout the audit.

However, owing to the inherent limitations of an audit recognised in SA 200, there is an unavoidable risk that some material misstatements may not be detected, even though the audit is properly planned and performed. This risk is higher in the case of fraud than error, because fraud may involve sophisticated and carefully organised schemes designed to conceal it, such as collusion, forgery, and deliberate failure to record transactions. In the present case, the fraud was committed by the finance manager through collusion with a supplier — precisely the kind of concealed, collusive fraud whose detection is subject to inherent limitations. Therefore, the mere fact that the fraud was subsequently discovered does not, by itself, establish that the auditor was negligent.

Whether the auditor is liable would depend on whether he performed the audit with reasonable care and skill, exercised professional scepticism and complied with the Standards on Auditing (SA 240 in particular). If he did so, he cannot be held guilty of negligence merely because a cleverly concealed collusive fraud escaped detection.

**Conclusion:** The management's contention is incorrect: detection of fraud is not the primary object of an audit, and provided the auditor exercised due care, professional scepticism and complied with the applicable SAs, he is not negligent merely because a concealed collusive fraud remained undetected.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards separating (a) primary objective = opinion, detection is incidental, from (b) primary responsibility for fraud = management (SA 240), and linking the collusion to inherent limitations; a common deduction is concluding on liability without the "reasonable care and skill" test.)*

### Q8. Ch: Audit Strategy, Audit Planning & Materiality (SA 300) — Benefits of Audit Planning (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** "Planning is not a discrete phase of an audit, but rather a continual and iterative process." In this context, explain the benefits that adequate planning of the audit work confers, as recognised in SA 300.

**Answer:** SA 300 "Planning an Audit of Financial Statements" requires the auditor to plan the audit so that it will be performed in an effective manner. Planning involves establishing the overall audit strategy for the engagement and developing an audit plan. The Standard notes that planning is not a discrete phase of an audit, but rather a continual and iterative process that often begins shortly after the completion of the previous audit and continues until the completion of the current engagement. Adequate planning benefits the audit of financial statements in several important ways, as explained below.

1. It helps the auditor to devote appropriate attention to important areas of the audit. By planning, the auditor is able to identify and concentrate his effort on the significant and high-risk areas of the financial statements, rather than dissipating effort uniformly.

2. It helps the auditor to identify and resolve potential problems on a timely basis. Anticipating difficulties — for example, in relation to complex accounting matters, valuation of assets or litigation — allows them to be addressed early instead of at the last moment.

3. It helps the auditor to properly organise and manage the audit engagement so that it is performed in an effective and efficient manner. A well-planned audit avoids duplication of work and wasted effort.

4. It assists in the selection of engagement team members with appropriate levels of capabilities and competence to respond to anticipated risks, and in the proper assignment of work to them.

5. It facilitates the direction and supervision of engagement team members and the review of their work.

6. It assists, where applicable, in the coordination of work done by auditors of components and experts, such as branch auditors and the auditor's experts.

The nature and extent of planning activities will vary according to the size and complexity of the entity, the key engagement team members' previous experience with the entity, and changes in circumstances that occur during the audit engagement.

**Conclusion:** Adequate audit planning benefits the audit by ensuring appropriate attention to important areas, timely identification and resolution of problems, efficient organisation and management of the engagement, proper selection, direction, supervision and review of the team, and effective coordination of the work of component auditors and experts.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards reproducing the six SA 300 benefits as full sentences plus the "continual and iterative process" opening; a common deduction is compressing the six benefits into two or three vague lines.)*

### Q9. Ch: Audit Strategy, Audit Planning & Materiality (SA 300) — Overall Audit Strategy vs Audit Plan (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between the "overall audit strategy" and the "audit plan" under SA 300, and state the relationship between the two.

**Answer:** SA 300 "Planning an Audit of Financial Statements" requires the auditor both to establish an overall audit strategy that sets the scope, timing and direction of the audit, and to develop an audit plan. Although closely related, the two are distinct, as explained below.

The overall audit strategy sets the scope, timing and direction of the audit, and guides the development of the more detailed audit plan. In establishing the overall audit strategy, the auditor shall (a) identify the characteristics of the engagement that define its scope, (b) ascertain the reporting objectives of the engagement to plan the timing of the audit and the nature of the communications required, (c) consider the factors that, in the auditor's professional judgement, are significant in directing the engagement team's efforts, (d) consider the results of preliminary engagement activities, and (e) ascertain the nature, timing and extent of resources necessary to perform the engagement. Thus the strategy is broad and describes, at a general level, how the audit is to be conducted.

The audit plan is more detailed than the overall audit strategy and includes the nature, timing and extent of audit procedures to be performed by the engagement team members. The audit plan shall include a description of (a) the nature, timing and extent of planned risk assessment procedures, as determined under SA 315; (b) the nature, timing and extent of planned further audit procedures at the assertion level, as determined under SA 330; and (c) such other planned audit procedures that are required to be carried out so that the engagement complies with the Standards on Auditing.

The distinction may be summarised as follows:

| Basis | Overall Audit Strategy | Audit Plan |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Broad; sets scope, timing and direction | Detailed; sets out actual procedures |
| Content | Characteristics, reporting objectives, significant factors, resources | Risk assessment procedures, further procedures, other procedures |
| Level | Overall/engagement level | Assertion/procedure level |
| Sequence | Established first | Developed from the strategy |
| Governing SA linkage | Guides the plan | SA 315 and SA 330 procedures |

The relationship between the two is that once the overall audit strategy has been established, the audit plan can be developed to address the various matters identified in the strategy, taking into account the need to achieve the audit objectives through the efficient use of the auditor's resources. The establishment of the strategy and the development of the plan are not necessarily discrete or sequential processes but are closely inter-related, since a change in one may result in a consequential change to the other.

**Conclusion:** The overall audit strategy sets the scope, timing and direction of the audit at a broad level, whereas the audit plan is a more detailed programme of the specific procedures to be performed; the plan flows from, and is inter-related with, the strategy.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the table plus the point that plan flows from strategy and that the two are inter-related (a change in one changes the other); a common deduction is giving only definitions without the five contents of the strategy and the three contents of the plan.)*

### Q10. Ch: Audit Strategy, Audit Planning & Materiality (SA 300) — Direction, Supervision and Review (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** In planning an audit, the auditor is required to plan the nature, timing and extent of direction and supervision of engagement team members and the review of their work. State the factors that influence the extent of direction, supervision and review required, and why proper planning of these is important.

**Answer:** SA 300 "Planning an Audit of Financial Statements" requires the auditor to include in the audit plan the nature, timing and extent of the direction and supervision of engagement team members and the review of their work. The purpose is to ensure that the audit is performed effectively and that the work of assistants is properly controlled and evaluated.

The nature, timing and extent of the direction and supervision of engagement team members and review of their work vary depending on many factors, including the following:

1. The size and complexity of the entity — Larger and more complex entities require more extensive direction, supervision and review.
2. The area of the audit — Areas involving higher assessed risks of material misstatement require greater direction and supervision and more detailed review of the work performed.
3. The assessed risks of material misstatement — The higher the assessed risk, the greater the extent of direction, supervision and review.
4. The capabilities and competence of the individual team members performing the audit work — Less experienced team members require closer direction and supervision and more detailed review of their work, whereas experienced members require relatively less.

The auditor plans the nature, timing and extent of direction and supervision of engagement team members on the basis of these factors. For example, if the engagement team includes members with less experience, the auditor may plan for more detailed direction and supervision and a more comprehensive review of their work. Proper planning of direction, supervision and review is important because it ensures that the audit work is carried out to the appropriate standard, that the audit evidence obtained is sufficient and appropriate, that errors in the work of assistants are detected and corrected, and that the audit objectives are ultimately met. It also facilitates the timely completion of the audit and supports the overall quality control over the engagement (consistent with SA 220 and SQC 1).

**Conclusion:** The extent of direction, supervision and review depends on the size and complexity of the entity, the audit area, the assessed risks of material misstatement, and the competence of the team members; proper planning of these is essential to ensure the effective and high-quality performance of the audit.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the four SA 300 factors plus the inverse relationship "less experienced = more supervision"; a common deduction is not linking direction/supervision to audit quality and SA 220/SQC 1.)*

### Q11. Ch: Audit Strategy, Audit Planning & Materiality (SA 300) — Changes to Planning Decisions During the Audit (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** SA 300 recognises that the overall audit strategy and the audit plan may need to be modified during the course of the audit. Explain, with a suitable example, the circumstances in which the auditor may need to modify the overall audit strategy and audit plan, and the auditor's responsibility when such modification is made.

**Answer:** SA 300 "Planning an Audit of Financial Statements" provides that the auditor shall update and change the overall audit strategy and the audit plan as necessary during the course of the audit. Planning is a continual and iterative process, and as a result of unexpected events, changes in conditions, or the audit evidence obtained from the performance of audit procedures, the auditor may need to modify the overall audit strategy and audit plan and thereby the resulting planned nature, timing and extent of further audit procedures.

Such modification may become necessary in circumstances such as the following:
1. Where information comes to the auditor's attention that differs significantly from the information available when the audit procedures were planned. For example, audit evidence obtained through substantive procedures may contradict the audit evidence obtained through tests of controls, indicating that controls are not operating effectively as originally assumed.
2. Where the auditor obtains new information about the entity indicating a higher (or lower) assessed risk of material misstatement than originally assessed.
3. Where events occur, such as a significant change in the entity's business, the discovery of a possible fraud, or a change in accounting policy, that were not anticipated when the plan was drawn up.

When such a change occurs, the auditor's responsibility is to reconsider and, where necessary, revise the assessed risks of material misstatement and to modify the further audit procedures accordingly, so that sufficient appropriate audit evidence is obtained. For instance, if audit evidence indicates that internal controls over sales are not operating effectively, the auditor should modify the audit plan to increase the extent of substantive procedures on sales transactions. The auditor should also document the significant changes made to the overall audit strategy and the audit plan and the reasons for such changes, as required by SA 300.

**Conclusion:** The auditor must update and change the overall audit strategy and the audit plan whenever unexpected events, changed conditions or new audit evidence so require, revise the planned procedures accordingly to obtain sufficient appropriate evidence, and document the changes and the reasons for them.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards a concrete example (controls found ineffective → increase substantive procedures) plus the documentation requirement; a common deduction is not mentioning that changes and reasons must be documented.)*

### Q12. Ch: Audit Strategy, Audit Planning & Materiality (SA 300) — Preliminary Engagement Activities & Knowledge of Client's Business (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Before commencing the detailed audit, the auditor performs certain preliminary engagement activities and acquires knowledge of the client's business. State the preliminary engagement activities required by SA 300 and explain why acquiring knowledge of the client's business is essential for planning the audit.

**Answer:** SA 300 "Planning an Audit of Financial Statements" requires the auditor to undertake certain preliminary engagement activities at the beginning of the current audit engagement. The performance of these activities enables the auditor to identify and evaluate events or circumstances that may adversely affect the auditor's ability to plan and perform the engagement.

The auditor shall undertake the following preliminary engagement activities at the beginning of the current audit engagement:
1. Performing procedures required by SA 220 "Quality Control for an Audit of Financial Statements" regarding the continuance of the client relationship and the specific audit engagement — that is, evaluating compliance with relevant ethical requirements and the integrity of the client.
2. Evaluating compliance with relevant ethical requirements, including independence, in accordance with SA 220.
3. Establishing an understanding of the terms of the engagement, as required by SA 210 "Agreeing the Terms of Audit Engagements."

Considering these matters at the beginning of the engagement assists the auditor in maintaining the necessary independence and ability to perform the engagement, ensuring that there are no issues with management integrity that may affect willingness to continue the engagement, and ensuring that there is no misunderstanding with the client as to the terms of the engagement.

Acquiring knowledge of the client's business is essential for planning the audit for the following reasons. An understanding of the entity and its environment enables the auditor to identify events, transactions and practices that may have a significant effect on the financial statements, to assess the risks of material misstatement, to determine areas requiring special audit consideration, to evaluate the reasonableness of accounting estimates and management representations, and to identify related parties and unusual transactions. Without an adequate knowledge of the client's business, the auditor cannot establish a meaningful overall audit strategy or develop an effective audit plan, nor can he exercise sound professional judgement in evaluating audit evidence.

**Conclusion:** The preliminary engagement activities under SA 300 comprise the client continuance procedures, evaluation of ethical requirements including independence, and establishing the terms of the engagement; and a sound knowledge of the client's business is indispensable, since it underlies the assessment of risk and the entire planning of the audit.

### Q13. Ch: Audit Strategy, Audit Planning & Materiality (SA 320) — Concept of Materiality & Materiality in Context (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the concept of "materiality" as understood in the context of an audit under SA 320, and state how the auditor's determination of materiality is a matter of professional judgement.

**Answer:** SA 320 "Materiality in Planning and Performing an Audit" deals with the auditor's responsibility to apply the concept of materiality in planning and performing an audit of financial statements. Although financial reporting frameworks often discuss materiality in the context of the preparation and presentation of financial statements, SA 320 explains materiality from the standpoint of the auditor.

Financial reporting frameworks generally explain the concept of materiality as follows: misstatements, including omissions, are considered to be material if they, individually or in the aggregate, could reasonably be expected to influence the economic decisions of users taken on the basis of the financial statements; judgements about materiality are made in the light of surrounding circumstances and are affected by the size or nature of a misstatement, or a combination of both; and judgements about matters that are material to users of the financial statements are based on a consideration of the common financial information needs of users as a group, and the possible effect of misstatements on specific individual users, whose needs may vary widely, is not considered.

The auditor's determination of materiality is a matter of professional judgement, and is affected by the auditor's perception of the financial information needs of the users of the financial statements. In making this judgement, it is reasonable for the auditor to assume that users:
1. Have a reasonable knowledge of business and economic activities and accounting, and a willingness to study the information with reasonable diligence;
2. Understand that financial statements are prepared, presented and audited to levels of materiality;
3. Recognise the uncertainties inherent in the measurement of amounts based on the use of estimates, judgement and the consideration of future events; and
4. Make reasonable economic decisions on the basis of the information in the financial statements.

The concept of materiality is applied by the auditor both in planning and performing the audit, and in evaluating the effect of identified misstatements on the audit and of uncorrected misstatements, if any, on the financial statements.

**Conclusion:** Materiality is the threshold above which misstatements, individually or in aggregate, could reasonably be expected to influence the economic decisions of users; its determination is a matter of the auditor's professional judgement, exercised on the reasonable assumptions about users set out in SA 320.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the "influence economic decisions of users" definition PLUS the four assumptions about users; a common deduction is omitting that materiality is judged by "size or nature or both" and by the needs of users as a group, not individual users.)*

### Q14. Ch: Audit Strategy, Audit Planning & Materiality (SA 320) — Materiality vs Performance Materiality (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between "materiality for the financial statements as a whole" and "performance materiality" under SA 320, and explain the purpose of setting performance materiality.

**Answer:** SA 320 "Materiality in Planning and Performing an Audit" requires the auditor, when establishing the overall audit strategy, to determine materiality for the financial statements as a whole and also to determine performance materiality. Both are distinct amounts serving distinct purposes.

Materiality for the financial statements as a whole is the amount set by the auditor at the planning stage, above which misstatements, individually or in the aggregate, could reasonably be expected to influence the economic decisions of users taken on the basis of the financial statements. If, in the specific circumstances of the entity, there is one or more particular classes of transactions, account balances or disclosures for which misstatements of lesser amounts than the materiality for the financial statements as a whole could reasonably be expected to influence the economic decisions of users, the auditor shall also determine the materiality level or levels to be applied to those particular classes, balances or disclosures.

Performance materiality means the amount or amounts set by the auditor at less than the materiality for the financial statements as a whole to reduce to an appropriately low level the probability that the aggregate of uncorrected and undetected misstatements exceeds the materiality for the financial statements as a whole. If applicable, performance materiality also refers to the amount or amounts set by the auditor at less than the materiality level or levels for particular classes of transactions, account balances or disclosures.

The distinction may be tabulated as follows:

| Basis | Materiality for FS as a whole | Performance Materiality |
|---|---|---|
| Amount | Higher threshold | Set at less than materiality for FS as a whole |
| Purpose | Threshold for influencing users' decisions | Buffer against aggregation of undetected/uncorrected misstatements |
| Focus | Financial statements overall | Reducing risk to an acceptably low level |
| Determination | Based on a benchmark and percentage | Based on the auditor's judgement, reflecting risk |

The purpose of setting performance materiality is to provide a margin or cushion so as to reduce, to an appropriately low level, the probability that the aggregate of misstatements that remain undetected (because the audit is based on testing) together with those that are detected but not corrected, exceeds the materiality for the financial statements as a whole. The determination of performance materiality is not a simple mechanical calculation and involves the exercise of professional judgement, being affected by the auditor's understanding of the entity, the nature and extent of misstatements identified in previous audits, and the auditor's expectations in relation to misstatements in the current period.

**Conclusion:** Materiality for the financial statements as a whole is the overall threshold above which users' decisions could be influenced, whereas performance materiality is a lower amount set to build in a buffer against the aggregation of undetected and uncorrected misstatements; the latter safeguards the reliability of the overall materiality judgement.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the precise definition of performance materiality as an amount "set at less than" overall materiality to reduce the aggregation risk; a common deduction is treating performance materiality as merely a percentage of overall materiality without explaining its buffer purpose.)*

### Q15. Ch: Audit Strategy, Audit Planning & Materiality (SA 320) — Revision of Materiality Due to New Information (Marks: 6) [Application]

**WN-1: Original materiality determined at planning stage**
Materiality was computed on budgeted profit before tax:
Materiality = 5% × ₹5,00,00,000 = ₹25,00,000.

**WN-2: Revised materiality based on actual (revised) results**
The actual profit before tax is now ₹3,20,00,000, which differs significantly from the budgeted figure. Applying the same benchmark and percentage:
Revised materiality = 5% × ₹3,20,00,000 = ₹16,00,000.

**WN-3: Effect on performance materiality**
Performance materiality is set at less than the materiality for the financial statements as a whole. If, for illustration, performance materiality had been set at 75% of overall materiality:
- Original performance materiality = 75% × ₹25,00,000 = ₹18,75,000.

|---|---|---|
| Benchmark: Profit before tax | 5,00,00,000 | 3,20,00,000 |
| Percentage applied | 5% | 5% |

2. ### Q16. Ch: Audit Strategy, Audit Planning & Materiality (SA 320) — Use of Benchmarks in Determining Materiality (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** In determining materiality for the financial statements as a whole, the auditor often applies a percentage to a chosen benchmark. Explain the factors that the auditor considers in identifying an appropriate benchmark, and give examples of benchmarks that may be used.

**Answer:** SA 320 "Materiality in Planning and Performing an Audit" states that determining materiality involves the exercise of professional judgement. A percentage is often applied to a chosen benchmark as a starting point in determining materiality for the financial statements as a whole. Identifying an appropriate benchmark is therefore a matter of professional judgement, and the auditor considers the following factors in this regard:

1. The elements of the financial statements — for example, assets, liabilities, equity, revenue and expenses, and which of these tends to attract the attention of the users of the particular entity's financial statements.
2. Whether there are items on which the attention of the users of the particular entity's financial statements tends to be focused — for example, for the purpose of evaluating financial performance, users may tend to focus on profit, revenue or net assets.
3. The nature of the entity, where the entity is in its life cycle, and the industry and economic environment in which the entity operates.
4. The entity's ownership structure and the way it is financed — for example, if an entity is financed solely by debt rather than equity, users may put more emphasis on assets, and claims on them, than on the entity's earnings.
5. The relative volatility of the benchmark — a stable benchmark is generally preferable to a volatile one.

Examples of benchmarks that may be appropriate, depending on the circumstances of the entity, include categories of reported income such as profit before tax, total revenue, gross profit and total expenses, total equity, or net asset value. Profit before tax from continuing operations is often used for profit-oriented entities; however, when profit before tax is volatile, other benchmarks such as gross profit or total revenue may be more appropriate. In relation to the chosen benchmark, relevant financial data ordinarily includes prior periods' financial results and financial positions, the period-to-date financial results and financial position, and budgets or forecasts for the current period, adjusted for significant changes in the circumstances of the entity.

Determining a percentage to be applied to a chosen benchmark also involves the exercise of professional judgement; there is a relationship between the percentage and the chosen benchmark, such that a percentage applied to profit before tax will normally be higher than a percentage applied to total revenue.

**Conclusion:** The auditor selects an appropriate benchmark by exercising professional judgement having regard to the elements of the financial statements, the focus of the users, the nature, life cycle, industry and financing of the entity, and the volatility of the benchmark; profit before tax, revenue, gross profit, total expenses and net assets are examples of benchmarks that may be used.

### Q18. Ch: Risk Assessment & Internal Control — Inherent vs Control vs Detection Risk (Marks: 6) [Application]

**Question:** "Audit risk is a function of the risks of material misstatement and detection risk." CA Meera is auditing *Zenith Ceramics Ltd.*, a company that manufactures decorative tiles, exports a substantial part of its output, and values a large closing stock of work-in-progress on the basis of complex percentage-of-completion estimates. During the year the company's accountant resigned and was not replaced for four months, and bank reconciliations were not prepared during that period. Identify and explain the three components of audit risk, classify each of the above facts under the appropriate component, and state the relationship the auditor must maintain between the components to keep audit risk acceptably low.

**Answer:**
As per **SA 200 "Overall Objectives of the Independent Auditor and the Conduct of an Audit in Accordance with Standards on Auditing"**, *audit risk* is the risk that the auditor expresses an inappropriate audit opinion when the financial statements are materially misstated. Audit risk is a function of the **Risks of Material Misstatement (RMM)** and **Detection Risk**. RMM at the assertion level in turn comprises two components — **inherent risk** and **control risk**. Thus audit risk has three underlying components, explained below.

**(i) Inherent Risk** — This is the susceptibility of an assertion about a class of transactions, account balance or disclosure to a misstatement that could be material, either individually or when aggregated with other misstatements, *before consideration of any related controls*. It is higher for assertions involving complex calculations, estimates and judgement, and for items exposed to external economic factors.

**(ii) Control Risk** — This is the risk that a misstatement that could occur in an assertion and that could be material will *not be prevented, or detected and corrected on a timely basis, by the entity's internal control*. Some control risk will always exist because of the inherent limitations of any internal control system.

**(iii) Detection Risk** — This is the risk that the procedures performed by the auditor to reduce audit risk to an acceptably low level will *not detect a misstatement that exists* and that could be material. It is a function of the effectiveness of the auditor's procedures and their application, and it is the only component the auditor can directly control by altering the nature, timing and extent of audit procedures.

**Classification of the given facts:**

| Fact in the scenario | Component of audit risk | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Complex percentage-of-completion estimate for WIP valuation | Inherent risk | Involves significant management judgement and complex computation, raising susceptibility to misstatement irrespective of controls |
| Substantial export sales (exposure to foreign-exchange fluctuation and cut-off) | Inherent risk | Nature of transactions/external factors increases susceptibility |
| Absence of an accountant for four months; bank reconciliations not prepared | Control risk | The entity's internal control failed to operate, so misstatements may go unprevented/undetected |
| Meera's own choice of extent of substantive testing on the above | Detection risk | Directly controllable by the auditor through her procedures |

**Relationship to be maintained:** Detection risk bears an **inverse relationship** to the assessed RMM. Because inherent risk (estimates, exports) and control risk (control breakdown) are both assessed as **high** here, the assessed RMM is high; to keep audit risk acceptably low, CA Meera must reduce detection risk by performing **more extensive and more effective substantive procedures**, closer to the year-end, using more experienced staff.

**Conclusion:** The WIP estimate and export exposure are inherent-risk factors, the control breakdown is a control-risk factor, and only detection risk is within the auditor's control; given high RMM, detection risk must be driven down through enhanced substantive testing.

*(Full-marks tip: examiners reward the explicit statement that Audit Risk = RMM × Detection Risk and that RMM = Inherent × Control risk. The common deduction is misclassifying the control breakdown as inherent risk, or failing to state the inverse relationship.)*

### Q22. Ch: Audit Evidence — Direction of Testing (Marks: 5) [Application]

**Question:** CA Rohan is planning substantive tests of details for *Vertex Traders Ltd.* He is particularly concerned that (a) some liabilities may be unrecorded, and (b) some recorded fixed assets may not physically exist. Explain the concept of "direction of testing", state the direction Rohan should adopt for each of the two concerns, and identify the assertion each test primarily addresses.

**Answer:**
**Concept:** *Direction of testing* refers to the **starting point** the auditor chooses when tracing a transaction between the accounting records and the source documents (or physical items), because the direction chosen determines *which type of misstatement* — overstatement or understatement — the test can detect. Two directions are possible:

- **Vouching (records → source/physical):** starting from the recorded entry in the books and tracing back to the supporting document or physical item. This detects **overstatement** and primarily tests the **existence / occurrence** assertion.
- **Tracing (source/physical → records):** starting from a source document or physical item and following it forward into the books. This detects **understatement / omission** and primarily tests the **completeness** assertion.

**Application to Rohan's two concerns:**

| Concern | Risk feared | Direction to adopt | Starting point → destination | Assertion tested |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| (a) Unrecorded liabilities | Understatement (omission) | **Trace** | From subsequent payments, unpaid invoices, GRNs after year-end → into the purchase/creditors ledger | **Completeness** of liabilities |
| (b) Recorded fixed assets may not exist | Overstatement | **Vouch** | From the fixed-asset register/ledger → to physical inspection of the asset | **Existence** of assets |

**Explanation:** To find *unrecorded* liabilities, Rohan must not begin from the books (an omitted item is not there to be found) — he must begin from *external evidence outside the books*, such as post-year-end payments and vendors' statements, and trace them into the ledger; anything not recorded is an unrecorded liability. Conversely, to confirm that recorded assets exist, he begins from the *asset register* and inspects the corresponding physical asset; any recorded asset that cannot be located is an overstatement.

**Conclusion:** For unrecorded liabilities, Rohan should trace from source documents into the records (completeness); for possibly non-existent assets, he should vouch from the records to the physical asset (existence). Choosing the wrong direction would leave the very risk he fears undetected.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner rewards linking overstatement→vouching→existence and understatement→tracing→completeness. Reversing the two directions is the classic error that loses most of the marks.)*

### Q23. Ch: Risk Assessment & Internal Control — Risk Assessment Procedures (Marks: 6) [Theory]

**Question:** SA 315 requires the auditor to perform "risk assessment procedures" to obtain an understanding of the entity and its environment, including its internal control. Explain what risk assessment procedures are, enumerate the procedures the auditor must perform, and state why risk assessment procedures alone do not provide a sufficient basis for the audit opinion.

**Answer:**
As per **SA 315 "Identifying and Assessing the Risks of Material Misstatement Through Understanding the Entity and Its Environment"**, **risk assessment procedures** are the audit procedures performed to obtain an understanding of the entity and its environment, including the entity's internal control, in order to **identify and assess the risks of material misstatement**, whether due to fraud or error, at the financial statement and assertion levels.

**Procedures the auditor must perform** — The risk assessment procedures shall include the following:

**(i) Inquiries of management and of others within the entity** — Inquiries directed to management, those responsible for financial reporting, internal audit, and others such as production, sales or legal personnel who may have information helpful in identifying risks of material misstatement.

**(ii) Analytical procedures** — Performed as risk assessment procedures to identify unusual or unexpected relationships, aspects of the entity of which the auditor was unaware, and areas that may indicate risks of material misstatement.

**(iii) Observation and inspection** — Such as observation of the entity's operations, inspection of documents, records and internal control manuals, and reading reports prepared by management and those charged with governance, and visits to premises and plant facilities.

The auditor must also **consider whether information obtained from client acceptance/continuance or from other engagements** is relevant, and, where the engagement is a **recurring** one, consider information obtained in **prior periods**. The engagement partner and key team members must also **discuss the susceptibility of the financial statements to material misstatement** (the "engagement team discussion").

**Why risk assessment procedures alone are not sufficient:** Risk assessment procedures by themselves **do not provide sufficient appropriate audit evidence on which to base the audit opinion**. They only establish *where* misstatements are likely to arise. The auditor must therefore supplement them with **further audit procedures** in the form of **tests of controls** (where the auditor intends to rely on the operating effectiveness of controls) and **substantive procedures** (tests of details and substantive analytical procedures) as required by SA 330.

**Conclusion:** Risk assessment procedures comprise inquiry, analytical procedures, and observation and inspection; they enable the auditor to identify and assess risks but must be followed by further audit procedures under SA 330 to obtain the evidence necessary to support the opinion.

**Question:** Under SA 330, when does the auditor perform tests of controls, and what does he test? Also state when the auditor is *required* to test the operating effectiveness of controls irrespective of his own choice.

**Answer:**
As per **SA 330 "The Auditor's Responses to Assessed Risks"**, having assessed the risks of material misstatement, the auditor must design and implement responses that are commensurate with those risks. One category of "further audit procedures" is **tests of controls**.

**Meaning and purpose:** A *test of controls* is an audit procedure designed to evaluate the **operating effectiveness** of controls in preventing, or detecting and correcting, material misstatements at the assertion level.

**When the auditor performs tests of controls:** The auditor shall design and perform tests of controls to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence about their operating effectiveness when:

**(a)** The auditor's **assessment of risks of material misstatement at the assertion level includes an expectation that the controls are operating effectively** — i.e., the auditor **intends to rely on** those controls to determine the nature, timing and extent of substantive procedures; or

**(b)** **Substantive procedures alone cannot provide sufficient appropriate audit evidence** at the assertion level.

**What the auditor tests:** In performing tests of controls, the auditor obtains evidence about:
1. **How** the controls were applied at relevant times during the period under audit;
2. The **consistency** with which they were applied; and
3. **By whom or by what means** (including the extent to which controls are automated) they were applied.

The auditor also determines whether the controls to be tested **depend upon other (indirect) controls**, and if so, whether it is necessary to obtain evidence supporting the effective operation of those indirect controls.

**When testing is mandatory irrespective of choice:** Where **substantive procedures alone cannot provide sufficient appropriate audit evidence** — typically where the entity conducts its business using IT and **no documentation of transactions is produced or maintained** other than through the IT system (for example, highly automated, high-volume online processing) — the auditor is **required** to test the operating effectiveness of the relevant controls, even if he would not otherwise have chosen to rely on them.

**Conclusion:** The auditor performs tests of controls when he intends to rely on controls, or when substantive procedures alone are insufficient; in the latter (typically automated) situations, testing of controls is compulsory.

*(Full-marks tip: state both trigger conditions (a) and (b), and specifically flag the automated-environment situation where testing is mandatory. Omitting condition (b) is the usual reason for lost marks.)*

### Q25. Ch: Risk Assessment & Internal Control — Communicating Deficiencies (SA 265) (Marks: 4) [Application]

**Question:** During the audit of *Crestline Foods Ltd.*, the auditor finds that the person who receives cash from customers also records the receipts in the books and reconciles the cash account, and that this weakness has allowed a teeming-and-lading fraud to remain undetected. Distinguish between a "deficiency in internal control" and a "significant deficiency", and state the auditor's reporting responsibility under SA 265 in this case.

**Answer:**
As per **SA 265 "Communicating Deficiencies in Internal Control to Those Charged with Governance and Management"**, the auditor must communicate appropriately to those charged with governance and management the deficiencies in internal control that he has identified during the audit.

**Distinction:**

| Basis | Deficiency in Internal Control | Significant Deficiency |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Exists when a control is designed, implemented or operated in such a way that it is **unable to prevent, or detect and correct, misstatements on a timely basis**; or a control **necessary to do so is missing** | A deficiency or **combination of deficiencies** that, in the auditor's **professional judgement**, is of **sufficient importance to merit the attention of those charged with governance** |
| Severity | Any control weakness | The more serious subset of deficiencies |
| Communication | Communicated to the **appropriate level of management** | Communicated **in writing** to **those charged with governance** |

**Application:** In Crestline Foods, one employee receives cash, records it, and reconciles the cash account. This is a **complete absence of segregation of duties** over cash — a control necessary to prevent or detect misstatement is missing, so it is a **deficiency in internal control**. Because this deficiency has actually **permitted a fraud (teeming and lading) to occur and remain undetected**, and involves susceptibility to loss of a material asset (cash), it is, in the auditor's professional judgement, a **significant deficiency**.

**Reporting responsibility:** The auditor must therefore communicate this significant deficiency **in writing to those charged with governance** on a **timely basis**. The written communication shall include (i) a **description of the deficiencies and an explanation of their potential effects**, and (ii) sufficient information to enable the recipients to understand the context — namely that the purpose was to express an opinion on the financial statements, that the audit considered internal control relevant to the audit, and that the matters reported are limited to those identified during the audit.

**Conclusion:** The lack of segregation over cash is a significant deficiency, which the auditor must report in writing to those charged with governance, describing the deficiency, its potential effects and the context of the communication.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner looks for the "in writing / to those charged with governance / timely" trio and the required content of the communication. Merely saying "report it to management" loses marks because significant deficiencies go to those charged with governance in writing.)*

### Q26. Ch: Audit Evidence — External Confirmation (SA 505) (Marks: 6) [Theory]

**Question:** "External confirmation is an important source of audit evidence." With reference to SA 505, explain (i) what an external confirmation is, (ii) the circumstances in which the use of external confirmation procedures is particularly warranted, (iii) the difference between a positive and a negative confirmation request, and (iv) the auditor's course of action if management refuses to allow him to send a confirmation request.

**Answer:**
**(i) Meaning:** As per **SA 505 "External Confirmations"**, an *external confirmation* is audit evidence obtained as a **direct written response to the auditor from a third party (the confirming party)**, in paper form, or by electronic or other medium. Because it is obtained directly from an independent external source, it is generally regarded as **more reliable** than evidence generated internally by the entity.

**(ii) Circumstances in which external confirmation is particularly warranted:** External confirmation procedures are frequently relevant when addressing assertions associated with certain account balances and their elements. Their use is particularly warranted for, among others:
- **Bank balances** and other information relevant to banking relationships;
- **Trade receivables** balances and terms (to test *existence* and *rights*);
- **Inventories held by third parties** at bonded warehouses for processing or on consignment;
- **Property title deeds held by lawyers or financiers** for safe custody or as security;
- **Investments** held for safekeeping by third parties or purchased but not delivered at the balance-sheet date;
- **Loans and amounts due to lenders**, including relevant terms of repayment and restrictive covenants; and
- **Accounts payable** balances and terms.
Broadly, confirmation is warranted when there is an **assessed risk of material misstatement** for an assertion and the relevant information is held by a **reliable external party** who can be expected to respond.

**(iii) Positive vs Negative confirmation request:**

| Basis | Positive Confirmation Request | Negative Confirmation Request |
|---|---|---|
| Response sought | The confirming party responds **in all cases** — indicating agreement, or supplying the requested information | The confirming party responds **only if he disagrees** with the information stated in the request |
| Reliability | More reliable — a non-response is followed up | Less reliable — a non-response may merely mean the letter was not received/read |
| Use | Preferred when RMM is higher | Used only when specified conditions are met (low RMM, large number of small homogeneous balances, low expected exception rate, and no reason to expect recipients will disregard the request) |

**(iv) Management refusal to permit a confirmation request:** If management refuses to allow the auditor to send a confirmation request, the auditor shall:
1. **Inquire into management's reasons** for the refusal and seek audit evidence as to their **validity and reasonableness**;
2. **Evaluate the implications** of the refusal on the auditor's assessment of the relevant risks of material misstatement, including the risk of fraud, and on the nature, timing and extent of other audit procedures; and
3. **Perform alternative audit procedures** designed to obtain relevant and reliable audit evidence.
If the auditor concludes that management's refusal is **unreasonable**, or he is **unable to obtain relevant and reliable audit evidence from alternative procedures**, he shall communicate the matter to those charged with governance under SA 260 and determine its implications for the audit opinion under **SA 705** (i.e., a possible qualification or disclaimer).

**Conclusion:** External confirmation is direct third-party written evidence, particularly warranted for balances such as receivables, bank and third-party inventory; positive requests always draw a reply while negative ones do only on disagreement; and an unreasonable management refusal that cannot be overcome by alternative procedures affects the audit opinion.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner rewards the three-step response to a management refusal and the four conditions for using negative confirmations. Merely defining positive and negative confirmation without the refusal procedure loses a third of the marks.)*

### Q27. Ch: Audit Evidence — Audit Evidence for Specific Items (SA 501) (Marks: 5) [Application]

**Question:** *Nucleus Pharma Ltd.* holds a material quantity of inventory, part of which is stored in a third-party cold-storage warehouse, and the company is a defendant in a product-liability suit. The auditor could not attend the physical inventory counting on the date of the count owing to a factory lockout. With reference to SA 501, state the auditor's responsibilities regarding (i) attendance at inventory counting, (ii) inventory held by the third party, and (iii) litigation and claims.

**Answer:**
As per **SA 501 "Audit Evidence — Specific Considerations for Selected Items"**, the auditor must obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence regarding the existence and condition of inventory, and the completeness of litigation and claims.

**(i) Attendance at physical inventory counting:** When inventory is material, the auditor shall obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence regarding its existence and condition by **attending physical inventory counting** (unless impracticable), to (a) evaluate management's instructions and procedures, (b) observe the performance of the count, (c) inspect the inventory, and (d) perform test counts. Where **attendance is impracticable** — as here, due to the lockout — the auditor shall attend on an **alternative date** and perform audit procedures on intervening transactions. If even **attendance is impracticable altogether**, he shall perform **alternative audit procedures** to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence about existence and condition; if that is not possible, he shall **modify the opinion** under SA 705. (The mere inconvenience of attending is not a valid reason for non-attendance.)

**(ii) Inventory held by a third party (the cold-storage warehouse):** When inventory under the custody and control of a third party is material, the auditor shall obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence regarding its **existence and condition** by performing **one or both** of the following:
- **Requesting confirmation from the third party** as to the quantities and condition of inventory held on behalf of the entity (an external confirmation under SA 505); and/or
- **Performing inspection or other audit procedures** appropriate in the circumstances — for example, attending (or arranging for another auditor to attend) the third party's physical count, obtaining a report on the adequacy of the third party's internal controls, or inspecting documentation regarding the inventory.

**(iii) Litigation and claims (the product-liability suit):** The auditor shall design and perform audit procedures to **identify litigation and claims involving the entity** that may give rise to a risk of material misstatement, including: inquiry of management and in-house legal counsel; reviewing minutes of those charged with governance and correspondence with the entity's external legal counsel; and reviewing legal expense accounts. Where there is a **risk of material misstatement**, or the auditor believes material litigation/claims may exist, he shall seek **direct communication with the entity's external legal counsel** through a letter of inquiry, prepared by management and sent by the auditor, requesting the lawyer to communicate directly with the auditor. If law/the lawyer prohibits direct communication or the response is inadequate and no alternative evidence is available, the auditor shall **modify the opinion** under SA 705. The auditor shall also request **written representations** from management that all known actual or possible litigation and claims have been disclosed and accounted for.

**Conclusion:** For the lockout-affected count the auditor should count on an alternative date or perform alternative procedures; for the third-party inventory he should confirm with the warehouse and/or inspect; and for the suit he should identify claims and communicate directly with the entity's legal counsel, obtaining written representations.

*(Full-marks tip: examiners reward the "one or both" options for third-party inventory and the direct communication with legal counsel. The common deduction is treating inconvenience as impracticability, or omitting the alternative-date requirement.)*

### Q28. Ch: Audit Evidence — Opening Balances / Initial Audit Engagement (SA 510) (Marks: 5) [Theory]

**Question:** CA Sneha has been appointed as auditor of *Orbit Industries Ltd.* for the financial year 2025-26; the previous year was audited by another firm. Explain, with reference to SA 510, what is meant by "opening balances", the audit objectives in an initial audit engagement, and the effect on the audit report if she is unable to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence regarding opening balances.

**Answer:**
As per **SA 510 "Initial Audit Engagements — Opening Balances"**, an *initial audit engagement* is one in which either the financial statements for the prior period were **not audited**, or were **audited by a predecessor auditor**. Here, since the prior year was audited by another firm, this is an initial audit engagement for CA Sneha.

**Meaning of "opening balances":** *Opening balances* are those account balances that exist at the **beginning of the period**. They are **based upon the closing balances of the prior period** and reflect the effects of (a) transactions and events of prior periods, and (b) accounting policies applied in the prior period. Opening balances also include matters requiring disclosure that existed at the beginning of the period, such as contingencies and commitments.

**Audit objectives in an initial audit engagement:** In conducting an initial audit engagement, the objective of the auditor with respect to opening balances is to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence about whether:
1. **Opening balances contain misstatements** that materially affect the current period's financial statements; and
2. **Appropriate accounting policies** reflected in the opening balances have been **consistently applied** in the current period's financial statements, or changes thereto are properly accounted for and adequately presented and disclosed.

**Audit procedures:** She shall read the most recent financial statements and the predecessor auditor's report (if any) for information relevant to opening balances; determine whether the prior period's closing balances have been correctly brought forward or, where appropriate, restated; and determine whether opening balances reflect appropriate accounting policies. She shall then either (a) find that the prior period was audited and review the predecessor auditor's working papers, or (b) perform **specific audit procedures** (for example, current-period procedures that also provide evidence about certain opening balances, such as collection of opening receivables) to obtain evidence.

**Effect on the audit report:**

| Situation | Type of modification (SA 705) |
|---|---|
| Unable to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence regarding **opening balances** | **Qualified opinion** or **Disclaimer of opinion**, as appropriate |
| Opening balances **contain a misstatement** that materially affects the current period's statements, and the effect is not properly accounted for/disclosed | **Qualified** or **Adverse** opinion |
| Current period's **accounting policies not consistently applied**, or a change not properly accounted for/disclosed | **Qualified** or **Adverse** opinion |

**Conclusion:** Opening balances are the brought-forward closing balances of the prior period; in an initial engagement CA Sneha must satisfy herself that they are free of material misstatement and reflect consistently applied policies, and where she cannot obtain sufficient appropriate evidence about them she must issue a qualified opinion or a disclaimer of opinion.

*(Full-marks tip: distinguish clearly between "cannot obtain evidence" → qualified/disclaimer, and "misstatement exists" → qualified/adverse. Mixing up these two outcomes is the most common error.)*

### Q29. Ch: Audit Evidence — Subsequent Events (SA 560) (Marks: 6) [Application]

**Question:** The financial statements of *Solaris Textiles Ltd.* for the year ended 31 March 2026 are being finalised. The following came to the auditor's notice:
1. On 20 April 2026 a major customer, whose balance of ₹85 lakh was outstanding at the year-end, was declared insolvent; the amount had not been provided for.
2. On 5 May 2026 a fire destroyed a godown of finished goods costing ₹40 lakh — this stock was acquired after the year-end.

The auditor's report is dated 12 May 2026. With reference to SA 560, distinguish between the two types of subsequent events, classify each of the above, and state the auditor's responsibility.

**Answer:**
As per **SA 560 "Subsequent Events"**, *subsequent events* are events occurring **between the date of the financial statements and the date of the auditor's report**, and facts that become known to the auditor after the date of the auditor's report. The related financial reporting framework (AS 4 "Contingencies and Events Occurring After the Balance Sheet Date") distinguishes two types.

| Type | Nature | Accounting treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **Adjusting events** | Provide **evidence of conditions that existed at the date of the financial statements** | **Adjust** the amounts recognised in the financial statements |
| **Non-adjusting events** | Indicative of **conditions that arose after the date of the financial statements** | **Do not adjust**; disclose if material |

**Classification of the given events:**

**Event 1 — Insolvency of a customer on 20 April 2026:** The customer's balance of ₹85 lakh existed **at the year-end (31 March 2026)**, and the insolvency provides **additional evidence of the debtor's inability to pay that existed at the balance-sheet date** (a debtor's financial condition rarely collapses overnight). This is therefore an **adjusting event**. The auditor must require management to **make a provision for the doubtful debt of ₹85 lakh** in the financial statements. If management refuses, the financial statements are materially misstated and the auditor must **modify the opinion** under SA 705.

**Event 2 — Fire destroying the godown on 5 May 2026:** The finished goods destroyed were **acquired after the year-end**, so the loss relates to a **condition (existence of that stock, and the fire) that arose after the balance-sheet date**. This is a **non-adjusting event** — no adjustment is required. However, if the loss is material to the going-concern or a proper understanding of the entity, appropriate **disclosure** in the financial statements is required, and the auditor evaluates the adequacy of that disclosure.

**Auditor's responsibility (up to the date of the report):** Since the auditor's report is dated 12 May 2026, both events fall in the period up to the date of the report. The auditor is required to perform audit procedures designed to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence that **all events up to the date of the report** that require adjustment of, or disclosure in, the financial statements have been identified — including reading minutes of meetings held after year-end, inquiring of management and legal counsel, and reading the latest available interim financial statements. He must also obtain **written representations** from management that all such subsequent events have been adjusted or disclosed.

**Conclusion:** The customer's insolvency is an adjusting event requiring a provision of ₹85 lakh; the post-year-end fire is a non-adjusting event requiring only disclosure if material. As both occurred before the date of his report, the auditor must ensure they are appropriately dealt with and, failing that, modify his opinion.

*(Full-marks tip: the acid test is whether the condition existed at the balance-sheet date. Examiners deduct marks where a student adjusts for the fire (wrong) or merely discloses the insolvency (wrong).)*

### Q30. Ch: Audit Evidence — Going Concern (SA 570) (Marks: 6) [Application]

**Question:** During the audit of *Pinnacle Steel Ltd.*, the auditor notes that the company's current liabilities exceed its current assets, a fixed-term loan is approaching maturity without a realistic prospect of renewal, and the company has lost a major customer. Management has, however, prepared a cash-flow forecast supporting a plan to sell a non-core division and refinance the loan. With reference to SA 570, state the auditor's responsibilities, the possible reporting outcomes, and the meaning of a "material uncertainty related to going concern".

**Answer:**
As per **SA 570 "Going Concern"**, financial statements are generally prepared on the **going concern basis of accounting** — i.e., on the assumption that the entity will continue in operation for the foreseeable future. The auditor's objectives are to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence regarding, and conclude on, the appropriateness of management's use of the going concern basis, and to determine whether a **material uncertainty** exists.

**Events/conditions indicating going-concern doubt (present in this case):** The facts disclose several recognised indicators — **net current liabilities** (current liabilities exceeding current assets), a **fixed-term borrowing approaching maturity without realistic prospects of renewal or repayment**, and **loss of a major customer/market**. These are, respectively, *financial* and *operating* events or conditions that may cast significant doubt on the entity's ability to continue as a going concern.

**Auditor's responsibilities:**
1. **Remain alert** throughout the audit for events or conditions that may cast significant doubt on going concern.
2. Where such events or conditions are identified, **obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence** to determine whether a material uncertainty exists, by evaluating **management's plans** for future actions — here, the plan to **sell the non-core division and refinance the loan**.
3. Where management has made an **assessment through a cash-flow forecast**, evaluate the **reliability of the underlying data** and the reasonableness of the **assumptions**; consider whether any additional facts have become available since the assessment.
4. **Request written representations** from management regarding its plans and their feasibility.

**Meaning of "material uncertainty":** A **material uncertainty** exists when the **magnitude of the potential impact** of the events or conditions, and the **likelihood of their occurrence**, is such that appropriate **disclosure of the nature and implications of the uncertainty is necessary** for the fair presentation of the financial statements.

**Possible reporting outcomes:**

| Situation | Reporting outcome (SA 570 / SA 705) |
|---|---|
| Going concern basis appropriate, **no** material uncertainty | Unmodified opinion; no special reporting |
| Going concern basis appropriate but a **material uncertainty exists** and is **adequately disclosed** | Unmodified opinion **plus a separate section headed "Material Uncertainty Related to Going Concern"** |
| Material uncertainty exists but is **not adequately disclosed** | **Qualified** or **Adverse** opinion under SA 705 |
| Use of going concern basis is **inappropriate** (entity will not continue) | **Adverse opinion** |
| Management **unwilling to make or extend its assessment** when requested | Consider implications, possibly **qualified opinion or disclaimer** |

**Conclusion:** The auditor must evaluate management's disposal-and-refinancing plans and cash-flow forecast; if a going-concern basis is appropriate but a material uncertainty exists and is adequately disclosed, he issues an unmodified opinion with a "Material Uncertainty Related to Going Concern" section, and modifies the opinion where disclosure is inadequate or the basis is inappropriate.

*(Full-marks tip: examiners specifically reward the "Material Uncertainty Related to Going Concern" heading (not the old "Emphasis of Matter") and the definition of material uncertainty. Confusing adequate vs inadequate disclosure outcomes is the standard error.)*

### Q31. Ch: Audit Evidence — Written Representations (SA 580) (Marks: 5) [Theory]

**Question:** With reference to SA 580, explain what written representations are and why the auditor obtains them, and state the auditor's course of action where (i) management does not provide a requested written representation, and (ii) the auditor has doubts about the reliability of the written representations provided.

**Answer:**
As per **SA 580 "Written Representations"**, a *written representation* is a **written statement by management provided to the auditor** to confirm certain matters or to support other audit evidence. Written representations are necessary audit evidence but they **do not, by themselves, provide sufficient appropriate audit evidence** about any of the matters with which they deal, and they do not affect the nature or extent of other audit evidence the auditor obtains.

**Why the auditor obtains written representations:** The auditor obtains written representations to confirm that management (and, where appropriate, those charged with governance) has **fulfilled its responsibility** for (a) the **preparation of the financial statements** in accordance with the applicable framework, and (b) the **completeness of the information** provided to the auditor, and access thereto. Written representations are also sought to **support other audit evidence** relevant to particular assertions where required by other SAs (for example, SA 240 on fraud, SA 501 on litigation, SA 560 on subsequent events). The representations must be for **all financial statements and periods** referred to in the auditor's report, must be **in the form of a representation letter addressed to the auditor**, and must be **dated as near as practicable to, but not after, the date of the auditor's report**.

**(i) Management does not provide a requested written representation:** If management does not provide one or more of the requested written representations, the auditor shall:
- **Discuss the matter** with management;
- **Re-evaluate the integrity of management** and evaluate the effect this may have on the reliability of representations (oral or written) and on audit evidence in general; and
- **Take appropriate actions**, including determining the possible effect on the opinion under SA 705.

**(ii) Doubts about the reliability of representations provided:** If the auditor has concerns about the **competence, integrity, ethical values or diligence** of management, or about its commitment to or enforcement of these, he shall determine the effect on the reliability of representations and audit evidence generally. In particular, if written representations are **inconsistent with other audit evidence**, the auditor shall perform audit procedures to attempt to resolve the matter; if the matter remains unresolved, he shall **reconsider the assessment of the competence, integrity, ethical values or diligence of management** and determine its effect on the audit.

**Effect of unreliable representations on the opinion:** The auditor shall **disclaim an opinion** if he concludes that (a) there is **sufficient doubt about the integrity of management** such that the representations about responsibilities are not reliable, or (b) management does not provide the representations about its **responsibilities** (preparation of the statements and completeness of information).

**Conclusion:** Written representations confirm management's fulfilment of its responsibilities and support other evidence but are not sufficient on their own; where they are refused or found unreliable, the auditor re-evaluates management's integrity and, in serious cases concerning management's basic responsibilities, disclaims the opinion.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner rewards the point that unreliable or refused representations about management's *responsibilities* lead to a **disclaimer of opinion**. Stating only "obtain a representation letter" without the failure-consequences loses marks.)*

### Q32. Ch: Audit Evidence — Sufficiency, Appropriateness, Relevance and Reliability (SA 500) (Marks: 5) [Theory]

**Question:** SA 500 requires the auditor to obtain "sufficient appropriate audit evidence". Explain the terms *sufficiency* and *appropriateness*, the two aspects of appropriateness, and the generalisations that assist the auditor in judging the *reliability* of audit evidence.

**Answer:**
As per **SA 500 "Audit Evidence"**, the objective of the auditor is to design and perform audit procedures in such a way as to enable him to obtain **sufficient appropriate audit evidence** to be able to draw reasonable conclusions on which to base the audit opinion.

**Sufficiency:** *Sufficiency* is the measure of the **quantity** of audit evidence. The quantity needed is affected by the auditor's **assessment of the risks of material misstatement** (the higher the assessed risk, the more evidence is likely to be required) and also by the **quality** of the evidence (the higher the quality, the less may be required).

**Appropriateness:** *Appropriateness* is the measure of the **quality** of audit evidence — that is, its **relevance** and its **reliability** in providing support for the conclusions on which the auditor's opinion is based.

**Two aspects of appropriateness:**

1. **Relevance** — deals with the **logical connection** with, or bearing upon, the purpose of the audit procedure and the assertion under consideration. A given set of procedures may provide evidence relevant to certain assertions but not others; the **direction of testing** is important — testing recorded items for overstatement gives evidence on *existence*, while testing from source documents for omission gives evidence on *completeness*.

2. **Reliability** — is influenced by its **source and nature**, and by the **circumstances** under which it is obtained.

**Generalisations on reliability:** While subject to exceptions, the following generalisations assist the auditor:
- Audit evidence is **more reliable when obtained from independent sources outside the entity**.
- Audit evidence generated **internally is more reliable when the related controls are effective**.
- Audit evidence obtained **directly by the auditor** (e.g., observation) is more reliable than evidence obtained indirectly or by inference.
- Audit evidence in **documentary form** (paper, electronic or other medium) is more reliable than evidence obtained **orally**.
- Audit evidence provided by **original documents** is more reliable than that provided by photocopies or facsimiles.

**Conclusion:** Sufficiency concerns the quantity and appropriateness the quality of audit evidence; appropriateness comprises relevance and reliability, and the reliability of evidence increases with its independence of source, effectiveness of related controls, directness, documentary form and originality.

*(Full-marks tip: keep sufficiency (quantity) and appropriateness (quality) firmly separate, and list all five reliability generalisations. Swapping the two definitions is the most common and most costly error.)*

### Q33. Ch: Audit Evidence — Cross-Topic: Risk-Based Confirmation of Receivables (Marks: 8) [Application]

**Question:** *Meridian Appliances Ltd.* sells through a large number of dealers on credit. The auditor's risk assessment reveals that (a) sales targets are linked to steep incentives for the sales team, (b) year-end sales spiked sharply, and (c) several dealer balances are long-outstanding. The auditor decides to obtain external confirmations of trade receivables. Explain how the auditor should design and perform this confirmation exercise, addressing: the assertions targeted, the choice between positive and negative confirmations, the auditor's control over the process, and the procedures where confirmations are not returned or reveal exceptions. Integrate SA 315, SA 330 and SA 505.

**Answer:**
**Step 1 — Link the risk assessment to assertions (SA 315):** The three factors identified are **fraud risk indicators**. Incentive-linked sales targets create **pressure/incentive** to overstate revenue; the sharp **year-end spike** suggests possible **cut-off manipulation or channel-stuffing**; and **long-outstanding balances** suggest possible **overstatement / impaired receivables**. Under **SA 315**, the auditor therefore assesses a **high risk of material misstatement** in trade receivables, principally in the assertions of **Existence** (do the recorded debtors and the underlying sales genuinely exist?), **Rights and Obligations** (does the entity own the receivable, or are goods on sale-or-return?), **Cut-off** (are year-end sales recorded in the correct period?) and **Valuation** (are long-outstanding balances recoverable?).

**Step 2 — Design the response (SA 330):** Because RMM is high, and because significant risks require substantive procedures responsive to them, the auditor designs **external confirmation of receivables** as a substantive test of details directed mainly at **existence** and **rights and obligations**. As a fraud-risk response, the auditor introduces an element of **unpredictability**, selects the sample at or near the **year-end**, and does **not** rely solely on the entity's controls.

**Step 3 — Choice between positive and negative confirmation (SA 505):** Given the **high assessed RMM and fraud indicators**, the auditor must use **positive confirmation requests** — the dealer is asked to respond in every case, either confirming the balance or supplying the amount he agrees. **Negative confirmations are inappropriate here**, because they may be used only when RMM is low, balances are numerous, small and homogeneous, the expected exception rate is low, and there is no reason to expect non-response — conditions **not** met in this scenario. For the largest and most doubtful balances the auditor may use the **"blank" form** of positive request (asking the dealer to fill in the amount without stating it), which yields stronger evidence.

**Step 4 — Maintain control over the confirmation process (SA 505):** To preserve reliability, the auditor must **maintain control over the confirmation requests**, by:
- Determining the **information to be confirmed** (usually the balance and terms);
- **Selecting the confirming parties** himself (not leaving the choice to management);
- **Designing the request**, ensuring it is properly addressed and that **responses are to be sent directly to the auditor**; and
- **Sending the requests**, including follow-ups, from his own office.
He must be alert to indications that responses may not be reliable (e.g., received via the client, or through suspect addresses), and, per SA 505, investigate such responses and consider their effect on the risk of fraud.

**Step 5 — Non-responses and exceptions (SA 505):**

| Situation | Auditor's procedure |
|---|---|
| **No response** to a **positive** request | Perform **alternative audit procedures** — e.g., examine subsequent cash receipts, matched to specific invoices; inspect shipping/dispatch documents and sales contracts around the year-end |
| **Response received but shows an exception** (dealer disagrees) | **Investigate** the exception to determine whether it indicates a misstatement (e.g., disputed goods, cut-off error, unrecorded return) and evaluate its effect on other balances and on assessed fraud risk |
| A **necessary** confirmation not obtained and alternatives insufficient | Consider the implications for the opinion under **SA 705** |

**Step 6 — Evaluate results:** The auditor evaluates whether the confirmations, together with alternative procedures, provide **sufficient appropriate audit evidence** (SA 500) or whether the exceptions indicate a **pervasive pattern** of overstated year-end sales requiring reconsideration of the overall fraud risk assessment and communication to those charged with governance.

**Conclusion:** The high, fraud-tinged RMM drives the auditor to positive (possibly blank) external confirmations of receivables targeting existence, rights and cut-off; he must retain full control over the process, perform alternative procedures for non-responses, investigate exceptions, and escalate to a modified opinion if he cannot obtain sufficient appropriate evidence.

### Q34. Ch: Risk Assessment & Internal Control — Cross-Topic: Controls, Assertions and Deficiencies in a Payroll Cycle (Marks: 8) [Application]

**Question:** *Everest Constructions Ltd.* employs a large casual and contract workforce at remote sites. The auditor's walkthrough of the payroll cycle reveals that: (a) the site supervisor prepares the attendance record, computes the wages, disburses cash wages and also maintains the wage register; (b) there is no independent verification of the existence of the workers on the muster roll; and (c) approved wage rates are not communicated to a person independent of the supervisor. Identify the internal control weaknesses, the payroll assertions each weakness threatens, the audit response under SA 330, and the auditor's communication obligation under SA 265.

**Answer:**
**Step 1 — Identify the internal control weaknesses (SA 315):** Under **SA 315**, an entity's internal control is relevant to the audit to the extent it bears on the preparation of the financial statements. The walkthrough reveals fundamental **control deficiencies** in the payroll cycle:

| Weakness | Nature of deficiency |
|---|---|
| (a) The site supervisor prepares attendance, computes wages, disburses cash **and** keeps the wage register | **Complete lack of segregation of duties** — one person controls the entire cycle, enabling the creation of "ghost/dummy workers" and misappropriation of cash wages |
| (b) No independent verification of the **existence of workers** on the muster roll | Absence of a control necessary to detect fictitious employees |
| (c) Approved wage rates not communicated to an **independent person** | No independent check on the rate applied — wages may be computed at inflated rates |

**Step 2 — Assertions threatened (SA 315 / SA 500):** Each weakness threatens specific payroll assertions:

| Weakness | Assertion(s) primarily threatened | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Ghost workers created by supervisor | **Occurrence** (of wage expense) / **Existence** | Wages recorded for non-existent employees — expense and cash overstated/misappropriated |
| No verification of workers' existence | **Occurrence / Existence** | Same — fictitious labour cost |
| Wage rates not independently checked | **Accuracy** | Wages computed and recorded at wrong (inflated) rates |

**Step 3 — Audit response (SA 330):** Because the controls over payroll are **absent/ineffective**, the auditor **cannot rely on them** (a test of controls would not support reliance) and must respond with **more extensive substantive procedures**, appropriately assessing this as an area of higher risk of material misstatement (including fraud risk):
- **Existence/occurrence:** conduct a **surprise attendance/headcount at the sites** on a payment day, comparing physically present workers to the muster roll; examine identity records; verify a sample of workers by independent inquiry.
- **Accuracy:** **re-compute** a sample of wage calculations and agree the **rates used to independently approved rate schedules**; test arithmetical accuracy of the wage register.
- **Completeness/cut-off:** reconcile total wages charged with contract-wise labour budgets and prior-period trends (substantive analytical procedures).
- Consider whether the pervasiveness of the weakness affects the assessment of **fraud risk** under SA 240 and the reliability of management's representations.

**Step 4 — Communication obligation (SA 265):** The weaknesses identified — particularly the **total absence of segregation of duties over cash wages** — are deficiencies in internal control where a control necessary to prevent or detect misstatement is missing. Given their potential to permit **material misappropriation and fraud**, they are, in the auditor's professional judgement, **significant deficiencies**. Under **SA 265**, the auditor must therefore:
- Communicate them **in writing** to **those charged with governance** on a **timely basis**; and
- Include in that communication a **description of the deficiencies and an explanation of their potential effects**, together with sufficient information on the context (that the audit considered internal control only to design appropriate procedures, not to express an opinion on internal control).
The auditor would also communicate, to an appropriate level of **management**, other lesser deficiencies not already communicated by other parties.

**Conclusion:** The concentration of incompatible payroll duties in the site supervisor and the absence of independent verification of workers and rates are significant deficiencies threatening the occurrence/existence and accuracy assertions; the auditor responds with extensive substantive procedures (surprise headcounts, re-computation, rate verification) under SA 330 and must report the significant deficiencies in writing to those charged with governance under SA 265.

*(Full-marks tip: the marks are distributed across weakness → assertion → SA 330 response → SA 265 reporting; a strong answer keeps these four columns aligned. The classic deduction is identifying the weaknesses but failing to map each to a specific assertion or to the correct SA 265 written communication.)*

### Q35. Ch: Audit Sampling & Analytical Procedures — Methods of Sample Selection (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** CA Vaidehi, while planning the test of controls over the purchase cycle of Meghdoot Traders Ltd., is undecided on the method of selecting items for her sample. Explain the principal methods of selecting a sample as recognised by SA 530, "Audit Sampling", and state one situation where each is appropriate.

**Answer:** SA 530, "Audit Sampling", requires that when designing an audit sample, the auditor shall select items in such a way that each sampling unit in the population has a chance of selection, so that the sample can be expected to be representative. Paragraph A13 of SA 530 recognises the following principal methods of sample selection:

| Method | Meaning as per SA 530 | Appropriate situation |
|---|---|---|
| **Random selection** | Applied through random number generators (e.g., random number tables or software), giving every sampling unit an equal and known probability of selection. | When the auditor wants an unbiased, statistically valid sample from a homogeneous population such as sequentially numbered sales invoices. |
| **Systematic selection** | The number of sampling units in the population is divided by the sample size to give a sampling interval (say every nth item); after a random start the auditor selects each nth item. | When items are recorded in a serial order with no pattern that coincides with the sampling interval, e.g., selecting every 20th voucher. |
| **Monetary Unit Sampling (MUS)** | A value-weighted selection in which sample size, selection and evaluation result in a conclusion in monetary amounts; the individual monetary unit ("rupee") is the sampling unit, so larger balances have a proportionately higher chance of selection. | When the auditor wants to direct testing towards larger-value items, e.g., testing the existence of high-value trade receivables. |
| **Haphazard selection** | The auditor selects the sample without following a structured technique, but avoids any conscious bias or predictability (no deliberate inclusion/exclusion of items). It is not appropriate when using statistical sampling. | Useful in non-statistical sampling when a random-number approach is impractical, e.g., picking physical inventory tags. |
| **Block selection** | Selection of a block (or blocks) of contiguous items from within the population, e.g., all vouchers of a particular week. | Ordinarily cannot produce a representative sample because most populations are structured so that items in sequence share similar characteristics; used rarely, and only with caution. |

As SA 530 cautions, block selection is generally not an appropriate technique for audit sampling because a block of contiguous items is unlikely to be representative of the whole population.

**Conclusion:** CA Vaidehi may adopt random or systematic selection (or MUS if she wishes to weight for value) for a representative sample; block selection should be avoided as it rarely yields a representative sample.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards naming all five methods AND explaining MUS as value-weighted and block selection as generally inappropriate; students commonly lose marks by omitting MUS or by failing to give the "appropriate situation" the question asks for.)*

### Q36. Ch: Audit Sampling & Analytical Procedures — Factors Affecting Sample Size (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** "The sample size for a test of details is influenced by several factors, and the direction of the effect of each factor is important." In the context of Appendix 3 to SA 530, explain how each of the following affects the sample size for tests of details, indicating whether the sample size increases or decreases: (i) an increase in the auditor's assessment of the risk of material misstatement; (ii) an increase in the use of other substantive procedures directed at the same assertion; (iii) an increase in the auditor's desired level of assurance; (iv) an increase in the tolerable misstatement; (v) an increase in the amount of misstatement the auditor expects to find; (vi) stratification of the population when appropriate.

**Answer:** SA 530, "Audit Sampling", provides in Appendix 3 the factors influencing the sample size for tests of details and the direction in which each factor affects that size. The effect of each factor cited is explained below:

| # | Factor | Effect on sample size | Reason (SA 530, Appendix 3) |
|---|---|---|---|
| (i) | Increase in the auditor's assessment of the risk of material misstatement | **Increase** | The higher the assessed RMM, the larger the sample size needs to be to reduce detection risk to an acceptably low level. |
| (ii) | Increase in the use of other substantive procedures directed at the same assertion | **Decrease** | The more assurance the auditor obtains from other substantive procedures (tests of details or analytical procedures) on the same assertion, the less assurance is required from sampling, hence a smaller sample. |
| (iii) | Increase in the auditor's desired level of assurance | **Increase** | The greater the level of assurance the auditor requires that results are not affected by sampling risk, the larger the sample size must be. |
| (iv) | Increase in tolerable misstatement | **Decrease** | The larger the tolerable misstatement, the smaller the sample size; as the auditor can accept more misstatement, less precision (and a smaller sample) is required. |
| (v) | Increase in the amount of misstatement the auditor expects to find | **Increase** | The greater the expected misstatement, the larger the sample size needs to be to make a reasonable estimate of the actual misstatement in the population. |
| (vi) | Stratification of the population when appropriate | **Decrease** | When there is a wide range (variability) in the monetary size of items, stratifying the population into sub-populations of similar value reduces variability, allowing a reduction in sample size without increasing sampling risk. |

**Conclusion:** Factors (i), (iii) and (v) increase the sample size, while (ii), (iv) and (vi) decrease it. Recognising the direction of each factor enables the auditor to design an efficient yet sufficient sample.

*(Full-marks tip: State the direction (increase/decrease) explicitly for each factor and give the one-line reason; examiners deduct marks where the candidate merely lists factors without the direction, and often confuse tolerable misstatement — larger tolerable misstatement means a SMALLER sample.)*

### Q37. Ch: Audit Sampling & Analytical Procedures — Systematic Selection with Random Start (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** During the audit of Sarovar Industries Ltd. for FY 2025-26, CA Nikhil decides to test the population of 8,400 sequentially pre-numbered goods-receipt notes (GRNs numbered 1001 to 9400). He fixes a sample size of 120 items and elects systematic selection. The random start supplied by audit software is the 46th item within the first interval. Determine the sampling interval and the GRN serial numbers of the first four and the last item to be selected.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Sampling interval.** 
Sampling interval = Population size ÷ Sample size = 8,400 ÷ 120 = **70**. 
(Every 70th sampling unit will be selected after the random start.)

**WN-2 — Position of the random start.** 
The random start is the 46th item within the first interval. Since GRNs begin at 1001, the position "1" corresponds to GRN 1001. 
Serial number of 1st selected item = 1001 + (46 − 1) = 1001 + 45 = **GRN 1046**.

**WN-3 — Successive selections.** 
Each subsequent item is 70 positions (i.e., serial numbers) after the previous one.

**Statement Showing Selected Sampling Units (Systematic Selection):**

| Selection No. | Formula | GRN Serial No. selected |
|---|---|---|
| 1st | 1001 + 45 | 1046 |
| 2nd | 1046 + 70 | 1116 |
| 3rd | 1116 + 70 | 1186 |
| 4th | 1186 + 70 | 1256 |
| … | … | … |
| 120th (last) | 1046 + (119 × 70) = 1046 + 8330 | 9376 |

**Cross-check (OR):** The 120th item = 9376, which is ≤ 9400 (the highest GRN), and the interval 9400 − 1046 = 8354 accommodates 119 further steps of 70 (119 × 70 = 8330 ≤ 8354). Hence the selection stays within the population — consistent.

**Answer:** Sampling interval = **70**; the first four GRNs selected are **1046, 1116, 1186 and 1256**, and the last (120th) GRN selected is **9376**.

### Q38. Ch: Audit Sampling & Analytical Procedures — Monetary Unit Sampling (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** CA Prerna is auditing the trade receivables of Kaveri Textiles Ltd. and wishes to select balances for confirmation using Monetary Unit Sampling (MUS). The total book value of trade receivables is ₹96,00,000. She fixes a sample size of 40 monetary units. The random start is ₹1,40,000. The population, listed in order, has the following cumulative balances:

| Customer | Individual balance (₹) | Cumulative balance (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| Alpha & Co. | 3,10,000 | 3,10,000 |
| Beta Ltd. | 1,80,000 | 4,90,000 |
| Gamma Traders | 2,40,000 | 7,30,000 |
| Delta Mills | 4,00,000 | 11,30,000 |

Determine the MUS sampling interval, and identify which of the above four customers get selected by the first three monetary units hit.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — MUS sampling interval.** 
Interval = Total book value ÷ Sample size = ₹96,00,000 ÷ 40 = **₹2,40,000**.

**WN-2 — Monetary units (hits) to be selected.** 
Starting at the random start ₹1,40,000 and adding the interval of ₹2,40,000 for each successive hit:

| Hit No. | Cumulative rupee selected (₹) |
|---|---|
| 1st | 1,40,000 |
| 2nd | 1,40,000 + 2,40,000 = 3,80,000 |
| 3rd | 3,80,000 + 2,40,000 = 6,20,000 |

**WN-3 — Mapping each hit to the customer whose cumulative band contains it.** 
A customer is selected if the selected rupee falls within its cumulative range.

| Hit (₹) | Falls within cumulative band | Customer selected |
|---|---|---|
| 1,40,000 | 0 – 3,10,000 (Alpha & Co.) | **Alpha & Co.** |
| 3,80,000 | 3,10,001 – 4,90,000 (Beta Ltd.) | **Beta Ltd.** |
| 6,20,000 | 4,90,001 – 7,30,000 (Gamma Traders) | **Gamma Traders** |

**Statement Showing MUS Selection Result:**

| Monetary hit | Amount (₹) | Customer whose balance is selected |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1,40,000 | Alpha & Co. |
| 2 | 3,80,000 | Beta Ltd. |
| 3 | 6,20,000 | Gamma Traders |

**Note (feature of MUS):** Because selection is value-weighted, any balance equal to or larger than the interval (₹2,40,000) — here Delta Mills at ₹4,00,000 — has a 100% chance of selection over the full run; the larger the balance, the higher its probability of selection.

**Answer:** MUS interval = **₹2,40,000**; the first three monetary hits select **Alpha & Co. (₹3,10,000), Beta Ltd. (₹1,80,000) and Gamma Traders (₹2,40,000)**.

### Q39. Ch: Audit Sampling & Analytical Procedures — Substantive Analytical Procedures (SA 520) (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** CA Rehan intends to design and perform substantive analytical procedures for the payroll expense of Ganga Software Pvt. Ltd. State the matters he should consider, as required by SA 520, "Analytical Procedures", before relying on such procedures as substantive tests.

**Answer:** SA 520, "Analytical Procedures", governs the auditor's use of analytical procedures as substantive procedures ("substantive analytical procedures") and as an aid in forming an overall conclusion. Paragraph 5 of SA 520 requires that when designing and performing substantive analytical procedures, the auditor shall consider the following matters:

1. **Suitability of the procedure to the assertion** — The auditor shall determine the suitability of particular substantive analytical procedures for given assertions, taking account of the assessed risks of material misstatement and tests of details, if any, for these assertions (para 5(a)). 2. **Reliability of the data** — The auditor shall evaluate the reliability of data from which the auditor's expectation is developed, considering the source, comparability, nature and relevance of the information available, and controls over its preparation (para 5(b)). 3. **Whether the expectation is sufficiently precise** — The auditor shall evaluate whether the expectation is sufficiently precise to identify a misstatement that, individually or when aggregated with others, may cause the financial statements to be materially misstated (para 5(c)).
4. **Amount of any acceptable difference** — The auditor shall determine the amount of any difference of recorded amounts from expected values that is acceptable without further investigation, as required by para 7 (para 5(d)).

Further, SA 520 para 7 requires that if analytical procedures identify fluctuations or relationships that are inconsistent with other relevant information, or that differ from expected values by a significant amount, the auditor shall investigate such differences by inquiring of management, obtaining appropriate corroborative evidence, and performing other audit procedures as necessary.

**Conclusion:** CA Rehan may rely on substantive analytical procedures for payroll only after satisfying himself on suitability, data reliability, precision of expectation and the acceptable difference; otherwise he must extend tests of details.

### Q42. Ch: Audit Sampling & Analytical Procedures — Projection of Misstatement (Marks: 6) [Problem]

| Particulars | Amount |
|---|---|
| Total book value of inventory population (₹) | 60,00,000 |
| Book value of items sampled (₹) | 9,00,000 |

### Q43. Ch: Audit of Items of Financial Statements — Verification of Cash on Hand (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** During the surprise cash count at the branch of Himalaya Retail Ltd. on the last day of the financial year, CA Ojas finds the physical cash to be short of the cash book balance, and the cashier produces several IOUs (informal borrowings) and a few post-dated cheques of employees kept in the cash box. State the substantive audit procedures CA Ojas should perform for verification of "Cash on Hand", and comment on the treatment of the IOUs and post-dated cheques.

**Answer:** Cash on hand is verified primarily by physical verification supported by reconciliation with the cash book. CA Ojas should perform the following substantive procedures:

1. **Physical count in the presence of the cashier.** Count the cash on hand, preferably by surprise and at the close of business on the reporting date, in the presence of the custodian (cashier), and obtain the custodian's acknowledgement that the cash was counted in his presence and returned intact — this prevents any later allegation of shortage against the auditor.
2. **Reconcile with the cash book.** Agree the counted cash with the balance shown by the cash book as on the date of count; investigate and explain any difference.
3. **Verify cut-off.** Ensure that receipts and payments around the year-end are recorded in the correct period, and that no fictitious receipts are shown to inflate the balance.
4. **Examine composition of the balance.** Ensure that the cash box contains only genuine cash; items that are not cash must be excluded.
5. **Treatment of IOUs and post-dated cheques.** IOUs (informal loans to staff) and post-dated cheques of employees are **not cash** and cannot be included in cash on hand. IOUs represent advances/loans to employees and should be reclassified under "loans and advances" (and their recoverability assessed); post-dated cheques have not yet become due and should be excluded from cash. Their presence in the box explains the physical shortage.
6. **Report the shortage.** The shortfall (physical cash being less than the book balance once non-cash items are excluded) indicates a possible defalcation or unauthorised lending; CA Ojas should quantify it, report it to management/those charged with governance, and consider its impact on the assertions of existence and valuation and on internal control.
7. **Balances at bank / other locations.** Where cash is held at more than one location, arrange simultaneous counts to prevent substitution/transfer between locations.

**Conclusion:** The physical cash must be reconciled after excluding IOUs and post-dated cheques (which are not cash); the resulting shortage should be reported and reclassified appropriately, and its effect on internal control assessed.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner specifically rewards the point that IOUs and post-dated cheques are NOT cash and must be excluded/reclassified, plus obtaining the cashier's acknowledgement and simultaneous counts across locations. Merely writing "count the cash" scores partial marks.)*

4. **Cheques issued but not presented (item i).** Scrutinise cheques issued in March 2026 but not presented even up to May 2026 (an unusually long lag). Long-outstanding cheques may indicate **window dressing** — cheques recorded (to reduce the bank balance/creditors at year-end) but deliberately not dispatched. CA Mahima should verify the dates of actual dispatch, examine whether the cheques were handed over to payees before year-end, and, if they were not genuinely issued, propose reversal so that both bank balance and the corresponding liability are restated correctly.
5. **Deposit in transit (item ii).** For the large deposit credited by the bank only on 15th April 2026, verify the pay-in slip and the party from whom received, and confirm from the April bank statement that it was actually cleared. 6. **Cut-off and subsequent clearance.** Review the bank statement for the period after year-end (subsequent clearance) to confirm that outstanding cheques and deposits in transit actually cleared, corroborating the reconciling items.
7. **Disclosure.** Ensure bank balances are correctly classified (current accounts, deposits with more than 12 months maturity, earmarked/margin money) and disclosed as per Schedule III.

**Conclusion:** Both items are red flags for window dressing/manipulation; long-outstanding issued cheques and a delayed deposit-in-transit must be corroborated by subsequent clearance and, if not genuine, adjusted so that bank balances are neither understated nor overstated.

### Q51. Ch: Audit Sampling & Analytical Procedures — Sampling Risk vs Non-Sampling Risk (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between "Sampling Risk" and "Non-Sampling Risk" as defined in SA 530, "Audit Sampling", and state the two adverse conclusions that sampling risk may lead to in the context of a test of details.

**Answer:** SA 530, "Audit Sampling", defines both terms and requires the auditor to reduce audit sampling risk to an acceptably low level.

| Basis | Sampling Risk | Non-Sampling Risk |
|---|---|---|
| **Meaning (SA 530)** | The risk that the auditor's conclusion based on a sample may be **different** from the conclusion if the entire population were subjected to the same audit procedure. | The risk that the auditor reaches an **erroneous conclusion for any reason not related to sampling risk**. |
| **Cause** | Arises because the auditor tests only a part (sample) of the population. | Arises from human/procedural factors — e.g., use of inappropriate procedures, misinterpretation of evidence, or failure to recognise a misstatement. |
| **Effect of larger sample** | Can be reduced by **increasing the sample size**. | **Cannot** be reduced by increasing sample size; addressed through proper planning, direction, supervision and review. |
| **Examples** | Concluding controls are effective when they are not; concluding a balance is not misstated when it is. | Auditor applies a wrong procedure or overlooks an error present in the sample. |

**Two adverse conclusions from sampling risk in a test of details:** For a **test of details**, sampling risk may lead the auditor to conclude that:
1. **A material misstatement does not exist when in fact it does** — this affects **audit effectiveness** and is more likely to lead to an inappropriate audit opinion; and
2. **A material misstatement exists when in fact it does not** — this affects **audit efficiency**, as it usually leads to additional work to establish that the initial conclusion was incorrect.

**Conclusion:** Sampling risk (reducible by larger samples) is distinct from non-sampling risk (reducible only by quality control); of the two, the risk of concluding no misstatement exists when it does is the graver, as it impairs audit effectiveness.

*(Full-marks tip: Present the distinction in a table and clearly label the two erroneous conclusions as affecting effectiveness vs efficiency; the examiner rewards the point that only sampling risk falls with a larger sample, whereas non-sampling risk does not.)*

### Q52. Ch: Audit in an Automated Environment — General vs Application Controls and How Each Is Audited (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** "IT General Controls (ITGCs) and application controls both live inside a computerised accounting system, yet the auditor must test them in a fixed sequence and for different reasons." Distinguish between the two categories of controls, state the dependency that governs the order of testing, and explain how the audit procedure for each differs. Support your answer with the relevant Standards on Auditing.

**Answer:**
The auditor's obligation to understand and, where reliance is planned, to test both categories of controls flows from **SA 315 "Identifying and Assessing the Risks of Material Misstatement through Understanding the Entity and Its Environment"** (which requires understanding of the information system and the controls, including IT general controls and application controls) and **SA 330 "The Auditor's Responses to Assessed Risks"** (which requires tests of the automated controls, and of the ITGCs supporting their continued effective operation, wherever reliance is placed on them).

**IT General Controls** are policies and procedures that apply to the IT environment as a whole and that **support the continued proper operation of application controls**; they do not themselves process any individual transaction. **Application controls** are controls embedded within a specific business-process application that operate at the level of **individual transactions** to secure their completeness, accuracy, validity and authorisation.

| Basis | IT General Controls (ITGCs) | Application Controls |
|---|---|---|
| **Level of operation** | Environment-wide; pervasive across all applications | Transaction level, within a single process (e.g., purchases, payroll) |
| **Function** | Make the *environment* trustworthy so other controls can be relied upon | Ensure completeness, accuracy, validity, authorisation of transactions |
| **Typical examples** | Access security (logical access, passwords, privileged access), change management, program development/SDLC, IT operations, backup and recovery | Input/validation checks (range, format, check-digit), existence checks against masters, reasonableness/limit checks, sequence/completeness checks, three-way match, configuration controls |
| **Effect of failure** | Pervasive — undermines reliance on *every* automated control at once | Localised — affects the assertions of one process |
| **How audited** | Enquiry, inspection of policies, inspection of change tickets/access-rights matrices, re-performance of access reviews | Walkthrough, inspection of configuration, **test data** to confirm the programmed control reacts, re-performance |

**The governing dependency and the sequence.** Application controls are effective **only when the underlying ITGCs are effective**. If, for example, a developer can migrate an unauthorised change to a live program overnight, even a perfectly designed three-way match cannot be relied upon. Therefore the audit sequence is fixed: **test ITGCs first → if they operate effectively, place reliance on the automated application controls → reduce substantive testing accordingly.** If ITGCs fail, no automated application control can be relied upon and the auditor must fall back on substantive procedures, usually executed through **CAATs** on the full population.

**Conclusion:** ITGCs are the environment-wide foundation and application controls are the transaction-level guards; because reliance on the latter is conditional on the former, the auditor tests ITGCs first and only then, if they hold, tests and relies upon the application controls (SA 315 and SA 330).

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner rewards the explicit statement of the dependency — "no ITGC assurance ⇒ no reliance on automated application controls" — and a correctly directioned example. Marks are lost for merely listing computer features without tying each control category to how it is tested.)*

### Q53. Ch: Audit in an Automated Environment — Understanding the Flow of Transactions in an ERP (Marks: 4) [Application]
**Question:** During the planning of the audit of Helios Manufacturing Ltd, which runs an integrated SAP ERP, the engagement partner instructs the junior to "document the end-to-end flow of the revenue transaction from initiation to the general ledger before assessing any control." The junior asks why understanding the *flow* is necessary at all when the team can simply vouch a sample of invoices at year-end. Advise the junior, citing the relevant Standard.

**Answer:**
The requirement to understand *how* significant classes of transactions are initiated, recorded, processed and reported through the IT system, and how they flow into the general ledger and financial statements, is an express requirement of **SA 315 "Identifying and Assessing the Risks of Material Misstatement."** The auditor cannot assess a risk in a system that has not been understood.

The junior's "vouch a sample" instinct is a *paper-world* response and is inadequate here for the following reasons:

1. **Risk cannot be located without the flow.** In an ERP, a sales transaction may be born automatically when a warehouse scanner logs a dispatch, priced from a configured price master, taxed from a configured tax table, and posted to the ledger by a program with **no human touch**. The auditor must trace this flow to identify *where* a misstatement could arise — a wrong price master, a mis-configured tax rate, a broken interface between the dispatch module and the billing module.

2. **Automated controls sit inside the flow.** Understanding the flow reveals which application controls (e.g., an existence check against the customer master, a three-way match) and which interfaces the assertions depend on. Only after locating them can the auditor decide whether to rely on controls or go substantive (SA 330).

3. **Sampling tests 25 items; the program governs the whole population.** Because processing is uniform and systematic, a logic or configuration error repeats on *every* transaction. Vouching 25 invoices tells the auditor about those 25, not about the 4-lakh-transaction population the program produced.

4. **The audit trail may be electronic and transient.** Understanding the flow tells the auditor what electronic evidence exists and when it must be captured before it is overwritten.

**Conclusion:** Documenting the end-to-end transaction flow is a mandatory SA 315 step because it is the only way to locate the risks arising from the use of IT and to design an audit response under SA 330; sample vouching cannot substitute for it in an integrated ERP.

*(Full-marks tip: connect the "flow" explicitly to *identifying risk* and *deciding the reliance strategy*; a mere description of an ERP without linking it to SA 315's risk-assessment purpose earns half marks.)*

### Q54. Ch: Audit in an Automated Environment — Data Analytics Use-Cases in a High-Volume Environment (Marks: 8) [Application]

**Answer:**
Audit data analytics is the analysis of patterns, deviations and anomalies in the data underlying the financial statements, usually over the **entire population** rather than a sample. Its deployment here is driven by **SA 315/SA 330** (risk assessment and response), **SA 240 "The Auditor's Responsibilities Relating to Fraud in an Audit of Financial Statements"** (journal-entry testing and testing for management override), and **SA 500 "Audit Evidence"** (reliability of the data analysed).

| **Duplicate and gap detection** | Detect duplicate invoice numbers/customer+amount combinations and gaps in the invoice-number sequence | Double-counting of revenue; omitted (incomplete) sales |
| **Cut-off testing** | Isolate invoices dated in the last/first days of the year and match to the dispatch date | Revenue recognised in the wrong period |
| **Trend and ratio analysis by store/product** | Compute gross-margin and sales trends store-wise; isolate outliers | Localised misstatement or manipulation masked in the consolidated total |
| **Benford's Law on unconstrained sales amounts** | Compare the leading-digit distribution to the expected Benford curve | Fabricated or padded amounts |

**(b) The two limitations that must be respected before concluding:**

1. **Completeness and reliability of the extract (the data-integrity limit).** ADA run on extracted data is only as trustworthy as the extract. If the query silently misses a store, a currency or an archived period, a "100%" test is 100% of the *wrong* population. Before analysis, the team must reconcile the record count and control totals of the extract back to the source system / trial balance and understand how the extract was produced. This is precisely why even a substantive-analytics strategy cannot escape ITGCs — the integrity of the data depends on them (SA 500).

2. **Analytics output is a lead, not a conclusion (the judgement limit).** Every flagged item is a *lead to investigate*, not proof of misstatement or fraud. Each exception must be corroborated with underlying evidence and management enquiry, exercising professional scepticism. Whole-population testing eliminates **sampling risk** for that test, but it does **not** eliminate audit risk — data-completeness risk and non-sampling (judgement) risk remain. Note too that Benford's Law is meaningful only on naturally distributed, unconstrained amounts; it must not be applied to capped, sequential or assigned-number fields.

**Conclusion:** The team should run full-population journal-entry, three-way-match, duplicate/gap, cut-off and Benford analyses (SA 240/SA 315/SA 330), but must first prove the extract is complete and reliable and must treat every flag as a lead requiring corroboration (SA 500) — analytics supplements, and does not replace, professional judgement.

*(Full-marks tip: the eight marks split between naming *specific, differentiated* use-cases each tied to a risk, and the two limitations. Candidates who list generic "use of CAATs" without the completeness-of-extract caveat and the "lead not conclusion" caveat routinely lose 3 marks.)*

### Q55. Ch: Audit in an Automated Environment — Change Management as the Linchpin ITGC (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Midway through the audit of Cygnus Ltd, the IT-audit specialist reports that during the year three developers held **standing access to the production environment** and that at least four program changes were migrated to the live revenue system **without documented testing or approval**. The engagement team had planned a controls-reliance strategy over the automated revenue-recognition control, having successfully tested that control at an interim date. Explain (i) which ITGC has failed, (ii) why the interim test of the automated control no longer supports year-long reliance, and (iii) the required audit response, citing the relevant Standard.

**Answer:**
**(i) The failed control.** The finding is a failure of the **Change Management** ITGC (compounded by an **access-security** weakness — standing developer access to production). Sound change management requires that only authorised, tested changes reach the live system, with strict **separation of the development, test and production environments** and no standing developer access to production. Both requirements have been breached.

**(ii) Why the interim test no longer supports year-long reliance.** The reason a *single* test of an automated control can be extrapolated across the whole period is that a computer is **deterministic** — given the same input it produces the same output every time, *until the code changes*. That extrapolation secretly depends on one premise: **the code did not change during the period.** Change-management ITGC is exactly what guarantees that premise. Here, four undocumented, untested changes reached the live revenue program, so the auditor **cannot conclude that the code tested at the interim date operated unchanged for the rest of the year.** The control could have been altered without trace after the interim test. The extrapolation collapses.

**(iii) The required response (SA 330).** Because the ITGC that justified reliance has failed, **reliance on the automated revenue-recognition control must be withdrawn**, regardless of how well it tested at the interim date. Under **SA 330 "The Auditor's Responses to Assessed Risks,"** the team must **pivot to a substantive strategy.** As revenue is high-volume, this means **CAATs / data analytics over the full revenue population** — independently recompute revenue against contract terms, perform cut-off testing, and run 100% duplicate/gap and three-way-match analysis. Two further consequences follow: (a) IT risk is **pervasive**, so this ITGC failure undermines reliance on *every* automated control in the system, not just revenue — the whole controls-reliance plan must be reassessed; and (b) the deficiency, if significant, must be **communicated to those charged with governance under SA 265** and may affect any reporting on internal financial controls.

**Conclusion:** The change-management ITGC has failed, the interim control test can no longer be extrapolated across the year, and the auditor must withdraw reliance and adopt substantive CAATs on the full revenue population while reassessing the pervasive impact and reporting the deficiency.

*(Full-marks tip: the marks concentrate on the *logic* linking change management to the "test once, rely all year" inference. State the determinism-plus-unchanged-code premise explicitly and flag pervasiveness; do not merely say "do more substantive work.")*

### Q56. Ch: Audit in an Automated Environment — The Systematic-Error Multiplier (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** Polaris Co-operative Bank runs interest on its recurring-deposit accounts through an overnight batch program. The sanctioned rate is **7.20% p.a.**, but a mis-configured rate table applies **7.15% p.a.** The bank has **20,00,000** such accounts, with an average balance of **₹60,000**, and interest is computed for a full year on the average balance. A sample-based auditor tested 30 accounts, found each posting reconciled to its input, and passed the population.

| Particular | Figure |
|---|---|
| Number of accounts | 20,00,000 |
| Average balance per account | ₹60,000 |
| Sanctioned (correct) rate | 7.20% p.a. |
| Configured (applied) rate | 7.15% p.a. |
| Period | Full year |

Compute the aggregate misstatement in interest, show why sampling failed to detect it, and state the correct audit approach.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Correct interest per account.**
Correct interest = ₹60,000 × 7.20% = **₹4,320.00**

**WN-2 — Posted interest per account.**
Posted interest = ₹60,000 × 7.15% = **₹4,290.00**

**WN-3 — Error per account.**
Error per account = ₹4,320.00 − ₹4,290.00 = **₹30.00** (understatement of interest expense)

**WN-4 — Aggregate misstatement.**
Aggregate error = Error per account × Number of accounts
= ₹30.00 × 20,00,000 = **₹6,00,00,000 = ₹6 crore.**

**Statement Showing Aggregate Misstatement in Interest**

| Line | Computation | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| Correct interest per account | 60,000 × 7.20% | 4,320.00 |
| Posted interest per account | 60,000 × 7.15% | 4,290.00 |
| Error per account | 4,320.00 − 4,290.00 | 30.00 |
| Number of accounts | — | 20,00,000 |
| **Aggregate misstatement** | 30.00 × 20,00,000 | **6,00,00,000** |

**OR — cross-check by an independent route.**
Aggregate error = Total balances × rate error = (20,00,000 × ₹60,000) × (7.20% − 7.15%)
= ₹1,20,00,00,00,000 × 0.05% = ₹1,20,000 crore of balances × 0.0005 = **₹6 crore.** ✓ (both routes agree.)

**Why sampling failed and the correct approach.** The error per account is a trivial **₹30** — invisible to any human and to any sample. But because the error is **systematic and one-directional** (every account is understated in the *same* direction), it does **not** self-cancel across the population the way a random manual error would; it aggregates to a **₹6 crore** misstatement, almost certainly material. The 30 sampled postings each "reconciled to input" because the fault lies in the **rule (the configured rate)**, not in the arithmetic. Sampling is built for random, self-cancelling error and is the wrong tool here. The correct approach is to audit **through the computer**, recomputing interest on the **entire population** using **parallel simulation / Generalised Audit Software**, and to test the **change-management and configuration ITGCs** over the rate table.

**Answer:** Aggregate misstatement = **₹6,00,00,000 (₹6 crore)**, understatement of interest expense; undetectable by sampling because the error is systematic, requiring 100% recomputation via CAATs.

### Q57. Ch: Audit in an Automated Environment — Test Data vs Parallel Simulation (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** A student writes in an examination: "Under parallel simulation the auditor feeds dummy transactions through the client's program, while under test data the auditor re-processes the client's real data using his own program." The examiner marks it wrong. Distinguish correctly between **test data** and **parallel simulation**, stating the objective of each and the principal risk to be managed.

**Answer:**
Both are **Computer-Assisted Audit Techniques (CAATs)** — the use of computer tools and data to perform audit procedures directly on the entity's electronic data and systems. The student has **reversed the two techniques.** The correct position is as follows.

| Basis | Test Data | Parallel Simulation |
|---|---|---|
| **Data used** | *Dummy* (auditor-invented) transactions, including deliberately *invalid* ones | *Real* client transactions |
| **Program used** | The *client's* own program | The *auditor's* independent program |
| **Question answered** | "Does the client's program handle transactions — especially exceptions — correctly?" | "Did the client's program produce the correct numbers on real data?" |
| **Primary objective** | Test the **programmed controls / application logic** (e.g., does the system reject a negative quantity, an out-of-range value, a non-existent vendor code?) | Test **processing accuracy** — recompute the output independently and compare |
| **Principal risk to manage** | Dummy data must **not corrupt live records** — run on a copy, or reverse the entries (as in an Integrated Test Facility) | The auditor's program must **faithfully replicate the client's intended logic**, else the comparison is meaningless |

**Memory hook (the correct direction):** **Test data = fake data through the *real* (client's) program; Parallel simulation = real data through the *fake* (auditor's) program.** The student wrote it the wrong way round on both counts.

**Conclusion:** Test data uses dummy data in the client's program to test whether programmed controls work; parallel simulation uses real data in the auditor's independent program to test whether processing was accurate — the student's statement inverted both the data and the program in each case.

*(Full-marks tip: the four marks are for the two-way distinction *and* naming the objective of each. Include the "don't corrupt live data" safeguard for test data — examiners specifically reward it.)*

### Q58. Ch: Audit in an Automated Environment — IT-Dependent Manual Control and Information Produced by the Entity (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** At Meridian Textiles Ltd, purchase-price variances are controlled as follows: the ERP generates a daily "Purchases above standard cost by more than 5%" exception report, and the procurement head reviews it and investigates each flagged item. Her review is meticulous and fully documented for every day of the year. The audit junior proposes to rely on this control for the accuracy of cost of goods sold and to perform minimal substantive testing. Identify the nature of the control, the hidden risk, and what the auditor must additionally test before relying on it.

**Answer:**
**Nature of the control.** This is an **IT-dependent manual control** — a person performs the control (the procurement head reviews and investigates), **but entirely on the basis of a system-generated report.** It is neither a purely automated control (the program does not itself resolve the variance) nor a purely manual control (the human depends completely on the ERP's output).

**The hidden risk.** The procurement head's diligence is genuine, but it is diligence applied to **whatever the report shows her.** The hidden risk is that the **exception report itself may be incomplete or inaccurate** — a wrong filter, a bug in the query, or a mis-set "standard cost" field could cause a genuinely variant purchase to be **silently omitted** from the report. The procurement head would then "correctly" review a list that already excludes the very items of concern, and the control fails **invisibly despite perfect human performance.** In other words, the reliability of an IT-dependent manual control is **capped by the reliability of the report it depends on** — the *information produced by the entity (IPE)*.

**What the auditor must additionally test.** Before relying on this control, the auditor must obtain assurance over the **completeness and accuracy of the exception report** itself:
1. **Verify the report logic** against the intended rule (does the query truly capture every purchase where price exceeds standard cost by more than 5%?).
2. **Reconcile the report to the full purchases population** — for example, use Generalised Audit Software to independently recompute which purchases breach the 5% threshold and compare that to the report; any item in the auditor's list but absent from the report is a silent omission.
3. **Test the ITGCs** underpinning the report — change management over the report/query logic, and access security over the standard-cost master data (who can change the standard cost?).

Only if the report is proven complete and accurate — and the supporting ITGCs are effective — may the auditor rely on the procurement head's review. The reliance is on **the human step *and* the report together**, never on the human step alone.

**Conclusion:** The control is an IT-dependent manual control whose reliability is limited by the exception report's integrity; the auditor must test the completeness and accuracy of that report (the IPE) and the supporting ITGCs before placing any reliance, notwithstanding the reviewer's evident diligence.

*(Full-marks tip: the mark-winning insight is that a diligent human review of a *silent* report is worthless — never conclude on the human step alone. Candidates who praise the documentation and stop there miss the point entirely.)*

### Q59. Ch: Audit in an Automated Environment — Benford's Law: Correct and Incorrect Application (Marks: 4) [Application]
**Question:** The audit team of Nebula Ltd wishes to use **Benford's Law** as part of its fraud-risk data analytics. The IT-analytics assistant proposes to run a Benford leading-digit test on each of the following four data fields and treat any large deviation as evidence of fraud: (a) individual expense-claim amounts (₹, uncapped); (b) sequential purchase-invoice numbers; (c) employee ID codes; (d) petty-cash reimbursements, each capped at a maximum of ₹5,000. Explain what Benford's Law states, identify on which field(s) it may validly be applied and on which it may not, and state the correct significance of a deviation.

**Answer:**
**What Benford's Law states.** In many naturally occurring, unconstrained financial datasets, the **leading digit is not uniformly distributed**: the digit **1 appears first about 30% of the time**, and the frequency falls away smoothly to about **4.6% for the digit 9**. Fabricated numbers — a fraudster inventing amounts, or padding figures just below an approval limit — tend to **violate** this expected distribution. A large deviation from the Benford curve therefore **flags a population for investigation.**

**Validity field by field.** Benford's Law applies only to **naturally distributed, unconstrained** numbers; it is meaningless on capped, sequential or assigned-number fields.

| Field | Valid for Benford's Law? | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| (a) Uncapped expense-claim amounts (₹) | **Yes** | Natural, unconstrained monetary amounts spanning several orders of magnitude — the ideal Benford dataset |
| (b) Sequential purchase-invoice numbers | **No** | Sequential/assigned numbers are not naturally distributed; leading digits follow the numbering scheme, not Benford |
| (c) Employee ID codes | **No** | Assigned identifiers with no natural magnitude distribution |
| (d) Petty-cash reimbursements capped at ₹5,000 | **No** | Constrained (ceiling of ₹5,000) — the cap distorts the leading-digit distribution, so any "deviation" is an artefact, not a signal |

**Correct significance of a deviation.** A deviation from the Benford distribution is a **lead to investigate, not proof of fraud.** The team must corroborate any flagged population with underlying evidence and professional scepticism. Treating a Benford deviation as "evidence of fraud" — as the assistant proposes — overstates the technique.

**Conclusion:** Benford's Law may validly be applied only to the uncapped expense-claim amounts (field a); it is invalid on the sequential invoice numbers, employee IDs and capped petty-cash field, and even a valid deviation is merely a signal for further investigation, never proof of fraud.

*(Full-marks tip: naming *when Benford does not apply* — constrained, sequential, assigned fields — is what separates a strong answer from a rote one, and the "lead not proof" qualifier is compulsory for the last mark.)*

### Q60. Ch: Audit in an Automated Environment — Auditing Around vs Through the Computer, Decided per Class of Transactions (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Orion Logistics Ltd runs three financially significant processes on the same ERP: (1) a **fixed-asset register** — low volume, few additions, strong printed authorisations and a visible trail; (2) a **freight-revenue engine** — 2 crore automatically generated invoices a year, no paper, priced and taxed by configured rules; (3) an **overnight interest-accrual on customer deposits** — high volume, purely programmed calculation. The audit manager proposes a single blanket decision: "audit around the computer for the whole engagement because the outputs reconcile to inputs." Critically evaluate this proposal and recommend the correct approach for each process.

**Answer:**
**The concept.** *Auditing around the computer* treats the system as a **black box** — it tests only inputs and outputs, and *assumes* that if outputs reconcile to inputs the processing must be correct. *Auditing through the computer* **opens the box** — it tests the processing, logic and programmed controls themselves, using CAATs (test data, ITF, parallel simulation, GAS).

**Why the blanket proposal is wrong.** The proposal rests on the syllogism "inputs correct → outputs reconcile → processing correct." Its hidden flaw is that **reconciliation only detects errors that *break* the input-output relationship.** A processing error that is *internally consistent* — a wrong-but-uniform rule (say, interest at 7.15% instead of 7.20%) — produces outputs that reconcile perfectly to inputs and are **still wrong**. Reconciliation tests the *arithmetic* of the process, **not the correctness of its rules.** Moreover, "around vs through" is **not an either/or verdict for a whole audit** — the correct approach is decided **per class of transactions**, driven by volume, complexity, degree of automation and audit-trail visibility.

**Recommended approach per process:**

| Process | Volume / trail / automation | Recommended approach | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| (1) Fixed-asset register | Low volume, visible printed trail, low automation | **Around the computer** is acceptable | Simple system with a strong visible audit trail; correct outputs genuinely imply correct processing. Vouching additions to authorised invoices is sufficient |
| (2) Freight-revenue engine | 2 crore invoices, no paper, configured pricing/tax | **Through the computer** | Risk sits in the *processing* (price/tax configuration). Recompute revenue on the full population (parallel simulation/GAS); test the configuration and its change-management ITGC; run cut-off, duplicate and gap analysis |
| (3) Overnight interest accrual | High volume, purely programmed calculation | **Through the computer** | A systematic logic error would repeat on every account and reconcile to inputs; only recomputation of the whole population (GAS/parallel simulation) plus ITGC testing exposes it |

**Conclusion:** The blanket "around the computer" proposal is unsound because input-output reconciliation cannot catch a uniform wrong rule; the auditor should decide per class of transactions — around for the simple, low-volume fixed-asset register, but through the computer (via CAATs on the full population, plus ITGC testing) for the high-volume, paper-less, configuration-driven revenue and interest processes.

*(Full-marks tip: the six marks reward (i) the precise failure mode of "around" — reconciliation tests arithmetic, not rules — and (ii) a *differentiated* per-process recommendation. A candidate who says "always audit through the computer" as a blanket rule is as wrong as the manager.)*

### Q61. Ch: Audit Documentation & Quality — Permanent Audit File vs Current Audit File (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** SA 230 requires the auditor to prepare and assemble the **audit file**, and in practice the file is organised into a **permanent audit file** and a **current audit file**. Distinguish between the two, giving at least four representative contents of each, and explain why the profession maintains the permanent file separately.

**Audit documentation** under **SA 230 "Audit Documentation"** is the record of the audit procedures performed, the relevant audit evidence obtained, and the conclusions reached; the **audit file** is the folder(s) or storage media, physical or electronic, containing that documentation for a specific engagement. In practice the file is split into a **permanent audit file** (matters of *continuing* relevance across many years) and a **current audit file** (matters relating to the *year* under audit).

### Q62. Ch: Audit Documentation & Quality — Ownership and Confidentiality of Working Papers (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** The management of Zenith Ltd, having paid the audit fee in full, demands that the auditor hand over all working papers, arguing that "we paid for the audit, so the papers are ours." A bank simultaneously requests copies of the same working papers directly from the auditor. State the correct legal and professional position on the **ownership** and **confidentiality** of audit working papers, and how the auditor should respond to both requests.

**Answer:**
The position is governed by **SA 230 "Audit Documentation"** (read with **SQC 1**, which requires firms to establish policies for the confidentiality, safe custody, integrity, accessibility and retrievability of documentation).

**Ownership.** Audit working papers are the **property of the auditor, not the client** — even though the client pays the audit fee. The fee purchases an **independent opinion**, not the auditor's private evidence of diligence. The rationale is structural: the working papers exist so that the auditor can defend an *independent* opinion, potentially *against* the client; if the client owned them, the client could withhold or destroy the very evidence the auditor needs to answer a regulator, and could pressure the auditor by threatening access. The auditor may, **at his own discretion**, make portions or extracts of the working papers available to the client, but this is a **courtesy, not a right** — the client cannot demand them.

**Confidentiality.** The auditor simultaneously owes a duty of **confidentiality** over the working papers: the client's information within them must **not be disclosed to third parties** without the client's authority or a legal/regulatory compulsion to disclose. The auditor must also keep the papers in **safe custody.**

**Response to the two requests:**
1. **Management's demand** to hand over all working papers must be **declined** — the papers are the auditor's property and the client has no right to them. The auditor may, if he chooses, share extracts, but is not obliged to.
2. **The bank's request** for copies must also be **declined** in the absence of the client's authority or legal compulsion, because disclosing the papers to a third party would breach the auditor's duty of **confidentiality** to the client.

**Conclusion:** Working papers are the auditor's property, so the client cannot compel their surrender despite paying the fee; and the auditor's duty of confidentiality bars handing them to the bank without the client's consent or legal compulsion. Both requests should therefore be refused.

*(Full-marks tip: the two halves — *ownership* (auditor's property) and *confidentiality* (no third-party disclosure) — must both appear and must be applied to *both* requests. Stating "the client paid, so they can have the papers" is an outright error the examiner is testing for.)*

### Q63. Ch: Audit Documentation & Quality — Engagement Quality Control Review: Triggers and Timing (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Vega Capital Ltd, a **listed** company, is being audited by your firm. Advise on (i) whether an EQCR is required at all here, and (ii) whether the report may be dated on 30 June, citing the relevant Standard.

The matter is governed by **SA 220 "Quality Control for an Audit of Financial Statements"** and **SQC 1 "Quality Control for Firms that Perform Audits and Reviews of Historical Financial Information, and Other Assurance and Related Services Engagements."**

The correct sequence is: complete the EQCR and resolve the goodwill question → then date the report. Audit-committee deadline pressure does not alter this non-negotiable sequence.

**Question:** Nimbus Foods Ltd's auditor's report is dated **31 August 2026**. The firm's SQC 1 policy adopts the "ordinarily not more than 60 days" assembly limit. (i) By what date must the final audit file be assembled? (ii) On the **40th day** of the assembly window, the engagement manager, while collating, discovers that the **inventory net-realisable-value testing was never actually performed**, quietly performs it now, and files the results as part of "assembly." Evaluate whether this is a valid use of the assembly period, and state the correct treatment, citing the relevant Standard.

**Answer:**
The matter is governed by **SQC 1** (assembly of the final file) and **SA 230 "Audit Documentation"** (documentation and post-report changes).

**(i) Assembly deadline.** SQC 1 requires the final audit file to be assembled **on a timely basis, ordinarily not more than 60 days after the date of the auditor's report.**
Report date = 31 August 2026. Counting 60 days from 31 August 2026: September (30 days) takes the count to 30 September (day 30); the remaining 30 days run into October, reaching **30 October 2026** (day 60). Therefore the final file must be assembled **on or before 30 October 2026.**

**(ii) Is performing the NRV testing on day 40 valid?** **No — it is invalid.** Assembly during the ≤60-day window is an **administrative completion only** — it permits collating, sorting, cross-referencing, discarding superseded drafts, and documenting evidence *already obtained, discussed and agreed with the team before the report date*. It does **not** permit performing **new audit procedures, drawing new conclusions, or obtaining fresh evidence** on matters unresolved at the report date.

Inventory **net-realisable-value testing is a substantive audit procedure**, not administrative collation. It should have been *concluded before the report date* of 31 August 2026. Performing it on day 40 is **new audit work masquerading as assembly** — a direct breach of the assembly rule. The **bright line is the report date for substance**, not the 60-day date. Worse, its discovery reveals that the report may have been **dated without sufficient appropriate evidence** on inventory valuation — meaning the opinion was potentially issued prematurely.

**Correct treatment.** This is *not* assembly. Because substantive work is being performed *after* the report date, SA 230's rule on **matters arising after the date of the auditor's report** applies: the auditor must document **the circumstances, the new procedures performed, the evidence obtained and conclusions reached, and when and by whom the file changes were made and reviewed**, and must **never delete** the original file contents. The auditor must also consider the implications for the **already-issued opinion** (linking to SA 560 "Subsequent Events").

**Conclusion:** The file must be assembled by 30 October 2026, but the NRV testing on day 40 is an invalid, substantive intruder into an administrative window; it must be documented under SA 230's after-the-report-date rule, and the auditor must assess whether the opinion was issued without sufficient appropriate evidence.

*(Full-marks tip: the trap is treating *anything done within 60 days* as fine. State the bright line — report date for substance, 60-day date only for administration — and route the intruder through SA 230's post-report rule, not through "assembly.")*

### Q66. Ch: Audit Documentation & Quality — The "Experienced Auditor" Benchmark and Identifying Characteristics (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** During an NFRA inspection of the audit of Stellar Ltd, the reviewer examines two working papers. Working Paper A, on the revenue cycle, states in full: *"Revenue tested on a sample basis, found to be in order."* Working Paper B, on a systematic sample of purchase vouchers, states: *"Sample of vouchers checked."* The engagement partner insists both areas were tested thoroughly and offers to explain verbally what was done. (i) State the benchmark SA 230 sets for the sufficiency of documentation and its three limbs. (ii) Explain why both working papers fail that benchmark, referring to the requirement to record "identifying characteristics." (iii) State whether the partner's offer of oral explanation cures the defect.

**Answer:**
The matter is governed by **SA 230 "Audit Documentation."**

**(i) The benchmark and its three limbs.** Documentation must be sufficient to enable an **experienced auditor, having no previous connection with the audit, to understand**:
- (a) the **nature, timing and extent** of the audit procedures performed;
- (b) the **results** of those procedures and the audit evidence obtained; and
- (c) the **significant matters** arising, the **conclusions** reached, and the **significant professional judgements** made in reaching those conclusions.

The benchmark reader is a *skilled but disconnected* peer: the file must stand alone, without the original auditor present, and without relying on context that lived only in his head.

**(ii) Why both working papers fail.**

*Working Paper A ("Revenue tested on a sample basis, found to be in order").* It discloses **none** of the three limbs. It does not state the **nature** (how revenue was tested — vouching? recomputation? confirmation?), the **timing**, or the **extent** (how many items, from what population). It does not record the **results** (what was actually found). And it records no **conclusion** beyond an unsupported "in order." An experienced auditor with no previous connection could reconstruct nothing.

*Working Paper B ("Sample of vouchers checked").* For a **systematic sample**, SA 230 requires the auditor to record the **identifying characteristics** of the items tested — for a systematic sample, the **source, the starting point and the sampling interval** (e.g., "every 40th voucher from voucher no. 3021, source: purchase vouchers 1 Apr–31 Mar"). Without these, "a sample of vouchers checked" is **unfalsifiable** — no one can re-perform or trace back to the exact items tested. Recording identifying characteristics is precisely what makes the work re-traceable, and it is entirely absent.

Additionally, neither paper records **who performed** the work and **the date**, nor **who reviewed** it, the **date and extent** of review — so the supervision trail is also missing.

**(iii) Does oral explanation cure the defect?** **No.** SA 230 is explicit that **oral explanations by the auditor, on their own, do not represent adequate support** for the work performed or the conclusions reached; they may only be used to *clarify or explain* information already contained in adequate documentation. A file that "would have been fine if the auditor had been available to explain it" has already failed, because the whole purpose of documentation is that the file must stand **without the auditor present.**

**Conclusion:** Both working papers fail the SA 230 "experienced auditor with no previous connection" benchmark — A discloses none of the nature/timing/extent, results or conclusions, and B omits the identifying characteristics needed to make a systematic sample re-traceable; the partner's offer of oral explanation cannot cure the deficiency, as talking may clarify but never substitute for the file.

*(Full-marks tip: reproduce the benchmark's exact wording and all three limbs, name the identifying characteristics required for a *systematic* sample (source + start + interval), and state the oral-explanation rule. Each is separately marked.)*

### Q67. Ch: Audit Documentation & Quality — SQC 1 Elements and Monitoring (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** A small audit firm asserts: "Our partners are experienced professionals, so our audits are automatically of high quality; we have never inspected a completed engagement, and we see no need for any firm-wide system." Evaluate this assertion by reference to the objective and the six elements of SQC 1, with particular emphasis on the monitoring requirement.

**Answer:**
The assertion is **incorrect** and reflects a misunderstanding of **SQC 1 "Quality Control for Firms that Perform Audits and Reviews of Historical Financial Information, and Other Assurance and Related Services Engagements."**

**Objective of SQC 1.** SQC 1 requires the firm to establish and maintain a **system of quality control** that provides **reasonable assurance** that (a) the firm and its personnel comply with professional standards and applicable legal and regulatory requirements, and (b) the reports issued are appropriate in the circumstances. Note it is *reasonable*, not *absolute*, assurance — but the firm cannot dispense with the system merely because its partners are experienced. Quality must be a **system, not a personal virtue**, because a rule that depends on each individual's discipline fails silently and invisibly.

**The six elements the firm must operate:**

| # | Element | What it requires |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Leadership responsibilities for quality** (tone at the top) | Leadership must ensure commercial considerations do not override the quality of work |
| 2 | **Ethical requirements, including independence** | Written independence confirmations from relevant personnel at least annually; safeguards against familiarity threats, including rotation |
| 3 | **Acceptance and continuance of clients and engagements** | Accept only clients the firm can serve competently, ethically, and whose integrity is acceptable |
| 4 | **Human resources** | Recruitment, competence, training, capabilities, performance evaluation and assignment of teams |
| 5 | **Engagement performance** | Consistent quality, direction/supervision/review, consultation, EQCR, resolution of differences, and the housing of documentation, assembly (≤60 days) and retention (≥7 years) with safe custody |
| 6 | **Monitoring** | Ongoing evaluation of the firm's system, including cyclical inspection of at least one completed engagement per engagement partner |

**Emphasis on monitoring (Element 6) — the direct answer to the assertion.** The firm's specific claim — that it has "never inspected a completed engagement" and needs no system — is a **direct breach of Element 6.** Monitoring requires the firm to appoint a competent person with sufficient authority to take responsibility for the monitoring process and to perform a **cyclical inspection of at least one completed engagement per engagement partner.** Monitoring is the **"audit of the audit system"** — the mechanism by which the *firm* discovers whether its quality-control system is actually working, before a regulator does. The competence of individual partners is **no substitute** for it: "trust the partners" is exactly the un-checked trust the whole architecture is designed to replace. Deficiencies found must be evaluated as isolated or systemic and communicated with recommended remedial action.

**Conclusion:** The assertion fails; SQC 1 requires every firm to operate all six elements — leadership, ethics/independence, acceptance/continuance, human resources, engagement performance and monitoring — and the firm's refusal to inspect completed engagements is a specific breach of the monitoring element, which experience of the partners cannot cure.

*(Full-marks tip: list all six elements by name and single out monitoring's specific detail — "cyclical inspection of at least one completed engagement per engagement partner." Vague answers ("the firm should review some files sometimes") lose the monitoring mark.)*

### Q68. Ch: Audit Documentation & Quality — EQC Reviewer Eligibility and the Consultation Trap (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Lyra Pharma Ltd is a **listed** company. During the audit, the engagement partner **repeatedly consults Partner K** on the accounting for a complex out-licensing arrangement, and K effectively co-develops the engagement team's conclusion on that significant matter. At the completion stage, the firm proposes to appoint **Partner K as the engagement quality control reviewer**, reasoning that "K already understands the licensing issue better than anyone else, so the review will be quick and well-informed." Evaluate whether K may be appointed as the EQC reviewer, and explain the underlying principle, citing the relevant guidance.

**Answer:**
The matter is governed by **SA 220 "Quality Control for an Audit of Financial Statements"** and **SQC 1**, which set the objectivity safeguards for an **Engagement Quality Control Review (EQCR)**.

**The purpose of an EQCR.** An EQCR is an **objective evaluation, by a reviewer who is *not* part of the engagement team**, of the significant judgements made by the team and the conclusions reached in formulating the report. Its entire reason for existing is to break the **self-review bias** of the people who made the judgements — the person who made a decision is the worst placed to spot its flaw.

**Application to Partner K.** The firm's efficiency argument is exactly **backwards.** Because K **helped form the very conclusion** on the out-licensing arrangement — one of the *significant judgements* the EQCR must evaluate — appointing K as EQC reviewer would have **K reviewing K's own judgement.** That recreates the precise self-review bias the EQCR exists to eliminate. SQC 1's objectivity safeguards provide that:
- the reviewer must **not have participated** in the engagement during the period under review (beyond the review itself);
- the engagement partner may **consult** the intended reviewer, but such consultation must **not compromise** the reviewer's eligibility to perform the review objectively.

Here, K's involvement was **not a minor consultation** — K **co-developed** the conclusion on a *significant matter*. This plainly compromises K's eligibility, at least on the licensing judgement. Therefore **K may not be appointed as the EQC reviewer.**

**The correct course.** The firm should appoint a **different eligible partner** (or a suitably qualified external reviewer) who **did not participate** in forming the engagement's conclusions. Note the nuance: had K's consultation been on a **minor, non-significant** matter, K's eligibility might have survived — the disqualifier bites where the consulted matter is one of the **significant judgements** the EQCR is required to evaluate.

**Conclusion:** Partner K cannot be appointed as the EQC reviewer, because his heavy consultation co-developed a significant judgement, and reviewing his own conclusion would recreate the self-review bias the EQCR exists to break; the firm must appoint an independent reviewer who took no part in forming the engagement's conclusions.

*(Full-marks tip: the mark-winning point is that heavy prior consultation *disqualifies* the intended reviewer, and that the disqualifier bites specifically on *significant* judgements. Candidates who accept K "because he knows it best" invert the mechanism and lose the core marks.)*

### Q69. Ch: Company Audit — First Auditor of a Government Company (Sec 139(7)) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** "Bharat Rail Infra Ltd." is a Government company in which the Central Government holds 74% of the paid-up share capital. The company was incorporated on 12th April 2025. The Board of Directors, being aware that the first auditor is to be appointed within 30 days of registration, appointed M/s Sen & Associates as the first auditor in its Board meeting held on 5th May 2025 and intimated the auditor. The Comptroller and Auditor-General of India (C&AG) later objected that this appointment is invalid. Examine the validity of the Board's action and state the correct procedure for appointment of the first auditor of a Government company, including the consequences of default.

**Answer:**
The appointment of the first auditor of a **Government company**, or any company owned or controlled directly or indirectly by the Central Government / State Government(s), is governed by **Section 139(7) of the Companies Act, 2013** and **not by Section 139(6)** which applies only to non-Government companies.

Under **Section 139(7)**, the first auditor of such a Government company shall be **appointed by the Comptroller and Auditor-General of India (C&AG) within 60 days** from the date of registration of the company. The Board of Directors has **no power to make the first appointment** in a Government company; its role is only a fallback and a further fallback lies with the members.

The statutory scheme, in sequence, is:

| Stage | Who appoints | Time limit (from date of registration) |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | C&AG | Within **60 days** |
| On C&AG's failure | **Board of Directors** | Within **next 30 days** |
| On Board's failure | Members at an **Extraordinary General Meeting (EGM)** | Within **60 days** (Board to inform members) |
| Tenure of first auditor | — | Till the **conclusion of the first Annual General Meeting** |

**Application to the facts:** Since Bharat Rail Infra Ltd. is a Government company (Central Government holding 74%, which is more than 51%, thus a Government company u/s 2(45)), the right of first appointment vests **exclusively with the C&AG** for a period of 60 days from 12th April 2025. The Board acted prematurely on 5th May 2025 — well within the C&AG's 60-day window — and therefore **usurped a power that did not belong to it**. The Board could have appointed only if the C&AG had **failed to appoint within 60 days**, i.e., only on or after 12th June 2025, and then only within the next 30 days.

**Conclusion:** The appointment of M/s Sen & Associates by the Board on 5th May 2025 is **invalid and void**. The C&AG's objection is correct. The C&AG retains the right to appoint the first auditor up to 11th June 2025; only on its failure does the power pass to the Board, and thereafter to the members in EGM.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards correctly distinguishing 139(7) [Government company, C&AG, 60 days] from 139(6) [non-Government, Board, 30 days]. Common deduction: applying the 30-day Board rule of 139(6) to a Government company, or omitting the two-tier fallback [Board 30 days, then members 60 days].)*

### Q70. Ch: Company Audit — Casual Vacancy Caused by Resignation & Auditor's Duty (Sec 139(8) & 140(2)/(3)) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** M/s Rao & Co., statutory auditors of Meridian Textiles Ltd. (a non-Government company), resigned on 20th August 2025 citing pre-occupation, with three years of their term still remaining. The Board wants to know: (a) how the resulting vacancy is to be filled and within what time; and (b) what compliance obligation, if any, rests on the resigning auditor. Advise, citing the relevant provisions, and state the consequence of the auditor's non-compliance.

**Answer:**
A vacancy created by the **resignation** of an auditor is a **casual vacancy** and is governed by **Section 139(8)(i)** of the Companies Act, 2013 for a non-Government company, read with **Section 140(2) and 140(3)** which impose obligations on the resigning auditor.

**(a) Filling the casual vacancy — Section 139(8)(i):**
Where a casual vacancy in the office of auditor arises **due to resignation**, it must be filled by the **Board of Directors within 30 days**; but because it is caused by resignation, such Board appointment **must also be approved by the company at a general meeting** convened **within 3 months** of the recommendation of the Board. The auditor so appointed holds office **only till the conclusion of the next Annual General Meeting**.

The distinction is important: a casual vacancy arising from a cause **other than resignation** (e.g., death or disqualification) can be filled by the Board alone; but one arising from **resignation** additionally requires **members' approval in general meeting**.

**(b) Obligation of the resigning auditor — Section 140(2):**
The auditor who has resigned shall, **within 30 days** from the date of resignation, **file a statement in Form ADT-3** with the **company** and with the **Registrar of Companies (ROC)** (and, in the case of a Government company or a Government-controlled company, also with the **C&AG**), indicating the **reasons and other facts** relevant to the resignation.

**Consequence of non-compliance — Section 140(3):** If the auditor does not comply with Section 140(2), he/it shall be **liable to a penalty of ₹50,000 or an amount equal to the remuneration of the auditor, whichever is less**, and in case of continuing failure, a **further penalty of ₹500 for each day** after the first during which such failure continues, **subject to a maximum of ₹5,00,000**.

**Conclusion:** The vacancy caused by M/s Rao & Co.'s resignation must be filled by the Board within 30 days and **ratified by members in a general meeting within 3 months**; the new auditor holds office only up to the next AGM. M/s Rao & Co. must file **Form ADT-3 within 30 days** of resignation, failing which the penalty under **Section 140(3)** is attracted.

*(Full-marks tip: State both the 30-day Board window and the 3-month general-meeting approval — resignation vacancies uniquely need members' approval. Common deduction: forgetting Form ADT-3 / the exact 140(3) penalty figures.)*

### Q71. Ch: Company Audit — Auditor's Right of Access to Records, Branches & Subsidiaries (Sec 143(1) & 143(8)) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** During the audit of Crest Holdings Ltd. for FY 2024-25, the following situations arose:
(i) The Managing Director refused the auditor access to the minutes of the Board's Nomination Committee, saying they were "confidential and not part of accounts."
(ii) The company has a branch at Nagpur whose accounts were audited by another firm, M/s Kale & Co. The management asserted the statutory auditor cannot look into the branch working papers.
(iii) Crest Holdings prepares consolidated financial statements including a subsidiary, Crest Logistics Pvt. Ltd., audited by a different auditor; the parent's management refused the statutory auditor access to the subsidiary's records.
Examine the auditor's rights in each situation with reference to the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:**
The rights and powers of a company auditor flow from **Section 143(1) of the Companies Act, 2013**, which confers a **right of access at all times to the books of account and vouchers of the company**, whether kept at the registered office or elsewhere, and a right to require from the officers of the company such information and explanation as the auditor considers necessary for the performance of his duties.

**(i) Access to Board / committee minutes:**
The right of access under Section 143(1) is **not confined to "books of account" in a narrow sense**; the auditor is entitled to all records, documents and information necessary to form an opinion — including minutes of the Board and its committees where they bear on transactions, approvals (e.g., related-party approvals, remuneration) and the truth and fairness of the financial statements. Withholding them **impedes the audit** and, if information required is not provided, the auditor must **report the fact** in the audit report under **Section 143(3)(b)/(h)**. Hence the MD's refusal is **not tenable**.

**(ii) Branch records — Section 143(8):**
Where a branch is audited by a person other than the company's auditor (here M/s Kale & Co.), the **branch auditor** prepares a report on the branch accounts and **sends it to the company auditor**, who deals with it in his report **in such manner as he considers necessary**. Crucially, the proviso to **Section 143(8)** gives the **company's (principal) auditor a right of access at all times to the books, accounts and vouchers of the branch**, whether audited by him or by a branch auditor. Therefore the statutory auditor **is entitled to examine the Nagpur branch records and the branch auditor's work**; management's assertion is incorrect.

**(iii) Subsidiary records for consolidation — proviso to Section 143(1):**
The **proviso to Section 143(1)** specifically extends the right of access of the company's auditor to **all the records of all its subsidiaries and associate companies** **in so far as it relates to the consolidation of financial statements** with that of the holding company. Since Crest Holdings prepares consolidated financial statements including Crest Logistics Pvt. Ltd., the statutory auditor of the holding company **has a right of access to the subsidiary's records** to the extent needed for consolidation. The parent management's refusal is **without legal basis**.

| Situation | Governing provision | Auditor's entitlement |
|---|---|---|
| Board/committee minutes | Sec 143(1) main | Right of access; refusal to be reported u/s 143(3) |
| Branch audited by another | Proviso to Sec 143(8) | Access to branch books/vouchers + branch auditor's report |
| Subsidiary records (CFS) | Proviso to Sec 143(1) | Access to subsidiary/associate records for consolidation |

**Conclusion:** In all three situations the auditor's rights **prevail over management's objections**. Denial of access in (i) must be reported under Section 143(3); the branch and subsidiary access rights under Sections 143(8) and 143(1) proviso are statutory and cannot be curtailed by management.

*(Full-marks tip: The subsidiary-access right is the proviso to 143(1) "in so far as it relates to consolidation" — quote that limitation. Branch access is the proviso to 143(8). Common deduction: mixing up the two provisos or claiming unlimited access to subsidiary records beyond consolidation.)*

### Q73. Ch: Company Audit — Auditor's Duty to Enquire u/s 143(1) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** The Companies Act, 2013 requires the auditor to make specific enquiries during the course of audit. In the audit of Sunbeam Alloys Ltd., the auditor notices that (i) the company has advanced ₹40 lakh as a loan to a related party against which no security has been taken; and (ii) several book entries reduce closing inventory by ₹12 lakh with a corresponding credit to a directors' current account near the year-end. Identify the statutory enquiries the auditor is bound to make under **Section 143(1)** and relate them to these two situations.

**Answer:**
**Section 143(1)** of the Companies Act, 2013 requires the auditor to **enquire** into certain specified matters. These enquiries are of the nature of **internal checks** the auditor must perform; the auditor is **not required to report** on them unless he has any special comments to make, i.e., he reports only if the answer to an enquiry is **unsatisfactory**.

The matters into which the auditor must enquire are:
(a) Whether **loans and advances** made by the company on the basis of security have been **properly secured** and whether the **terms** on which they are made are **prejudicial** to the interests of the company or its members;
(b) Whether **transactions** of the company which are **represented merely by book entries** are **prejudicial** to the interests of the company;
(c) Where the company is **not an investment company or a banking company**, whether so much of the **assets** of the company as consist of **shares, debentures and other securities** have been **sold at a price less than that at which they were purchased**;
(d) Whether **loans and advances** made by the company have been **shown as deposits**;
(e) Whether **personal expenses** have been **charged to revenue account**;
(f) Where it is stated in the books and documents that any **shares have been allotted for cash**, whether cash has **actually been received**, and if not, whether the position stated in the books is correct, regular and not misleading.

**Application:**
- **Situation (i) — ₹40 lakh unsecured loan to a related party:** This attracts enquiry **(a)**. The auditor must examine whether the loan is **properly secured** and whether its **terms are prejudicial** to the company/members. As no security has been taken and the borrower is a related party, the auditor must assess whether the terms are prejudicial and, if so, comment appropriately (and also consider CARO 2020 clause 3(iii) reporting).
- **Situation (ii) — inventory reduced by ₹12 lakh through book entries against a director's account:** This attracts enquiry **(b)** — transactions represented **merely by book entries** that may be **prejudicial to the interests of the company**. The auditor must investigate the substance of these entries, whether they are genuine, and whether they mask a diversion in favour of the director.

**Conclusion:** Both situations fall squarely within the auditor's statutory enquiry duty under **Section 143(1)(a) and (b)** respectively. The auditor need not make a general report on these enquiries, but where the results are **unsatisfactory** (as they may well be here), he must record his **comments/qualification** in the report.

*(Full-marks tip: Emphasise that 143(1) enquiries are reported only if the answer is unsatisfactory — that "report by exception" nuance is a mark. Common deduction: confusing 143(1) enquiry matters with 143(3) reporting clauses.)*

### Q74. Ch: Company Audit — Reappointment & Non-Reappointment of Retiring Auditor (Sec 139(9) & 139(10)) (Marks: 4) [Application]
**Question:** At the AGM of Delta Ceramics Ltd., the retiring auditor M/s Iyer & Co. (eligible and willing, having completed only its first term of appointment) was neither reappointed nor was any express resolution passed either for its reappointment or for appointing someone else — the item simply lapsed for want of time. The management believes the office of auditor is now vacant and the Board can appoint a new auditor as a casual vacancy. Is this view correct? Advise.

**Answer:**
The reappointment of a **retiring auditor** is governed by **Sections 139(9) and 139(10)** of the Companies Act, 2013.

Under **Section 139(9)**, a **retiring auditor may be reappointed** at an AGM if — (a) he is **not disqualified** for reappointment; (b) he has **not given the company a notice in writing of his unwillingness** to be reappointed; and (c) a **special resolution has not been passed** at that meeting appointing some other auditor or providing expressly that he shall **not be reappointed**.

Under **Section 139(10)**, where at any AGM **no auditor is appointed or reappointed**, the **existing auditor shall continue to be the auditor** of the company.

**Application:** M/s Iyer & Co. is eligible and willing, has not given a notice of unwillingness, and no special resolution was passed appointing another auditor or expressly barring its reappointment. All three conditions of Section 139(9) are satisfied. Since the item merely lapsed and **no auditor was appointed or reappointed at the AGM**, Section 139(10) operates — the **existing auditor (M/s Iyer & Co.) continues** in office.

**Conclusion:** The management's view is **incorrect**. There is **no casual vacancy**; the office is not vacant. By virtue of Section 139(10), M/s Iyer & Co. continues as the auditor, and the Board cannot appoint a fresh auditor treating the situation as a casual vacancy.

### Q75. Ch: Company Audit — C&AG's Powers over the Audit of a Government Company (Sec 143(5), (6) & (7)) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the special powers of the Comptroller and Auditor-General of India (C&AG) in relation to the audit of a Government company under **Section 143** of the Companies Act, 2013, covering the auditor's report to the C&AG, supplementary audit, and test audit.

**Answer:**
In the case of a **Government company** (or a company owned or controlled by Government), the audit is subject to the **special oversight of the C&AG** under **Sections 143(5), 143(6) and 143(7)** of the Companies Act, 2013, in addition to the ordinary duties of the auditor.

**Section 143(5) — Directions and report to the C&AG:**
The **C&AG appoints the auditor** under Section 139(5)/(7) and may **direct the manner** in which the company's accounts shall be audited. The auditor so appointed must, in his audit report, **give a report to the C&AG** stating compliance with such directions and their impact on the accounts, financial statements, etc. The auditor is required to **submit a copy of the audit report to the C&AG**.

**Section 143(6) — Supplementary audit and comments:**
The C&AG has the right, **within 60 days** from the date of receipt of the audit report, to —
(a) conduct a **supplementary audit** of the financial statements by such person(s) as it may authorise, and require information/additional information as it may direct; and
(b) issue **comments upon or a supplement to** the audit report.
Any **comments or supplement** so issued must be **sent to every person entitled to copies of the audited financial statements** and must be **placed before the AGM** at the same time and in the same manner as the audit report.

**Section 143(7) — Test audit:**
Without prejudice to the above, the C&AG may, if it considers necessary, by an **order, cause a "test audit"** to be conducted of the accounts of such Government company, and the provisions of **Section 19A of the Comptroller and Auditor-General's (Duties, Powers and Conditions of Service) Act, 1971** apply to such test audit.

**Conclusion:** The audit of a Government company is a **two-tier process** — the statutory auditor appointed by the C&AG audits and reports (Sec 143(5)), the C&AG may then conduct a **supplementary audit and issue comments within 60 days** (Sec 143(6)), and may additionally order a **test audit** (Sec 143(7)) — thereby subjecting Government company accounts to the C&AG's superintendence.

*(Full-marks tip: The 60-day supplementary-audit window and the requirement that C&AG comments be placed before the AGM are frequently-tested specifics. Common deduction: omitting the test-audit power under 143(7) / Section 19A.)*

### Q76. Ch: Audit Report — Forming an Opinion & Qualified Opinion Wording (SA 700 & SA 705) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** In the audit of Novena Pharma Ltd. for FY 2024-25, the auditor concludes that inventories are overstated by ₹85 lakh because the company valued finished goods at cost without writing them down to net realisable value, contrary to AS 2. The misstatement is **material but not pervasive** to the financial statements as a whole. (a) State the basis on which an auditor forms an opinion under SA 700. (b) Identify the type of opinion to be expressed and **draft the "Basis for Qualified Opinion" and "Qualified Opinion" paragraphs** as they would appear in the report.

**Answer:**
**(a) Forming an opinion — SA 700 (Revised):**
Under **SA 700, "Forming an Opinion and Reporting on Financial Statements,"** the auditor shall form an opinion on whether the financial statements are prepared, in all material respects, **in accordance with the applicable financial reporting framework**. To form that opinion, the auditor concludes whether he has obtained **reasonable assurance** about whether the financial statements as a whole are **free from material misstatement**, whether due to fraud or error. This conclusion takes into account whether **sufficient appropriate audit evidence** has been obtained (SA 330), whether **uncorrected misstatements** are material individually or in aggregate (SA 450), and qualitative aspects such as the appropriateness of accounting policies, reasonableness of estimates and adequacy of disclosures.

**(b) Type of opinion — SA 705 (Revised):**
Under **SA 705, "Modifications to the Opinion in the Independent Auditor's Report,"** the auditor expresses a **modified opinion** when he concludes that the financial statements as a whole are **not free from material misstatement**. Here the misstatement (₹85 lakh NRV write-down not made) is **material but not pervasive**; accordingly the auditor expresses a **Qualified Opinion** (not adverse). Because the modification arises from a **material misstatement**, the auditor uses the heading **"Basis for Qualified Opinion"** and, where practicable, **quantifies the financial effect**.

Illustrative report paragraphs:

> **Basis for Qualified Opinion**
> The Company's inventories of finished goods are carried in the Balance Sheet at ₹___, valued at cost. However, in accordance with AS 2, "Valuation of Inventories," inventories should be valued at the lower of cost and net realisable value. Had the finished goods been written down to their net realisable value, inventories, profit before tax and shareholders' funds would each have been **lower by ₹85 lakh**. Our opinion is qualified in respect of this matter.

> **Qualified Opinion**
> In our opinion and to the best of our information and according to the explanations given to us, **except for the effects of the matter described in the Basis for Qualified Opinion section** of our report, the aforesaid financial statements give the information required by the Companies Act, 2013 in the manner so required and give a true and fair view in conformity with the accounting principles generally accepted in India, of the state of affairs of the Company as at 31st March 2025, and its profit and its cash flows for the year ended on that date.

**Conclusion:** As the AS 2 non-compliance is **material but not pervasive**, the auditor expresses a **Qualified Opinion** with the effect quantified at **₹85 lakh** in the Basis paragraph, using the "except for" phrasing in the opinion paragraph.

*(Full-marks tip: Use the exact phrases — "Basis for Qualified Opinion", "except for the effects", and quantify. Common deduction: escalating to adverse [material AND pervasive] when the facts say material but not pervasive.)*

### Q77. Ch: Audit Report — Adverse Opinion & Disclaimer of Opinion with Example Wording (SA 705) (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Distinguish, under SA 705 (Revised), between an **Adverse Opinion** and a **Disclaimer of Opinion** in terms of the circumstances that give rise to each. For each, state a typical scenario and **draft the opinion paragraph** as it would read in the auditor's report.

**Answer:**
**SA 705 (Revised), "Modifications to the Opinion in the Independent Auditor's Report,"** provides for three types of modified opinion. The choice depends on **two factors**: (i) the **nature** of the matter (a **material misstatement**, or an **inability to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence**); and (ii) the auditor's **judgement about the pervasiveness** of its effects.

| Basis | Adverse Opinion | Disclaimer of Opinion |
|---|---|---|
| Nature of matter | Financial statements **are materially misstated** | Auditor is **unable to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence** |
| Pervasiveness | Effects are **both material and pervasive** | Possible effects are **both material and pervasive** |
| Auditor's conclusion | The financial statements **do not give a true and fair view** | The auditor **does not express an opinion** |
| Basis heading used | "Basis for Adverse Opinion" | "Basis for Disclaimer of Opinion" |

**Adverse Opinion — typical scenario:** A subsidiary that should have been consolidated has been excluded, or several accounting policies are misapplied, so that the misstatements are **so material and pervasive** that the financial statements as a whole are misleading.

> **Adverse Opinion**
> In our opinion and to the best of our information and according to the explanations given to us, **because of the significance of the matter discussed in the Basis for Adverse Opinion section**, the aforesaid financial statements **do not give a true and fair view** in conformity with the accounting principles generally accepted in India, of the state of affairs of the Company as at 31st March 2025, and its loss and its cash flows for the year then ended.

**Disclaimer of Opinion — typical scenario:** The auditor was appointed after the year-end and **could not observe the physical count of inventory** and there were **no alternative procedures** to satisfy himself; combined with unavailability of records, the possible effects are material and pervasive.

> **Disclaimer of Opinion**
> We were engaged to audit the financial statements of ___ Limited. **We do not express an opinion** on the accompanying financial statements of the Company. **Because of the significance of the matter described in the Basis for Disclaimer of Opinion section** of our report, we have not been able to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence to provide a basis for an audit opinion on these financial statements.

**Conclusion:** An **Adverse Opinion** is expressed when the statements **are** materially and pervasively **misstated**; a **Disclaimer** is expressed when the auditor **cannot obtain evidence** and the possible effects are material and pervasive, so that no opinion can be given.

*(Full-marks tip: The two-axis matrix — nature (misstatement vs inability) × pervasiveness — is the heart of the answer. In a disclaimer, note the wording shift to "We were engaged to audit" and "We do not express an opinion." Common deduction: giving an adverse-opinion scenario for a disclaimer or vice-versa.)*

### Q78. Ch: Audit Report — Emphasis of Matter Paragraph with Example Wording (SA 706) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Corvus Constructions Ltd. is a defendant in a major litigation the outcome of which is uncertain; the matter is **appropriately disclosed** in the notes to the financial statements, and the auditor concurs with the accounting and disclosure. The auditor nevertheless wishes to draw users' attention to this matter. (a) State when an **Emphasis of Matter (EOM)** paragraph is used under SA 706 and its pre-conditions. (b) Draft an illustrative EOM paragraph and state where it is placed relative to the Opinion section.

**Answer:**
**(a) When an Emphasis of Matter paragraph is used — SA 706 (Revised):**
Under **SA 706, "Emphasis of Matter Paragraphs and Other Matter Paragraphs in the Independent Auditor's Report,"** an **Emphasis of Matter (EOM) paragraph** is a paragraph included in the auditor's report that refers to a matter **appropriately presented or disclosed** in the financial statements that, in the auditor's judgement, is of **such importance that it is fundamental to users' understanding** of the financial statements.

An auditor may include an EOM paragraph provided **two conditions** are met: (i) the auditor **would not be required to modify the opinion** under SA 705 as a result of the matter — i.e., the matter is **correctly accounted for and disclosed**; and (ii) when SA 701 applies, the matter has **not been determined to be a key audit matter** to be communicated in the report. An EOM is **not a substitute** for a modified opinion, nor for disclosures the framework requires, nor for reporting a KAM.

**(b) Illustrative EOM paragraph and its placement:**
The EOM paragraph is placed in a **separate section** with the heading **"Emphasis of Matter,"** immediately **after the Opinion section** (and after the Basis for Opinion section), and it must state that the auditor's opinion is **not modified** in respect of the matter emphasised.

> **Emphasis of Matter**
> We draw attention to Note ___ to the financial statements, which describes the uncertainty relating to the outcome of the litigation filed against the Company. Our opinion is **not modified** in respect of this matter.

**Conclusion:** Since the litigation is **appropriately disclosed** and the accounting is correct, no modification is warranted; the auditor uses an **Emphasis of Matter paragraph under SA 706**, placed after the Opinion section, expressly stating that the opinion is **not modified**.

*(Full-marks tip: Always add the sentence "Our opinion is not modified in respect of this matter" and cross-reference the specific note — omitting either loses marks. Common deduction: using EOM as a disguised qualification, or confusing EOM [disclosed matter] with Other Matter [not disclosed in the FS].)*

### Q79. Ch: Audit Report — Signing of the Report & Essential Elements (Sec 145 & SA 700) (Marks: 4) [Theory]

Under **Section 145 of the Companies Act, 2013**, the **person appointed as auditor** of the company shall **sign the auditor's report** or sign or certify any other document of the company, in accordance with the provisions of **Section 141(2)** (i.e., where a firm is appointed, only a partner who is a Chartered Accountant may sign). **Key elements of the auditor's report — SA 700 (Revised):**

| Element | Content |
|---|---|

| **Responsibilities of Management / TCWG** | For the financial statements and internal control |

### Q80. Ch: Audit Report — CARO 2020 Clause 3(ii): Inventory & Working Capital Limits (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** During the FY 2024-25 audit of Highland Foods Ltd., the auditor gathers the following:
| Particulars | Details |
|---|---|
| Physical verification of inventory | Conducted by management **once a year**, only at the year-end |
| Discrepancies on verification | Book stock ₹9.60 crore vs physical stock ₹9.18 crore |
| Working capital limits sanctioned | ₹7 crore aggregate, sanctioned by a consortium of banks on the security of current assets |
| Quarterly returns/statements filed with banks | Stock and receivable statements filed, but figures **differ materially** from books of account |
State the reporting requirements under **CARO 2020 Clause 3(ii)** and how the auditor should report on the above.

**Answer:**
**Clause 3(ii) of the Companies (Auditor's Report) Order, 2020 (CARO 2020)** deals with **inventory and working-capital limits**, and has two sub-clauses:

**Clause 3(ii)(a) — Physical verification of inventory:**
The auditor must report whether **physical verification of inventory** has been conducted **at reasonable intervals** by the management; whether, in the auditor's opinion, the **coverage and procedure** of such verification is **appropriate**; and whether **any discrepancies of 10% or more in the aggregate for each class of inventory** were noticed and, if so, whether they have been **properly dealt with in the books of account**.

**Clause 3(ii)(b) — Working-capital limits:**
The auditor must report whether, during any point of time of the year, the company has been **sanctioned working-capital limits in excess of ₹5 crore, in aggregate, from banks or financial institutions on the basis of security of current assets**; and whether the **quarterly returns or statements filed** by the company with such banks/financial institutions **are in agreement with the books of account** of the company, and if not, give details.

**Application to Highland Foods Ltd.:**

- **Frequency/coverage (3(ii)(a)):** Verification only **once a year at year-end** may not be at "reasonable intervals" for a food company with perishable/fast-moving stock; the auditor should evaluate the appropriateness of coverage and procedure and comment adversely if inadequate.
- **Discrepancy (3(ii)(a)):** Discrepancy = ₹9.60 cr − ₹9.18 cr = **₹0.42 cr**, i.e., ₹0.42/₹9.60 = **4.375%**, which is **less than 10%**. Hence, although the discrepancy is to be assessed for proper accounting, it is **below the 10% reporting threshold** for the specific comment; the auditor should still confirm it has been dealt with in the books.
- **Working-capital limits (3(ii)(b)):** Sanctioned limits are **₹7 crore (> ₹5 crore)** on the security of current assets — so the clause is **applicable**. Since the **quarterly stock/receivable statements filed with the banks differ materially from the books**, the auditor must report this **disagreement and give particulars** of the differences.

**Conclusion:** Under Clause 3(ii)(a), the auditor comments on the adequacy of verification; the discrepancy of **4.375%** is below 10% but must be confirmed as properly recorded. Under Clause 3(ii)(b), as limits of ₹7 crore exceed ₹5 crore, the auditor must **report that the quarterly returns are not in agreement with the books**, with details.

*(Full-marks tip: Compute the discrepancy percentage against the 10% threshold and the working-capital limit against the ₹5 crore threshold — those two numeric tests are where marks lie. Common deduction: missing that 3(ii)(b) requires the returns to be "on the basis of security of current assets".)*

### Q81. Ch: Audit Report — CARO 2020 Clause 3(vii): Statutory Dues (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** The following data pertains to Zenith Steels Ltd. for the year ended 31st March 2025:
| Statutory due | Position at year-end |
|---|---|
| Provident Fund | ₹6.5 lakh outstanding for **8 months**, undisputed |
| GST | ₹22 lakh; the company **disputes** the demand and has filed an appeal before the Appellate Authority; ₹4 lakh deposited under protest |
| Income-tax (TDS) | ₹1.2 lakh deducted but **not deposited**, outstanding for 5 months, undisputed |
| Customs duty | ₹15 lakh disputed, matter pending before CESTAT |
State the reporting requirements under **CARO 2020 Clause 3(vii)** and indicate how the auditor should report the above.

**Answer:**
**Clause 3(vii) of CARO 2020** deals with **statutory dues** and has two sub-clauses:

**Clause 3(vii)(a) — Regularity and undisputed dues:**
The auditor must report whether the company is **regular in depositing undisputed statutory dues** — including Goods and Services Tax, provident fund, employees' state insurance, income-tax, sales tax, service tax, duty of customs, duty of excise, value added tax, cess and any other statutory dues — to the appropriate authorities; and if not, the **extent of the arrears** of outstanding statutory dues as at the last day of the financial year concerned **for a period of more than six months** from the date they became payable shall be indicated.

**Clause 3(vii)(b) — Disputed dues:**
Where **statutory dues have not been deposited on account of any dispute**, then the **amounts involved and the forum where the dispute is pending** shall be mentioned. (A mere representation to the concerned department is **not** treated as a dispute.)

**Application to Zenith Steels Ltd.:**

| Due | Treatment under Clause 3(vii) |
|---|---|
| Provident Fund ₹6.5 lakh, 8 months, undisputed | Undisputed and **outstanding > 6 months** → report the **arrear of ₹6.5 lakh** under **3(vii)(a)** |

| TDS ₹1.2 lakh, 5 months, undisputed | Undisputed but outstanding **< 6 months** → **not** to be reported as an arrear under 3(vii)(a) (only note it is regularly assessed) |
| Customs duty ₹15 lakh, disputed, before CESTAT | **Disputed** → report under **3(vii)(b)**: amount **₹15 lakh**, forum = **CESTAT** |

**Conclusion:** Under **Clause 3(vii)(a)**, only the **PF arrear of ₹6.5 lakh** (undisputed and over six months) is reported; the TDS of ₹1.2 lakh, being under six months, is not reported as an arrear. Under **Clause 3(vii)(b)**, the **disputed GST of ₹18 lakh (before the Appellate Authority)** and the **disputed customs duty of ₹15 lakh (before CESTAT)** are reported with amounts and forums.

*(Full-marks tip: Apply the "> 6 months" test for 3(vii)(a) and the "amount + forum" requirement for 3(vii)(b); net off the amount paid under protest. Common deduction: reporting under-six-month undisputed dues, or omitting the forum for disputed dues.)*

### Q82. Ch: Audit Report — CARO 2020 Clause 3(xiii): Related-Party Transactions (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** In the audit of Orion Systems Ltd. for FY 2024-25, the auditor finds that the company entered into several transactions with entities in which directors are interested. Some of these were **not approved by the Audit Committee** where such approval was required, and the disclosures in the financial statements are partially incomplete. State the reporting requirement under **CARO 2020 Clause 3(xiii)** and how the auditor should respond.

**Answer:**
**Clause 3(xiii) of CARO 2020** requires the auditor to report whether all transactions with **related parties** are **in compliance with Sections 177 and 188 of the Companies Act, 2013** where applicable, and whether the **details** of such related-party transactions have been **disclosed in the financial statements**, etc., as required by the **applicable accounting standards** (AS 18, "Related Party Disclosures").

The clause therefore involves a **two-fold verification**:
1. **Compliance with law** — whether the transactions comply with **Section 188** (approval of the Board / members and disclosure of interest for specified related-party contracts, unless in the ordinary course of business and at arm's length) and **Section 177** (approval and, where applicable, omnibus approval by the **Audit Committee**, for companies required to constitute one).
2. **Adequacy of disclosure** — whether the details required by **AS 18** and the Companies Act have been fully disclosed in the financial statements.

**Application to Orion Systems Ltd.:**
- Since some related-party transactions requiring **Audit Committee approval under Section 177 were entered into without such approval**, there is **non-compliance**; the auditor must report the **fact of non-compliance** under Clause 3(xiii), specifying the transactions concerned.
- Since the **disclosures in the financial statements are incomplete** (contrary to AS 18 / the Act), the auditor must report that the **required details have not been fully disclosed**.
- The auditor should also consider the **impact on the opinion** under SA 705 if the inadequate disclosure results in a **material misstatement**, and evaluate compliance implications under Section 188.

**Conclusion:** Under **CARO 2020 Clause 3(xiii)**, the auditor must report that **not all related-party transactions were in compliance with Sections 177 and 188** (Audit Committee approval was not obtained where required) and that the **details have not been fully disclosed** as required by AS 18 — giving the particulars of the non-compliances.

### Q83. Ch: Audit Report — Integrated CARO 2020 Case: Inventory, Statutory Dues & Related Party (Marks: 8) [Application]

### Q84. Ch: Audit Report — Determination of Type of Opinion & Drafting the Basis (SA 700/705) (Marks: 10) [Application]
**Question:** For each of the following **independent** situations arising in different audits for FY 2024-25, determine the **type of audit opinion** the auditor should express under SA 700/SA 705, giving reasons; and for situation (iv), also **draft the "Basis" paragraph**:
| # | Situation |
|---|---|
| (i) | The company has not provided depreciation on a class of plant amounting to ₹30 lakh (profit before tax ₹5 crore). Management refuses to correct it. |
| (ii) | A material long-term investment (30% of total assets) is carried at cost though its value has permanently declined; no evidence is available to quantify the decline, and the effect could be pervasive. |
| (iii) | The company did not consolidate its only material subsidiary; the effect on the group financial statements is both material and pervasive. |
| (iv) | Trade receivables include ₹75 lakh (profit before tax ₹6 crore) which are doubtful of recovery and against which no provision has been made; the matter is material but not pervasive. |

**Answer:**
The type of opinion is determined by applying **SA 705 (Revised)** using two axes — the **nature** of the matter (a **material misstatement** or an **inability to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence**) and its **pervasiveness**.

**Situation (i): Non-provision of depreciation ₹30 lakh**
- Nature: **Material misstatement** (non-compliance with AS 10 / Schedule II).
- Pervasiveness: ₹30 lakh against PBT of ₹5 crore is **material but not pervasive** (affects one class of asset).
- **Opinion: Qualified Opinion** ("except for"), with the effect quantified in the Basis for Qualified Opinion.

**Situation (ii): Long-term investment (30% of assets), permanent decline not quantifiable**
- Nature: **Inability to obtain sufficient appropriate audit evidence** to quantify the decline.
- Pervasiveness: The item is **30% of total assets** and the possible effect **could be pervasive**.
- **Opinion: Disclaimer of Opinion** — the auditor cannot obtain sufficient appropriate evidence and the possible effects are material and pervasive.

**Situation (iii): Material subsidiary not consolidated**
- Nature: **Material misstatement** (non-compliance with the consolidation requirement / AS 21).
- Pervasiveness: Stated to be **both material and pervasive**.
- **Opinion: Adverse Opinion** — the group financial statements do not give a true and fair view.

**Situation (iv): Doubtful receivables ₹75 lakh, no provision**
- Nature: **Material misstatement** (overstatement of receivables/profit).
- Pervasiveness: ₹75 lakh against PBT of ₹6 crore is **material but not pervasive**.
- **Opinion: Qualified Opinion.**

Draft Basis paragraph for situation (iv):

> **Basis for Qualified Opinion**
> Included in trade receivables shown in the Balance Sheet is an amount of ₹75 lakh which has been outstanding for a significant period and is, in our opinion, doubtful of recovery. The Company has not made any provision for the possible loss that may arise on this account. Had such a provision been made, the profit before tax for the year, trade receivables and shareholders' funds would each have been **lower by ₹75 lakh**. Our opinion is qualified in respect of this matter.

Summary:

### Q85. Ch: Professional Ethics & Independence — Sharing of Fees / Profits with a Non-Member [Clause (2) & (3), Part I, First Schedule] (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** CA. Vikram, a practising Chartered Accountant, enters into an arrangement with Mr. Sen, a retired bank manager who is not a member of the Institute. Under the arrangement, Mr. Sen refers loan-documentation and project-report clients to CA. Vikram, and in return CA. Vikram pays Mr. Sen 20% of the fees earned from every such referred client. Separately, CA. Vikram agrees to receive from Mr. Diwan, a practising Company Secretary, one-third of the fees that Mr. Diwan earns on secretarial assignments jointly handled. Examine whether CA. Vikram is guilty of professional misconduct.

**Answer:** The conduct of CA. Vikram must be examined under Part I of the First Schedule to the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949, which deals with professional misconduct in relation to Chartered Accountants in practice.

Clause (2) of Part I of the First Schedule provides that a Chartered Accountant in practice is deemed to be guilty of professional misconduct if he "pays or allows or agrees to pay or allow, directly or indirectly, any share, commission or brokerage in the fees or profits of his professional business, to any person other than a member of the Institute or a partner or a retired partner or the legal representative of a deceased partner, or a member of any other professional body or with such other persons having such qualifications as may be prescribed." The object of this clause is to restrain a member from parting with a portion of his professional earnings with a person who has secured him the business by way of a canvassing or referral arrangement, since this indirectly amounts to sharing the profits of the profession with an outsider and encourages touting.

In the first arrangement, CA. Vikram agrees to pay 20% of his fees to Mr. Sen, who is a retired bank manager and neither a member of the Institute nor a member of any other recognised professional body. This is a payment of a share in the fees of his professional business to an outsider, and CA. Vikram is therefore **guilty of professional misconduct under Clause (2) of Part I of the First Schedule.**

Clause (3) of Part I of the First Schedule provides that a member in practice is deemed guilty of misconduct if he "accepts or agrees to accept any part of the profits of the professional work of a person who is not a member of the Institute." However, there is an exception recognised where the sharing is with a member of a prescribed professional body. In the second arrangement, CA. Vikram receives one-third of the fees of Mr. Diwan, who is a **practising Company Secretary** — a member of another recognised professional body (the ICSI). | Arrangement | Counter-party | Clause attracted | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pay 20% of fees for referrals | Retired bank manager (outsider) | Clause (2), Part I, Sch. I | Guilty |

### Q86. Ch: Professional Ethics & Independence — Contingent / Percentage-Based Fees [Clause (10), Part I, First Schedule & Regulation 192] (Marks: 6) [Application]

(ii) CA. Rehman is appointed as the liquidator of a company and agrees to charge his fees at 3% of the assets realised in the course of liquidation.
(iii) CA. Farah agrees to render fund-raising services to a client and stipulates that her fee shall be 1% of the loan finally arranged for the client.
(iv) CA. Om is engaged as a valuer of shares and agrees to a fee equal to 0.5% of the value of the shares so valued.

Clause (10) of Part I of the First Schedule provides that a Chartered Accountant in practice is deemed to be guilty of professional misconduct if he "charges or offers to charge, accepts or offers to accept, in respect of any professional employment, fees which are based on a percentage of profits or which are contingent upon the findings, or results of such employment, except as permitted under any regulations made under this Act." The rationale is that fees dependent on the outcome may impair the member's objectivity and independence.

The exceptions are contained in **Regulation 192**, which permits fees to be based on a percentage or to be contingent in certain specified cases — inter alia (a) in the case of a **receiver or a liquidator**, the fees may be based on a percentage of the realisation or disbursement of the assets; (b) in the case of an **auditor of a co-operative society**, fees may be based on a percentage of the paid-up capital, working capital, gross or net income or profits; (c) in the case of a **valuer** for the purposes of direct taxes and duties, fees may be based on a percentage of the value of the property valued; and (d) in the case of certain **management consultancy and other services** such as fund-raising services, fees may be based on a percentage of the amount raised.

The situations are analysed below:

| Situation | Nature of engagement | Percentage of | Permitted by Reg. 192? | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| (i) Audit fee at 2% of net profit | Statutory/audit assignment | Profits | No | Guilty |
| (ii) Liquidator's fee at 3% of assets realised | Liquidator | Realisation of assets | Yes | Not guilty |
| (iii) Fund-raising fee at 1% of loan arranged | Fund-raising service | Amount raised | Yes | Not guilty |
| (iv) Valuer's fee at 0.5% of value of shares | Valuer | Value of property/shares | Yes | Not guilty |

(i) CA. Nandini's audit fee is based on a percentage of the net profit disclosed by the very accounts she audits. This is squarely a fee based on a percentage of profits in an audit engagement and is **not** covered by any exception in Regulation 192; she is therefore **guilty** of professional misconduct under Clause (10).

(ii), (iii) and (iv) fall within the specific exceptions permitted by Regulation 192 — a liquidator charging on realisation, a fund-raiser charging on the amount raised, and a valuer charging on the value determined — and therefore CA. Rehman, CA. Farah and CA. Om are **not guilty** of professional misconduct.

**Conclusion:** Only CA. Nandini in situation (i) is **guilty** of professional misconduct under Clause (10) of Part I of the First Schedule; the members in situations (ii), (iii) and (iv) are **not guilty**, being covered by the exceptions in Regulation 192.

### Q87. Ch: Professional Ethics & Independence — Acceptance of Audit without Communication & without Ascertaining Compliance [Clauses (8) & (9), Part I, First Schedule] (Marks: 5) [Application]

The object of this requirement of "communication with the retiring auditor" is that the incoming auditor should, as a matter of professional courtesy and professional obligation, ascertain from the outgoing auditor whether there are any circumstances — such as unpaid undisputed fees, or professional/other reasons — which the incoming auditor ought to know before accepting the appointment. CA. Prakash made no such enquiry, and in fact the previous auditor had been removed before expiry of his term without Central Government approval; CA. Prakash is therefore also **guilty of professional misconduct under Clause (9).**

| Did not communicate in writing with CA. Menon | Clause (8), Part I, Sch. I | Communication with retiring auditor |
| Did not ascertain compliance with Sec. 139/140 | Clause (9), Part I, Sch. I | Ascertaining statutory validity of appointment |

**Conclusion:** CA. Prakash is **guilty of professional misconduct under both Clause (8) and Clause (9)** of Part I of the First Schedule, as he neither communicated in writing with the retiring auditor nor ascertained compliance with Sections 139 and 140 of the Companies Act, 2013 before accepting the appointment.

### Q88. Ch: Professional Ethics & Independence — Gross Negligence / Failure to Obtain Sufficient Information [Clauses (7), (8) & (9), Part I, Second Schedule] (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** CA. Ishita is the statutory auditor of Trident Traders Ltd. During the year, the company changed its method of inventory valuation resulting in a material overstatement of profit, and it also failed to make any provision for a large, known and admitted liability. CA. Ishita, being pressed for time, signed the audit report expressing an unmodified opinion without examining the change in the inventory policy, without obtaining any information about the unprovided liability which was within her knowledge, and without drawing attention to the departure from the accepted method of valuation. Examine her conduct under the Second Schedule to the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949.

CA. Ishita signed an unmodified audit report while being pressed for time, without examining a material change in inventory valuation policy and without evaluating a known admitted liability. Signing a report without applying the care and diligence expected of a competent auditor, in circumstances that clearly called for further examination, constitutes a failure to exercise due diligence and amounts to gross negligence; she is therefore **guilty under Clause (7).**

The departure from the accepted method of inventory valuation was material and should have been highlighted; CA. Ishita's silence on it makes her **guilty under Clause (9)** as well. | No information on inventory change & unprovided liability | Clause (8) — insufficient information |

### Q93. Ch: Audit of Different Entities — Audit of a Partnership Firm: Special Considerations (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** The partners of M/s Sterling & Associates, a firm of traders, have decided to get the accounts of the firm audited even though there is no statutory requirement to do so. As the auditor appointed by the firm, state the special matters that you would consider while conducting the audit of a partnership firm.

**Answer:** Although the audit of a partnership firm is not compulsory under any general law (except where the turnover/receipts exceed the limits prescribed under the tax laws), partners frequently get the accounts audited to obtain an independent verification of the accounts and to avoid disputes among themselves. The audit is governed by the terms of engagement agreed between the auditor and the firm, and the auditor should keep in mind the following special matters:

**1. Confirming the auditor's authority and scope.** The auditor should obtain, and study, the letter of appointment/engagement signed by all the partners so as to confirm the exact nature and scope of the work entrusted to him and the limitations, if any, placed on his duties. Since there is no statutory scope, the terms of engagement define his responsibilities.

**2. Examination of the partnership deed.** The auditor should study the partnership deed to ascertain the terms relating to the capital contribution of each partner, the profit-sharing ratio, interest on capital and drawings, salary or commission payable to any partner, the powers and duties of the partners, and the provisions governing admission, retirement, death and dissolution. He must verify that the accounts have been prepared in accordance with these terms.

**3. Authority and validity of transactions.** The auditor should verify that the business is being carried on in accordance with the provisions of the partnership deed and the Indian Partnership Act, 1932, and that transactions which fall outside the ordinary authority of a partner (for example, transactions requiring the consent of all partners) have been duly authorised by the partners.

**4. Proper division of profits.** He should ensure that the profit or loss for the year has been correctly ascertained and divided among the partners in the agreed profit-sharing ratio, after giving effect to interest on capital, interest on drawings, salaries and any other adjustments provided in the deed.

**5. Distinguishing capital and revenue, and personal and business items.** The auditor should ensure that there is a clear separation between the private/personal transactions of the partners and the business transactions of the firm, and that capital and revenue items have been properly distinguished, so that the firm's profit and financial position are fairly stated.

**6. Adequacy of records and verification of assets and liabilities.** He should ensure that proper books of account and records are maintained, and should verify the existence, ownership, valuation and disclosure of the assets and the completeness of the liabilities, as in any other audit, applying the relevant Standards on Auditing.

**7. Special reliance on minutes / correspondence.** In the absence of a formal deed, or where the deed is silent, the auditor should refer to the correspondence, minutes of partners' meetings and the provisions of the Indian Partnership Act, 1932 (for example, equal sharing of profits in the absence of an agreement) to resolve doubts.

**Conclusion:** The audit of a partnership firm is essentially governed by the letter of engagement and the partnership deed; the auditor must satisfy himself as to the scope of his work, the authority behind the transactions, the correct division of profits and the proper maintenance of accounts, and then report in terms of his engagement.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the engagement-letter + partnership-deed anchor and the "correct division of profits/authority of partners" points specific to a firm; a common deduction is writing a generic audit-procedure answer with nothing peculiar to a partnership.)*

### Q94. Ch: Audit of Different Entities — Audit of a Sole Proprietary Concern & Its Advantages (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Mr. Ashok runs a sole-proprietary trading concern and is not required by any law to get his accounts audited. He nevertheless wishes to appoint you as his auditor. **Answer:** In the case of a sole-proprietary concern, there is no statutory requirement compelling the proprietor to get the accounts audited, except where the turnover or gross receipts exceed the limits laid down under the tax laws (such as a tax audit under the Income-tax Act, 1961). (iv) Audited accounts serve as a reliable basis for the settlement of accounts and negotiation of price at the time of sale, admission of a partner, or conversion of the concern into a partnership or company, and for the settlement of insurance and other claims.

### Q95. Ch: Audit of Different Entities — Audit of a Company under Insolvency (CIRP) & Going Concern (Marks: 8) [Application]
**Question:** Horizon Ltd. is undergoing the Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP) under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016. The affairs of the company are being managed by an Interim Resolution Professional / Resolution Professional, the board of directors having been suspended. As the statutory auditor of Horizon Ltd. for the year, explain the special considerations you would bear in mind, with particular reference to the going concern assumption, management representations, and reporting.

**Answer:** Where a company is undergoing the Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP) under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 (IBC), the powers of the board of directors stand suspended and the management of the affairs of the corporate debtor vests in the Interim Resolution Professional / Resolution Professional (IRP/RP). The statutory audit under the Companies Act, 2013 continues, but the auditor must bring to bear a number of special considerations.

**1. Who charges the auditor and to whom he reports.** Since the board is suspended, the auditor should recognise that the person now charged with governance and with the management of the company is the RP. Management representations that would ordinarily be signed by the directors will be furnished by, and the auditor's communication with those charged with governance (SA 260) will be directed to, the Resolution Professional.

**2. Going concern assumption (SA 570).** The commencement of CIRP, the existence of a large defaulted debt, and the pending resolution are events or conditions that may cast significant doubt on the entity's ability to continue as a going concern. Under SA 570 "Going Concern," the auditor must:
- evaluate management's (the RP's) assessment of the entity's ability to continue as a going concern, ordinarily covering a period of at least twelve months;
- consider whether the financial statements have been prepared on a going concern basis or on some other appropriate basis (for instance, a liquidation/realisable-value basis if a resolution appears unlikely and liquidation is probable);
- determine whether a **material uncertainty** exists relating to going concern; and
- assess the adequacy of the disclosures made in the financial statements about the CIRP, the material uncertainty, and the basis of preparation.

**3. Impact on reporting (SA 570 & SA 705).**
- If the going concern basis is appropriate **but a material uncertainty exists** and is **adequately disclosed**, the auditor expresses an unmodified opinion and includes a separate **"Material Uncertainty Related to Going Concern"** section in the audit report.
- If the material uncertainty exists but is **not adequately disclosed**, the auditor expresses a **qualified or adverse opinion** under SA 705, as appropriate.
- If the use of the going concern basis is **inappropriate** (for example, liquidation is imminent and the statements are still drawn up on a going concern basis), the auditor expresses an **adverse opinion.**

**4. Audit evidence and risk (SA 315 / SA 330).** The auditor should reassess the risks of material misstatement in this changed environment — in particular the recoverability and impairment of assets (SA 315, and impairment under the applicable AS), the completeness and valuation of liabilities and claims admitted in the CIRP, related party transactions and any preferential/undervalued/fraudulent transactions, and the classification of borrowings (which may become current on default). Given the heightened insolvency environment, the auditor should exercise increased professional scepticism and be alert to the risk of fraud (SA 240).

**5. Management representations (SA 580).** The auditor should obtain written representations from the Resolution Professional (as the person now managing the affairs) covering, inter alia, the completeness of information regarding claims received and admitted, the status and expected outcome of the CIRP, and the appropriateness of the basis of preparation of the financial statements.

**6. Subsequent events (SA 560) and other reporting.** The auditor should evaluate events after the balance sheet date, including developments in the CIRP (approval of a resolution plan, or an order for liquidation), for their effect on the financial statements and disclosures, and should address the relevant CARO/Companies Act reporting requirements (for example, default in repayment to lenders).

**Conclusion:** In auditing a company under CIRP, the auditor must recognise that the RP is now charged with management, rigorously apply SA 570 to the going concern assumption and the basis of preparation, obtain written representations from the RP, and modify the report or include a Material Uncertainty section as the circumstances of the insolvency warrant.

### Q97. Ch: Audit of Different Entities — Advantages of Audit to a Partnership Firm & Matters in the Engagement (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** "Even though the audit of a partnership firm is not obligatory under the general law, an audit confers distinct advantages on the firm and its partners." Discuss the advantages that an audit offers to a partnership firm, and state the matters that should ordinarily be specified in the letter of engagement.

**Answer:** The audit of a partnership firm is not compulsory under general law, but many firms voluntarily get their accounts audited because an independent audit lends credibility to the accounts and protects the interests of the partners. The audit is a matter of agreement between the firm and the auditor, and its scope is defined by the engagement.

**Advantages of an audit to a partnership firm:**

(i) **Reliable and authoritative accounts.** Audited accounts provide an independent and convincing verification of the financial position and results of the firm, and are generally accepted as reliable by outsiders.

(ii) **Basis for settlement among partners.** Audited accounts furnish a reliable and fair basis for the settlement of accounts between the partners on the admission, retirement or death of a partner, thereby minimising the possibility of disputes over the figures.

(iii) **Detection and prevention of errors and fraud.** An audit provides a moral check on the employees and on the partners handling the accounts, and helps in the detection and prevention of errors and fraud.

(iv) **Acceptance by tax and other authorities.** Audited accounts are readily accepted by the income-tax and other authorities as a fair basis for assessment, and by banks and lenders for the grant of credit facilities.

(v) **Valuation and negotiation.** Audited accounts are of great help in valuing the goodwill and assets of the firm and in negotiating the price at the time of sale or conversion of the firm.

**Matters to be specified in the letter of engagement:**

The letter of engagement (which should be signed by or on behalf of all the partners) should ordinarily specify — the exact nature and scope of the audit work; whether the auditor is to undertake a complete or only a partial audit and the limitations, if any, on the scope; whether the audit is to include the preparation of the tax return and other allied work; the responsibility for the maintenance of accounting records (which rests with the partners); the period covered; and the basis of the auditor's remuneration. Recording these matters avoids any later misunderstanding as to the auditor's duties and responsibilities.

**Conclusion:** An audit affords a partnership firm reliable accounts, an equitable basis for settlement among partners, protection against errors and fraud, and ready acceptance by outsiders; and to avoid disputes over the scope of the work, the terms should be clearly recorded in a letter of engagement signed by all the partners.

### Q98. Ch: Audit of Different Entities — Audit of a Sole-Proprietary Concern vs a Company (Distinction) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between the audit of a sole-proprietary concern and the statutory audit of a company, bringing out the essential differences with regard to the basis of appointment, scope, and reporting.

**Answer:** The audit of a sole-proprietary concern and the statutory audit of a company differ fundamentally, because the former is a voluntary, agreement-based audit whereas the latter is governed by statute. The essential differences are set out below.

| Basis | Audit of a Sole-Proprietary Concern | Statutory Audit of a Company |
|---|---|---|
| Legal compulsion | Not compulsory under general law (except a tax audit where turnover exceeds prescribed limits) | Compulsory under the Companies Act, 2013 (Sec. 139) for every company |
| Appointment | Appointed by the proprietor by private agreement | Appointed by the members in general meeting / Board / C&AG as per Sec. 139; qualifications governed by Sec. 141 |
| Scope of work | Determined wholly by the agreement/instructions of the proprietor; may be full or limited | Determined by the statute; the auditor must cover all matters required by the Act and cannot restrict scope by agreement |
| Rights and duties | Flow only from the terms of engagement | Flow from the Companies Act, 2013 (rights of access under Sec. 143, duty to report, etc.) |
| Reporting | Report is addressed to the proprietor in the agreed form | Report is addressed to the members and must state the matters prescribed under Sec. 143 and CARO |
| Removal | Governed by the agreement | Governed by Sec. 140, with prescribed procedure and safeguards |
| Applicability of SAs | Standards on Auditing apply to the audit conducted | Standards on Auditing apply and compliance is mandatory |

The audit of a sole-proprietary concern is thus essentially a private, contractual engagement in which the proprietor decides the scope, and the auditor's responsibility is limited to what has been agreed. The statutory audit of a company, on the other hand, is a public-interest audit in which the scope, rights, duties, manner of appointment and removal, and the contents of the report are all laid down by law and cannot be curtailed by any private arrangement.

**Conclusion:** The key distinction is that the audit of a sole-proprietary concern is voluntary and governed by the client's agreement, whereas the statutory audit of a company is obligatory and governed by the Companies Act, 2013, which fixes the auditor's appointment, scope, duties and reporting.

### Q99. Ch: Audit of Different Entities — Audit of a Firm under Dissolution / Insolvency (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** M/s Anand & Brothers, a partnership firm, has become insolvent and is being wound up, its assets being realised and its liabilities discharged. You have been engaged to audit the accounts prepared on realisation. Explain the special matters you would examine while auditing the accounts of a partnership firm that is being dissolved / wound up on insolvency.

**Answer:** When a partnership firm is dissolved and wound up — particularly on account of insolvency — the going concern assumption no longer holds, and the accounts are prepared on a realisation (break-up) basis rather than a going concern basis. The auditor engaged in such circumstances should direct his attention to the following special matters:

**1. Authority and basis of dissolution.** The auditor should examine the partnership deed, the agreement of dissolution, and, where applicable, the order of the court or the relevant proceedings, to establish that the dissolution is valid and to ascertain the agreed manner of realisation of assets, discharge of liabilities and distribution among the partners. He should apply the rules of the Indian Partnership Act, 1932, including the rules in Section 48 governing the mode of settlement of accounts on dissolution.

**2. Basis of preparation.** He should verify that, since the firm is no longer a going concern, the assets have been stated at their realisable (net realisable) values and not at historical cost, and that all liabilities, including contingent and unrecorded liabilities, have been fully brought into account.

**3. Realisation of assets.** He should vouch the realisation of each asset with the supporting evidence (sale deeds, receipts, bank credits), verify that the assets have been realised at fair values, and ensure that any profit or loss on realisation has been correctly transferred to the Realisation Account and shared among the partners in the agreed profit-sharing ratio.

**4. Discharge of liabilities and order of payment.** He should verify that the liabilities have been discharged in the proper order — outside creditors first, then partners' loans/advances, and finally the partners' capital — and, in an insolvency, that the rule in **Garner v. Murray** (where a partner's capital account is in deficit and he is insolvent, the solvent partners bear the deficiency in the ratio of their last-agreed capitals) has been correctly applied, if relevant.

**5. Settlement of partners' accounts.** He should verify the adjustment of the partners' capital and current accounts, including interest, drawings and shares of realisation profit/loss, and confirm that the final distribution among the partners is in accordance with the deed and the law.

**6. Completeness and disclosure.** He should ensure that all transactions of the realisation period are completely recorded, that no assets or liabilities have been omitted, and that the accounts fairly present the results of the winding-up.

**Conclusion:** In auditing the accounts of a partnership firm being wound up on insolvency, the auditor must verify the validity and basis of dissolution, the realisable-value basis of the accounts, the proper realisation of assets and discharge of liabilities in the correct order, and the settlement of partners' accounts in accordance with Section 48 of the Indian Partnership Act, 1932 and, where a partner is insolvent, the rule in Garner v. Murray.

### Q100. Ch: Audit of Different Entities — Audit of a Non-Corporate Entity: Audit under Statute vs Voluntary Audit & Engagement Considerations (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** In the context of the audit of non-corporate entities (such as sole proprietors, partnership firms, trusts and associations), explain briefly the difference between an audit that is required under a statute and an audit that is undertaken voluntarily, and state why, in either case, the auditor should insist on a clear letter of engagement.

**Answer:** Non-corporate entities — sole-proprietary concerns, partnership firms, trusts, societies and associations of persons — may get their accounts audited either because a statute requires it or because they voluntarily choose to do so. The distinction is important because it determines the source and scope of the auditor's duties.

**Audit required under a statute.** In this case some law makes the audit obligatory and, more importantly, fixes the scope, and often the form of the report — for example, a tax audit under Section 44AB of the Income-tax Act, 1961 where the turnover/gross receipts exceed the prescribed limits, or the audit of a public trust or a co-operative society under the relevant State Act, or the audit of an LLP where its turnover/contribution exceeds the prescribed thresholds under the LLP Act, 2008. Here the scope of the audit is governed by the statute, and the auditor cannot restrict it by agreement; he must cover all that the law requires and report in the prescribed manner.

**Audit undertaken voluntarily.** Here there is no legal compulsion, and the entity gets its accounts audited of its own accord — for the benefit of the proprietor, the partners, the members or the lenders. The scope of such an audit is determined entirely by the agreement between the entity and the auditor, and may be a full audit or a limited/specific-purpose audit as agreed.

**Why a clear letter of engagement is essential in either case.** Whether the audit is statutory or voluntary, the auditor should insist on a written letter of engagement (SA 210 "Agreeing the Terms of Audit Engagements") because it records the objective and scope of the audit, the respective responsibilities of the management (for maintaining the records and preparing the statements) and of the auditor, the applicable financial reporting framework, and the form of the report. In a voluntary audit this is especially important, because in the absence of a statute the engagement letter is the only document that defines and limits the auditor's duties and protects him against later allegations that he ought to have done more; in a statutory audit it usefully records the statutory scope and the terms agreed.

**Conclusion:** A statutory audit of a non-corporate entity derives its scope from the governing law, whereas a voluntary audit derives it wholly from the agreement between the parties; in both cases a clear letter of engagement, consistent with SA 210, is essential to define the scope, fix responsibilities and avoid disputes.
