# Q&A — Foundation: Theoretical Framework of Accounting

## Section A — Theory (model answers show "how to present for full marks")

### Q1. Distinguish between Book-keeping, Accounting and Accountancy. (4 marks)

**Model answer.**
Present as a labelled comparison — examiners reward structure:

| Basis | Book-keeping | Accounting | Accountancy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Recording & classifying financial transactions | Recording, classifying, summarising, analysing & communicating | The whole body of knowledge / the discipline & profession |
| Scope | Narrow (routine, clerical) | Wider (includes book-keeping + interpretation) | Widest (governs both) |
| Skill | Clerical | Analytical, judgment | Conceptual |
| Output | Journals & ledgers | Financial statements + interpretation | Principles, standards, best practice |
| Dependence | Independent starting point | Begins where book-keeping ends | Sets the framework for accounting |

*Closing line for the mark:* "Thus book-keeping is a **part of** accounting, and accounting is a **part of** accountancy."

---

### Q2. "Only three fundamental accounting assumptions need no disclosure if followed." Name them and state the disclosure rule. (4 marks)

**Model answer.**
As per **AS-1 (Disclosure of Accounting Policies)**, the three **fundamental accounting assumptions** are:
1. **Going Concern** — the enterprise will continue operations for the foreseeable future and has neither the intention nor the necessity to liquidate or curtail materially the scale of operations.
2. **Consistency** — accounting policies are applied consistently from one period to the next.
3. **Accrual** — revenues and costs are recognised as they are earned or incurred (not as cash is received or paid) and recorded in the period to which they relate.

**Disclosure rule:** These assumptions are **presumed** to be followed, so **no specific disclosure is required** if they are followed. However, **if any assumption is NOT followed, that fact must be disclosed.** *(Memory hook: G-A-C.)*

---

### Q3. Explain the Business Entity concept and state why Capital appears on the liabilities side. (3 marks)

**Model answer.**
The **Business Entity concept** treats the business as an entity **separate and distinct from its owner(s)**. The business's transactions are recorded from the *business's* point of view, not the owner's. Consequently, the owner's personal assets, liabilities and expenses are kept out of the business books.

When the owner contributes capital, the **business is regarded as owing that amount back to the owner**. Since it is an amount payable by the business to an outsider (the owner, treated as separate), **Capital is shown as a liability**. Similarly, cash or goods taken by the owner for personal use are treated as **Drawings** (a reduction of capital), never as a business expense. This concept makes it possible to measure the performance of the *business itself*.

---

### Q4. State any two limitations of the Money Measurement concept. (2 marks)

**Model answer.**
1. **Non-monetary facts are ignored:** valuable qualitative information — e.g., skill and loyalty of the workforce, quality of management, customer satisfaction — cannot be expressed in money and is therefore **omitted**, even though it affects the business.
2. **Assumes a stable monetary unit:** it ignores **changes in the purchasing power of money** (inflation). Assets bought at different times are added together as if the rupee's value were constant, which can distort the true picture.

---

### Q5. "Prudence and Full Disclosure can conflict." Explain with an example, and state the resolving principle. (4 marks)

**Model answer.**
- **Prudence (Conservatism)** requires that profits be not anticipated but all foreseeable losses be provided for — it leans toward **understatement** of income/assets.
- **Full Disclosure** requires that **all material information** be disclosed so users are not misled.

**Conflict example:** A firm faces a lawsuit it *might* lose. Prudence might tempt an over-cautious accountant to quietly create a large provision without explanation, while another might suppress it to avoid alarming investors. Full disclosure demands the *facts* (nature and estimated amount of the contingency) be stated in the notes.

**Resolution:** The overriding objective is a **true and fair view**. Prudence must not become *deliberate* understatement (secret reserves), and disclosure must not be sacrificed for caution. Where they pull apart, the accountant discloses the material fact and provides only to the extent evidence justifies — serving the *user's* need to make an informed decision.

---

### Q6. Define "Accounting Policies" and list the three considerations governing their selection. (3 marks)

**Model answer.**
**Accounting policies** are the **specific accounting principles and the methods of applying those principles** adopted by an enterprise in preparing and presenting its financial statements — e.g., method of depreciation (SLM/WDV), method of inventory valuation (FIFO/weighted average), valuation of investments, treatment of goodwill.

The **major considerations** governing the selection of accounting policies (AS-1) are:
1. **Prudence** — do not anticipate profits; provide for all known liabilities and losses on available evidence.
2. **Substance over form** — account for transactions according to their **economic reality**, not merely their legal form.
3. **Materiality** — disclose all items material enough to influence the decisions of users.

*Add for the mark:* All significant accounting policies should be disclosed at one place, and any change with a material effect should be disclosed along with the amount of the effect.

---

### Q7. Differentiate between a change in accounting **policy** and a change in accounting **estimate**, with one example each. (4 marks)

**Model answer.**

| Basis | Change in accounting **policy** | Change in accounting **estimate** |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Change in the **principle/method** of accounting | Revision of an **approximation** due to new information |
| Trigger | Required by law/AS, or for better presentation | Fresh facts / experience |
| Example | Changing inventory valuation from FIFO to weighted average | Revising the estimated useful life of a machine; revising provision for doubtful debts |
| Treatment | Disclose the change and its material effect | Applied **prospectively** — affects current & future periods only; past is not restated |
| Seriousness | More significant; needs justification | Normal, recurring part of accrual accounting |

*Key line:* "A change in estimate is **not** the correction of an error and is never applied retrospectively."

---

### Q8. What is a Contingent Liability? How does its treatment differ from a Provision? (4 marks)

**Model answer.**
A **Contingent Liability** is a **possible obligation** arising from past events whose existence will be confirmed only by the occurrence or non-occurrence of one or more **uncertain future events** not wholly within the enterprise's control; OR a present obligation that is **not recognised** because an outflow is **not probable** or **cannot be measured reliably**.

| | Provision | Contingent Liability |
|---|---|---|
| Obligation | **Present** obligation | **Possible** obligation, or present but not probable/measurable |
| Outflow of resources | **Probable** | Not probable / uncertain |
| Measurable | Yes, reliably | Often not |
| Accounting treatment | **Recognised** — charged to P&L, shown as a liability | **Not recognised** — only **disclosed** in notes |

Examples of contingent liabilities: pending lawsuits, bills receivable discounted, guarantees given, disputed tax demands. If the possibility of outflow is **remote**, even disclosure is not required.

---

### Q9. Why are Contingent Assets not recognised in the books? (2 marks)

**Model answer.**
Because of the **Prudence (Conservatism) convention** — *"anticipate no profit."* A contingent asset arises from an *uncertain* future event and recognising it would amount to recognising income that **may never be realised**, overstating assets and profit. It is therefore **not recognised**; it is disclosed (in the approving authority's report) only when the inflow of economic benefits is **probable**, and recognised in the accounts only when realisation becomes **virtually certain** (at which point it is no longer contingent).

---

### Q10. List the four measurement bases recognised by the Framework and state which is most commonly used and why. (3 marks)

**Model answer.**
The four measurement bases are:
1. **Historical Cost** — assets recorded at the cash (or equivalent) paid to acquire them.
2. **Current Cost** — the amount that would have to be paid to acquire the same asset currently.
3. **Realisable (Settlement) Value** — the amount obtainable by selling the asset in an orderly disposal.
4. **Present Value** — the discounted value of future net cash flows the item is expected to generate.

**Historical cost is the most commonly used** basis in Indian GAAP because it is **objective and verifiable** — it is supported by documentary evidence (the invoice/agreement), whereas the other bases rely on estimates and current opinions that are open to manipulation.

### Q11. State whether each is Capital or Revenue, giving a one-line reason. (5 marks)

(a) ₹50,000 spent on white-washing the factory (routine).
(b) ₹2,00,000 paid for a second-hand machine plus ₹40,000 to make it operational.
(c) ₹10,000 legal fees to defend a trademark used in daily trade.
(d) ₹1,50,000 loan taken from a bank.
(e) Sale of old office furniture for ₹8,000.

**Model answer.**

| Item | Classification | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| (a) White-washing (routine) | **Revenue expenditure** | Maintains, does not improve; benefit within the year |
| (b) Machine ₹2,00,000 + ₹40,000 to make operational | **Capital expenditure ₹2,40,000** | Cost of asset + cost to bring it to working condition |
| (c) Legal fees to defend trademark in daily trade | **Revenue expenditure** | Protects existing operations, no new asset created |
| (d) Bank loan ₹1,50,000 | **Capital receipt** | Not from ordinary trading; creates a liability |
| (e) Sale of old furniture ₹8,000 | **Capital receipt** | Sale of a fixed asset, not trading income |

### Q12. Explain the Accrual concept and why cash-basis accounting is not acceptable under Accounting Standards. (3 marks)

**Model answer.**
Under the **Accrual concept**, revenues and costs are recognised in the period in which they are **earned or incurred**, **regardless of when cash is received or paid**. Thus rent owed but unpaid at year-end is recorded as an expense (with an outstanding-liability), and income earned but not yet received is recorded as income (with a receivable).

Cash-basis accounting records transactions only when cash moves. It therefore **distorts periodic profit** — a business could show low profit in the year it *delivered* goods (because cash came later) and high profit in the year it merely *collected*. Accrual matches efforts with rewards of the *same* period, giving a **true and fair** measure of performance, which is why AS-1 lists accrual as a **fundamental assumption**.

---

### Q13. What is meant by "Accounting as an information system"? (3 marks)

**Model answer.**
It means accounting operates like any information system — it takes **inputs**, **processes** them, and produces **outputs** for users, with **feedback**:
- **Input:** business transactions and events of a financial character.
- **Process:** the accounting cycle — identifying, recording (journal), classifying (ledger), summarising (trial balance, final accounts) with adjustments.
- **Output:** financial statements (P&L, Balance Sheet) and reports.
- **Users & feedback:** internal and external users use the output to make decisions, which generate fresh transactions, restarting the cycle.

The purpose of the system is to convert raw economic data into **decision-useful information** for its users.

---

### Q14. State the role/objectives of Accounting Standards. (4 marks)

**Model answer.**
Accounting Standards (issued by the ICAI's Accounting Standards Board) serve to:
1. **Standardise** the diverse accounting policies and practices, eliminating (to the extent possible) the choices that make statements non-comparable.
2. Enhance the **reliability** of financial statements by prescribing recognition, measurement, presentation and disclosure norms.
3. Improve **comparability** — across enterprises and across periods.
4. Provide a **benchmark** against which auditors, regulators and courts can evaluate the accounts.
5. Assist in presenting a **true and fair view** and in meeting legal requirements (they are notified under the Companies Act, 2013).

*Add limitation for the extra mark:* AS still permit alternatives and require judgment, and cannot override the law — so they reduce, but do not wholly eliminate, differences.

### Q15. Accounting Equation. (6 marks)

Prepare the accounting equation and the closing Balance Sheet from:
1. Started business with cash ₹8,00,000.
2. Bought goods for cash ₹3,00,000 and on credit ₹2,00,000.
3. Sold goods costing ₹2,50,000 for ₹3,50,000 (cash).
4. Paid rent ₹40,000.
5. Owner withdrew ₹30,000 cash for personal use.

**Solution — effect on Assets = Liabilities + Capital:**

| # | Transaction | Cash | Stock | = | Creditors | Capital | Working on Capital |
|---|---|---:|---:|:-:|---:|---:|---|
| 1 | Capital introduced | +8,00,000 | | = | | +8,00,000 | |
| 2a | Goods for cash | −3,00,000 | +3,00,000 | = | | | |
| 2b | Goods on credit | | +2,00,000 | = | +2,00,000 | | |
| 3 | Sale (cash) | +3,50,000 | −2,50,000 | = | | +1,00,000 | profit 3,50,000−2,50,000 |
| 4 | Rent paid | −40,000 | | = | | −40,000 | expense |
| 5 | Drawings | −30,000 | | = | | −30,000 | |
| | **Balances** | **7,80,000** | **2,50,000** | = | **2,00,000** | **8,30,000** | |

**Reconcile Capital:** 8,00,000 + 1,00,000 (profit on sale) − 40,000 (rent) − 30,000 (drawings) = **8,30,000**. ✔
**Reconcile Cash:** 8,00,000 − 3,00,000 + 3,50,000 − 40,000 − 30,000 = **7,80,000**. ✔
**Reconcile Stock:** 3,00,000 + 2,00,000 − 2,50,000 = **2,50,000**. ✔

**Check equation:** Assets = 7,80,000 + 2,50,000 = **10,30,000**; Liab + Capital = 2,00,000 + 8,30,000 = **10,30,000**. ✔

**Balance Sheet:**

| Liabilities | ₹ | Assets | ₹ |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Capital | 8,30,000 | Cash | 7,80,000 |
| Creditors | 2,00,000 | Stock | 2,50,000 |
| **Total** | **10,30,000** | **Total** | **10,30,000** |

### Q16. Cash vs Accrual profit. (6 marks)

For the year ended 31 Mar 2026 a professional reports:
- Fees earned (accrued) ₹9,00,000, of which ₹1,50,000 is still receivable.
- Cash received this year for **last year's** fees ₹80,000.
- Office rent paid ₹2,40,000, including ₹40,000 prepaid for April 2026.
- Salaries paid ₹1,60,000; salary for March 2026 still outstanding ₹20,000.

Compute profit on (a) cash basis and (b) accrual basis, and reconcile the difference.

**Solution.**

**(a) Cash basis:**
- Cash revenue = (9,00,000 − 1,50,000 receivable) + 80,000 last year = 7,50,000 + 80,000 = **8,30,000**
- Cash expenses = Rent 2,40,000 + Salaries 1,60,000 = **4,00,000**
- **Cash profit = 8,30,000 − 4,00,000 = ₹4,30,000**

**(b) Accrual basis:**
- Revenue earned this year = **9,00,000** (last year's ₹80,000 excluded)
- Rent expense = 2,40,000 − 40,000 prepaid = 2,00,000
- Salary expense = 1,60,000 + 20,000 outstanding = 1,80,000
- Total accrual expenses = 2,00,000 + 1,80,000 = **3,80,000**
- **Accrual profit = 9,00,000 − 3,80,000 = ₹5,20,000**

**Reconciliation (Accrual − Cash = 5,20,000 − 4,30,000 = ₹90,000):**

| Adjustment | ₹ |
|---|---:|
| + Fees earned but not received (asset) | +1,50,000 |
| − Fees received this year but earned last year | −80,000 |
| + Rent prepaid (not this year's expense) | +40,000 |
| − Salary outstanding (this year's expense added) | −20,000 |
| **Net difference** | **+90,000** ✔ |

### Q17. Correcting capital/revenue misclassification. (6 marks)

A machine costing ₹5,00,000 was bought during the year. In addition: carriage to bring it in ₹20,000, installation ₹30,000, and first-year insurance on the machine ₹15,000. The accountant charged the **entire ₹5,65,000** to the Profit & Loss Account and reported a net profit of ₹2,40,000. Compute the correct profit and the value at which the machine should appear.

**Solution.**

**Step 1 — classify:**

| Item | ₹ | Classification |
|---|---:|---|
| Machine cost | 5,00,000 | Capital |
| Carriage inward on machine | 20,000 | Capital (cost to bring asset to location) |
| Installation | 30,000 | Capital (cost to bring asset to working condition) |
| Insurance (annual) | 15,000 | Revenue (recurring running cost) |

Capitalised cost of machine = 5,00,000 + 20,000 + 30,000 = **₹5,50,000**.
Correctly-revenue amount = **₹15,000** (insurance).

**Step 2 — correct the profit.** The accountant wrongly charged ₹5,65,000; only ₹15,000 was truly revenue. Over-charge to P&L = 5,65,000 − 15,000 = **₹5,50,000**.

| | ₹ |
|---|---:|
| Reported profit (wrong) | 2,40,000 |
| Add back capital cost wrongly expensed | 5,50,000 |
| **Correct profit** | **7,90,000** |

**Step 3 — balance-sheet effect.** Machine appears as a fixed asset at **₹5,50,000**. Profit rose by ₹5,50,000 (→ capital up ₹5,50,000) exactly matching the ₹5,50,000 asset created — balance sheet still balances. ✔ *(Only depreciation on ₹5,50,000, not the whole cost, will hit P&L in future years.)*

### Q18. Prudence — valuation of closing stock at lower of cost or NRV. (5 marks)

| Item | Qty | Cost/unit ₹ | Selling price/unit ₹ | Cost to sell/unit ₹ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| P | 200 | 150 | 180 | 15 |
| Q | 100 | 400 | 390 | 30 |
| R | 250 | 90 | 130 | 10 |

Value the closing stock.

**Solution.**

**NRV/unit = selling price − cost to sell:**
- P: 180 − 15 = 165 → lower of (cost 150, NRV 165) = **150**
- Q: 390 − 30 = 360 → lower of (cost 400, NRV 360) = **360**
- R: 130 − 10 = 120 → lower of (cost 90, NRV 120) = **90**

**Value:**

| Item | Qty | Value/unit ₹ | Total ₹ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| P | 200 | 150 | 30,000 |
| Q | 100 | 360 | 36,000 |
| R | 250 | 90 | 22,500 |
| | | **Closing stock** | **88,500** |

*Note:* Item Q is written down by 100 × (400−360) = ₹4,000 (anticipated loss recognised); items P and R are kept at cost — their higher NRV is **not** booked (anticipate no profit). Stock at pure cost would have been 30,000 + 40,000 + 22,500 = 92,500; prudence reduces it to **88,500**, a write-down of ₹4,000. ✔

### Q19. Contingent liability vs provision — identification. (5 marks)

For each, state whether to (i) create a provision, (ii) disclose as a contingent liability, or (iii) ignore, with reason:
(a) A customer has sued for ₹5,00,000; the firm's lawyer says loss is **probable** and estimates ₹3,00,000 payable.
(b) A supplier has sued for ₹2,00,000; lawyer says the firm will **possibly** but not probably lose.
(c) The firm has discounted bills receivable of ₹1,00,000 with its bank.
(d) A former employee's claim of ₹50,000 is considered **remote**.
(e) The firm expects to **win** a counter-claim of ₹4,00,000 (virtually certain).

**Model answer.**

| Case | Treatment | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| (a) | **Create a provision of ₹3,00,000** (P&L) | Present obligation, outflow **probable**, amount **reliably measurable** |
| (b) | **Disclose as contingent liability** | Outflow only **possible**, not probable → do not recognise, only disclose |
| (c) | **Disclose as contingent liability** | Firm may have to pay if the acceptor defaults — a possible obligation |
| (d) | **Ignore** | Possibility of outflow is **remote** → no provision, no disclosure |
| (e) | Recognise the asset (₹4,00,000) — **not** a contingent asset now | Inflow **virtually certain**, so it is no longer contingent; a merely *probable* gain would only be disclosed, and a *possible* gain ignored (prudence) |

### Q20. Going concern impact. (4 marks)

A firm owns a machine bought for ₹10,00,000 with accumulated depreciation of ₹6,00,000 (book value ₹4,00,000). Its current forced-sale (liquidation) value is ₹1,50,000. At what value is it carried (a) as a going concern and (b) if the firm is about to be liquidated? Explain.

**Solution.**
(a) **As a going concern:** the machine is carried at **book value ₹4,00,000** (cost ₹10,00,000 − accumulated depreciation ₹6,00,000). The going concern assumption justifies the historical-cost-less-depreciation basis, because the asset will continue to be *used*, not sold.

(b) **If about to be liquidated:** the going concern assumption **no longer holds**, so assets are restated at **net realisable / liquidation value = ₹1,50,000**. The difference of ₹4,00,000 − ₹1,50,000 = **₹2,50,000** is written off as a loss. AS-1 requires this departure from the going-concern basis to be **disclosed**.

### Q21. Depreciation method as an accounting policy — consistency & effect of change. (5 marks)

A firm charged depreciation on machinery (cost ₹8,00,000, no residual value, life 8 years) using the **Straight Line Method (SLM)** for 2 years. In year 3, with the machine now at book value after 2 years, it switches to a policy where it wants comparability. Compute the SLM depreciation per year and the book value at the end of year 2, and state how a change of method must be treated.

**Solution.**
- **SLM depreciation p.a.** = Cost ÷ Life = 8,00,000 ÷ 8 = **₹1,00,000 per year**.
- After 2 years: accumulated depreciation = 2 × 1,00,000 = 2,00,000. **Book value end of year 2 = 8,00,000 − 2,00,000 = ₹6,00,000.**

**Treatment of a change in depreciation method / policy:**
- Under the **Consistency** convention, the firm should apply the **same** method year after year so results are comparable.
- A change is permitted only if required by statute/AS or if it results in a **more appropriate presentation**. When changed, the change and its **effect (amount)** must be **disclosed** (AS-1 / AS-5). Under current AS-10, a change in the *method* of depreciation is treated as a **change in accounting estimate**, applied **prospectively** on the remaining book value over the remaining useful life. Here the remaining ₹6,00,000 would be depreciated over the remaining 6 years under the new estimate.

*(Foundation-level takeaway: state the consistency rule + mandatory disclosure of the change and its effect. The prospective mechanics are refined in Inter AS-10.)*

### Q22. Materiality judgment. (3 marks)

A company with annual turnover of ₹500 crore buys 50 waste-paper baskets at ₹300 each (total ₹15,000), each expected to last 5 years. The accountant capitalises them as furniture and plans to depreciate over 5 years. Comment.

**Model answer.**
Although the baskets technically meet the definition of a fixed asset (benefit > 1 year), the amount (**₹15,000**) is **utterly immaterial** relative to a ₹500 crore turnover — it cannot possibly influence any user's decision. Under the **Materiality** convention, capitalising and depreciating them over 5 years adds cost and clutter for no informational benefit. The correct treatment is to **charge the entire ₹15,000 to the Profit & Loss Account as revenue expenditure** in the year of purchase. Materiality here **overrides** the strict capital/revenue rule — a practical application of "accounting serves the user, not the rulebook."

### Q23. Realisation concept applied. (4 marks)

State, with reasons, how much revenue (if any) is recognised in the year ended 31 Mar 2026 in each case:
(a) An order worth ₹6,00,000 received on 20 Mar 2026; goods not yet delivered.
(b) Goods worth ₹4,00,000 delivered on 28 Mar 2026; invoice raised; payment due in April.
(c) Advance of ₹1,00,000 received on 15 Mar 2026 for goods to be delivered in May 2026.
(d) Goods worth ₹2,00,000 sold and delivered on 30 Mar 2026 for cash.

**Model answer.**

| Case | Revenue recognised in FY 2025-26 | Reason |
|---|---:|---|
| (a) | **Nil** | Only an order; sale not complete — Realisation not met. No revenue on mere order. |
| (b) | **₹4,00,000** | Goods **delivered**, enforceable claim arises — realised, even though cash comes later (Accrual). |
| (c) | **Nil** | Advance received but goods **not yet delivered** — it is a **liability** (advance from customer), not income. |
| (d) | **₹2,00,000** | Sold and delivered — fully realised. |

**Total revenue recognised = 4,00,000 + 2,00,000 = ₹6,00,000.** The ₹6,00,000 order and the ₹1,00,000 advance are deliberately **excluded** — recognising them would create fictitious profit, violating Realisation and Prudence.

### Q24. Comprehensive — build corrected profit from mixed adjustments. (6 marks)

A trader's draft P&L shows a profit of **₹5,00,000**. On review you find:
1. ₹80,000 spent on a new computer was charged to P&L (it should be capitalised; ignore depreciation for simplicity).
2. Closing stock was valued at cost ₹1,20,000; its NRV is only ₹95,000.
3. Rent ₹36,000 for the whole year was paid, but ₹6,000 relates to next year (prepaid) and was fully charged this year.
4. Commission of ₹10,000 earned this year has not been recorded (still receivable).
5. A provision for doubtful debts of ₹12,000 needs to be created (not yet done).

Compute the corrected profit.

**Solution — adjust the draft profit item by item:**

| # | Adjustment | Effect on profit ₹ | Reason |
|---|---|---:|---|
| — | Draft profit | 5,00,000 | starting point |
| 1 | Add back computer wrongly expensed | +80,000 | capital, not revenue |
| 2 | Reduce stock to NRV: write down 1,20,000 → 95,000 | −25,000 | Prudence; lower value = higher COGS/lower profit |
| 3 | Rent prepaid wrongly charged this year | +6,000 | belongs to next year |
| 4 | Commission earned but not recorded | +10,000 | Accrual — income of this year |
| 5 | Provision for doubtful debts to be created | −12,000 | Prudence — anticipated loss |
| | **Corrected profit** | **5,59,000** | |

**Reconciliation:** 5,00,000 + 80,000 − 25,000 + 6,000 + 10,000 − 12,000 = **₹5,59,000**. ✔

**Balance-sheet notes for full marks:** computer ₹80,000 now shown as a fixed asset; closing stock shown at ₹95,000; prepaid rent ₹6,000 shown as an asset; commission receivable ₹10,000 shown as an asset; provision for doubtful debts ₹12,000 shown as a deduction from debtors. Each adjustment simultaneously fixes the profit **and** a balance-sheet line — the Matching and Dual-Aspect concepts working together.
