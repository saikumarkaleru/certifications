## Part A — Theory

### Q1. What is meant by "single entry system" or "incomplete records"? State its main features.

**Model answer.**
"Single entry system" is a loose label for a set of accounting records that are **not maintained on the full double-entry principle**. The technically correct term is **incomplete records**, because no genuine system deliberately records only one side of every transaction; instead the records are a *mixture* — some transactions are recorded in both aspects, some in only one, and some not at all.

*How to present for full marks — list the features as bullets:*
- Records are **incomplete and unsystematic**; different traders keep them differently.
- Usually **only cash/bank and personal accounts** (debtors and creditors) are maintained.
- **Real accounts** (assets) and **nominal accounts** (sales, purchases, expenses, incomes) are generally not kept.
- A **trial balance cannot be prepared**, so arithmetical accuracy is unverified.
- **Final accounts cannot be drawn directly**; profit is only *estimated* or *reconstructed*.
- Suited to **sole proprietors and small firms**; **legally barred for companies**.

### Q2. State the limitations of maintaining accounts under incomplete records.

**Model answer.** Present as a crisp point-wise list:
1. **No trial balance** can be prepared — arithmetical accuracy cannot be checked.
2. **True profit cannot be ascertained** — only estimated by net-worth, or reconstructed by conversion.
3. **Financial position is unreliable** — only a Statement of Affairs (an estimate), not a proper Balance Sheet.
4. **Frauds and errors go undetected** because the second entry that would expose them is missing.
5. **Not acceptable to law, tax authorities, or banks** — companies are prohibited from using it.
6. **No basis for comparison or planning** — ratio analysis and decision-making are handicapped by missing data.

### Q3. Distinguish between a Statement of Affairs and a Balance Sheet.

**Model answer (table format earns marks):**

| Basis | Statement of Affairs | Balance Sheet |
|---|---|---|
| Source of figures | Estimates / physical counts of assets and liabilities | Ledger balances produced by double entry |
| Capital figure | **Balancing figure** (Assets − Liabilities) | An independently maintained ledger balance |
| Reliability | Low — depends on estimates; items may be omitted | High — every figure is verified against its double |
| Objective | To ascertain **capital** (for the net-worth method) | To show a true and fair **financial position** |
| Trial balance basis | Not preceded by a trial balance | Preceded by an agreed trial balance |

**One-line clincher:** *"They look identical in layout, but a Balance Sheet proves capital, whereas a Statement of Affairs assumes it."*

### Q4. Differentiate between the single-entry and double-entry systems.

**Model answer (table):**

| Basis | Single Entry / Incomplete Records | Double Entry |
|---|---|---|
| Recording of aspects | Both, one, or none recorded | Both aspects of every transaction |
| Accounts maintained | Mainly cash and personal | Personal, real and nominal — all |
| Trial balance | Cannot be prepared | Can be prepared |
| Profit ascertainment | Estimated / reconstructed | Precise, via P&L Account |
| Financial position | Statement of Affairs | Balance Sheet |
| Fraud/error detection | Weak | Strong |
| Legal acceptability | Not accepted for companies/tax | Accepted universally |

### Q5. Explain the two methods of ascertaining profit under incomplete records. When is each used?

**Model answer.**
- **Net Worth (Statement of Affairs / Capital Comparison) method** — profit is inferred from the **increase in capital** over the year, after adjusting for drawings and additional capital:
  *Profit = Closing Capital + Drawings − Additional Capital − Opening Capital.*
  Used when the question asks **only for the amount of profit or loss** and does not require a full set of final accounts. It reveals the *amount* of profit but **not its composition** (no gross profit, no expense breakdown).
- **Conversion method** — the incomplete records are **converted into full double-entry** by preparing control accounts (Total Debtors, Total Creditors, Bills R/P) whose balancing figures give the missing **credit sales** and **credit purchases**; a full **Trading, P&L and Balance Sheet** is then prepared. Used when the question demands **complete final accounts** or figures like gross profit, sales, or purchases.

### Q6. Why can a trial balance not be prepared under the single-entry system?

**Model answer.** A trial balance lists the debit and credit balances of **all ledger accounts** and works only because, under double entry, **every debit has an equal credit**, so total debits must equal total credits. Under incomplete records, many transactions are recorded in **only one aspect** (or not at all) and **nominal and most real accounts are never opened**. Since the equal-and-opposite second entries are missing, the debit and credit totals would not — and could not meaningfully — agree. Hence no trial balance, and no independent arithmetical-accuracy check.

### Q7. State the three types of single entry.

**Model answer.**
- **Pure single entry** — only personal accounts are kept; no cash book and no real accounts. Rarely found in practice.
- **Simple single entry** — personal accounts **and** a cash book are maintained. The common real-world form.
- **Quasi single entry** — simple single entry **plus** some subsidiary books (sales book, purchases book, bills books). The most complete and easiest to convert into double entry.

### Q8. What is the "memorandum trading account" technique, and when is it used?

**Model answer.** When a figure **inside the Trading Account itself** is missing — typically **closing stock, purchases, or sales** — and the question supplies a **gross-profit rate**, we prepare a *rough* (memorandum) Trading Account and let the unknown emerge as the balancing figure, using:
*COGS = Opening Stock + Purchases − Closing Stock* and *Gross Profit = Sales − COGS* (with GP = Sales × rate if the rate is on sales, or COGS × rate if on cost). It is essentially the balancing-figure logic applied to the Trading Account when a gross-profit relationship is known.

### Q9. "The net-worth method tells you how much you earned but not how you earned it." Explain.

**Model answer.** The net-worth method computes profit purely as the **change in capital** adjusted for drawings and fresh capital. It never touches sales, purchases, or individual expenses, so it produces a **single profit figure with no breakdown** — no gross profit, no expense analysis, no sales total. To learn *how* the profit arose (margins, cost control, sales growth), one must use the **conversion method**, which reconstructs the Trading and P&L Account line by line. Hence the net-worth method answers "how much," while conversion answers "how much, and made of what."

### Q10. List the adjustments commonly applied under the net-worth method and their effect on profit.

**Model answer (table):**

| Adjustment | Effect on profit |
|---|---|
| Depreciation not yet charged | Less |
| Bad debts / provision for doubtful debts | Less |
| Interest on capital | Less |
| Outstanding (unpaid) expenses | Less |
| Prepaid expenses | Add |
| Accrued (earned but unreceived) income | Add |
| Interest on drawings | Add |

*Presentation tip:* start from "profit before adjustments," then show a two-column *Add / Less* statement so the examiner can trace each item.

## Part B — Numerical Problems

### Q11. Compute opening capital from a Statement of Affairs.

A trader's position on 1 April 2023: Cash ₹2,000; Debtors ₹18,000; Stock ₹22,000; Plant ₹30,000; Creditors ₹14,000; Outstanding expenses ₹2,000. Find the opening capital.

**Solution.**

| Liabilities | ₹ | Assets | ₹ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Creditors | 14,000 | Cash | 2,000 |
| Outstanding expenses | 2,000 | Debtors | 18,000 |
| **Capital (bal. fig.)** | **56,000** | Stock | 22,000 |
|  |  | Plant | 30,000 |
| **Total** | **72,000** | **Total** | **72,000** |

Capital = Assets 72,000 − Liabilities 16,000 = **₹56,000**. ✔

### Q12. Ascertain profit by the net-worth method (basic).

Opening capital ₹56,000; closing capital ₹78,000; drawings during the year ₹15,000; additional capital introduced ₹5,000. Find profit.

**Solution.**
Profit = Closing Capital + Drawings − Additional Capital − Opening Capital
= 78,000 + 15,000 − 5,000 − 56,000 = **₹32,000**.

*Check:* 78,000 + 15,000 = 93,000; − 5,000 = 88,000; − 56,000 = **32,000**. ✔

### Q13. Net-worth method with adjustments.

For Mr. X: opening capital ₹1,00,000; closing capital (before adjustments) ₹1,30,000; drawings ₹20,000; additional capital ₹10,000. Adjustments: depreciation on machinery ₹5,000; provision for doubtful debts ₹1,500; interest on capital @5% on opening capital; interest on drawings ₹600. Find net profit.

**Solution.**

Profit before adjustments = 1,30,000 + 20,000 − 10,000 − 1,00,000 = **₹40,000**.

| Particulars | ₹ | ₹ |
|---|---|---|
| Profit before adjustments |  | 40,000 |
| Add: Interest on drawings |  | 600 |
|  |  | 40,600 |
| Less: Depreciation on machinery | 5,000 |  |
| Less: Provision for doubtful debts | 1,500 |  |
| Less: Interest on capital (5% × 1,00,000) | 5,000 | (11,500) |
| **Net Profit** |  | **29,100** |

*Check:* 40,000 + 600 = 40,600; deductions 5,000 + 1,500 + 5,000 = 11,500; net profit = 40,600 − 11,500 = **₹29,100**. ✔

### Q14. Find credit sales from the Total Debtors Account.

Opening debtors ₹32,000; closing debtors ₹43,000; cash received from debtors ₹1,45,000; discount allowed ₹3,000; bad debts ₹2,000; sales returns ₹4,000; bills receivable received from debtors ₹15,000. Find credit sales.

**Solution.**

| Dr — Total Debtors A/c | ₹ | Cr | ₹ |
|---|---|---|---|
| To Balance b/d | 32,000 | By Cash / Bank | 1,45,000 |
| To **Credit Sales (bal. fig.)** | **1,80,000** | By Discount allowed | 3,000 |
|  |  | By Bad debts | 2,000 |
|  |  | By Sales returns | 4,000 |
|  |  | By Bills Receivable received | 15,000 |
|  |  | By Balance c/d | 43,000 |
| **Total** | **2,12,000** | **Total** | **2,12,000** |

Credit sales = (1,45,000 + 3,000 + 2,000 + 4,000 + 15,000 + 43,000) − 32,000 = 2,12,000 − 32,000 = **₹1,80,000**. ✔

### Q16. Bills Receivable Account — find cash collected on bills.

Opening B/R ₹8,000; bills receivable received during the year ₹20,000; B/R dishonoured ₹2,000; closing B/R ₹6,000. Find the amount of B/R honoured (collected in cash).

**Solution.**

| Dr — Bills Receivable A/c | ₹ | Cr | ₹ |
|---|---|---|---|
| To Balance b/d | 8,000 | By Cash / Bank (honoured, bal. fig.) | 20,000 |
| To Debtors (B/R received) | 20,000 | By Debtors (dishonoured) | 2,000 |
|  |  | By Balance c/d | 6,000 |
| **Total** | **28,000** | **Total** | **28,000** |

B/R honoured = 28,000 − 2,000 − 6,000 = **₹20,000**. ✔

### Q17. Find missing drawings.

Opening capital ₹60,000; closing capital ₹85,000; additional capital introduced ₹8,000; profit for the year ₹30,000. Find the drawings.

**Solution.**
From Profit = Closing Capital + Drawings − Additional Capital − Opening Capital:
30,000 = 85,000 + Drawings − 8,000 − 60,000
30,000 = 17,000 + Drawings ⟹ **Drawings = ₹13,000**.

*Check:* 85,000 + 13,000 − 8,000 − 60,000 = **30,000**. ✔

### Q18. Memorandum Trading Account — find credit purchases from a gross-profit rate.

Opening stock ₹20,000; closing stock ₹25,000; total sales ₹2,00,000; gross profit is 25% **on sales**; cash purchases ₹30,000. Find credit purchases.

**Solution.**
Gross profit = 25% × 2,00,000 = ₹50,000, so COGS = 2,00,000 − 50,000 = ₹1,50,000.
COGS = Opening Stock + Purchases − Closing Stock
1,50,000 = 20,000 + Purchases − 25,000 ⟹ Purchases = **₹1,55,000**.
Credit purchases = 1,55,000 − 30,000 (cash) = **₹1,25,000**.

*Memorandum Trading Account check:*

| Dr | ₹ | Cr | ₹ |
|---|---|---|---|
| To Opening stock | 20,000 | By Sales | 2,00,000 |
| To Purchases | 1,55,000 | By Closing stock | 25,000 |
| To Gross Profit | 50,000 |  |  |
| **Total** | **2,25,000** | **Total** | **2,25,000** |

Both sides foot to 2,25,000; GP = 50,000 = 25% of sales. ✔

### Q19. Find closing debtors.

Opening debtors ₹30,000; credit sales ₹1,60,000; cash received from debtors ₹1,50,000; discount allowed ₹2,500; bad debts ₹1,500. Find closing debtors.

**Solution.**
Closing debtors = Opening + Credit sales − Cash received − Discount allowed − Bad debts
= 30,000 + 1,60,000 − 1,50,000 − 2,500 − 1,500 = **₹36,000**.

*Check (Total Debtors A/c):* Dr = 30,000 + 1,60,000 = 1,90,000; Cr = 1,50,000 + 2,500 + 1,500 + 36,000 = 1,90,000. ✔

### Q20. Comprehensive — prepare Trading, P&L and Balance Sheet.

Mr. P keeps incomplete records. Opening Balance Sheet (1 Apr 2023): Creditors ₹15,000; Cash and Bank ₹10,000; Debtors ₹20,000; Stock ₹25,000; Machinery ₹30,000.

Opening cash ₹5,000. Receipts: from debtors ₹90,000; cash sales ₹20,000. Payments: to creditors ₹60,000; expenses ₹15,000; drawings ₹? Closing cash ₹12,000. Find drawings.

### Q22. Net-worth method producing a loss (sign practice).

Opening capital ₹90,000; closing capital ₹80,000; drawings ₹22,000; additional capital ₹6,000. Ascertain profit or loss.

**Solution.**
Profit = Closing Capital + Drawings − Additional Capital − Opening Capital
= 80,000 + 22,000 − 6,000 − 90,000 = **₹6,000 profit**.

*Interpretation:* although capital **fell** by ₹10,000, the fall was caused by ₹22,000 of drawings partly offset by ₹6,000 of fresh capital. Stripping those out shows the business actually **earned ₹6,000**. *Check:* 80,000 + 22,000 = 1,02,000; − 6,000 = 96,000; − 90,000 = **6,000**. ✔ (Had the result been negative it would be a loss.)

### Q23. Two-step: opening capital, then profit.

On 1 Apr 2023 a trader had: Stock ₹18,000; Debtors ₹12,000; Cash ₹4,000; Furniture ₹16,000; Creditors ₹10,000. On 31 Mar 2024 his closing capital was ₹52,000. During the year drawings were ₹9,000 and he introduced ₹4,000 additional capital. Find the profit.

**Solution.**
Opening capital = (18,000 + 12,000 + 4,000 + 16,000) − 10,000 = 50,000 − 10,000 = **₹40,000**.
Profit = 52,000 + 9,000 − 4,000 − 40,000 = **₹17,000**.
*Check:* 52,000 + 9,000 = 61,000; − 4,000 = 57,000; − 40,000 = **17,000**. ✔

### Q24. Find total purchases with returns.

Credit purchases (from Total Creditors A/c) ₹1,12,000; cash purchases ₹28,000; purchase returns (returns outward) already recorded within the creditors account ₹3,000. State the **net purchases** to be carried to the Trading Account, and the gross purchases before returns.

**Solution.**
The purchase returns of ₹3,000 have already been captured **inside** the Total Creditors Account (on its debit side), so the credit-purchases balancing figure of ₹1,12,000 is **already net of** returns on credit account.
- Total (net) purchases for the Trading Account = Credit purchases 1,12,000 + Cash purchases 28,000 = **₹1,40,000**.
- Gross purchases before returns = 1,40,000 + 3,000 = ₹1,43,000 (shown only if the format asks for purchases and returns outward separately).

*Presentation note:* in the Trading Account you normally show **Purchases 1,43,000** on the debit side and **Less: Returns outward 3,000**, netting to 1,40,000 — never double-deduct the ₹3,000. ✔
