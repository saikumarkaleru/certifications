# Q&A — Foundation: Inventories

*Practice bank for the Foundation chapter on Inventories (CA Foundation Paper 1 — Accounting, 2024 New Scheme). Every number is self-verified so that COGS + Closing Stock = Goods Available, unit counts reconcile, and totals tie out.*

## Section A — Theory (with model answers)

### Q1. Define "inventory" as per AS 2 and state its three categories. *(3 marks)*

**Model answer.**
As per **AS 2 (Valuation of Inventories)**, inventories are assets:
(a) **held for sale** in the ordinary course of business (finished goods / stock-in-trade);
(b) **in the process of production** for such sale (work-in-progress); and
(c) in the form of **materials or supplies to be consumed** in the production process or in the rendering of services (raw materials, stores, spares, packing material).

*Presentation tip:* list the three categories as (a)/(b)/(c) with a one-word example each — examiners award a mark per correctly identified category.

---

### Q2. What is Net Realisable Value (NRV)? How does it differ from replacement cost and market value? *(4 marks)*

**Model answer.**
**NRV = Estimated selling price in the ordinary course of business − estimated costs of completion − estimated costs necessary to make the sale.**

It is an **entity-specific, net output value** — what *this* business will actually realise, net of what it must still spend to complete and sell the goods.

- **Replacement cost** is an *input* price — what it would cost to buy/produce the item again. NRV is an *output* (selling) figure.
- **Market/fair value** is a general exchange price between market participants; NRV is specific to the entity and is *net* of its own completion and selling costs.

*Presentation tip:* always write the full NRV formula first, then the two distinctions — full marks require naming *both* deductions (complete + sell).

---

### Q3. "Inventory is valued at lower of cost and NRV." Explain the accounting principle behind this and why it is one-directional. *(4 marks)*

**Model answer.**
The rule flows from the **prudence (conservatism) concept:** anticipate all probable losses but do not anticipate gains.

An asset must never be carried at more than the amount expected to be realised from it. If cost exceeds NRV, the excess represents a loss that has *already* become probable; carrying stock at cost would overstate both the asset and profit, so the stock is **written down to NRV now.**

It is **one-directional** because when NRV exceeds cost, writing the stock *up* would recognise an **unrealised gain** on goods not yet sold — which prudence forbids. Hence: write down for a loss, never write up for a gain. Profit on stock is recognised only when it is actually sold.

---

### Q4. State the items included in and excluded from the cost of inventories. *(5 marks)*

**Model answer.**
**Included (cost of purchase + conversion + other):**
- Purchase price; import duties and other **non-refundable** taxes; freight inward; insurance in transit; other directly attributable acquisition costs.
- **Less:** trade discounts, rebates, duty drawbacks and **refundable** taxes (e.g. GST input credit).
- Cost of conversion: direct labour, direct expenses, and a systematic allocation of fixed and variable **production overheads**.
- Other costs incurred to bring inventories to their **present location and condition**.

**Excluded (charged to P&L as period expense):**
- Abnormal wastage of materials, labour or overheads.
- Storage costs (unless necessary in the production process before a further stage).
- Administrative overheads not contributing to present location/condition.
- Selling and distribution costs.
- Interest / borrowing costs (generally).

*Presentation tip:* the one-line principle — "a cost enters inventory only if it helped bring the goods to their present location and condition, ready for sale" — earns the concept mark.

---

### Q5. Distinguish between the Periodic and Perpetual inventory systems. *(4 marks)*

**Model answer.**

| Basis | Periodic (Physical) | Perpetual (Continuous) |
|---|---|---|
| Stock determination | Physical count at period-end | Continuous record after every movement |
| Cost of goods sold | Balancing figure (Op. stock + Purchases − Cl. stock) | Recorded directly at each sale |
| Records | No running stock ledger | Stores ledger / bin cards maintained |
| Control | Weak — losses hidden in COGS | Strong — book vs physical reveals shortage |
| Cost & suitability | Cheap; small traders | Costlier; large / high-value inventories |

*Key line for full marks:* "Under periodic, closing stock is counted and COGS is the balancing figure; under perpetual, stock records are live and COGS is recorded as goods are issued."

---

### Q6. Name the cost formulas permitted under AS 2. Why is LIFO not one of them, and what is the effect of FIFO in a period of rising prices? *(4 marks)*

**Model answer.**
AS 2 permits: **Specific Identification** (for items not ordinarily interchangeable), **FIFO (First-In-First-Out)**, and **Weighted Average Cost.**

**LIFO is NOT permitted** under AS 2. LIFO leaves the *oldest, cheapest* costs in closing stock, understating the inventory value on the Balance Sheet in periods of rising prices and allowing profit manipulation; AS 2 therefore disallows it.

**Effect of FIFO in rising prices:** the earliest (cheaper) costs are charged to COGS while closing stock is valued at the **latest (dearer)** costs. Result: **lower COGS, higher gross profit, and higher closing-stock value** than Weighted Average.

---

### Q7. Explain the effect of over- and under-statement of closing inventory on the profit of the current and the following year. *(4 marks)*

**Model answer.**
Since **COGS = Opening stock + Purchases − Closing stock**, and this year's closing stock becomes next year's opening stock:

| Error in closing stock | Current year profit | Next year profit |
|---|---|---|
| Overstated | Overstated (COGS too low) | Understated (opening too high) |
| Understated | Understated (COGS too high) | Overstated (opening too low) |

The error **self-reverses** over two years (the two profits are wrong by equal and opposite amounts), but **each year's profit is individually misstated.** This is why closing-stock manipulation is a common tool to shift profit between years.

---

### Q8. Why is the lower-of-cost-or-NRV rule applied item-by-item rather than on the total? *(3 marks)*

**Model answer.**
Applying it **item-by-item** ensures that every item whose NRV has fallen below cost is fully written down, while no item is written up above cost. If the comparison were made on the **total**, an *unrealised gain* on items whose NRV exceeds cost would be netted against *real losses* on items whose NRV is below cost — masking losses and overstating inventory. That netting violates prudence, so AS 2 requires the item-by-item (or similar-group) basis, which always gives the lower, more prudent value.

---

### Q9. Distinguish inventory from a fixed asset. Give an example where the same item can be either. *(3 marks)*

**Model answer.**
The distinction is by **purpose**, not by the nature of the item:
- An item **held for sale or for consumption in production** is **inventory** (a current asset).
- An item **held for use** in operations is a **fixed asset (PPE)**.

**Example:** a motor car is **inventory** (stock-in-trade) for a **car dealer** who sells cars, but a **fixed asset** for a **courier company** that uses it for deliveries.

---

### Q10. When would the Net Realisable Value of inventory fall below its cost? State the AS 2 disclosure requirements at a basic level. *(4 marks)*

**Model answer.**
**NRV falls below cost when:**
- Selling prices have declined (market glut, competition).
- The goods are damaged, obsolete or out of fashion.
- The estimated costs to complete or to sell the goods have increased.

**Disclosures (basic):**
- The **accounting policy** adopted for measuring inventories, including the **cost formula** used (FIFO / Weighted Average).
- The **total carrying amount** of inventories, with classification appropriate to the entity (raw materials, WIP, finished goods, stores & spares).

## Section B — Numerical problems (fully solved)

### Q11. FIFO and Weighted Average under the periodic system.

**Data (periodic system):**

| Date | Particulars | Units | Rate (Rs) | Value (Rs) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Opening | Opening stock | 100 | 25 | 2,500 |
| — | Purchase | 200 | 30 | 6,000 |
| — | Purchase | 300 | 35 | 10,500 |
| — | Purchase | 400 | 40 | 16,000 |
| | **Total available** | **1,000** | | **35,000** |

Units sold = **750** (selling price Rs 60 each). Compute closing stock and gross profit under (a) FIFO and (b) Weighted Average.

**Solution.**
Closing stock in units = 1,000 − 750 = **250 units.** Sales = 750 × 60 = **Rs 45,000.**

*(a) FIFO* — closing stock = latest costs. The last purchase was 400 @ Rs 40; the 250 closing units come from it:
Closing stock = 250 × 40 = **Rs 10,000.**
COGS = 35,000 − 10,000 = **Rs 25,000.**
Gross profit = 45,000 − 25,000 = **Rs 20,000.**

*(b) Weighted Average* — rate = 35,000 ÷ 1,000 = **Rs 35 per unit.**
Closing stock = 250 × 35 = **Rs 8,750.**
COGS = 35,000 − 8,750 = **Rs 26,250.**
Gross profit = 45,000 − 26,250 = **Rs 18,750.**

**Verification:** FIFO 25,000 + 10,000 = 35,000 ✓; WA 26,250 + 8,750 = 35,000 ✓. FIFO gross profit (20,000) > WA (18,750), as expected in rising prices.

### Q12. FIFO under the perpetual system (stock card).

**Data (perpetual):**

| Date | Transaction | Units | Rate (Rs) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Jun | Opening | 40 | 100 |
| 5 Jun | Purchase | 60 | 110 |
| 10 Jun | Sale | 70 | — |
| 18 Jun | Purchase | 50 | 120 |
| 25 Jun | Sale | 40 | — |

Prepare the FIFO stock card and find closing stock and total COGS.

**Solution — stock card (oldest issued first):**

| Date | Receipts | Issues (COGS) | Balance |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Jun | — | — | 40 @ 100 = 4,000 |
| 5 Jun | 60 @ 110 = 6,600 | — | 40 @ 100; 60 @ 110 |
| 10 Jun | — | 40 @ 100 = 4,000; 30 @ 110 = 3,300 → **7,300** | 30 @ 110 = 3,300 |
| 18 Jun | 50 @ 120 = 6,000 | — | 30 @ 110; 50 @ 120 |
| 25 Jun | — | 30 @ 110 = 3,300; 10 @ 120 = 1,200 → **4,500** | 40 @ 120 = 4,800 |

**Closing stock = 40 units @ Rs 120 = Rs 4,800.**
**Total COGS = 7,300 + 4,500 = Rs 11,800.**

**Verification:** Goods available = 4,000 + 6,600 + 6,000 = 16,600. COGS + closing = 11,800 + 4,800 = **16,600** ✓. Units: available 150, sold 110, closing 40 → 110 + 40 = 150 ✓.

### Q13. Moving (perpetual) Weighted Average.

**Data (perpetual):**

| Date | Transaction | Units | Rate (Rs) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Start | Opening | 100 | 20 |
| — | Purchase | 100 | 30 |
| — | Sale | 120 | — |
| — | Purchase | 120 | 30 |
| — | Sale | 100 | — |

Compute COGS and closing stock using the **moving weighted average** (a fresh average is struck after each purchase).

**Solution.**

| Step | Units | Value (Rs) | Avg rate (Rs) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opening | 100 | 2,000 | 20.00 |
| + Purchase 100 @ 30 | 200 | 5,000 | **25.00** |
| − Sale 120 @ 25 | 80 | 2,000 | 25.00 |
| + Purchase 120 @ 30 | 200 | 5,600 | **28.00** |
| − Sale 100 @ 28 | 100 | 2,800 | 28.00 |

Sale 1 COGS = 120 × 25 = **Rs 3,000.** Sale 2 COGS = 100 × 28 = **Rs 2,800.**
**Total COGS = 3,000 + 2,800 = Rs 5,800.**
**Closing stock = 100 units @ Rs 28 = Rs 2,800.**

**Verification:** Goods available = 2,000 + 3,000 + 3,600 = 8,600. COGS + closing = 5,800 + 2,800 = **8,600** ✓.

### Q14. Lower of cost and NRV — item-by-item.

**Data (year-end):**

| Item | Cost (Rs) | NRV (Rs) |
|---|---|---|
| P | 80,000 | 72,000 |
| Q | 45,000 | 50,000 |
| R | 25,000 | 15,000 |

At what value should inventory be shown, and what write-down goes to the P&L?

**Solution — item-by-item lower:**

| Item | Cost | NRV | Lower | Write-down |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| P | 80,000 | 72,000 | 72,000 | 8,000 |
| Q | 45,000 | 50,000 | 45,000 | — |
| R | 25,000 | 15,000 | 15,000 | 10,000 |
| **Total** | **1,50,000** | | **1,32,000** | **18,000** |

**Inventory value = Rs 1,32,000.** Write-down charged to P&L = 1,50,000 − 1,32,000 = **Rs 18,000.**

*Note:* Item Q's NRV exceeds cost, so it is **not** written up — carried at cost Rs 45,000. (A wrong "total" comparison would give lower of total cost 1,50,000 vs total NRV 1,37,000 = 1,37,000, overstating inventory by Rs 5,000 — the unrealised gain on Q masking loss on R.)

### Q15. Determining the cost of purchase.

A trader gives the following for a lot of goods:

| Particulars | Rs |
|---|---|
| List price | 1,00,000 |
| Trade discount @ 10% | ? |
| Freight inward | 5,000 |
| Insurance in transit | 2,000 |
| Import duty (non-refundable) | 8,000 |
| GST paid (input credit available) | 12,000 |
| Loading & unloading | 1,000 |
| Abnormal wastage in transit | 3,000 |
| Godown storage of finished goods | 4,000 |

Compute the cost of the inventory.

**Solution.**

| Particulars | Rs |
|---|---|
| List price | 1,00,000 |
| Less: Trade discount @ 10% | (10,000) |
| Net purchase price | 90,000 |
| Add: Freight inward | 5,000 |
| Add: Insurance in transit | 2,000 |
| Add: Import duty (non-refundable) | 8,000 |
| Add: Loading & unloading | 1,000 |
| **Cost of inventory** | **1,06,000** |

**Excluded:** GST Rs 12,000 (refundable — input credit), abnormal wastage Rs 3,000, storage of finished goods Rs 4,000 — all charged to P&L, not inventory.

**Cost of inventory = Rs 1,06,000.**

### Q16. NRV with completion and selling costs.

A manufacturer holds 500 units. Cost per unit = Rs 500. Estimated selling price = Rs 550 per unit. Estimated cost to complete = Rs 40 per unit; estimated selling cost = Rs 30 per unit. At what value is the inventory carried?

**Solution.**
NRV per unit = 550 − 40 − 30 = **Rs 480.**
Compare: Cost Rs 500 vs NRV Rs 480 → lower = **Rs 480 per unit.**
Inventory value = 500 × 480 = **Rs 2,40,000.**
Write-down = 500 × (500 − 480) = 500 × 20 = **Rs 10,000** to P&L.

**Verification:** Cost value 500 × 500 = 2,50,000; carried value 2,40,000; difference 10,000 = write-down ✓.

### Q17. Closing-stock error and its two-year reversal.

Year 1 correct figures: Sales Rs 8,00,000; Opening stock Rs 50,000; Purchases Rs 5,00,000; correct closing stock Rs 90,000. By error, Year 1 closing stock is **understated by Rs 20,000** (recorded Rs 70,000). Year 2 (all correct): Sales Rs 9,00,000; Purchases Rs 5,40,000; correct closing stock Rs 1,00,000. Show the effect on each year's gross profit.

**Solution.**

*Year 1:*

| | Correct (Rs) | With error (Rs) |
|---|---|---|
| Sales | 8,00,000 | 8,00,000 |
| Opening stock | 50,000 | 50,000 |
| Add: Purchases | 5,00,000 | 5,00,000 |
| Less: Closing stock | (90,000) | (70,000) |
| COGS | 4,60,000 | 4,80,000 |
| **Gross Profit** | **3,40,000** | **3,20,000** |

→ Year 1 profit **understated by Rs 20,000.**

*Year 2* (opening = Year 1 recorded closing):

| | Correct (Rs) | Carrying error (Rs) |
|---|---|---|
| Sales | 9,00,000 | 9,00,000 |
| Opening stock | 90,000 | 70,000 |
| Add: Purchases | 5,40,000 | 5,40,000 |
| Less: Closing stock | (1,00,000) | (1,00,000) |
| COGS | 5,30,000 | 5,10,000 |
| **Gross Profit** | **3,70,000** | **3,90,000** |

→ Year 2 profit **overstated by Rs 20,000.**

**Verification:** Two-year total correct = 3,40,000 + 3,70,000 = **7,10,000**; with error = 3,20,000 + 3,90,000 = **7,10,000** ✓. The error self-reverses.

### Q18. Book stock vs physical stock (perpetual — shortage detection).

Under a perpetual system, opening stock was 200 units, purchases 800 units, sales 750 units. A physical count at year-end shows **220 units** actually present. Identify the book stock and the shortage; state where the shortage is recorded.

**Solution.**
**Book stock** (per records) = 200 + 800 − 750 = **250 units.**
**Physical stock** = 220 units.
**Shortage = 250 − 220 = 30 units.**

The 30-unit shortage (pilferage, breakage, evaporation etc.) is written off — the stock records are reduced to the physical figure and the loss is charged to the P&L. If the units are valued at, say, Rs 40 each, the write-off = 30 × 40 = **Rs 1,200.**

*Teaching point:* only a **perpetual** system can reveal this shortage, because it maintains a book stock to compare against the count. Under a periodic system the missing 30 units would simply be absorbed into COGS and go unnoticed.

### Q19. Cost of goods sold from incomplete information.

A trader's records show: Opening stock Rs 60,000; Purchases Rs 4,00,000; Purchase returns Rs 10,000; Carriage inward Rs 8,000; Closing stock Rs 75,000. Compute COGS.

**Solution.**

| Particulars | Rs |
|---|---|
| Opening stock | 60,000 |
| Add: Purchases | 4,00,000 |
| Less: Purchase returns | (10,000) |
| Add: Carriage inward | 8,000 |
| Cost of goods available for sale | 4,58,000 |
| Less: Closing stock | (75,000) |
| **Cost of Goods Sold** | **3,83,000** |

**Verification:** Net purchases 4,00,000 − 10,000 = 3,90,000; + opening 60,000 + carriage 8,000 = 4,58,000; − closing 75,000 = **3,83,000** ✓. (Carriage inward is a purchase-related cost, so it is added; purchase returns reduce purchases.)

### Q20. FIFO vs Weighted Average — impact on gross profit (periodic).

**Data:** Opening 150 units @ Rs 40; Purchase 250 units @ Rs 48; Purchase 100 units @ Rs 60. Units sold = 350 @ Rs 90. Compute gross profit under FIFO and Weighted Average (periodic).

**Solution.**
Total available = 150 + 250 + 100 = **500 units;** total cost = (150×40) + (250×48) + (100×60) = 6,000 + 12,000 + 6,000 = **Rs 24,000.**
Closing stock = 500 − 350 = **150 units.** Sales = 350 × 90 = **Rs 31,500.**

*FIFO* — closing = latest costs: 100 @ 60 (=6,000) + 50 @ 48 (=2,400) = **Rs 8,400.**
COGS = 24,000 − 8,400 = **Rs 15,600.** Gross profit = 31,500 − 15,600 = **Rs 15,900.**

*Weighted Average* — rate = 24,000 ÷ 500 = **Rs 48 per unit.**
Closing = 150 × 48 = **Rs 7,200.** COGS = 24,000 − 7,200 = **Rs 16,800.** Gross profit = 31,500 − 16,800 = **Rs 14,700.**

**Verification:** FIFO 15,600 + 8,400 = 24,000 ✓; WA 16,800 + 7,200 = 24,000 ✓. FIFO GP (15,900) > WA GP (14,700) — rising prices.

### Q21. Trading Account with closing-stock adjustment (presentation).

From the following, prepare the Trading Account: Opening stock Rs 30,000; Purchases Rs 2,00,000; Sales Rs 3,10,000; Wages Rs 20,000; Carriage inward Rs 5,000; Closing stock (given as adjustment) Rs 45,000.

**Solution — Trading Account for the year:**

| Dr | Rs | Cr | Rs |
|---|---|---|---|
| To Opening stock | 30,000 | By Sales | 3,10,000 |
| To Purchases | 2,00,000 | By Closing stock | 45,000 |
| To Wages | 20,000 | | |
| To Carriage inward | 5,000 | | |
| To Gross Profit c/d | 1,00,000 | | |
| **Total** | **3,55,000** | **Total** | **3,55,000** |

**Verification:** Gross profit = Sales 3,10,000 + Closing 45,000 − (Opening 30,000 + Purchases 2,00,000 + Wages 20,000 + Carriage 5,000) = 3,55,000 − 2,55,000 = **Rs 1,00,000** ✓. Both sides tie at Rs 3,55,000.

*Trap note:* closing stock here was given as an **adjustment** (outside the Trial Balance), so it appears on the **credit** of the Trading Account **and** as a current asset in the Balance Sheet. Had it already been in the Trial Balance, it would go to the Balance Sheet **only.**

### Q22. Raw-material NRV — should it be written down?

A company holds raw material costing Rs 2,00,000. Its current replacement cost has fallen to Rs 1,70,000. The finished goods made from this material are expected to sell **at a profit** (i.e. their NRV exceeds their cost). Should the raw material be written down?

**Solution / model answer.**
**No.** Under AS 2, raw materials and stores are **not** written down below cost if the **finished products** in which they will be incorporated are expected to be **sold at or above cost.** Since the finished goods here will sell at a profit, the raw material is carried at its **cost of Rs 2,00,000**, even though its replacement cost (Rs 1,70,000) is lower.

A raw-material write-down is triggered **only** when the finished good itself is expected to be sold **below cost** — in which case replacement cost is the best available measure of the material's NRV. This is a favourite conceptual trap: a fall in a material's price alone does not justify a write-down.

**Answer: carry at Rs 2,00,000 (no write-down).**
