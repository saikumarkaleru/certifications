### Q1. Ch: AS 2 Inventory — Retail Method of Cost Measurement (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** Trendline Retail Ltd. is a garment retailer that finds it impracticable to compute cost of its large volume of rapidly changing items with similar margins by any other method, and therefore uses the **Retail Method** permitted by AS 2. From the following particulars for the year ended 31st March, 2026, compute the cost of the closing inventory that should appear in the Balance Sheet.

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Opening inventory — at cost | 3,20,000 |
| Opening inventory — at retail (selling) price | 4,00,000 |
| Purchases during the year — at cost | 18,80,000 |
| Purchases during the year — at retail price | 24,50,000 |
| Additional mark-up on goods during the year (at retail) | 1,50,000 |
| Sales during the year (at retail) | 25,00,000 |
| Closing inventory at retail (as per physical count) | 5,00,000 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Determination of goods available for sale (at cost and at retail)**

| Particulars | At Cost (₹) | At Retail (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| Opening inventory | 3,20,000 | 4,00,000 |
| Add: Purchases | 18,80,000 | 24,50,000 |
| Add: Additional mark-up | — | 1,50,000 |
| **Goods available for sale** | **22,00,000** | **30,00,000** |

**WN-2: Computation of cost-to-retail ratio (gross percentage of cost to retail)**

As required by AS 2, the cost of inventory under the retail method is determined by reducing the sales value of inventory by the appropriate percentage of gross margin; the practical equivalent is to apply the **cost-to-retail ratio** to the closing inventory at retail.

Cost-to-retail ratio = Goods available at cost ÷ Goods available at retail
= ₹22,00,000 ÷ ₹30,00,000 = **73.33%** (0.7333)

**WN-3: Cross-check of closing inventory at retail**

| Particulars | At Retail (₹) |
|---|---|
| Goods available for sale | 30,00,000 |
| Less: Sales | (25,00,000) |
| Closing inventory at retail (computed) | 5,00,000 |

The computed retail closing inventory (₹5,00,000) equals the physical count (₹5,00,000); hence there is no shrinkage/pilferage to adjust.

**Statement Showing Cost of Closing Inventory (Retail Method)**

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Closing inventory at retail price | 5,00,000 |
| Cost-to-retail ratio | 73.33% |
| **Cost of closing inventory = ₹5,00,000 × 0.7333** | **3,66,667** |

**Answer:** The closing inventory to be carried in the Balance Sheet as at 31st March, 2026 under the Retail Method is **₹3,66,667** (rounded).

*(Full-marks tip: ICAI rewards inclusion of the additional mark-up in the retail column of goods available for sale before striking the ratio; a common deduction is applying the ratio to sales instead of to closing retail inventory, or omitting the mark-up so the ratio is understated.)*

### Q2. Ch: AS 2 Inventory — Treatment of Abnormal Wastage & Storage Costs (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** Precision Castings Ltd. manufactured 10,000 units of a component during the year ended 31st March, 2026, of which 8,500 units were sold and 1,500 units remained in stock. The following costs were incurred:

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Direct materials consumed | 40,00,000 |
| Direct labour | 15,00,000 |
| Variable production overheads | 8,00,000 |
| Fixed production overheads (normal capacity 10,000 units) | 12,00,000 |
| Cost of abnormal wastage of material (fire in one batch) | 2,50,000 |
| Storage cost of finished goods before dispatch | 1,20,000 |
| Storage cost of raw material necessary before a further processing stage | 60,000 |
| Selling and distribution overheads | 3,00,000 |

Determine the value at which the 1,500 units of closing inventory should be carried as per AS 2, assuming net realisable value is higher than cost.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Identification of costs to be included / excluded (AS 2)**

Per AS 2, cost of inventory comprises costs of purchase, costs of conversion and other costs incurred in bringing inventories to their present location and condition. The following are **excluded** from cost and charged to the Statement of Profit and Loss:
- **Abnormal wastage ₹2,50,000** — abnormal amounts of wasted materials are excluded (para 13).
- **Storage cost of finished goods ₹1,20,000** — storage costs are excluded **unless** necessary in the production process prior to a further stage; storage of finished goods before dispatch is not part of production and is excluded (para 13).
- **Selling & distribution overheads ₹3,00,000** — excluded (para 13).

The **raw-material storage cost of ₹60,000 is included**, as it is necessary before a further processing stage.

**WN-2: Computation of cost of production (10,000 units)**

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Direct materials consumed | 40,00,000 |
| Direct labour | 15,00,000 |
| Variable production overheads | 8,00,000 |
| Fixed production overheads (normal capacity — fully absorbed) | 12,00,000 |
| Storage cost necessary before further processing | 60,000 |
| **Total cost of production of 10,000 units** | **75,60,000** |

Since actual production (10,000 units) equals normal capacity, fixed overhead is fully absorbed and no under-absorption arises.

**WN-3: Cost per unit**
Cost per unit = ₹75,60,000 ÷ 10,000 units = **₹756 per unit**

**Statement Showing Value of Closing Inventory**

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Cost per unit | 756 |
| Number of units in closing stock | 1,500 |
| **Value of closing inventory (at cost) = 1,500 × ₹756** | **11,34,000** |

As NRV is stated to be higher than cost, inventory is valued at cost.

**Answer:** The closing inventory of 1,500 units is to be valued at **₹11,34,000**. Abnormal wastage (₹2,50,000), finished-goods storage (₹1,20,000) and selling & distribution overheads (₹3,00,000) are charged as period expenses.

*(Full-marks tip: State the para-13 exclusions with reasons; the frequent deduction is including finished-goods storage or abnormal wastage in cost, or wrongly excluding the raw-material storage that is necessary for further processing.)*

### Q3. Ch: AS 2 Inventory — NRV where Selling Price Fell After Year-End (Marks: 4) [Application]
**Question:** While finalising the accounts of Aurora Textiles Ltd. for the year ended 31st March, 2026, the following situation is noted. A batch of fabric having a cost of ₹18,00,000 was held in stock on 31st March, 2026. At the year-end the estimated selling price was ₹22,00,000 and estimated selling costs ₹1,50,000. However, on 20th April, 2026 (before approval of accounts), the market price of that fabric collapsed and the entire batch was sold for a net realisable amount of only ₹15,20,000. How should this inventory be valued in the financial statements as at 31st March, 2026? Support with reasoning.

**Answer:** As per AS 2 "Valuation of Inventories", inventories should be valued at the **lower of cost and net realisable value (NRV)**. NRV is the estimated selling price in the ordinary course of business less the estimated costs of completion and the estimated costs necessary to make the sale.

AS 2 further provides that the estimate of NRV is based on the most reliable evidence available **at the time the estimate is made**, and that events occurring after the balance sheet date, to the extent they **confirm conditions existing at the balance sheet date**, are to be considered in estimating NRV. Read with AS 4 (Contingencies and Events Occurring After the Balance Sheet Date), the post-year-end fall in market price and the actual sale at ₹15,20,000 provide evidence of the NRV of the goods already held on the balance sheet date and is therefore an **adjusting event**.

Accordingly, the comparison is:

| Basis | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Cost | 18,00,000 |
| NRV as confirmed by post-year-end sale | 15,20,000 |
| **Value in Balance Sheet (lower of the two)** | **15,20,000** |

The write-down of ₹2,80,000 (₹18,00,000 − ₹15,20,000) is recognised as an expense in the year ended 31st March, 2026.

**Conclusion:** The fabric must be carried at **₹15,20,000**, and a write-down of ₹2,80,000 charged to the Statement of Profit and Loss for the year 2025-26, because the post-balance-sheet sale confirms the NRV of a condition existing on the balance sheet date.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards citing that the price fall is an adjusting event confirming a year-end condition; a common error is treating it as a non-adjusting event and valuing at the original ₹20,50,000 NRV.)*

### Q4. Ch: AS 2 Inventory — Cost Formulas & Exclusions (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the cost formulas permitted under AS 2 for assigning cost to inventories, and list the categories of costs that AS 2 specifically excludes from the cost of inventories.

**Answer:** AS 2 "Valuation of Inventories" prescribes how the cost of inventories is to be assigned and which costs are to be excluded.

**Cost formulas:**
1. **Specific identification** — The cost of inventories of items that are **not ordinarily interchangeable**, and goods or services produced and segregated for specific projects, is assigned by specific identification of their individual costs (i.e., specific costs are attributed to identified items).
2. Where specific identification is not appropriate (i.e., there are large numbers of ordinarily interchangeable items), cost is assigned using either:
   - **First-In, First-Out (FIFO)** — assumes items purchased or produced first are sold first, so that items remaining in inventory are those most recently purchased/produced; or
   - **Weighted Average Cost** — cost is determined from the weighted average of the cost of similar items at the beginning of a period and cost of similar items purchased/produced during the period.

AS 2 does **not** permit LIFO.

**Costs excluded from cost of inventories (para 13):** Such excluded costs are recognised as expenses in the period incurred:

| Excluded cost | Reason |
|---|---|
| Abnormal amounts of wasted materials, labour or other production costs | Not incurred in bringing inventory to present location/condition |
| Storage costs | Excluded unless necessary in the production process prior to a further production stage |
| Administrative overheads not contributing to present location and condition | Not attributable to production |
| Selling and distribution costs | Incurred after production is complete |

**Conclusion:** Cost is assigned by specific identification for non-interchangeable items and otherwise by FIFO or weighted average; abnormal wastage, non-essential storage, non-attributable administrative overheads and selling costs are excluded from inventory cost.

*(Full-marks tip: Naming the storage-cost exception — necessary before a further production stage — earns the last mark; merely listing FIFO/weighted average without the specific-identification rule loses marks.)*

### Q5. Ch: AS 7 Construction Contracts — Foreseeable Loss (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** Sterling Infra Ltd. secured a fixed-price contract to build a bridge for a total contract price of ₹120 crore. Details as at 31st March, 2026 (end of the second year of the contract) are:

| Particulars | ₹ crore |
|---|---|
| Costs incurred to date | 84 |
| Estimated further costs to complete | 56 |
| Progress billings raised to date | 70 |
| Amounts received from customer to date | 60 |

Determine the profit or loss to be recognised for the year and the amounts to be presented in the financial statements as per AS 7, given that in the first year the contract had recognised a profit of ₹4 crore on costs of ₹30 crore (percentage of completion then being 25%).

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Total estimated outcome of the contract**

| Particulars | ₹ crore |
|---|---|
| Costs incurred to date | 84 |
| Add: Estimated further costs to complete | 56 |
| Total estimated contract cost | 140 |
| Contract price | 120 |
| **Estimated total loss on contract (120 − 140)** | **(20)** |

**WN-2: Recognition of foreseeable loss (AS 7 para 35)**

When it is probable that total contract costs will exceed total contract revenue, the **expected loss should be recognised as an expense immediately**, irrespective of the stage of completion. Hence the **entire loss of ₹20 crore** must be reflected in the accounts to date.

**WN-3: Loss to be recognised in the current year (2025-26)**

| Particulars | ₹ crore |
|---|---|
| Total loss required to be recognised to date | (20) |
| Less: Profit already recognised in Year 1 | (4) |
| **Loss to be recognised in current year** | **(24)** |

The profit of ₹4 crore booked in Year 1 must be reversed and the full ₹20 crore loss provided, giving a current-year charge of ₹24 crore.

**WN-4: Stage of completion (for revenue disclosure)**
Percentage of completion = ₹84 ÷ ₹140 = **60%**
Contract revenue to date = 60% × ₹120 crore = ₹72 crore
Contract cost to date = ₹84 crore → Loss to date = ₹72 − ₹84 = ₹(12) crore by proportion; plus additional provision for foreseeable loss to bring total to ₹(20) crore.

**Statement Showing Amounts Recognised (₹ crore)**

| Particulars | To date | Year 1 | Current year |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contract revenue recognised | 72 | 30 | 42 |
| Contract cost recognised | 84 | 26 | 58 |
| Provision for foreseeable loss (additional) | 8 | — | 8 |
| **Profit / (Loss)** | **(20)** | **4** | **(24)** |

**WN-5: Balance Sheet presentation ("due to/from customers")**

| Particulars | ₹ crore |
|---|---|
| Costs incurred + recognised profits − recognised losses (84 − 20) | 64 |
| Less: Progress billings | (70) |
| **Gross amount due to customers (shown as liability)** | **(6)** |

**Answer:** A **loss of ₹24 crore** is recognised in the year ended 31st March, 2026 (reversing the ₹4 crore earlier profit and providing the full ₹20 crore foreseeable loss). The Balance Sheet shows an amount **due to customers of ₹6 crore** and a trade receivable of ₹10 crore (billings ₹70 − received ₹60).

*(Full-marks tip: ICAI awards the reversal of the earlier-year profit plus immediate full-loss recognition; the classic error is to spread the loss by percentage completion instead of recognising it in full immediately.)*

### Q6. Ch: AS 7 Construction Contracts — Change in Estimate (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** Kaveri Constructions Ltd. is executing a contract with a contract price of ₹500 lakh. The position over three years is as follows. In Year 3 the estimate of total cost changed. Compute the contract revenue, cost and profit to be recognised each year using the percentage-of-completion method (based on cost incurred to total estimated cost).

| Particulars | Year 1 (₹ lakh) | Year 2 (₹ lakh) | Year 3 (₹ lakh) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cost incurred to date | 80 | 250 | 420 |
| Estimated further cost to complete | 320 | 170 | Nil (completed) |

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Total estimated cost and stage of completion each year**

| Particulars | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cost incurred to date (A) | 80 | 250 | 420 |
| Further cost to complete (B) | 320 | 170 | — |
| Total estimated cost (A + B) | 400 | 420 | 420 |
| Stage of completion = A ÷ (A+B) | 20% | 59.524% | 100% |

The rise in total estimated cost from ₹400 lakh to ₹420 lakh in Year 2 (and confirmed in Year 3) is a **change in accounting estimate** under AS 7 read with AS 5, applied **prospectively**; earlier years are not restated.

**WN-2: Contract revenue recognised each year**

Cumulative revenue = Stage of completion × Contract price (₹500 lakh)

| Particulars | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cumulative revenue | 100.00 | 297.62 | 500.00 |
| Less: Recognised in earlier years | — | (100.00) | (297.62) |
| **Revenue for the year** | **100.00** | **197.62** | **202.38** |

Year 2 cumulative revenue = 59.524% × 500 = ₹297.62 lakh.

**WN-3: Contract cost recognised each year (equals cost incurred in the year)**

| Particulars | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cost incurred to date | 80 | 250 | 420 |
| Less: Recognised earlier | — | (80) | (250) |
| **Cost for the year** | **80** | **170** | **170** |

**Statement Showing Profit Recognised Each Year (₹ lakh)**

| Particulars | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Contract revenue | 100.00 | 197.62 | 202.38 | 500.00 |
| Contract cost | 80.00 | 170.00 | 170.00 | 420.00 |
| **Profit for the year** | **20.00** | **27.62** | **32.38** | **80.00** |

**Cross-check:** Total profit = Contract price ₹500 − Total cost ₹420 = **₹80 lakh**, which reconciles with the sum of yearly profits.

**Answer:** Profit recognised is **₹20 lakh (Year 1), ₹27.62 lakh (Year 2) and ₹32.38 lakh (Year 3)**, aggregating to ₹80 lakh. The change in total cost estimate is accounted for prospectively without restating Year 1.

*(Full-marks tip: State that a change in cost estimate is a change in accounting estimate under AS 5 applied prospectively; a common deduction is restating Year 1 revenue or applying the revised percentage retrospectively.)*

### Q7. Ch: AS 7 Construction Contracts — Disclosure Requirements (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the disclosures that an enterprise should make in respect of construction contracts as per AS 7.

**Answer:** As per AS 7 "Construction Contracts", an enterprise should make the following disclosures:

1. **Contract revenue recognised** as revenue in the period.
2. **Methods used to determine the contract revenue** recognised in the period.
3. **Methods used to determine the stage of completion** of contracts in progress (e.g., proportion of costs incurred, surveys of work performed, or physical proportion of the contract work).

For contracts in progress at the reporting date, the enterprise should disclose each of the following:
4. The **aggregate amount of costs incurred and recognised profits** (less recognised losses) up to the reporting date.
5. The **amount of advances received**.
6. The **amount of retentions** (amounts billed but not paid until satisfaction of conditions or defects are rectified).

Further, the enterprise should present:
7. The **gross amount due from customers** for contract work as an **asset**; and
8. The **gross amount due to customers** for contract work as a **liability**.

The gross amount due **from** customers is the net amount of costs incurred plus recognised profits, **less** the sum of recognised losses and progress billings, for all contracts in progress where costs incurred plus recognised profits (less losses) exceeds progress billings. The gross amount due **to** customers is the opposite (where progress billings exceed costs incurred plus recognised profits less losses).

**Conclusion:** AS 7 requires disclosure of contract revenue, the methods for revenue and stage of completion, and — for contracts in progress — aggregate costs plus recognised profits, advances, retentions, and the separate presentation of amounts due from and due to customers.

*(Full-marks tip: Distinguishing "due from" (asset) and "due to" (liability) customers with the underlying formula earns full marks; simply writing "disclose revenue and profit" is insufficient.)*

### Q8. Ch: AS 7 Construction Contracts — Contract Costs & Percentage Completion (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** Highland Projects Ltd. is executing a contract of ₹200 lakh. As at 31st March, 2026, the following data are available. Determine the stage of completion and the profit to be recognised, after correctly deciding which items form part of contract costs incurred for measuring the stage of completion.

| Particulars | ₹ lakh |
|---|---|
| Materials issued to site and consumed | 60 |
| Materials at site, not yet consumed (purchased for the contract) | 8 |
| Labour and site supervision | 30 |
| Depreciation of plant used on the contract | 10 |
| Advance paid to a subcontractor for future work (work not yet done) | 12 |
| General administration cost not attributable to the contract | 5 |
| Estimated further costs to complete | 60 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Contract costs incurred to date for measuring stage of completion (AS 7)**

AS 7 provides that costs used to determine the stage of completion **exclude** contract costs that relate to **future activity** — such as materials delivered to site but not yet consumed and advances to subcontractors for work not yet performed — and **exclude** costs that cannot be attributed to contract activity (general administration not reimbursable).

| Particulars | Include (₹ lakh) | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Materials consumed | 60 | Relates to work done |
| Materials at site, unconsumed | — | Relates to future activity — excluded |
| Labour and site supervision | 30 | Contract cost |
| Depreciation of plant on contract | 10 | Contract cost |
| Advance to subcontractor (work not done) | — | Future activity — excluded |
| General administration (non-attributable) | — | Not attributable — excluded |
| **Contract cost incurred to date** | **100** | |

**WN-2: Total estimated contract cost and stage of completion**

| Particulars | ₹ lakh |
|---|---|
| Contract cost incurred to date | 100 |
| Add: Estimated further cost to complete | 60 |
| **Total estimated contract cost** | **160** |

Stage of completion = ₹100 ÷ ₹160 = **62.5%**

**WN-3: Revenue, cost and profit recognised**

| Particulars | ₹ lakh |
|---|---|
| Contract revenue = 62.5% × ₹200 | 125.00 |
| Contract cost recognised | 100.00 |
| **Profit recognised** | **25.00** |

**Cross-check (total contract profit):** Contract price ₹200 − Total cost ₹160 = ₹40 lakh; recognised profit ₹25 lakh = 62.5% × ₹40 = ₹25 lakh. Reconciles.

**Answer:** Stage of completion is **62.5%** and profit to be recognised is **₹25 lakh**. Unconsumed materials (₹8 lakh) and the subcontractor advance (₹12 lakh) are carried as contract assets, not treated as cost incurred; non-attributable administration (₹5 lakh) is expensed.

*(Full-marks tip: Excluding future-activity costs from the numerator of the stage-of-completion ratio is the key mark; students often include unconsumed materials and the subcontractor advance and overstate completion.)*

### Q9. Ch: AS 9 Revenue Recognition — Interest, Royalty & Dividend (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** State, with reasons, the amount of revenue Meridian Investments Ltd. should recognise for the year ended 31st March, 2026 in each of the following independent situations, applying AS 9:

| No. | Situation |
|---|---|
| (i) | The company holds 10% debentures of face value ₹40,00,000 acquired on 1st October, 2025; interest is payable half-yearly on 30th September and 31st March but the March interest was received only on 12th April, 2026. |
| (ii) | The company licensed a patent for a lump-sum royalty of ₹18,00,000 for 3 years plus a running royalty of ₹5 per unit; the licensee produced 60,000 units in the year. Assume the lump sum is non-refundable and the arrangement is for continued use. |
| (iii) | A dividend of ₹6,00,000 on its equity investment was declared by the investee company on 5th April, 2026 for the year ended 31st March, 2026. |
| (iv) | Interest of ₹90,000 is due on a loan given to a party who has become insolvent and recovery is uncertain. |

**Answer:** As per AS 9 "Revenue Recognition", revenue arising from the use by others of enterprise resources yielding interest, royalties and dividends should be recognised only when **no significant uncertainty as to measurability or collectability exists**, and on the following bases:
- **Interest** — on a **time proportion basis** taking into account the amount outstanding and the rate applicable.
- **Royalties** — on an **accrual basis** in accordance with the terms of the relevant agreement.
- **Dividends** — when the owner's **right to receive payment is established**.

| No. | Recognition | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| (i) | Interest accrues on a time-proportion basis for 6 months (1 Oct 2025 – 31 Mar 2026); receipt after year-end does not affect accrual: ₹40,00,000 × 10% × 6/12 | **2,00,000** |
| (ii) | Lump-sum royalty for a 3-year right is recognised over the period of use (₹18,00,000 ÷ 3 = ₹6,00,000) **plus** running royalty on accrual (60,000 × ₹5 = ₹3,00,000) | **9,00,000** |
| (iii) | Right to receive is established on the date of declaration (5 April 2026), which is after the year-end; **no revenue** in 2025-26 | **Nil** |
| (iv) | Collectability is significantly uncertain; recognition is **postponed** until the uncertainty is resolved | **Nil** |

**Conclusion:** Meridian recognises **₹2,00,000** interest, **₹9,00,000** royalty (₹6,00,000 amortised lump sum + ₹3,00,000 running), **Nil** dividend (right established after year-end) and **Nil** on the doubtful interest.

*(Full-marks tip: Spreading a lump-sum royalty over the licence period and deferring dividend to the declaration date are the discriminating marks; a common error is recognising the entire ₹18,00,000 royalty or accruing the post-year-end dividend.)*

### Q10. Ch: AS 9 Revenue Recognition — Goods Sold with Warranty (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Voltech Appliances Ltd. sold 5,000 refrigerators during March 2026 at ₹25,000 each. Each sale carries a **standard free warranty** for 24 months against manufacturing defects. Based on past experience, warranty claims are estimated at 3% of the number of units sold, with an average repair cost of ₹1,800 per unit. The company also offers, at the customer's option, an **extended warranty** for a further 12 months at a separately priced charge of ₹1,500 per unit; 800 customers purchased the extended warranty in March 2026. Advise on the amount of revenue to be recognised for March 2026 and the accounting for warranties under AS 9.

**Answer:** As per AS 9 "Revenue Recognition", in a transaction involving the sale of goods, revenue is recognised when the **seller has transferred to the buyer the property in the goods** (all significant risks and rewards) and no significant uncertainty exists regarding the amount of consideration and its collectability.

**(a) Sale of refrigerators (with standard free warranty):**
The standard free warranty is an obligation attaching to a sale already made; it does not defer recognition of revenue on the sale. The **entire sale revenue is recognised on delivery**, and the expected warranty cost is **provided for** as a liability (an expense matched against the sale), consistent with AS 29 principles.

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Sale revenue = 5,000 × ₹25,000 | 12,50,00,000 |
| Provision for standard warranty = (5,000 × 3%) × ₹1,800 = 150 units × ₹1,800 | 2,70,000 |

Revenue recognised = **₹12,50,00,000**; warranty provision of ₹2,70,000 is charged to the Statement of Profit and Loss.

**(b) Separately priced extended warranty:**
The extended warranty is a **separate service** sold for a distinct consideration and covers a period **after** the free-warranty obligation. The consideration for it should be **deferred and recognised as revenue over the period of the extended warranty** (12 months, on a straight-line/time basis, beginning after the free-warranty period), because the service is rendered over that period.

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Extended warranty received = 800 × ₹1,500 | 12,00,000 |
| Recognised in March 2026 (service not yet rendered) | Nil — deferred |

**Conclusion:** For March 2026, recognise sale revenue of **₹12,50,00,000** and provide **₹2,70,000** for the standard warranty; the **₹12,00,000** extended-warranty consideration is deferred and recognised over the future extended-warranty period.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards recognising full sale revenue with a warranty provision, but deferring the separately-priced extended warranty over its service period; a common error is deferring part of the sale price for the free warranty or recognising the extended warranty upfront.)*

### Q11. Ch: AS 9 Revenue Recognition — Conditions & Postponement (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** When is revenue from the sale of goods recognised under AS 9? Explain the circumstances in which recognition of revenue is postponed.

**Answer:** As per AS 9 "Revenue Recognition", revenue from the **sale of goods** should be recognised when **both** the following conditions are fulfilled:
1. The seller has **transferred to the buyer the property in the goods for a price**, or all **significant risks and rewards of ownership** have been transferred to the buyer and the seller retains no effective control of the goods to a degree usually associated with ownership; and
2. **No significant uncertainty exists** regarding the amount of consideration that will be derived from the sale of goods.

**Postponement of recognition:** Where the ability to assess the ultimate collection with reasonable certainty is lacking at the time of raising the claim (e.g., for export incentives or where consideration is not reasonably determinable), **revenue recognition is postponed** to the extent of that uncertainty. In such cases, revenue is recognised **only when it is reasonably certain that the ultimate collection will be made**.

It is important to distinguish two situations:

| Situation | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Uncertainty exists **at the time of sale** (collectability doubtful) | Postpone recognition of revenue until reasonably certain |
| Revenue already recognised, uncertainty arises **later** on collectability | Recognise revenue in full; make a **separate provision** for the doubtful amount (do not adjust the revenue originally recorded) |

**Conclusion:** Revenue on sale of goods is recognised on transfer of significant risks and rewards coupled with no significant uncertainty of consideration; where collectability is uncertain at the outset, recognition is postponed, whereas uncertainty arising after recognition is dealt with by a separate provision.

*(Full-marks tip: The distinction between postponing recognition (uncertainty at outset) and providing separately (uncertainty later) is the high-value point examiners look for.)*

### Q15. Ch: AS 10 Property, Plant & Equipment — Recognition & Subsequent Costs (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the recognition criteria for an item of Property, Plant and Equipment under AS 10, and state how subsequent costs (including the cost of major inspections and replacement of parts) are dealt with.

**Answer:** As per AS 10 "Property, Plant and Equipment", the cost of an item of PP&E should be recognised as an asset **if, and only if**:
1. It is **probable that future economic benefits** associated with the item will flow to the enterprise; and
2. The **cost of the item can be measured reliably**.

This recognition principle applies both to costs incurred **initially** to acquire or construct an item of PP&E and to costs incurred **subsequently** to add to, replace part of, or service it.

**Subsequent costs:**

| Type of subsequent cost | Treatment under AS 10 |
|---|---|
| Day-to-day servicing (repairs and maintenance) | Recognised in the **Statement of Profit and Loss** as incurred (cost of labour, consumables, small parts) |
| Replacement of a part at regular intervals (e.g., relining a furnace, replacing aircraft interior) | **Capitalised** in the carrying amount if the recognition criteria are met, and the **carrying amount of the replaced part is derecognised** |
| Cost of major inspections (e.g., regulatory overhaul) as a condition of continuing operation | **Capitalised** as a replacement if the recognition criteria are met; any remaining carrying amount of the previous inspection is derecognised |

An enterprise does not recognise in the carrying amount the costs of day-to-day servicing; these are expensed.

**Conclusion:** An item of PP&E is recognised only when future economic benefits are probable and cost is measurable; the same test governs subsequent costs — replacements and major inspections meeting the criteria are capitalised (with derecognition of the replaced part), while routine servicing is expensed.

*(Full-marks tip: Mentioning derecognition of the replaced part when a new part is capitalised is the mark that distinguishes a full answer from a generic one.)*

**Question:** Examine the following independent matters relating to Zenith Manufacturing Ltd. for the year ended 31st March, 2026 and state, with reasons and computations, the correct accounting treatment. Cite the applicable Accounting Standard in each case.

### Q18. Ch: AS 4 — Events Occurring After the Balance Sheet Date — Adjusting vs Non-adjusting on Facts (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Sunrise Ltd is finalising its financial statements for the year ended 31st March 2026. The financial statements were approved by the Board of Directors on 30th May 2026. The following events occurred after the balance sheet date. State, with reasons, the treatment required under AS 4:

| # | Event |
|---|-------|
| (i) | A debtor owing ₹8,00,000 (outstanding and considered good on 31.03.2026) was declared insolvent on 20.04.2026 and only ₹1,00,000 is expected to be recovered. |
| (ii) | A fire on 15.05.2026 destroyed finished goods of ₹25,00,000; the loss is not covered by insurance. |
| (iii) | The Board, at its meeting on 28.04.2026, proposed a dividend of ₹12,00,000 for the year 2025-26. |
| (iv) | On 10.04.2026 the Court delivered its judgment confirming a liability of ₹6,00,000 in respect of a suit that was pending on the balance sheet date and had earlier been disclosed as a contingent liability. |

**Answer:**
As per AS 4 "Contingencies and Events Occurring After the Balance Sheet Date", events occurring after the balance sheet date are those events, both favourable and unfavourable, that occur between the balance sheet date and the date on which the financial statements are approved by the Board. Adjusting events are those which provide additional evidence of conditions that **existed at the balance sheet date**, and assets and liabilities are adjusted for such events. Non-adjusting events are those that arise from conditions **arising after** the balance sheet date and require only disclosure (if material) and no adjustment.

**(i) Insolvency of debtor — Adjusting event.** The debtor's financial condition (leading to insolvency on 20.04.2026) existed on the balance sheet date; the subsequent event only confirms it. The company must write down the debtor and provide for the loss of ₹7,00,000 (₹8,00,000 – ₹1,00,000) in the accounts for 2025-26.

**(ii) Fire destroying goods — Non-adjusting event.** The fire occurred on 15.05.2026 and the condition (fire) did **not** exist on 31.03.2026. No adjustment is made to the financial statements. However, since the loss of ₹25,00,000 is material, it should be **disclosed** in the Directors'/Board's report; if it affects the going concern assumption, the fundamental accounting assumption itself would require reconsideration (not the case here).

**(iii) Proposed dividend — Non-adjusting, not recognised as a liability.** As per AS 4 (as amended by the Companies (Accounting Standards) Amendment Rules), dividends proposed or declared **after** the balance sheet date but before approval of the financial statements **are not to be recognised as a liability** at the balance sheet date; the amount is only **disclosed** in the notes. Hence no provision of ₹12,00,000 is made.

**(iv) Court judgment on a pending suit — Adjusting event.** The obligation existed on the balance sheet date (the suit was pending on 31.03.2026); the judgment on 10.04.2026 merely provides additional evidence confirming the amount. The contingent liability crystallises into a present obligation and a **provision of ₹6,00,000** must be made in 2025-26.

**Conclusion:** Items (i) and (iv) are adjusting events requiring provision of ₹7,00,000 and ₹6,00,000 respectively; item (ii) is a non-adjusting event requiring disclosure only; item (iii) is disclosed in the notes and not provided for.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the "condition existed on the balance sheet date?" test applied event-by-event. The most common deduction is providing for proposed dividend — post-amendment AS 4 forbids this; another is adjusting for the fire.)*

### Q19. Ch: AS 5 — Net Profit/Loss, Prior Period Items & Extraordinary Items (Marks: 4) [Application]
**Question:** State how the following items should be classified and disclosed by Meridian Ltd in its Statement of Profit and Loss for the year ended 31st March 2026, giving reasons under AS 5:

| # | Item |
|---|------|
| (a) | Loss of ₹15,00,000 of stock destroyed by an earthquake. |
| (b) | Depreciation of ₹3,00,000 relating to the year 2024-25 which was inadvertently omitted and is now being recorded. |
| (c) | A refund of ₹4,00,000 of excise duty received in the current year, which had been paid and charged to the Statement of Profit and Loss in 2023-24. |
| (d) | ₹2,50,000 written back on account of an excess provision for doubtful debts made in an earlier year. |

**Answer:**
As per AS 5 "Net Profit or Loss for the Period, Prior Period Items and Changes in Accounting Policies", the net profit or loss for the period comprises (i) profit or loss from **ordinary activities** and (ii) **extraordinary items**. **Extraordinary items** are income or expenses that arise from events or transactions that are **clearly distinct from the ordinary activities** of the enterprise and are **not expected to recur** frequently or regularly; they are disclosed as a part of net profit but shown separately. **Prior period items** are income or expenses which arise in the current period as a result of **errors or omissions** in the preparation of financial statements of one or more prior periods.

| Item | Classification | Reason |
|------|----------------|--------|
| (a) Earthquake loss ₹15,00,000 | **Extraordinary item** | Arises from an event clearly distinct from ordinary activities and not expected to recur; disclosed separately such that its impact on current profit can be perceived. |

| (c) Excise refund ₹4,00,000 | **Ordinary activity item** | A recovery of an expense of ordinary activities; it is **not** a prior period item (no error/omission — the earlier charge was correct on the then facts). If material, disclosed separately as an item of income from ordinary activities. |
| (d) Excess provision written back ₹2,50,000 | **Ordinary activity item (change in estimate)** | Provision for doubtful debts is an accounting estimate; its revision is a change in estimate recognised in the current period — **not** a prior period item. Separately disclosed if material. |

**Conclusion:** (a) is an extraordinary item and (b) a prior period item — both separately disclosed; (c) and (d) are items of ordinary activities (the latter a change in estimate), disclosed separately only if material.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner tests the fine line — a prior period item requires an *error or omission*, not merely a benefit relating to an earlier year. Mislabelling the excise refund or the excess provision write-back as "prior period" is the standard deduction.)*

### Q20. Ch: AS 11 — Forward Exchange Contract Hedging an Existing Monetary Item (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** On 1st January 2026, Apex Ltd imported goods worth **USD 1,00,000** on credit, payment due on 30th June 2026, when the spot rate was ₹83/USD. On the same date, to hedge this liability, the company entered into a **6-month forward contract** to buy USD 1,00,000 at a forward rate of **₹84/USD**. The contract is not for trading or speculation. On 31st March 2026 (year-end) the spot rate was **₹85/USD**. Show the accounting treatment and the amounts charged/credited to the Statement of Profit and Loss for the year ended 31st March 2026, with journal entries.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Nature and treatment (AS 11, paras 36–38).** Since the forward contract is taken to hedge an existing foreign currency monetary item (the creditor) and is **not** for trading/speculation, (a) the **premium/discount** at inception is amortised over the life of the contract, (b) the **exchange difference** on the contract (movement in spot rate) is recognised in the Statement of Profit and Loss, and (c) the underlying monetary item (creditor) is **separately restated** at the closing spot rate under paras 11 and 13.

**WN-2: Premium on forward contract and its amortisation.**
Premium = (Forward rate − Spot rate at inception) × USD = (₹84 − ₹83) × 1,00,000 = **₹1,00,000** (to be amortised over 6 months).
Amortised for the year (1 Jan–31 Mar = 3 months) = ₹1,00,000 × 3/6 = **₹50,000** (expense to P&L). Balance ₹50,000 carried forward.

**WN-3: Exchange difference on the forward contract.**
= (Spot rate on 31.03.2026 − Spot rate at inception) × USD = (₹85 − ₹83) × 1,00,000 = **₹2,00,000 (gain)** credited to P&L.

**WN-4: Restatement of the creditor (underlying monetary item).**
Recorded value = 1,00,000 × ₹83 = ₹83,00,000; closing value = 1,00,000 × ₹85 = ₹85,00,000.

### Q24. Ch: AS 18 — Related Party Relationships and Disclosures (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Examine, with reasons under AS 18, whether the following are related parties of Orbit Ltd, and state which transactions require disclosure:

| # | Situation |
|---|-----------|
| (i) | Orbit Ltd holds 55% of the equity of Comet Ltd. |
| (ii) | Nova Ltd holds 30% of the equity of Orbit Ltd and exercises significant influence over it. |
| (iii) | Mr. Ravi is the Managing Director (KMP) of Orbit Ltd. |

| (v) | Orbit Ltd and Vega Ltd have one common director but neither controls nor significantly influences the other. |

### Q25. Ch: AS 19 — Operating vs Finance Lease Classification (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Horizon Ltd (lessee) takes an equipment on lease from Falcon Ltd on 1st April 2025 on the following terms. Classify the lease under AS 19, applying the relevant tests:

| Particulars | Amount |
|-------------|--------|
| Fair value of the equipment | ₹10,00,000 |
| Estimated economic (useful) life | 8 years |
| Non-cancellable lease term | 6 years |
| Annual lease rental (payable at the end of each year) | ₹2,00,000 |
| Interest rate implicit in the lease | 12% |
| Transfer of ownership / bargain purchase option | None |

(PVIFA at 12% for 6 years = 4.1114.)

**Answer:**
As per AS 19 "Leases", a lease is classified as a **finance lease** if it transfers **substantially all the risks and rewards** incidental to ownership; otherwise it is an **operating lease**. Classification is made at the inception of the lease based on the substance of the transaction. AS 19 lists indicators — transfer of ownership, bargain purchase option, lease term for the **major part** of the economic life, present value of minimum lease payments amounting to **substantially all** of the fair value, and specialised nature of the asset.

**Application of the tests:**

| Test | Result | Indicates |
|------|--------|-----------|
| Transfer of ownership at end of term | No | Operating |
| Bargain purchase option | No | Operating |
| Lease term as % of economic life | 6/8 = **75%** → major part | **Finance** |
| PV of MLP vs fair value | 2,00,000 × 4.1114 = **₹8,22,280** = 82.2% of ₹10,00,000 → substantially all | **Finance** |
| Specialised asset | Not stated | — |

Although ownership does not pass and there is no purchase option, the lease term covers a **major part (75%)** of the equipment's economic life **and** the present value of the minimum lease payments (₹8,22,280) amounts to **substantially all (82.2%)** of the fair value. AS 19 provides that the presence of **any** indicator individually or in combination that transfers substantially all risks and rewards leads to finance-lease classification.

**Conclusion:** The lease is a **finance lease**. Horizon Ltd should capitalise the asset at the **lower** of fair value and present value of MLP, i.e., **₹8,22,280**, with a corresponding lease liability, and depreciate it over the shorter of the lease term and useful life.

*(Full-marks tip: Compute both the life ratio (75%) and the PV-of-MLP ratio (82.2%) with figures; a bare statement "it is a finance lease" without the two computed percentages earns only part marks.)*

### Q28. Ch: AS 24 — Discontinuing Operations (Marks: 4) [Application]

### Q29. Ch: AS 25 — Interim Financial Reporting — Recognition of Items (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** State how the following should be dealt with in the **quarterly** interim financial report (quarter ended 30th June 2025) of Cascade Ltd under AS 25:

| # | Item |
|---|------|
| (i) | The company earns 70% of its annual revenue in the festive third quarter; management wishes to spread/anticipate this revenue evenly across quarters. |
| (ii) | A year-end staff bonus, payable only if the employee is in service on 31st March 2026, is expected to be ₹24 lakh for the year. |
| (iii) | The estimated weighted-average annual income-tax rate is 25%; the pre-tax quarterly profit is ₹80 lakh. |
| (iv) | Uninsured flood loss of ₹18 lakh incurred entirely in this quarter. |

**Answer:**
As per AS 25 "Interim Financial Reporting", an enterprise applies the **same accounting policies** in its interim statements as in its annual statements, and recognises revenues and costs as they are earned or incurred — an item is anticipated or deferred at an interim date only if such anticipation/deferral would be appropriate **at the year-end**. Income-tax is accrued using the **estimated weighted-average annual effective tax rate**.

| Item | Treatment |
|------|-----------|
| (i) Seasonal revenue | Revenues that are **received seasonally** must be recognised **when they occur** and **cannot be anticipated or deferred** at an interim date. Only the revenue actually earned in the quarter is recognised; the festive-quarter revenue is **not** spread. (Disclosure of the seasonal nature is, however, encouraged.) |
| (ii) Year-end bonus | A bonus is provided in the interim period **only if** there is a present (legal or constructive) obligation and a reliable estimate. As the bonus is contingent on service up to 31.03.2026, a proportionate accrual (₹24 lakh × 3/12 = **₹6 lakh** for the quarter) is made only if it constitutes a constructive obligation; otherwise it is **not** provided. |
| (iii) Income tax | Tax expense for the quarter = pre-tax profit × estimated annual effective rate = ₹80 lakh × 25% = **₹20 lakh**. |
| (iv) Flood loss | Costs incurred **unevenly** during the year are anticipated or deferred at interim date only if it would be appropriate at year-end. A flood loss cannot be anticipated/deferred; it is recognised **in full (₹18 lakh) in the quarter** in which it is incurred. |

**Conclusion:** Seasonal revenue is recognised only when earned (not spread); the bonus is accrued proportionately only if a constructive obligation exists; interim tax of ₹20 lakh is provided at the annual effective rate of 25%; and the flood loss of ₹18 lakh is charged fully in the current quarter.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the "same basis as year-end" principle applied item-by-item and the use of the **estimated annual effective tax rate** for interim tax. Spreading seasonal revenue or the flood loss across quarters is the classic error.)*

| (a) | Research on a new manufacturing process | 10 |
| (b) | Development of the process after technical and commercial feasibility, and all recognition criteria, were established | 25 |
| (c) | Cost of internally generating and promoting a brand name | 15 |
| (d) | Purchase of a patent from an outside party | 8 |
| (e) | Advertising and promotion to launch a new product | 5 |

**Answer:**
As per AS 26 "Intangible Assets", an intangible asset is recognised only if it is **identifiable**, the enterprise has **control** over it, **future economic benefits** are probable, and the **cost can be measured reliably**. |------|-----------|--------|
| (a) Research ₹10 lakh | **Charge to P&L** | Research-phase expenditure cannot be recognised as an asset. |
| (b) Development ₹25 lakh | **Capitalise as intangible asset** | All recognition criteria for the development phase are met; amortise over useful life. |
| (c) Internally generated brand ₹15 lakh | **Charge to P&L** | AS 26 prohibits recognition of internally generated brands (not distinguishable from developing the business as a whole). |

### Q32. Ch: AS 29 — Provision vs Contingent Liability vs Contingent Asset (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** State, with reasons under AS 29, how Beacon Ltd should deal with each of the following in its financial statements for the year ended 31st March 2026:

| # | Situation |
|---|-----------|
| (a) | A customer has filed a suit for ₹20 lakh; the company's legal counsel opines it is **probable (about 70%)** that the company will lose. |
| (b) | The company has guaranteed a bank loan of a wholly-owned subsidiary; the subsidiary is financially sound and the possibility of any outflow is **remote**. |
| (c) | The company itself has filed a suit claiming damages of ₹10 lakh; it is **probable** that ₹10 lakh will be recovered. |
| (d) | The company sells products under a warranty; based on past experience, warranty claims for the year are reliably estimated at ₹6 lakh. |

**Answer:**
As per AS 29 "Provisions, Contingent Liabilities and Contingent Assets", a **provision** is recognised when (i) an enterprise has a **present obligation** as a result of a past event, (ii) it is **probable** that an outflow of resources will be required to settle it, and (iii) a **reliable estimate** can be made. A **contingent liability** is a possible obligation, or a present obligation for which an outflow is not probable or cannot be reliably measured — it is **not recognised, only disclosed** (unless the possibility of outflow is remote, in which case even disclosure is not required). A **contingent asset** is a possible asset arising from past events whose existence will be confirmed by uncertain future events — it is **not recognised**; it is disclosed only where an inflow of economic benefits is probable.

| Situation | Treatment | Reason |
|-----------|-----------|--------|
| (a) Suit — 70% likely to lose | **Provide ₹20 lakh** | Present obligation exists, outflow is probable, and the amount is reliably estimable — all three conditions met. |
| (b) Guarantee, remote outflow | **No provision, no disclosure** | Only a possible obligation and the possibility of outflow is **remote** — even contingent-liability disclosure is not required. |
| (c) Company's own damages claim | **Contingent asset — do not recognise** | It is a possible asset; recognition would anticipate income. As inflow is probable, **disclose** it in the report; recognise only when realisation is virtually certain. |
| (d) Warranty | **Provide ₹6 lakh** | A present (constructive/legal) obligation arises on sale; outflow is probable and reliably estimable — provision required. |

**Conclusion:** Provisions of ₹20 lakh (lawsuit) and ₹6 lakh (warranty) are recognised; the guarantee (remote) needs neither provision nor disclosure; and the company's own claim is a contingent asset, disclosed but not recognised.

*(Full-marks tip: The three tests must be shown for each item; the tested distinctions are that a *remote* contingency needs no disclosure, and a probable *contingent asset* is disclosed but never recognised as income. Providing for the contingent asset is the standard deduction.)*

### Q35. Ch: AS 3 Cash Flow Statements — Classification of Interest, Dividend and Tax (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State, with reasons, how the following are classified while preparing a Cash Flow Statement as per AS 3 (Revised) in the case of (i) a **non-financial (manufacturing) enterprise** and (ii) a **financial enterprise**: interest paid, interest received, dividend received, dividend paid, and cash flows arising from taxes on income.

**Answer:**
The classification of cash flows from interest, dividend and taxes is governed by **AS 3 "Cash Flow Statements"**. AS 3 requires that cash flows from interest and dividends received and paid should each be **disclosed separately** and be classified in a **consistent manner from period to period**.

**(i) Non-financial (manufacturing) enterprise:**
- **Interest paid** is classified as a cash flow from **financing activities**, because it is the cost of obtaining financial resources (borrowings).
- **Interest received** and **dividend received** are classified as cash flows from **investing activities**, because they are returns on investments (loans given and shares held).
- **Dividend paid** is classified as a cash flow from **financing activities**, because it is a distribution to the providers of equity finance.

**(ii) Financial enterprise (bank / financing company):**
- **Interest paid, interest received and dividend received** are classified as cash flows from **operating activities**, because for such an enterprise these arise from its principal revenue-producing activities (lending and investing form its main business).
- **Dividend paid** remains a **financing** cash flow, as it is a distribution to shareholders in every case.

**Taxes on income (for both):** Cash flows arising from taxes on income are **separately disclosed** and are classified as cash flows from **operating activities** **unless** they can be **specifically identified** with financing or investing activities. Where tax cash flow is allocable to an item classified as investing or financing, it is classified accordingly.

| Item | Financial enterprise | Non-financial enterprise |
|---|---|---|
| Interest paid | Operating | Financing |
| Interest received | Operating | Investing |
| Dividend received | Operating | Investing |
| Dividend paid | Financing | Financing |
| Tax on income | Operating (unless identifiable with investing/financing) | Same |

**Conclusion:** The same item may be classified differently depending on the nature of the enterprise, but the treatment, once adopted, must be applied consistently and disclosed separately.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the reason "principal revenue-producing activity" for a financial enterprise. A common deduction is forgetting that dividend paid is financing for BOTH types, and forgetting the "unless specifically identifiable" rider on tax.)*

### Q38. Ch: AS 3 Cash Flow Statements — Cash and Cash Equivalents (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Define "Cash" and "Cash Equivalents" as per AS 3. State how (i) a bank overdraft, (ii) short-term marketable investments maturing in two months, and (iii) redeemable preference shares acquired one month before their redemption date should be treated.

**Answer:**
As per **AS 3 "Cash Flow Statements"**, **Cash** comprises **cash on hand and demand deposits** with banks. **Cash equivalents** are **short-term, highly liquid investments** that are **readily convertible into known amounts of cash** and which are subject to an **insignificant risk of changes in value**.

Cash equivalents are held for the purpose of **meeting short-term cash commitments** rather than for investment or other purposes. For an investment to qualify as a cash equivalent, it must have a **short maturity of, say, three months or less** from the date of acquisition. **Investments in equity shares are excluded** from cash equivalents unless they are, in substance, cash equivalents.

**Application to the given cases:**

| Item | Treatment |
|---|---|
| (i) Bank overdraft | Generally treated as **financing activity**. However, where a bank overdraft repayable on demand forms an **integral part of an enterprise's cash management**, it may be included as a component of cash and cash equivalents (netted). |
| (ii) Marketable investments maturing in 2 months | Qualify as **cash equivalents**, since maturity is less than three months and the risk of change in value is insignificant. |
| (iii) Redeemable preference shares acquired 1 month before redemption | Treated as **cash equivalents**, because although they are equity/preference instruments, they are in substance a short-term deposit with a fixed redemption imminent (short maturity, insignificant risk). |

**Conclusion:** Only short-maturity, low-risk, readily convertible items qualify as cash equivalents; the acquisition and disposal among cash equivalents themselves are **not** shown as cash flows because they are part of cash management.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner looks for the "insignificant risk of change in value" and the "three-month maturity from date of acquisition" tests. Common deduction: forgetting the exception that preference shares near redemption can be cash equivalents.)*

### Q41. Ch: Redemption of Preference Shares — Conditions under Section 55 (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the conditions and sources prescribed by **Section 55 of the Companies Act, 2013** read with the relevant Rules for the redemption of preference shares by a company limited by shares.

**Answer:**
The redemption of preference shares is governed by **Section 55 of the Companies Act, 2013** and the **Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules, 2014**. The salient conditions are as follows:

1. **No irredeemable preference shares** — a company limited by shares **cannot issue** preference shares that are irredeemable [Section 55(1)]. Preference shares must be redeemable within a maximum period of **20 years** from the date of issue (30 years for shares issued for specified infrastructure projects) [Section 55(2)].
2. **Only fully paid shares** can be redeemed — preference shares can be redeemed **only if they are fully paid up** [Section 55(2)(a)].
3. **Sources of redemption** — the shares may be redeemed **only out of (a) the profits of the company which would otherwise be available for dividend**, or **(b) out of the proceeds of a fresh issue of shares** made for the purpose of redemption [Section 55(2)(b)].
4. **Premium on redemption** — any premium payable on redemption must be provided for out of the **profits of the company** (or, for specified companies, out of the **Securities Premium account**) before the shares are redeemed [Section 55(2)(c)].
5. **Capital Redemption Reserve (CRR)** — where the redemption is out of **profits**, a sum equal to the **nominal amount of the shares redeemed** must be transferred to the **Capital Redemption Reserve account** [Section 55(2)(d)]. The CRR may be used only for issuing **fully paid bonus shares**.

**Conclusion:** Redemption is permitted only for fully paid shares, funded exclusively from distributable profits or a fresh issue, with a mandatory CRR transfer that protects the capital base of creditors.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards citing Section 55(2)(a)–(d) precisely and the reason for CRR — to maintain the company's capital intact for creditors (capital maintenance). Common deduction: omitting that CRR can only be used for fully paid bonus shares.)*

### Q45. Ch: Redemption of Preference Shares — Sources and Purpose of CRR (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** "The Capital Redemption Reserve is created to maintain the capital of the company intact." In the light of this statement, explain (i) which reserves/profits can and cannot be used for creating the CRR, and (ii) the permissible utilisation of the CRR.

**Answer:**
The **Capital Redemption Reserve (CRR)** is created under **Section 55(2)(d) of the Companies Act, 2013** when preference shares are redeemed out of profits. Its object is **capital maintenance** — when shares are redeemed out of divisible profits, those profits (which could otherwise have been distributed) are "locked up" in the CRR so that the company's overall capital base available to creditors remains **undiminished**.

**(i) Sources — reserves that CAN and CANNOT be used:**
Only **free reserves / profits otherwise available for dividend** can be transferred to CRR. These include:

| Can be used for CRR | Cannot be used for CRR |
|---|---|
| General Reserve | Securities Premium account |
| Profit & Loss (Cr.) balance | Capital Reserve (capital profit) |
| Dividend Equalisation Reserve | Revaluation Reserve |
| Any other free (revenue) reserve | Profit prior to incorporation |

The excluded items are either capital profits or statutorily earmarked reserves not "available for dividend".

**(ii) Permissible utilisation of CRR:**
The CRR is treated as **capital** and can be used **only for one purpose** — the issue of **fully paid bonus shares** to the members [proviso to Section 55(2)(d) read with Section 63]. It **cannot** be used to write off losses, to pay dividend, or for any other purpose.

**Conclusion:** CRR is funded only from distributable (free) profits and, being a substitute for the redeemed capital, may be applied solely to issue fully paid bonus shares.

*(Full-marks tip: Examiner rewards the capital-maintenance rationale and the precise list of eligible vs ineligible reserves. Common deduction: stating that securities premium can create CRR — it cannot; it is only for the premium on redemption.)*

### Q47. Ch: Buy-back of Securities — Conditions and Sources under Section 68 (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Enumerate the **conditions to be fulfilled and the sources** out of which a company may buy back its own shares under **Section 68 of the Companies Act, 2013**.

**Answer:**
A company may purchase its own shares or other specified securities ("buy-back") under **Section 68 of the Companies Act, 2013**, subject to the following:

**Sources of buy-back [Section 68(1)]:** Buy-back can be made only out of —
(a) its **free reserves**; or
(b) the **securities premium account**; or
(c) the **proceeds of the issue of any shares or other specified securities** (but **not** out of the proceeds of an earlier issue of the **same kind** of shares).

**Conditions [Section 68(2)]:**
1. **Authorisation** — the buy-back must be authorised by the **Articles of Association**.
2. **Special resolution** — a **special resolution** in general meeting is required. (A Board resolution suffices where the buy-back is **10% or less** of the total paid-up equity capital and free reserves.)
3. **25% limit** — the buy-back in any financial year shall **not exceed 25% of the aggregate of paid-up capital and free reserves**; for **equity shares**, buy-back in a year shall not exceed **25% of the total paid-up equity capital**.
4. **Debt-equity ratio** — after buy-back, the ratio of the **aggregate of secured and unsecured debts** to the **paid-up capital and free reserves** shall **not be more than 2:1**.
5. **Fully paid** — the shares/securities bought back must be **fully paid up**.
6. **Cooling-off / completion** — buy-back must be **completed within 1 year** of the special/Board resolution, and no further buy-back within **1 year** from the closure of the preceding one.

**Conclusion:** Buy-back is permitted only from the three specified sources and only if all the quantitative (25% and 2:1) and procedural (authorisation, resolution, fully paid, one-year) conditions are simultaneously satisfied.

### Q50. Ch: Buy-back of Securities — CRR and Prohibitions (Sections 69 & 70) (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the requirement of transferring an amount to the **Capital Redemption Reserve on buy-back** under **Section 69**, and state the circumstances in which a company is **prohibited** from buying back its securities under **Section 70**.

**Answer:**
**Transfer to CRR — Section 69 of the Companies Act, 2013:** Where a company purchases its own shares **out of free reserves or the securities premium account**, a sum **equal to the nominal value of the shares so purchased** shall be transferred to the **Capital Redemption Reserve (CRR) account**, and the particulars must be disclosed in the balance sheet. The object is **capital maintenance** — the paid-up capital extinguished on buy-back is replaced by the CRR so that the capital available to creditors is not reduced. Where the buy-back is out of the **proceeds of a fresh issue**, that portion does **not** require a CRR transfer. The CRR so created can be utilised only for issuing **fully paid bonus shares**.

**Prohibition of buy-back — Section 70:**
1. No company shall buy back its securities through **any subsidiary company** (including its own subsidiaries).
2. No company shall buy back its securities through any **investment company** or group of investment companies.
3. A company shall not buy back its securities if it has **defaulted** in the repayment of deposits, redemption of debentures or preference shares, payment of dividend, or repayment of any term loan/interest to a bank or financial institution — **unless** the default has been **remedied and a period of three years has lapsed** after such default ceased.
4. Buy-back is also prohibited if the company has **not complied** with Sections **92 (annual return), 123 (declaration of dividend), 127 (payment of dividend) and 129 (financial statements)**.

**Conclusion:** Section 69 protects creditors by mandating a CRR equal to the face value bought back out of reserves, while Section 70 bars buy-back by defaulting companies or through subsidiaries/investment companies.

*(Full-marks tip: Examiner rewards the "capital maintenance" rationale and the exact CRR rule — face value bought back out of reserves (not the fresh-issue portion). Common deduction: forgetting the three-year cure period for defaults under Section 70.)*

### Q52. Ch: Bonus Issue — Sources & Restrictions on Capitalisation (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the sources from which a company may issue fully paid-up bonus shares and list the statutory conditions and restrictions attached to a bonus issue under Section 63 of the Companies Act, 2013 read with Rule 14 of the Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules, 2014.

**Answer:**
A bonus issue is the capitalisation of a company's accumulated profits/reserves by issuing fully paid-up shares to existing members without any fresh inflow of cash. It is governed by **Section 63 of the Companies Act, 2013**.

**Permissible sources [Section 63(1)]** — a bonus issue of fully paid-up shares may be made **only** out of:
(a) its **free reserves**;
(b) the **securities premium account**; and
(c) the **capital redemption reserve account**.

**Restriction on source [Section 63(1), proviso]:** No bonus issue shall be made by capitalising reserves created by the **revaluation of assets**, because such reserves are unrealised and notional.

**Conditions [Section 63(2)]:** A company may capitalise its profits/reserves for a bonus issue only if —
1. it is **authorised by its Articles of Association**;
2. it has, on the **recommendation of the Board**, been **authorised in general meeting** of the company (ordinary resolution);
3. it has **not defaulted** in payment of interest or principal in respect of fixed deposits or debt securities issued by it;
4. it has **not defaulted** in payment of statutory dues of employees such as contribution to provident fund, gratuity and bonus;
5. the **partly paid-up shares**, if any, outstanding on the date of allotment are **made fully paid-up**;
6. it complies with such conditions as may be prescribed.

**Further restrictions [Rule 14 / Section 63(3) & (5)]:** The bonus shares shall **not be issued in lieu of dividend**, and a bonus issue once announced by the Board on the recommendation being made to members **cannot be subsequently withdrawn**.

**Conclusion:** A bonus issue must be funded solely out of free reserves, securities premium or capital redemption reserve, can never draw upon a revaluation reserve, and is subject to the safeguards in Section 63(2).

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the explicit "cannot use revaluation reserve" and "partly-paid shares must first be made fully paid" points and the citation of Section 63. Marks are lost for merely listing sources without the four sub-clauses of Section 63(2).)*

### Q56. Ch: Rights Issue — Statutory Requirements (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the manner in which a company having a share capital must make a further issue of shares to its existing equity shareholders and the treatment where a shareholder declines the offer, under Section 62(1)(a) of the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:**
A "rights issue" is the pre-emptive right of existing equity shareholders to subscribe to any further shares issued by the company, so that their proportionate holding is not diluted. It is governed by **Section 62(1)(a) of the Companies Act, 2013**.

Where a company having a share capital proposes to increase its subscribed capital by the issue of further shares, such shares must first be **offered to persons who, on the date of the offer, are holders of equity shares of the company in proportion, as nearly as circumstances admit, to the paid-up share capital on those shares** [Sec 62(1)(a)].

The offer is subject to the following conditions:
1. **Notice of offer [Sec 62(1)(a)(i)]:** The offer must be made by a notice specifying the **number of shares offered** and limiting a time **not less than fifteen days and not exceeding thirty days** from the date of the offer within which the offer, if not accepted, shall be **deemed to have been declined**.
2. **Right of renunciation [Sec 62(1)(a)(ii)]:** Unless the Articles otherwise provide, the offer shall be deemed to include a **right to renounce** the shares offered in favour of any other person; the notice must state this right.
3. **Consequence of decline [Sec 62(1)(a)(iii)]:** After the expiry of the time in the notice, or on earlier receipt of intimation that the offer is declined, the **Board of Directors may dispose of the unsubscribed shares in such manner as is not disadvantageous to the shareholders and the company.**

**Conclusion:** Existing equity shareholders enjoy a statutory pre-emptive right proportionate to their holding; if they neither accept nor renounce within 15–30 days the offer lapses, and the Board may allot the balance in the most beneficial manner.

*(Full-marks tip: The exact "not less than 15 days nor more than 30 days" and the right to renounce are the scoring points. Merely saying "offered to existing shareholders" without the notice period and renunciation/disposal rule earns only half marks.)*

### Q58. Ch: ESOP — Vesting Conditions & Their Accounting (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between the different types of "vesting conditions" that may attach to an Employee Stock Option Plan and state how each is dealt with while measuring and recognising the employee compensation cost, as per the Guidance Note on Accounting for Employee Share-based Payments.

**Answer:**
Under an ESOP, an option "vests" when the employee becomes **unconditionally entitled** to it. A **vesting condition** is a condition that must be satisfied for the employee to become entitled to the shares/options. The Guidance Note classifies vesting conditions as follows:

| Type of vesting condition | Nature | Accounting treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **Service condition** | Requires the employee to complete a specified period of service. | Taken into account by estimating the **number of options expected to vest** and revising the estimate each period; cost spread over the vesting period. |
| **Performance condition — non-market** | A target linked to the entity's own operations (e.g. profit, sales, EPS growth) other than the share price. | Considered in estimating the **number** of options expected to vest; the estimate is trued-up each year until it equals the number that actually vest. |
| **Performance condition — market** | A target linked to the **market price** of the entity's shares (e.g. a target share price / TSR). | Taken into account in estimating the **fair value** of the option **at grant date** itself; it is **NOT** revised subsequently, whether or not the target is met. |

**Key principle:** For service and non-market performance conditions the *quantity* of options is trued-up to actual; the *grant-date fair value per option* is never revised. For a market condition, the effect is already built into the grant-date fair value, so no true-up is made even if the target is missed (provided the service condition is met).

**Conclusion:** Non-market and service conditions affect the *number* of options that vest and are revised each year; a market condition affects only the *grant-date fair value* and is never revised.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner looks for the sharp contrast — "quantity is trued-up (service/non-market)" vs "priced into fair value and never revised (market)". A table comparing the three earns full marks.)*

### Q60. Ch: ESOP — Treatment of Options that Lapse After Vesting (Marks: 5) [Application]

### Q61. Ch: Underwriting of Shares & Debentures — Key Concepts (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the following terms in the context of underwriting of shares: (i) Marked applications, (ii) Unmarked applications, (iii) Firm underwriting, and (iv) the distinction between complete and partial underwriting. State how the benefit of unmarked and firm applications is dealt with while determining an underwriter's liability.

**Answer:**
Underwriting is a contract by which an underwriter, for a commission, undertakes to subscribe to the shares/debentures of a company to the extent they are not taken up by the public, thereby guaranteeing the success of the issue.

**(i) Marked applications:** Applications that bear the **stamp or code of a particular underwriter**, showing that they were procured through that underwriter's efforts. The benefit of a marked application is credited to that **specific** underwriter.

**(ii) Unmarked applications:** Applications received **directly by the company** without any underwriter's stamp (i.e. not traceable to any underwriter). The benefit is distributed among **all** underwriters in the ratio of their **gross liability**.

**(iii) Firm underwriting:** An arrangement whereby an underwriter agrees to **definitely take up a stated number of shares/debentures** irrespective of the public response, in addition to bearing his ordinary underwriting liability. These firm shares are taken up by the underwriter **even if the issue is fully subscribed by the public**.

**(iv) Complete vs partial underwriting:**

| Complete underwriting | Partial underwriting |
|---|---|
| The **whole** of the issue is underwritten (by one or more underwriters). | Only a **part** of the issue is underwritten; the company is treated as having **underwritten the balance itself**. |
| Entire shortfall falls on the underwriters. | The company bears the shortfall attributable to the un-underwritten portion. |

**Treatment:** The benefit of **unmarked** applications is shared among underwriters in the **gross-liability ratio**; **firm underwriting** may be treated **either** as marked applications of the respective underwriter (benefit to that underwriter) **or** as unmarked applications (benefit shared by all), depending on the terms of the agreement.

**Conclusion:** Marked applications reduce the individual underwriter's liability, unmarked applications reduce the collective liability in gross ratio, and firm underwriting is an absolute, additional commitment.

*(Full-marks tip: Do not forget point (iv) and the treatment paragraph — many candidates define only marked/unmarked/firm and lose the marks for the complete/partial distinction and the "firm can be treated as marked or unmarked" nuance.)*

### Q65. Ch: Redemption of Debentures — DRR & Investment Requirements (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the requirements relating to the creation of a Debenture Redemption Reserve (DRR) and the Debenture Redemption Investment/deposit under Section 71(4) of the Companies Act, 2013 read with Rule 18(7) of the Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules, 2014, as amended.

**Answer:**
Where debentures are issued by a company, **Section 71(4) of the Companies Act, 2013** requires that a **Debenture Redemption Reserve (DRR)** be created out of the **profits of the company available for payment of dividend**, and the amount credited to such reserve shall **not be utilised by the company except for the redemption of debentures**. The detailed requirements are prescribed by **Rule 18(7) of the Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules, 2014**, as amended in 2019:

| Class of company | DRR requirement | Debenture Redemption Investment/deposit |
|---|---|---|
| **All-India Financial Institutions & Banking companies** | No DRR | No investment requirement |
| **Listed companies** — NBFCs & HFCs (public issue/private placement) and other listed companies | **No DRR required** | Must **invest/deposit** amounts for debentures maturing (see below) |
| **Unlisted companies** — NBFCs & HFCs | No DRR | Must maintain the investment/deposit |
| **Unlisted companies (other than NBFC/HFC)** | DRR = **10%** of the value of the outstanding debentures | Must maintain the investment/deposit |

**Debenture Redemption Investment [Rule 18(7)(c)]:** Every company required to maintain it shall, on or **before 30th April** of each year, **invest or deposit a sum not less than 15%** of the amount of its debentures **maturing during the year ending on 31st March of the next year** in specified securities/deposits (e.g. deposits with scheduled banks, Central/State Government securities). This amount shall not be used for any purpose other than redemption of debentures maturing during the year, and the balance must not at any time fall below 15% of the debentures maturing in that 31st March.

**Conclusion:** Only unlisted non-NBFC/HFC companies must now create a DRR (10%); all companies required to redeem must additionally keep aside 15% of the debentures maturing during the year in specified investments by 30th April.

*(Full-marks tip: The post-2019 position — "no DRR for listed companies; 10% for unlisted others; plus 15% investment by 30th April" — is the current-law scoring point. Quoting the old 25% DRR loses marks.)*

### Q71. Ch: Investment Accounts — Treatment of Right & Bonus Shares and Cost of Investment (AS 13) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain, with reference to **AS 13 "Accounting for Investments"**, (a) how the **cost of an investment** is determined, and (b) the accounting treatment of **bonus shares**, **right shares subscribed**, and **the sale of rights renounced**, distinguishing between current and long-term investments.

**Answer:**
**(a) Cost of investment (AS 13, para 9–11):** The cost of an investment includes **acquisition charges** such as brokerage, fees and duties. Where an investment is acquired, or partly acquired, by the **issue of shares or other securities**, the acquisition cost is the **fair value of the securities issued**. Where an investment is acquired in **exchange for another asset**, the acquisition cost is determined by reference to the **fair value of the asset given up** or the fair value of the investment acquired, whichever is more clearly evident. When an investment is acquired on a **cum-dividend / cum-interest** basis, the portion of the price attributable to pre-acquisition dividend or accrued interest is **reduced from the cost** and is not treated as income.

**(b) Bonus shares (AS 13, para 13):** When bonus shares are received, **no amount is paid** and hence **no cost is recorded**; only the **number of shares increases**. The result is a reduction in the average cost per share of the entire holding.

**Right shares subscribed:** The amount **actually paid** to subscribe to right shares is **added to the cost** of the investment.

**Sale of rights renounced (AS 13, para 13):** Where rights are not subscribed but **renounced (sold)** in the market:

| Nature of investment | Treatment of proceeds of rights renounced |
|---|---|
| **Long-term investment** | Proceeds are taken to the **Profit & Loss Account** (income); cost of the holding is not disturbed — *except* where the price of the original shares has, after becoming ex-rights, fallen below cost, in which case the proceeds may be applied to reduce the carrying amount. |
| **Current investment** | Proceeds are normally **reduced from the carrying amount** of the investment. |

**Conclusion:** Cost includes acquisition charges and excludes pre-acquisition income; bonus shares carry no cost; subscription money for rights is capitalised; and the treatment of sold rights depends on whether the holding is long-term or current.

*(Full-marks tip: State the exact AS 13 rule for each item and give the long-term vs current distinction for sold rights in a table. Common deduction: writing that bonus shares are recorded at face value.)*

### Q75. Ch: Internal Reconstruction — Internal vs External Reconstruction & Legal Basis (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between **Internal Reconstruction** and **External Reconstruction**, and state the legal provision that governs a reduction of share capital under a scheme of internal reconstruction.

**Answer:**
**Internal reconstruction** is a scheme under which a company, without being wound up and without forming a new company, **re-arranges its capital structure** — typically by reducing/altering share capital, revaluing assets and writing off accumulated losses and fictitious assets — so that its balance sheet reflects a true and fair position. **External reconstruction** is a scheme in which the **existing company is wound up and a new company is formed** to take over its business, the assets and liabilities passing to the new entity (accounted for under the amalgamation framework of AS 14).

| Basis | Internal Reconstruction | External Reconstruction |
|---|---|---|
| Formation of new company | No new company is formed | A new company is formed to take over the business |
| Liquidation | The existing company continues; it is **not** liquidated | The existing (transferor) company is **liquidated** |
| Legal framework | Reduction of capital under **Section 66 of the Companies Act, 2013** (with Tribunal approval) | Amalgamation / absorption governed by **AS 14** and Sections 230–232 |
| Accounting effect | Capital Reduction / Reconstruction A/c; surplus to Capital Reserve | Purchase consideration; goodwill/capital reserve in transferee's books |

**Legal basis:** A reduction of share capital is permitted by **Section 66 of the Companies Act, 2013**, which requires a **special resolution** and **confirmation by the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT)**. The words **"and reduced"** may be required to be added to the company's name if the Tribunal so directs, and creditors' interests must be protected.

**Conclusion:** Internal reconstruction re-organises capital within the surviving company under Section 66, whereas external reconstruction transfers the business to a newly formed company and follows the amalgamation route.

*(Full-marks tip: Cite Section 66 and the requirement of a special resolution plus NCLT confirmation. Common deduction: omitting the statutory reference or confusing external reconstruction with mere amalgamation.)*

### Q77. Ch: Amalgamation of Companies (AS 14) — Merger vs Purchase (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Under **AS 14 "Accounting for Amalgamations"**, distinguish between an **amalgamation in the nature of merger** and an **amalgamation in the nature of purchase**, and state the accounting method applicable to each.

**Answer:**
AS 14 classifies amalgamations into two categories. An **amalgamation in the nature of merger** is one that satisfies **all five conditions** in **para 3(e)**; if any condition is not met, it is an **amalgamation in the nature of purchase** (para 3(f)).

**The five conditions for a merger (para 3(e)):**
(i) **All assets and liabilities** of the transferor become those of the transferee company.
(ii) **Shareholders holding not less than 90%** of the face value of the equity shares of the transferor (other than shares already held) become shareholders of the transferee.
(iii) The consideration is discharged **wholly by the issue of equity shares** of the transferee, except that cash may be paid for fractional shares.
(iv) The **business** of the transferor is **intended to be carried on** by the transferee.
(v) **No adjustment** is intended to the **book values** of the assets and liabilities taken over, except to achieve uniformity of accounting policies.

| Basis | Nature of Merger | Nature of Purchase |
|---|---|---|
| Method of accounting | **Pooling of Interests Method** (paras 33–35) | **Purchase Method** (paras 36–39) |
| Recording of assets/liabilities | At **existing book values** | At **agreed / revalued values** |
| Reserves of transferor | **Carried forward** as they were | **Not** carried forward (except statutory reserves) |
| Difference on amalgamation | Adjusted in **reserves** | Recorded as **Goodwill or Capital Reserve** |

**Conclusion:** If all five para-3(e) conditions are met, the amalgamation is a merger accounted for by the pooling-of-interests method; otherwise it is a purchase accounted for by the purchase method.

*(Full-marks tip: List all five merger conditions verbatim and match each nature to its method. Common deduction: quoting "51%" instead of the correct "90%" threshold, or omitting the fractional-share cash exception.)*

### Q81. Ch: Amalgamation of Companies (AS 14) — Treatment of Reserves, Goodwill & Statutory Reserves (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** In the context of **AS 14**, answer the following with reasons: (a) How are the **reserves** of the transferor company treated under the pooling-of-interests method and under the purchase method? (b) How is **goodwill** arising on amalgamation dealt with subsequently? (c) Why is an **Amalgamation Adjustment Reserve** created, and how is it presented?

**Answer:**
**(a) Treatment of reserves:**

| Item | Pooling-of-Interests (para 33–35) | Purchase Method (para 36–39) |
|---|---|---|
| Free reserves (General Reserve, P&L) | **Carried forward** and shown in the transferee's books as they appeared in the transferor's books | **Not carried forward**; they lose their identity |
| Difference on amalgamation | Adjusted in **reserves** | Recorded as **Goodwill / Capital Reserve** |
| Statutory reserves | Carried forward | Carried forward **only** to comply with law, via Amalgamation Adjustment Reserve |

**(b) Goodwill (AS 14 para 19):** Goodwill arising on amalgamation represents a payment for **anticipated future income** and its value diminishes over time. It should be **amortised to the Profit & Loss Account on a systematic basis over its useful life**, and unless a longer period can be justified, the amortisation period **should not exceed five years**.

**(c) Amalgamation Adjustment Reserve (para 18 & 42):** Where the transferor company is required by **statute** (e.g. an Export Profit Reserve or Investment Allowance Reserve) to maintain certain reserves for a specified period, the identity of such reserves must be preserved even under the purchase method. This is achieved by crediting the statutory reserve and debiting an **Amalgamation Adjustment Reserve**, which is **disclosed as a separate line item** in the balance sheet. When the statutory requirement lapses, both accounts are **reversed**.

**Conclusion:** Free reserves survive only under pooling; goodwill is amortised over not more than five years; and statutory reserves are preserved under purchase accounting through the Amalgamation Adjustment Reserve.

*(Full-marks tip: State the "not more than five years" goodwill rule and the exact purpose of the Amalgamation Adjustment Reserve. Common deduction: saying goodwill is not amortised, or that free reserves survive a purchase amalgamation.)*

### Q87. Ch: Branch Accounts — Independent Branch and Items in Transit (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** What is meant by an "independent branch"? Explain how items-in-transit (goods-in-transit and cash-in-transit) are dealt with while reconciling and incorporating the accounts of an independent branch.

**Answer:** An **independent branch** is a branch that maintains a complete, self-balancing set of books, prepares its own trial balance, trading and profit & loss account and balance sheet, and operates with a large measure of autonomy — it purchases goods from outside parties as well as from Head Office, incurs and pays its own expenses, and maintains its own bank account. The Head Office and the branch each maintain a reciprocal current account: the **Branch Account** in the Head Office books and the **Head Office Account** in the branch books. In theory these two accounts should show equal and opposite balances.

In practice, the two balances frequently disagree at the year-end because of **items-in-transit** — transactions recorded by one office but not yet recorded by the other:

- **Goods-in-transit:** goods despatched by Head Office (and debited to Branch Account) but not yet received and recorded by the branch, or goods returned by the branch not yet received by Head Office. The receiving office has made no entry, so its current account is understated by the value of the goods.
- **Cash-in-transit:** remittances sent by the branch to Head Office (or by Head Office to the branch) recorded by the sender but not yet received and recorded by the receiver.

While **reconciling**, the difference between the two current-account balances is analysed and is generally adjusted in the books of the office that has **not yet recorded** the item. On **incorporation**, the Head Office passes an adjusting entry — e.g., *Goods-in-Transit A/c Dr. / To Branch A/c* — so that the Branch Account balance agrees with the Head Office Account balance in the branch books. Items-in-transit are then shown as a **separate asset (Goods-in-Transit / Cash-in-Transit) in the consolidated Balance Sheet**, ensuring the same asset is neither omitted nor counted twice.

**Conclusion:** Recognising items-in-transit in the books of the office that has not recorded them makes the reciprocal current accounts agree and ensures the assets appear once, and only once, in the final accounts.

*(Full-marks tip: state clearly that the adjustment is made in the books of the office that has NOT recorded the item; illustrate with the *Goods-in-Transit A/c Dr. To Branch A/c* entry to earn the application mark.)*

### Q90. Ch: Departmental Accounts — Distinction from Branch Accounts (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between Departmental Accounts and Branch Accounts.

**Answer:** Both departmental accounting and branch accounting are techniques to ascertain the result of each **segment** of a business, but they differ fundamentally in the location of the segment and the manner of record-keeping.

| Basis | Departmental Accounts | Branch Accounts |
|---|---|---|
| Location | All departments operate under **one roof / one location**. | Branches are **geographically separate** from the Head Office. |
| Books maintained | A **single set of books** is maintained for the whole organisation; departmental columns/analysis are used. | A **separate set of books** may be maintained (independent branch) or the Head Office keeps the branch's accounts (dependent branch). |
| Objective | To find the **profit or loss of each department** and enable inter-departmental comparison. | To find the **profit or loss of each branch** and to control branch operations from the Head Office. |
| Allocation of common expenses | Common expenses are **apportioned** among departments on suitable bases. | Head Office expenses are usually **charged to the branch** as a whole, not apportioned within it. |
| Reconciliation | No question of reconciling current accounts (single entity, one ledger). | For an independent branch, the **Branch Account and Head Office Account** must be reconciled and items-in-transit adjusted. |
| Inter-segment dealings | **Inter-departmental transfers** at cost or cost-plus require a provision for unrealised profit. | **Inter-branch / branch–H.O. transfers** of goods at invoice price require a stock reserve on unsold stock. |

**Conclusion:** Departmental accounts analyse the results of divisions within a single location using one ledger, whereas branch accounts deal with geographically separate units that may keep their own books and whose current accounts must be reconciled with the Head Office.

*(Full-marks tip: a clean tabular contrast on at least four bases earns full marks; mentioning the parallel adjustments — unrealised profit (departments) versus stock reserve (branches) — shows conceptual depth.)*

### Q93. Ch: Consolidated Financial Statements — Pre-acquisition vs Post-acquisition Profits (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the distinction between pre-acquisition (capital) profits and post-acquisition (revenue) profits of a subsidiary, and describe how each is treated while preparing the Consolidated Balance Sheet under AS 21.

**Answer:** When a holding company acquires shares in a subsidiary, the reserves and surplus of the subsidiary existing at the **date of acquisition** must be separated from those earned **after** acquisition, because the two have entirely different accounting treatments under **AS 21 "Consolidated Financial Statements."**

**Pre-acquisition profits (capital profits)** are the reserves and undistributed profits of the subsidiary that already existed on the date the parent acquired control. From the parent's point of view these are **capital in nature** — the parent effectively paid for them through the cost of investment. The parent's share of pre-acquisition profits is therefore **set off against the cost of investment** in computing the Cost of Control: if the cost of investment exceeds the parent's share of (paid-up capital + capital profits), the excess is **Goodwill**; if it is less, the difference is a **Capital Reserve**. The pre-acquisition profits do **not** appear as free reserves in the Consolidated Balance Sheet.

**Post-acquisition profits (revenue profits)** are the profits earned by the subsidiary **after** the date of acquisition. The parent's share of these is **revenue in nature** and is **added to the consolidated Reserves / Profit & Loss balance**, as it represents genuine earnings of the group during the period of control.

**Minority Interest** is computed on the subsidiary's **total** net assets (share capital + all reserves and surplus, pre- and post-acquisition, plus any revaluation surplus) as on the Balance Sheet date, since the minority shareholders own their proportion of the whole.

| Item | Nature | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Parent's share of pre-acquisition profit | Capital | Adjusted against cost of investment → Goodwill / Capital Reserve |
| Parent's share of post-acquisition profit | Revenue | Added to consolidated reserves / P&L |
| Minority's share of all profits | — | Included in Minority Interest |

Where acquisition takes place **during the year**, the current-year profit is split on a **time basis** (unless otherwise indicated) between the pre- and post-acquisition periods.

**Conclusion:** Correctly classifying profits as capital or revenue is the crux of consolidation — it determines goodwill/capital reserve, the free reserves carried forward, and the minority interest.

*(Full-marks tip: link pre-acquisition profit to the Cost of Control computation and post-acquisition profit to consolidated reserves; mentioning the time-apportionment rule for a mid-year acquisition secures the application mark.)*

### Q95. Ch: Accounting for LLP — LLP versus Traditional Partnership Firm (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between a Limited Liability Partnership (LLP) and a traditional partnership firm.

**Answer:** A **Limited Liability Partnership** is a body corporate formed and incorporated under the **Limited Liability Partnership Act, 2008**, combining the flexibility of a partnership with the benefit of limited liability, whereas a **traditional partnership firm** is governed by the **Indian Partnership Act, 1932**.

| Basis | Limited Liability Partnership (LLP) | Traditional Partnership Firm |
|---|---|---|
| Governing law | LLP Act, 2008 | Indian Partnership Act, 1932 |
| Legal status | **Separate legal entity / body corporate**, distinct from its partners | **No separate legal entity**; firm and partners are the same in law |
| Liability of partners | **Limited** to agreed contribution (except for own fraud/wrongful acts) | **Unlimited**, joint and several |
| Perpetual succession | Has perpetual succession; unaffected by change/death of partners | No perpetual succession; affected by admission, retirement or death |
| Maximum number of partners | **No maximum limit** | Limited to 50 (per Companies Act rules) |
| Registration | **Compulsory** (registered with the Registrar of LLPs) | Optional (though an unregistered firm suffers disabilities) |
| Ownership of property | Property held in the **name of the LLP** | Property held in the names of the partners |

**Conclusion:** An LLP is a hybrid corporate form offering limited liability and a separate legal personality with perpetual succession, features not available to a traditional partnership firm.

*(Full-marks tip: emphasise the two decisive distinctions — separate legal entity and limited liability — and cite the two governing statutes to anchor the answer.)*

### Q98. Ch: Company Financial Statements — Classification of Current vs Non-current under Schedule III (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** State the criteria laid down in the General Instructions to Schedule III, Division I, for classifying an asset as a "current asset" and a liability as a "current liability." Also explain the term "operating cycle."

**Answer:** Schedule III, Division I to the Companies Act, 2013 requires every asset and liability in the Balance Sheet to be classified as **current** or **non-current** on the basis of the following tests.

**An asset is classified as a current asset when it satisfies any one of the following:**
(a) it is expected to be **realised in, or is intended for sale or consumption in, the company's normal operating cycle**;
(b) it is held **primarily for the purpose of being traded**;
(c) it is expected to be **realised within twelve months** after the reporting date; or
(d) it is **cash or a cash equivalent**, unless it is restricted from being exchanged or used to settle a liability for at least twelve months after the reporting date.
All other assets are classified as **non-current**.

**A liability is classified as a current liability when it satisfies any one of the following:**
(a) it is expected to be **settled in the company's normal operating cycle**;
(b) it is held **primarily for the purpose of being traded**;
(c) it is **due to be settled within twelve months** after the reporting date; or
(d) the company **does not have an unconditional right to defer settlement** of the liability for at least twelve months after the reporting date.
All other liabilities are classified as **non-current**.

**Operating cycle:** An operating cycle is the **time between the acquisition of assets for processing and their realisation in cash or cash equivalents**. Where the normal operating cycle **cannot be identified, it is assumed to have a duration of twelve months.**

**Conclusion:** The twin yardsticks are the **normal operating cycle** and the **twelve-month period** from the reporting date; an item qualifying under either test as current must be shown as current, otherwise it is non-current.

*(Full-marks tip: list all four sub-clauses for each of assets and liabilities and define the operating cycle with the twelve-month default. Omitting the "unconditional right to defer settlement" test for liabilities is a frequent lapse.)*

### Q99. Ch: Consolidated Financial Statements — Dividend out of Pre-acquisition Profits (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** H Ltd. acquired 80% of the equity shares of S Ltd. on 1st April, 2025 for ₹3,00,000. In August 2025, S Ltd. declared and paid a dividend of ₹50,000 wholly out of profits that existed on the date of acquisition. H Ltd. received its share and credited the whole amount to its Profit & Loss Account. Explain the correct accounting treatment, pass the rectifying entry in H Ltd.'s books, and state the effect on the Cost of Control.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — H Ltd.'s share of the dividend** = 80% × ₹50,000 = **₹40,000**.

**WN-2 — Nature of the dividend.** The dividend has been paid **out of pre-acquisition (capital) profits** of S Ltd. From H Ltd.'s standpoint this is a **return of a part of the purchase consideration**, not income earned after acquisition. It is therefore a **capital receipt** and must be **credited to the Investment Account (reducing its cost)**, not to the Profit & Loss Account. Crediting it to P&L wrongly inflates H Ltd.'s revenue profit.

**WN-3 — Rectifying entry in the books of H Ltd.**

| Particulars | Dr. (₹) | Cr. (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| Profit & Loss A/c … Dr. | 40,000 | |
| &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;To Investment in S Ltd. A/c | | 40,000 |
| *(Dividend of ₹40,000 received out of pre-acquisition profits, wrongly credited to P&L, now transferred to reduce the cost of investment.)* | | |

**WN-4 — Effect on Cost of Control.** After the correction, the cost of investment falls from ₹3,00,000 to **₹2,60,000**, which correspondingly **reduces the goodwill (or increases the capital reserve)** computed on consolidation by ₹40,000. Simultaneously, H Ltd.'s consolidated Profit & Loss balance is reduced by ₹40,000, so the group result is stated correctly.

**Answer:** The ₹40,000 dividend is a **capital receipt**; it must be **credited to the Investment Account**, reducing the cost of investment to **₹2,60,000** and thereby reducing goodwill by ₹40,000. Only dividends out of **post-acquisition** profits may be credited to Profit & Loss.

*(Full-marks tip: the deciding factor is the SOURCE of the dividend — pre-acquisition profit is capital (reduce investment), post-acquisition profit is revenue (credit P&L). State both the rectifying entry and the goodwill impact to earn full marks.)*

### Q100. Ch: Branch Accounts — Stock Reserve on Branch Stock at Invoice Price (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Nova Ltd. invoices goods to its branch at cost plus 33⅓%. The branch stock (at invoice price) was ₹1,20,000 on 1st April, 2025 and ₹1,80,000 on 31st March, 2026. Compute the branch stock reserve required at the beginning and at the end of the year, state the amount to be charged/credited in respect of the reserve, and pass the journal entries in the books of the Head Office.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Rate of loading.** Goods invoiced at cost + 33⅓%. If cost = 100, invoice = 133⅓; loading = (33⅓ / 133⅓) = **25% of invoice price**.

**WN-2 — Stock reserve at the beginning and end.**
- Opening stock reserve = 25% × 1,20,000 = **₹30,000**.
- Closing stock reserve = 25% × 1,80,000 = **₹45,000**.
- Net additional reserve to be created (charged to Profit & Loss) = 45,000 − 30,000 = **₹15,000**.

**WN-3 — Journal Entries in the books of Head Office**

| Particulars | Dr. (₹) | Cr. (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| (1) Stock Reserve A/c … Dr. | 30,000 | |
| &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;To Profit & Loss A/c | | 30,000 |
| *(Opening stock reserve on branch stock written back.)* | | |
| (2) Profit & Loss A/c … Dr. | 45,000 | |
| &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;To Stock Reserve A/c | | 45,000 |
| *(Closing stock reserve on branch stock at invoice price created.)* | | |

**OR (combined net entry):**

| Particulars | Dr. (₹) | Cr. (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| Profit & Loss A/c … Dr. | 15,000 | |
| &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;To Stock Reserve A/c | | 15,000 |
| *(Net increase in stock reserve, ₹45,000 − ₹30,000.)* | | |

**Answer:** Stock reserve required — **₹30,000 (opening)** and **₹45,000 (closing)**; net amount **₹15,000 charged to Profit & Loss Account.** In the Balance Sheet, the closing stock reserve of ₹45,000 is **deducted from branch stock**, reducing it to cost (₹1,35,000).

*(Full-marks tip: convert the "cost + 33⅓%" mark-up to 25% of invoice before applying it to the invoice-priced stock; the reserve is provided only on the profit element of UNSOLD stock and is deducted from stock in the Balance Sheet. Charging 33⅓% on invoice price is the standard error.)*
