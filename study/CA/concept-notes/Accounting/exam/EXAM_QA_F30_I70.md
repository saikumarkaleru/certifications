### Q1. Ch: Theoretical Framework — Accounting Concepts and Conventions (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** "Accounting is based on certain fundamental accounting assumptions." Explain the three fundamental accounting assumptions recognised by AS 1 "Disclosure of Accounting Policies". State the consequence, as per AS 1, when any one of these assumptions is not followed.

**Answer:**
As per **Accounting Standard (AS) 1 "Disclosure of Accounting Policies"** issued by the Institute of Chartered Accountants of India, certain fundamental accounting assumptions underlie the preparation and presentation of financial statements. They are usually not specifically stated because their acceptance and use are assumed; however, disclosure is necessary if they are **not** followed. AS 1 recognises three fundamental accounting assumptions:

**1. Going Concern.** It is assumed that the enterprise is a going concern and will continue its operations for the foreseeable future. The enterprise has neither the intention nor the necessity of liquidation or of curtailing materially the scale of its operations. It is because of this assumption that assets are recorded at cost and depreciated over their useful life rather than at their break-up (realisable) value.

**2. Consistency.** It is assumed that accounting policies are consistent from one period to another. This enables meaningful comparison of the financial statements of an enterprise over different accounting periods. For example, the method of depreciation once adopted should be applied consistently year after year.

**3. Accrual.** Under the accrual assumption, revenues and costs are recognised as they are earned or incurred (and not as money is received or paid) and are recorded in the financial statements of the periods to which they relate. Thus, outstanding expenses, prepaid expenses, accrued income and income received in advance are all accounted for.

**Consequence of non-observance:** AS 1 provides that if the fundamental accounting assumptions — Going Concern, Consistency and Accrual — are followed in the financial statements, **specific disclosure is not required.** However, **if any of these assumptions is not followed, the fact should be specifically disclosed** along with, wherever possible, the reasons for such departure.

**Conclusion:** The three fundamental accounting assumptions under AS 1 are Going Concern, Consistency and Accrual; their non-observance must be specifically disclosed in the financial statements.

*(Full-marks tip: Examiner rewards naming AS 1 and the exact disclosure rule; students lose marks for confusing these three "assumptions" with the wider list of accounting concepts/conventions.)*

### Q2. Ch: Theoretical Framework — Accounting Concepts (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the following accounting concepts in brief: (i) Money Measurement Concept; (ii) Business Entity Concept; (iii) Dual Aspect Concept; (iv) Matching Concept.

**Answer:**
The following are fundamental accounting concepts that govern the recording of transactions:

**(i) Money Measurement Concept.** According to this concept, only those transactions and events which can be expressed in terms of money are recorded in the books of account. Facts which cannot be measured in monetary terms — such as the skill of the workforce, the honesty of the management or good employee morale — however important, are not recorded, since accounting is a language of money.

**(ii) Business Entity Concept.** For accounting purposes, the business is treated as a separate and distinct entity from its owner(s). All transactions are recorded from the point of view of the business and not the proprietor. Consequently, capital introduced by the proprietor is a liability of the business (owed to the owner), and drawings are treated as a reduction of that capital.

**(iii) Dual Aspect Concept.** Every business transaction has two aspects — a debit and a credit of equal amount. This gives rise to the fundamental accounting equation: **Assets = Liabilities + Capital.** It is the basis of the double-entry system of book-keeping, under which every debit has an equal and corresponding credit.

**(iv) Matching Concept.** The costs (expenses) of an accounting period should be matched against the revenues of the same period to determine the correct profit or loss. It is on account of this concept that adjustments for outstanding expenses, prepaid expenses, closing stock, depreciation, etc., are made.

**Conclusion:** These concepts together ensure that financial statements present a true and fair view based on objective, comparable and consistent records.

*(Full-marks tip: Give one crisp application/example for each concept — the examiner allots part marks for the illustration, not just the definition.)*

### Q3. Ch: Theoretical Framework — Capital vs Revenue Expenditure (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between Capital Expenditure and Revenue Expenditure. State any four points of distinction and give one example of each.

**Answer:**
The classification of expenditure into capital and revenue is fundamental because it determines whether an item is shown in the **Balance Sheet** (asset) or in the **Statement of Profit and Loss** (expense). A wrong classification distorts both the profit and the financial position.

**Capital Expenditure** is expenditure incurred for acquiring or bringing into existence a fixed asset, or for extending/improving a fixed asset so as to increase its earning capacity or reduce its cost of operation. Its benefit extends over more than one accounting period.

**Revenue Expenditure** is expenditure incurred for the day-to-day running of the business or for maintaining the earning capacity of fixed assets. Its benefit is exhausted within the accounting period in which it is incurred.

| Basis | Capital Expenditure | Revenue Expenditure |
|---|---|---|
| **Purpose** | To acquire/improve a fixed asset or increase earning capacity | To run the business and maintain existing earning capacity |
| **Period of benefit** | Benefit extends over several accounting periods | Benefit is consumed within the current period |
| **Treatment in accounts** | Shown as an asset in the Balance Sheet (capitalised) | Charged to the Statement of Profit and Loss |
| **Recurring nature** | Generally non-recurring | Generally recurring |
| **Example** | Purchase of machinery; cost of installation | Wages, rent, repairs, cost of raw material |

**Conclusion:** Capital expenditure creates or enhances an asset and appears in the Balance Sheet, whereas revenue expenditure maintains operations and is written off to the Statement of Profit and Loss; correct classification is essential to arrive at true profit and a true and fair financial position.

*(Full-marks tip: A four-column table plus one example each scores full; students lose marks by giving only definitions without the "period of benefit" and "treatment" points.)*

### Q4. Ch: Theoretical Framework — Capital, Revenue and Deferred Revenue Items (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** State, with reasons, whether each of the following is a Capital Expenditure, Revenue Expenditure, Deferred Revenue Expenditure, or Capital Receipt:
(i) ₹50,000 spent on installation of a newly purchased machine.
(ii) ₹8,000 paid for annual repairs of the machine.
(iii) ₹1,20,000 spent on a heavy advertisement campaign for launching a new product, the benefit of which is expected to last for three years.
(iv) ₹2,00,000 received on issue of equity shares.
(v) ₹15,000 spent on white-washing of the factory building.
(vi) ₹40,000 spent on replacing a worn-out part of a machine (no increase in capacity).

**Answer:**
The classification of each item, with reasons, is given below:

**(i) Installation of newly purchased machine — ₹50,000: Capital Expenditure.** Expenditure incurred to bring a fixed asset to its working condition and location forms part of the cost of the asset. It is added to the cost of the machine and capitalised.

**(ii) Annual repairs of machine — ₹8,000: Revenue Expenditure.** It is incurred to maintain the existing earning capacity of the asset and its benefit is exhausted within the year. It is charged to the Statement of Profit and Loss.

**(iii) Heavy advertisement for new product (benefit ≈ 3 years) — ₹1,20,000: Deferred Revenue Expenditure.** Although revenue in nature, the benefit extends beyond the current period. Such expenditure is written off over the periods expected to benefit (₹40,000 per year for three years), and the unwritten-off balance is shown on the assets side.

**(iv) Issue of equity shares — ₹2,00,000: Capital Receipt.** It represents the owner's contribution to capital, not income; it is shown as Share Capital (a liability) in the Balance Sheet.

**(v) White-washing of factory building — ₹15,000: Revenue Expenditure.** It is a routine maintenance cost that does not increase the earning capacity of the building; charged to the Statement of Profit and Loss.

**(vi) Replacement of a worn-out part (no increase in capacity) — ₹40,000: Revenue Expenditure.** Since the replacement merely restores the machine to its original condition without enhancing its capacity, it is revenue in nature.

**Conclusion:** Correct identification of the nature of each item ensures that profit is not overstated or understated and that the Balance Sheet reflects a true and fair position.

*(Full-marks tip: Every mark hinges on the REASON, not the label — always justify using "increases earning capacity", "benefit period" or "owner's contribution".)*

### Q9. Ch: Accounting Process — Distinguish Trade Discount and Cash Discount (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between Trade Discount and Cash Discount. State any four points of difference.

**Answer:**
Both trade discount and cash discount are reductions allowed by a seller to a buyer, but they differ fundamentally in purpose and in accounting treatment.

**Trade Discount** is a reduction allowed by the seller to the buyer on the list price (catalogue price) of goods, usually to promote bulk buying. It is deducted before recording the transaction and is therefore **never** shown separately in the books of account.

**Cash Discount** is a reduction allowed by the seller to the buyer to encourage prompt payment within a stipulated period. It is recorded in the books — as Discount Allowed (an expense) by the seller and Discount Received (an income) by the buyer.

| Basis | Trade Discount | Cash Discount |
|---|---|---|
| **Purpose** | To encourage bulk/quantity buying | To encourage prompt/early payment |
| **Basis of allowance** | Allowed on the list price of goods | Allowed on the amount due for prompt payment |
| **Recording in books** | Not recorded separately; deducted from invoice | Recorded separately in the books of account |
| **When allowed** | At the time of purchase/sale | At the time of payment/receipt |

**Conclusion:** Trade discount is an off-invoice deduction that never enters the ledger, whereas cash discount is a payment-linked concession that is separately recorded as an expense or income.

*(Full-marks tip: The "not recorded vs recorded" contrast is the key markable point; a neat four-row table scores full.)*

### Q10. Ch: Bank Reconciliation Statement — Nature and Causes (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** What is a Bank Reconciliation Statement? State any four reasons that cause a difference between the balance shown by the Cash Book (bank column) and the balance shown by the Pass Book.

**Answer:**
A **Bank Reconciliation Statement (BRS)** is a statement prepared periodically (usually at the end of a month) to reconcile the balance shown by the bank column of the **Cash Book** with the balance shown by the **Pass Book (bank statement)**, by identifying and listing the items of difference between the two. It is not an account; it is a statement prepared to explain why the two balances differ and to arrive at one from the other.

**Reasons for the difference:**

**1. Cheques issued but not yet presented for payment.** When a cheque is issued, the Cash Book is credited immediately, but the bank balance falls only when the cheque is presented and paid. Till then, the Pass Book shows a higher balance.

**2. Cheques deposited but not yet collected/credited.** The Cash Book is debited on deposit, but the bank credits the account only on realisation. Till collection, the Pass Book shows a lower balance.

**3. Bank charges, interest and commission debited by the bank.** The bank debits these directly in the Pass Book, but they are recorded in the Cash Book only when the entry is intimated to the customer.

**4. Interest allowed / amounts collected by the bank directly** (e.g., interest on deposits, dividends or a customer's cheque collected on standing instructions) are credited in the Pass Book but not yet entered in the Cash Book.

Other causes include direct payments made by the bank on standing instructions and errors committed in either book.

**Conclusion:** A BRS reconciles the Cash Book and Pass Book balances by adjusting for timing differences, direct bank entries and errors, thereby verifying the accuracy of both records.

*(Full-marks tip: Define BRS as a "statement, not an account", then give the four timing/entry causes; naming which book is affected earns the application marks.)*

### Q13. Ch: Rectification of Errors — Types of Errors (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the different types of errors that may occur in the accounting process. Also state which of these errors affect the agreement of the Trial Balance.

**Answer:**
Errors in accounting may occur at the stage of recording, posting, casting or carrying forward. They are broadly classified as follows:

**1. Errors of Omission.** Failure to record a transaction in the books. If a transaction is completely omitted (not recorded at all), it is a **complete omission** and does **not** affect the Trial Balance. If it is recorded in one book but its posting to one account is omitted, it is a **partial omission**, which **does** affect the Trial Balance.

**2. Errors of Commission.** Errors arising from wrong recording, wrong posting, wrong casting (totalling) or wrong carry-forward — e.g., posting a wrong amount, or posting to the correct side but wrong amount. Many errors of commission (such as wrong casting or posting to the wrong side/amount) **affect** the Trial Balance, though posting the right amount to the wrong account of the same class does not.

**3. Errors of Principle.** Errors arising from a failure to distinguish between capital and revenue items — e.g., treating the purchase of machinery as a purchase of goods. Since the debit and credit remain equal, such errors do **not** affect the Trial Balance.

**4. Compensating Errors.** Two or more errors that cancel out each other's effect, so that the Trial Balance still agrees. These do **not** affect the agreement of the Trial Balance.

| Type of Error | Affects Trial Balance? |
|---|---|
| Complete omission | No |
| Partial omission | Yes |
| Error of principle | No |
| Compensating error | No |
| Wrong casting / one-sided posting error | Yes |

**Conclusion:** Errors of principle, complete omissions and compensating errors do not disturb the agreement of the Trial Balance, whereas one-sided errors (partial omission, wrong casting, single posting errors) do; a Suspense Account is opened for the latter.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the clear "affects / does not affect" mapping — a two-sided error keeps the TB balanced, a one-sided error breaks it.)*

### Q16. Ch: Depreciation Accounting — SLM vs WDV Methods (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between the Straight Line Method (SLM) and the Written Down Value (WDV) method of charging depreciation. State the accounting treatment prescribed by AS 10 (Property, Plant and Equipment) when an enterprise changes its method of depreciation.

**Answer:**
Depreciation is the systematic allocation of the depreciable amount of an asset over its useful life, as defined under **AS 10 "Property, Plant and Equipment"**. The two most common methods of allocating this depreciable amount are the Straight Line Method (SLM) and the Written Down Value (WDV) method, and they differ fundamentally in the pattern in which cost is written off.

Under the **Straight Line Method**, a fixed and equal amount of depreciation is charged every year over the useful life of the asset. It is computed as (Cost − Residual Value) ÷ Useful Life, so the annual charge remains constant and the book value reduces to the residual value at the end of the useful life. Under the **Written Down Value Method** (also called the Reducing Balance / Diminishing Balance Method), depreciation is charged at a fixed percentage on the opening book value (written down value) of the asset each year. As the book value falls year after year, the amount of depreciation also falls, giving a higher charge in the earlier years and a lower charge in the later years.

| Basis | Straight Line Method (SLM) | Written Down Value Method (WDV) |
|---|---|---|
| Basis of charge | Fixed % on **original cost** | Fixed % on **written down value** |
| Amount of depreciation | Equal every year | Higher in early years, reducing later |
| Book value at end | Becomes **nil / residual value** | Never becomes exactly nil |
| Suitability | Assets with uniform utility (e.g., buildings) | Assets losing value faster + higher repairs later (e.g., machinery) |
| Total burden (Depn + Repairs) | Rises over life (repairs increase) | Fairly even over life |

As per **AS 10**, the method of depreciation is to be **reviewed at least at each financial year end**, and if there is a significant change in the expected pattern of consumption of the future economic benefits, the method shall be changed to reflect the changed pattern. Such a change is treated as a **change in accounting estimate** (not a change in accounting policy), and is therefore applied **prospectively** — the carrying amount as on the date of change is depreciated over the remaining useful life using the new method. No retrospective recomputation of past depreciation is done.

**Conclusion:** SLM spreads cost evenly while WDV front-loads it; a change of method is a change in estimate under AS 10 and is accounted for prospectively.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the AS 10 "change in estimate → prospective" point; students lose marks by wrongly calling a method change a change in accounting policy applied retrospectively.)*

### Q19. Ch: Inventory Valuation — Cost vs Net Realisable Value (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the principle of valuation of inventories under AS 2 (Valuation of Inventories). Explain the meaning of "Net Realisable Value" and briefly state which costs are **excluded** from the cost of inventories.

**Answer:**
As per **AS 2 "Valuation of Inventories"**, inventories should be valued at the **lower of cost and net realisable value (NRV)**. This is a direct application of the prudence concept, ensuring that anticipated losses are recognised but anticipated profits are not. The comparison of cost and NRV should ordinarily be made on an **item-by-item** basis, not on the total of all inventories.

The **cost of inventories** comprises all costs of purchase (purchase price, duties and taxes not subsequently recoverable, freight inwards and other directly attributable acquisition costs, net of trade discounts and rebates), costs of conversion (direct labour and a systematic allocation of fixed and variable production overheads), and other costs incurred in bringing the inventories to their present location and condition.

**Net Realisable Value (NRV)** is defined as the **estimated selling price in the ordinary course of business less the estimated costs of completion and the estimated costs necessary to make the sale.** In other words, it is the net amount the enterprise expects to realise from the sale of inventory. Where the cost exceeds this NRV (for example, due to a fall in selling prices, obsolescence, or damage), the inventory is written down to NRV.

The following costs are **excluded** from the cost of inventories and are recognised as expenses of the period in which they are incurred:
- Abnormal amounts of wasted materials, labour or other production costs;
- Storage costs, unless those costs are necessary in the production process prior to a further stage of production;
- Administrative overheads that do not contribute to bringing the inventories to their present location and condition;
- Selling and distribution costs.

**Conclusion:** Inventory is valued at the lower of cost and NRV on an item-by-item basis; abnormal wastage, most storage, general administrative and selling/distribution costs are excluded from cost.

*(Full-marks tip: State "lower of cost and NRV", the exact NRV definition, and give the four excluded costs — omitting the excluded-costs list is the most common deduction.)*

### Q21. Ch: Bills of Exchange — Theory (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the meaning of a "Bill of Exchange". Distinguish between (a) a Trade bill and an Accommodation bill, and (b) explain what is meant by "noting charges" and "days of grace".

**Answer:**
A **Bill of Exchange**, as defined in **Section 5 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881**, is "an instrument in writing containing an unconditional order, signed by the maker, directing a certain person to pay a certain sum of money only to, or to the order of, a certain person or to the bearer of the instrument." It involves three parties — the **drawer** (who makes the order, usually the seller/creditor), the **drawee/acceptor** (who is ordered to pay, usually the buyer/debtor), and the **payee** (who receives payment).

**(a) Trade bill vs Accommodation bill**

| Basis | Trade Bill | Accommodation Bill |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Drawn to settle a **genuine trade transaction** (sale/purchase of goods) | Drawn to **raise finance / mutual accommodation**, without any trade behind it |
| Consideration | Backed by consideration (goods sold) | No trade consideration; a financial arrangement |
| Nature | Represents a real debt | Represents temporary borrowing between parties |

**(b) Noting charges** are the fees charged by a **Notary Public** for recording (noting) the fact of dishonour of a bill when it is not paid on the due date. When a bill is dishonoured, it may be formally "noted" to obtain authentic evidence of dishonour, and these noting charges are ultimately borne by the party responsible for the dishonour (i.e., the acceptor), even though initially paid by the holder.

**Days of grace** are the **three extra days** that are, by custom and law, added to the nominal due date of a time bill (a bill payable after a certain period) to arrive at the legal date of maturity. Thus, a bill dated 1 June payable "3 months after date" legally matures on 4 September (1 September + 3 days of grace). Bills payable "on demand" or "at sight" are not entitled to days of grace.

**Conclusion:** A bill of exchange is an unconditional written order to pay; trade bills rest on genuine trade while accommodation bills merely raise finance, and noting charges (on dishonour) and 3 days of grace (on maturity) are standard incidents of a time bill.

*(Full-marks tip: Quote Section 5 of the NI Act, name the three parties, and correctly state that days of grace = 3 and apply only to time bills — students often wrongly add grace days to demand bills.)*

### Q24. Ch: Not-for-Profit Organisations — Receipts & Payments vs Income & Expenditure (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between a **Receipts and Payments Account** and an **Income and Expenditure Account** prepared by a not-for-profit organisation.

**Answer:**
Not-for-profit organisations (such as clubs, societies, hospitals and charitable trusts) do not prepare a Trading and Profit & Loss Account because their objective is not profit. Instead, they prepare a **Receipts and Payments Account** and an **Income and Expenditure Account**, which serve very different purposes.

A **Receipts and Payments Account** is a **summary of the cash book** for the year. It is a real account that records all cash and bank receipts on the debit side and all cash and bank payments on the credit side, irrespective of whether they relate to the current, previous or next period, and irrespective of whether they are of a capital or revenue nature. It begins with the opening cash/bank balance and ends with the closing cash/bank balance.

An **Income and Expenditure Account** is a **nominal account** akin to a Profit & Loss Account. It records only **revenue items relating to the current year** on an accrual basis, excluding capital receipts and payments. Its balance is described as a **surplus (excess of income over expenditure)** or a **deficit (excess of expenditure over income)**, which is transferred to the Capital/General Fund.

| Basis | Receipts & Payments A/c | Income & Expenditure A/c |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Real account (cash summary) | Nominal account |
| Basis | Cash basis | Accrual basis |
| Items included | Capital + revenue, all years | Only revenue, current year |
| Opening/closing balance | Opening & closing cash/bank | No opening balance; result = surplus/deficit |
| Depreciation / outstanding | Not recorded | Recorded |

**Conclusion:** The Receipts & Payments Account shows how cash moved during the year, whereas the Income & Expenditure Account matches current-year revenue income and expenditure to arrive at the surplus or deficit.

*(Full-marks tip: Stress "cash basis, all items, all years" for R&P versus "accrual, only current-year revenue items" for I&E, and note depreciation/outstandings appear only in I&E — the classic distinguishing points.)*

### Q30. Ch: Company Accounts (Introduction) — Nature and Share Capital (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the various categories of share capital of a company — Authorised, Issued, Subscribed, Called-up and Paid-up capital — with reference to the Companies Act, 2013. Also state what is meant by "Calls-in-Arrears" and "Calls-in-Advance".

**Answer:**
The capital of a company limited by shares is described in several ways depending on the stage of raising and receiving money, as recognised under **Section 2 of the Companies Act, 2013** and disclosed under **Schedule III**.

**Authorised (Nominal / Registered) Capital** [Sec 2(8)] is the maximum amount of share capital that a company is authorised to issue by its **Memorandum of Association**. A company cannot issue shares beyond this amount without first altering the Memorandum.

**Issued Capital** [Sec 2(50)] is that part of the authorised capital which the company **actually offers** to the public or others for subscription. It may be equal to or less than the authorised capital.

**Subscribed Capital** [Sec 2(86)] is that part of the issued capital which has been **taken up (subscribed) by the shareholders**. Where the shares are fully subscribed, subscribed capital equals issued capital.

**Called-up Capital** [Sec 2(15)] is that part of the subscribed capital which the company has **called upon the shareholders to pay**. A company may not call the entire face value at once.

**Paid-up Capital** [Sec 2(64)] is the amount of the called-up capital that has **actually been paid** by the shareholders. Paid-up capital = Called-up capital − Calls-in-Arrears.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Authorised | Maximum capital permitted by the Memorandum |
| Issued | Portion offered for subscription |
| Subscribed | Portion taken up by shareholders |
| Called-up | Portion the company has demanded |
| Paid-up | Portion actually received |

**Calls-in-Arrears** is the amount called up by the company but **not yet paid** by some shareholders; it is shown as a **deduction from called-up capital** in the Balance Sheet, and interest may be charged on it (up to 10% p.a. as per the company's articles / Table F).

**Calls-in-Advance** [Sec 50] is the amount **paid by a shareholder in excess of the amount called up** — i.e., money received before the call is actually made. It is shown as a separate liability, and the company may pay interest on it (up to 12% p.a. as per Table F).

**Conclusion:** Share capital narrows down from authorised to paid-up as money is progressively called and received; calls-in-arrears (unpaid) reduce paid-up capital, while calls-in-advance (over-paid) form a separate liability.

*(Full-marks tip: Cite the Section 2 sub-clauses and the exact chain Authorised → Issued → Subscribed → Called-up → Paid-up; note the Table F interest rates — 10% on arrears and 12% on advance — for the extra edge.)*

### Q31. Ch: AS 1 Disclosure of Accounting Policies — Fundamental Assumptions & Selection of Policies (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** "Accounting policies are not rigid; they are selected on the basis of certain considerations and, once selected, their disclosure is essential." In the light of AS 1, (i) name the three fundamental accounting assumptions, (ii) state the major considerations governing the selection of an accounting policy, and (iii) explain how a change in an accounting policy is to be disclosed.

**Answer:**
Accounting Standard (AS) 1 "Disclosure of Accounting Policies" deals with the disclosure of significant accounting policies followed in preparing and presenting the financial statements, so that a meaningful comparison of statements — both inter-period and inter-firm — becomes possible.

**(i) Fundamental accounting assumptions.** AS 1 recognises three fundamental accounting assumptions which underlie the preparation of financial statements: (a) **Going Concern** — the enterprise is normally viewed as a continuing entity, with neither the intention nor the necessity of liquidation or of curtailing materially the scale of operations; (b) **Consistency** — accounting policies are followed consistently from one period to another; and (c) **Accrual** — revenues and costs are recognised as they are earned or incurred (and not as money is received or paid) and recorded in the financial statements of the periods to which they relate. If these assumptions are followed, no specific disclosure is required; if any of them is **not** followed, that fact must be disclosed.

**(ii) Considerations governing selection of accounting policies.** The primary consideration is that the financial statements should represent a **true and fair view** of the state of affairs and of the profit or loss. For this purpose the major considerations are:

| Consideration | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Prudence** | Profits are not anticipated but recognised only when realised; provision is made for all known liabilities and losses even if the amount is estimated. |
| **Substance over form** | Transactions are accounted for in accordance with their economic substance and not merely their legal form. |
| **Materiality** | Financial statements should disclose all "material" items — items whose knowledge might influence the decisions of a user. |

**(iii) Disclosure of a change in accounting policy.** Any change in an accounting policy which has a **material effect** should be disclosed. The **amount** by which any item in the financial statements is affected by such change should also be disclosed to the extent ascertainable; where it is not ascertainable, wholly or in part, the fact should be indicated. If a change has no material effect in the current period but is reasonably expected to have a material effect in later periods, the fact of such change should be appropriately disclosed in the period of change.

**Conclusion:** All significant accounting policies should be disclosed at one place forming part of the financial statements, and any change with a material effect, together with its monetary impact, must be disclosed.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards naming all three assumptions AND the three considerations by their exact terms (Prudence, Substance over form, Materiality); a common deduction is omitting that non-following of an assumption must be disclosed.)*

### Q34. Ch: AS 2 Valuation of Inventories — Scope Exclusions & Cost Formulas (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** (i) State the categories of inventories to which AS 2 does **not** apply. (ii) Explain the cost formulas permitted by AS 2 for assigning cost to inventories and the one that is specifically prohibited.

**Answer:**
AS 2 "Valuation of Inventories" prescribes the basis of measuring inventories, viz. the **lower of cost and net realisable value.**

**(i) Inventories outside the scope of AS 2.** The Standard does not apply to: (a) work-in-progress arising under **construction contracts**, including directly related service contracts (dealt with by **AS 7**); (b) work-in-progress arising in the ordinary course of business of **service providers**; (c) shares, debentures and other **financial instruments** held as stock-in-trade; and (d) **producers' inventories** of livestock, agricultural and forest products, and mineral oils, ores and gases to the extent that they are measured at net realisable value in accordance with well-established practices in those industries.

**(ii) Cost formulas.** The cost of inventories of items that are **not** ordinarily interchangeable, and of goods or services produced and segregated for specific projects, should be assigned by **Specific Identification** of their individual costs. For other inventories, cost should be assigned by using the **First-In, First-Out (FIFO)** formula or the **Weighted Average Cost** formula. The **Last-In, First-Out (LIFO)** method is **not permitted** under AS 2.

| Basis | When used |
|---|---|
| Specific Identification | Non-interchangeable items / goods for specific projects |
| FIFO | Interchangeable items — earliest goods deemed sold first |
| Weighted Average | Interchangeable items — average of opening + purchases |

**Conclusion:** AS 2 permits Specific Identification, FIFO and Weighted Average, but prohibits LIFO, and excludes construction WIP, service-provider WIP, financial instruments held as stock and certain producers' inventories from its scope.

### Q36. Ch: AS 3 Cash Flow Statements — Classification of Activities & Cash Equivalents (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the classification of cash flows into three activities under AS 3. How should interest and dividends, and the acquisition of a subsidiary, be classified? Also define "cash equivalents".

**Answer:**
AS 3 "Cash Flow Statements" requires the cash flow statement to report cash flows during the period classified into three activities.

| Activity | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Operating** | Principal revenue-producing activities and other activities that are not investing or financing | Cash from sale of goods, payments to suppliers and employees |
| **Investing** | Acquisition and disposal of long-term assets and other investments not included in cash equivalents | Purchase/sale of fixed assets and investments |
| **Financing** | Activities that change the size and composition of owners' capital and borrowings | Issue of shares/debentures, repayment of loans, dividend paid |

**Interest and dividends.** In the case of a **financial enterprise**, interest paid, interest received and dividends received are classified as **operating** activities. In the case of **other enterprises**, interest and dividends **received** are classified as **investing** activities and interest and dividends **paid** as **financing** activities. This classification must be applied consistently.

**Acquisition/disposal of subsidiaries.** The aggregate cash flows arising from acquisitions and disposals of subsidiaries or other business units are presented **separately** and classified as **investing** activities.

**Cash equivalents.** Cash equivalents are short-term, highly liquid investments that are readily convertible into known amounts of cash and which are subject to an **insignificant risk** of changes in value. An investment normally qualifies only when it has a short maturity of, say, **three months or less** from the date of acquisition.

**Conclusion:** Correct classification under AS 3 — operating, investing and financing — with consistent treatment of interest and dividends, ensures that users can assess the enterprise's ability to generate cash and cash equivalents.

*(Full-marks tip: The distinction between financial and non-financial enterprises for interest/dividend classification is the high-value point; the "three months or less" test for cash equivalents is frequently omitted.)*

### Q38. Ch: AS 4 Contingencies & Events After the Balance Sheet Date — Disclosure of Non-Adjusting Events (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Define a "contingency" as per AS 4 and explain the treatment and disclosure requirements for non-adjusting events occurring after the balance sheet date. When must such events, even though non-adjusting, be considered?

**Answer:**
AS 4 "Contingencies and Events Occurring After the Balance Sheet Date" defines a **contingency** as a condition or situation, the ultimate outcome of which — gain or loss — will be known or determined only on the occurrence, or non-occurrence, of one or more uncertain future events. (Note: with the notification of **AS 29** "Provisions, Contingent Liabilities and Contingent Assets", the portions of AS 4 dealing with contingencies stand largely withdrawn; provisions and contingent liabilities are now governed by AS 29.)

**Events occurring after the balance sheet date** are those significant events, both favourable and unfavourable, that occur between the balance sheet date and the date on which the financial statements are approved by the Board of Directors.

**Treatment of non-adjusting events.** A non-adjusting event is one that does **not** relate to conditions existing at the balance sheet date. Such events are **not** adjusted in the financial statements. However, disclosure is made in the **report of the approving authority** (Directors' Report) of the nature of the event and an **estimate of its financial effect**, or a statement that such an estimate cannot be made, where non-disclosure would affect the ability of users to make proper evaluations and decisions.

**When non-adjusting events must still be considered.** Two situations require action beyond mere disclosure: (a) where the event is a **proposed dividend** — disclosed in notes; and importantly (b) where an event occurring after the balance sheet date indicates that the **going concern assumption** is no longer appropriate — in which case the financial statements must be re-cast on a basis other than going concern, even though the event is otherwise non-adjusting.

**Conclusion:** Non-adjusting events are disclosed, not adjusted, unless they undermine the going concern assumption, in which event the whole basis of preparation changes.

*(Full-marks tip: Mention that AS 29 now governs contingencies and that the going-concern impairment is the key exception forcing re-drawing of accounts.)*

### Q39. Ch: AS 5 Net Profit or Loss for the Period, Prior Period Items & Changes — Concepts (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish, under AS 5, between (i) a "prior period item" and a "change in accounting estimate", and (ii) explain the accounting treatment of each. Also state how an "extraordinary item" is disclosed.

**Answer:**
AS 5 "Net Profit or Loss for the Period, Prior Period Items and Changes in Accounting Policies" prescribes the classification and disclosure of certain items in the statement of profit and loss so that all enterprises prepare and present the statement on a uniform basis.

**Prior period item** is defined as income or expense which arises in the current period as a result of an **error or omission** in the preparation of the financial statements of one or more prior periods. A **change in accounting estimate** results from new information or new developments and the consequent revision of estimates (e.g., revision of the useful life of an asset or of provision for doubtful debts); it is not the correction of an error.

| Basis | Prior Period Item | Change in Accounting Estimate |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | Error or omission of a past period | New information / development |
| Nature | Correction of the past | Revision, not a correction |
| Treatment | Shown **separately** in current statement of P&L so that impact on current profit is known | Effect included in P&L of the period of change (and future periods if affected) — **prospective** |

**Treatment.** Prior period items should be **separately disclosed** in the statement of profit and loss in a manner that their impact on the current profit or loss can be perceived; they are included in the determination of net profit or loss for the current period. A change in an accounting estimate is accounted for **prospectively** and, if material, its effect is disclosed.

**Extraordinary items** are income or expenses that arise from events or transactions clearly distinct from the ordinary activities of the enterprise and are not expected to recur frequently or regularly. They should be **disclosed separately** in the statement of profit and loss as a part of net profit or loss, and their nature and amount disclosed so that their impact on current profit can be perceived.

**Conclusion:** Prior period items and extraordinary items are separately disclosed within current profit, while a change in accounting estimate is recognised prospectively.

*(Full-marks tip: Emphasise "prospective" for estimates and "separate disclosure within current profit" for prior-period/extraordinary items; wrongly restating opening balances for a change in estimate is a common error.)*

### Q43. Ch: AS 7 Construction Contracts — Recognition Principles & Combining/Segmenting (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain, as per AS 7, (i) the treatment of contract revenue and contract costs when the outcome of a construction contract can be estimated reliably and when it cannot, and (ii) the conditions for combining a group of contracts and segmenting a single contract.

**Answer:**
AS 7 "Construction Contracts" prescribes the accounting for revenue and costs associated with construction contracts in the financial statements of contractors.

**(i) Recognition of contract revenue and costs.**

When the outcome of a construction contract **can be estimated reliably**, contract revenue and contract costs associated with the contract should be recognised as revenue and expenses respectively by reference to the **stage of completion** of the contract activity at the reporting date (the "percentage of completion method"). An **expected loss** on the contract should be recognised as an expense immediately.

When the outcome **cannot be estimated reliably**: (a) revenue should be recognised only to the extent of contract costs incurred of which recovery is **probable**; and (b) contract costs should be recognised as an expense in the period in which they are incurred. Thus no profit is recognised, but any expected loss is still recognised immediately.

**(ii) Combining and segmenting contracts.**

| Situation | Condition |
|---|---|
| **Segmenting** a single contract into components | When separate proposals were submitted for each asset, each asset was subject to separate negotiation and its costs and revenues can be identified. |
| **Combining** a group of contracts | When the group is negotiated as a single package, the contracts are so closely interrelated that they are part of a single project with an overall profit margin, and are performed concurrently or in a continuous sequence. |

**Conclusion:** AS 7 recognises revenue by stage of completion where the outcome is reliably estimable, restricts revenue to recoverable costs where it is not, always recognises expected losses immediately, and lays down objective tests for segmenting and combining contracts.

*(Full-marks tip: The "recognise revenue only to the extent of recoverable costs" rule for unreliable outcomes, plus immediate loss recognition, are the marks-carrying points.)*

AS 9 "Revenue Recognition" deals with the recognition of revenue arising from the sale of goods, the rendering of services, and the use by others of enterprise resources yielding interest, royalties and dividends. Revenue from the sale of goods is recognised when the seller has **transferred the property in the goods** (i.e., the significant risks and rewards of ownership) to the buyer for a consideration, and **no significant uncertainty** exists regarding the amount of the consideration and its collectability.

**Statement of Revenue Recognition**

| Case | Treatment | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| (a) Sold 28.03.2026, delivered later | **Recognise ₹5,00,000 in FY 2025-26** | Property and significant risks/rewards passed on 28.03.2026; delivery date is not decisive. |
| (b) Goods on approval | **Do not recognise** | On approval/sale-or-return basis, revenue is recognised only when the buyer confirms/accepts the goods or the time limit expires. |
| (c) Interest accrued | **Recognise ₹90,000** | Interest is recognised on a **time-proportion basis**; receipt is not necessary. |
| (d) Dividend declared 10.04.2026 | **Do not recognise in FY 2025-26** | Dividend is recognised only when the **right to receive** it is established, i.e., on declaration (10.04.2026 — next year). |
| (e) Advance received | **Do not recognise** | Goods not yet manufactured/delivered; risks and rewards not transferred. Treat ₹3,00,000 as an advance (liability). |

**Answer:** Recognise (a) ₹5,00,000 and (c) ₹90,000 in FY 2025-26; defer (b), (d) and (e) — (d) is recognised in FY 2026-27 on declaration and (e) is a liability until delivery.

*(Full-marks tip: The dividend "right to receive established on declaration" and the "sale-or-return" rule are the discriminating points; recognising revenue merely on receipt of advance is the common error.)*

### Q45. Ch: AS 9 Revenue Recognition — Rendering of Services & Postponement (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain under AS 9 (i) the methods of recognising revenue from the rendering of services, (ii) the circumstances in which recognition of revenue is postponed, and (iii) the items that are outside the scope of AS 9.

**Answer:**
AS 9 "Revenue Recognition" defines **revenue** as the gross inflow of cash, receivables or other consideration arising in the course of the ordinary activities of an enterprise from the sale of goods, the rendering of services, and the use by others of enterprise resources yielding interest, royalties and dividends.

**(i) Revenue from rendering of services.** Revenue from service transactions is usually recognised as the service is performed, either by the **proportionate completion method** or by the **completed service contract method**:

| Method | Basis of recognition |
|---|---|
| **Proportionate completion method** | Revenue recognised proportionately by reference to the performance of each act, as services are rendered. |
| **Completed service contract method** | Revenue recognised only when the rendering of services under the contract is **completed or substantially completed**. |

**(ii) Postponement of recognition.** Revenue recognition is **postponed** when, at the time of raising any claim, there is **significant uncertainty** regarding the **ultimate collection** of the consideration. In such cases revenue is recognised only when it becomes **reasonably certain** that the ultimate collection will be made. Where the ability to assess the ultimate collection with reasonable certainty is lacking at the time of the sale itself, revenue recognition is postponed to the extent of that uncertainty.

**(iii) Items outside the scope of AS 9.** AS 9 does **not** deal with revenue arising from: (a) construction contracts (AS 7); (b) hire-purchase and lease agreements; (c) government grants and other similar subsidies (AS 12); and (d) revenue of insurance companies arising from insurance contracts. It also excludes realised or unrealised gains from the disposal of and changes in the value of fixed assets and investments, and natural increases in herds and agricultural/forest products.

**Conclusion:** Service revenue is recognised on either the proportionate completion or completed service basis; recognition is postponed where collection is significantly uncertain; and construction contracts, leases, hire-purchase, grants and insurance revenue fall outside AS 9.

*(Full-marks tip: Naming both service-recognition methods and the "significant uncertainty of collection" postponement, plus at least three scope exclusions, secures full marks.)*

### Q46. Ch: AS 10 Property, Plant & Equipment — Componentisation & Depreciation (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** Vertex Ltd. purchased a plant on 1st April 2023 for ₹50,00,000. The plant comprises a major component "A" costing ₹20,00,000 with a useful life of 5 years, and the balance of the plant with a useful life of 10 years. Residual values are nil and the straight-line method is used. On 1st April 2025, component "A" was replaced with a new one costing ₹24,00,000, having a useful life of 5 years. Compute the depreciation for FY 2025-26 and the carrying amount of the plant as at 31st March 2026 as per AS 10.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Split of original cost.**
- Component "A" = ₹20,00,000 (life 5 years).
- Remaining plant = ₹50,00,000 − ₹20,00,000 = ₹30,00,000 (life 10 years).

AS 10 requires that each part of an item of PP&E with a cost significant in relation to the total cost, and having a **different useful life**, be depreciated **separately** (component approach).

**WN-2: Annual depreciation before replacement.**
- Component "A": ₹20,00,000 ÷ 5 = **₹4,00,000 p.a.**
- Remaining plant: ₹30,00,000 ÷ 10 = **₹3,00,000 p.a.**

**WN-3: Carrying amounts as on 1st April 2025** (after two years — FY 2023-24 and FY 2024-25):
- Component "A": ₹20,00,000 − (2 × ₹4,00,000) = ₹20,00,000 − ₹8,00,000 = **₹12,00,000.**
- Remaining plant: ₹30,00,000 − (2 × ₹3,00,000) = ₹30,00,000 − ₹6,00,000 = **₹24,00,000.**

**WN-4: Derecognition of old component "A" on replacement (AS 10).** When a component is replaced, the **carrying amount of the replaced part is derecognised** (charged as a loss), and the cost of the new part is capitalised.
- Loss on derecognition of old "A" = its carrying amount = **₹12,00,000** (charged to P&L in FY 2025-26).
- New component "A" capitalised = **₹24,00,000** (life 5 years).

**WN-5: Depreciation for FY 2025-26.**
- New component "A": ₹24,00,000 ÷ 5 = **₹4,80,000.**
- Remaining plant: **₹3,00,000** (unchanged).
- **Total depreciation for FY 2025-26 = ₹4,80,000 + ₹3,00,000 = ₹7,80,000.**

**WN-6: Carrying amount of plant as at 31st March 2026.**

| Component | Cost/Opening carrying (₹) | Less: Dep. FY 25-26 (₹) | Carrying amount 31.03.2026 (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|
| New component "A" | 24,00,000 | 4,80,000 | 19,20,000 |
| Remaining plant | 24,00,000 | 3,00,000 | 21,00,000 |
| **Total** | **48,00,000** | **7,80,000** | **40,20,000** |

**Answer:** Depreciation for FY 2025-26 = **₹7,80,000**; a loss on derecognition of the old component of **₹12,00,000** is charged to P&L; and the carrying amount of the plant as at 31st March 2026 = **₹40,20,000.**

*(Full-marks tip: The two high-value points are derecognising the old component's carrying amount (₹12,00,000) as a loss and capitalising the new component separately; simply adding replacement cost without derecognition is the classic error.)*

### Q47. Ch: AS 10 Property, Plant & Equipment — Revaluation Model (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** Meridian Ltd. owns a building purchased on 1st April 2022 for ₹40,00,000, depreciated on straight-line basis over 40 years (nil residual value). On 1st April 2025 the company adopts the revaluation model and the building is revalued at ₹50,00,000. Compute the revaluation surplus, the depreciation for FY 2025-26, and explain the treatment of the surplus as per AS 10.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Annual depreciation (before revaluation)** = ₹40,00,000 ÷ 40 years = **₹1,00,000 p.a.**

**WN-2: Carrying amount on 1st April 2025** (after 3 years — FY 2022-23, 2023-24, 2024-25):
= ₹40,00,000 − (3 × ₹1,00,000) = ₹40,00,000 − ₹3,00,000 = **₹37,00,000.**

**WN-3: Revaluation surplus.**
= Revalued amount − Carrying amount = ₹50,00,000 − ₹37,00,000 = **₹13,00,000.**

**Treatment (AS 10).** When an asset's carrying amount is **increased** as a result of a revaluation, the increase should be credited directly to owners' interests under the heading **Revaluation Surplus** (through other comprehensive income / directly to a revaluation reserve). However, the increase should be recognised in the **statement of profit and loss** to the extent that it **reverses a revaluation decrease** of the same asset previously recognised in profit and loss. Here there is no previous downward revaluation, so the entire ₹13,00,000 is credited to the **Revaluation Surplus.**

**WN-4: Depreciation for FY 2025-26 (post-revaluation).**
Remaining useful life = 40 − 3 = **37 years.** The revalued amount is depreciated over the remaining life:
= ₹50,00,000 ÷ 37 years = **₹1,35,135 (approx.)** per annum.

**WN-5: Carrying amount as at 31st March 2026** = ₹50,00,000 − ₹1,35,135 = **₹48,64,865.**

**Note on transfer of surplus.** AS 10 permits the revaluation surplus to be transferred to retained earnings as the asset is used; the amount so transferred each year equals the **difference between depreciation on the revalued amount and depreciation on the original cost** = ₹1,35,135 − ₹1,00,000 = **₹35,135**, transferred from Revaluation Surplus to Retained Earnings (not routed through P&L).

**Answer:** Revaluation surplus credited = **₹13,00,000**; depreciation for FY 2025-26 = **₹1,35,135**; carrying amount at 31.03.2026 = **₹48,64,865**, with ₹35,135 of surplus transferable to retained earnings.

*(Full-marks tip: Credit the surplus to reserve (not P&L) unless reversing a prior decrease, and depreciate the revalued amount over the REMAINING life; using 40 years instead of 37 is the common deduction.)*

### Q49. Ch: AS 11 The Effects of Changes in Foreign Exchange Rates — Monetary vs Non-Monetary Items & Forward Contracts (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** M/s Exim Ltd., an Indian company, undertook the following foreign-currency transactions during the year ended 31st March, 2026. You are required to state, with reasons, the amount to be recognised in the Statement of Profit and Loss / Balance Sheet as per AS 11.

| S.No. | Transaction | Details |
|---|---|---|
| (i) | Imported machinery on 15.02.2026 | USD 1,00,000; rate on 15.02.2026 = ₹74; rate on 31.03.2026 = ₹76 |
| (ii) | Creditor for above machinery (unpaid at year-end) | Same USD 1,00,000 outstanding |
| (iii) | Investment in equity shares of a US company on 01.01.2026 | USD 50,000; rate on 01.01.2026 = ₹73 (carried at cost) |
| (iv) | Export debtor on 10.03.2026 | USD 40,000; rate on 10.03.2026 = ₹75; rate on 31.03.2026 = ₹76 |
| (v) | Forward contract taken on 01.03.2026 to sell USD 40,000 (against the export debtor); forward rate ₹77 maturing 30.04.2026 | Spot on 01.03.2026 = ₹75; spot on 31.03.2026 = ₹76 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Classification of items (para 7 & 8 of AS 11):**
Monetary items are money held and assets/liabilities to be received or paid in fixed or determinable amounts of money. Non-monetary items are all others. The machinery and the equity investment (carried at cost) are **non-monetary**; the creditor and the export debtor are **monetary**.

**WN-2 — Machinery [para 9 & 11]:** A non-monetary item carried at historical cost is recorded at the exchange rate on the transaction date and is **not** restated. Machinery = USD 1,00,000 × ₹74 = **₹74,00,000** (fixed; no exchange difference).

**WN-3 — Creditor (monetary) [para 11 & 13]:** Restated at closing rate.
Initial = 1,00,000 × ₹74 = ₹74,00,000; Closing = 1,00,000 × ₹76 = ₹76,00,000.
Exchange loss = ₹76,00,000 − ₹74,00,000 = **₹2,00,000 (charged to P&L)**. (AS 11 does not permit capitalising this in the asset; para 46/46A optionality is not the base rule.)

**WN-4 — Equity investment (non-monetary at cost) [para 11(b)]:** Not restated. Carried at USD 50,000 × ₹73 = **₹36,50,000**. No exchange difference.

**WN-5 — Export debtor (monetary) [para 11 & 13]:** Initial = 40,000 × ₹75 = ₹30,00,000; Closing = 40,000 × ₹76 = ₹30,40,000. Exchange **gain = ₹40,000 (credited to P&L).**

**WN-6 — Forward contract (for hedging a recognised asset) [para 36 & 37]:** The premium/discount is the difference between the forward rate and the spot rate at inception, amortised over the life of the contract; the exchange difference on the contract is recognised each period.

|---|---|---|
| Machinery (non-monetary) | 74,00,000 (asset) | — |
| Creditor (monetary) | 76,00,000 (liability) | 2,00,000 Loss |
| Equity investment (non-monetary) | 36,50,000 (asset) | — |
| Export debtor (monetary) | 30,40,000 (asset) | 40,000 Gain |
| Forward — exchange difference | — | 40,000 Loss |
| Forward — premium amortised | — | 40,000 Gain |
| **Net P&L impact** | | **1,60,000 Loss** |

**Answer:** Machinery ₹74,00,000 and investment ₹36,50,000 are not restated; creditor ₹76,00,000, debtor ₹30,40,000. Net charge to P&L = **₹1,60,000 (loss)**.

### Q50. Ch: AS 12 Accounting for Government Grants — Grant Related to Depreciable Asset & Refund (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** Sunrise Ltd. received a government grant of ₹40 lakhs on 01.04.2023 towards a plant costing ₹200 lakhs (useful life 10 years, SLM, nil residual value). Show the accounting under **both** methods permitted by AS 12 for the year ended 31.03.2024. On 01.04.2025 the company breached a grant condition and had to refund the entire grant. Show the treatment of the refund under the "deduction from asset" method. Ignore tax.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Two methods permitted by AS 12 (para 8.4) for grants relating to depreciable assets:**
(a) **Deduction from gross value of asset**, or (b) treat as **deferred income** credited to P&L over the asset's life in proportion to depreciation.

**WN-2 — Method (a): Reduce cost of asset.**
Net book cost = ₹200 − ₹40 = ₹160 lakhs. Depreciation p.a. = ₹160 / 10 = **₹16 lakhs**. Carrying amount on 31.03.2024 = ₹160 − ₹16 = **₹144 lakhs**.

**WN-3 — Method (b): Deferred income.**
Depreciation = ₹200 / 10 = ₹20 lakhs charged to P&L. Grant of ₹40 lakhs held as deferred income; released = ₹40 / 10 = **₹4 lakhs** credited to P&L. Net effect on P&L = ₹20 − ₹4 = ₹16 lakhs (same as method a). Deferred income balance on 31.03.2024 = ₹36 lakhs (shown after Reserves & Surplus).

**WN-4 — Refund on 01.04.2025 under Method (a) [para 21]:** A grant that becomes refundable and relates to a depreciable asset is recorded by **increasing the carrying amount of the asset** by the amount refundable. The additional depreciation that would have been charged to date (had there been no grant) is charged immediately to P&L.
- Refund = ₹40 lakhs → add back to asset.
- Depreciation short-charged for 2 years (2023-24, 2024-25) = (₹20 − ₹16) × 2 = ₹4 × 2 = **₹8 lakhs charged to P&L immediately**.
- Revised carrying amount on 01.04.2025 = (Book value under grant method ₹128) + ₹40 − ₹8 = ₹160 lakhs, i.e. the value the asset would have had without the grant (₹200 − ₹20 × 2 = ₹160). ✔ Reconciles.

**Answer:** Under Method (a) the plant is carried at ₹144 lakhs (31.03.2024) with ₹16 lakhs depreciation; under Method (b) the same net ₹16 lakhs hits P&L with ₹36 lakhs deferred income remaining. On refund, the asset is grossed up by ₹40 lakhs and **₹8 lakhs of arrear depreciation is charged to P&L**, restoring the carrying amount to ₹160 lakhs.

*(Full-marks tip: Show BOTH methods and prove the P&L effect is identical; on refund the arrear-depreciation catch-up of ₹8 lakhs is what most students miss.)*

### Q52. Ch: AS 14 Accounting for Amalgamations — Nature of Merger vs Purchase & Purchase Consideration (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** A Ltd. agreed to absorb B Ltd. as on 31.03.2026. The Balance Sheet of B Ltd. showed: Equity share capital (shares of ₹10 each) ₹20,00,000; General Reserve ₹6,00,000; P&L (Cr) ₹4,00,000; 12% Debentures ₹5,00,000; Sundry liabilities ₹3,00,000. Assets totalled ₹38,00,000. A Ltd. agreed to (i) discharge the debentures at a 10% premium by issuing its own 12% debentures, (ii) issue 3 equity shares of ₹10 each (market value ₹15) for every 2 shares held in B Ltd., and (iii) pay ₹2 cash per share of B Ltd. Compute the purchase consideration and state whether the amalgamation is in the nature of merger or purchase, giving reasons; also compute goodwill/capital reserve assuming assets are taken at ₹40,00,000 and liabilities at book value.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Number of shares in B Ltd.:** ₹20,00,000 / ₹10 = **2,00,000 shares**.

**WN-2 — Purchase Consideration (payable to shareholders only) [para 3(g)]:** PC excludes amounts paid to debenture-holders and creditors (they are settled separately).
- Equity shares issued = 2,00,000 × 3/2 = 3,00,000 shares × ₹15 (market/fair value) = ₹45,00,000.
- Cash = 2,00,000 × ₹2 = ₹4,00,000.
- **Purchase Consideration = ₹45,00,000 + ₹4,00,000 = ₹49,00,000.**
(Debentures discharged at ₹5,50,000 are NOT part of PC.)

**WN-3 — Nature of amalgamation [para 3(e) — five conditions for merger]:** All five conditions must be satisfied for a **merger**: (1) all assets & liabilities taken over; (2) ≥90% of shareholders (by value) of transferor become shareholders of transferee; (3) consideration discharged **wholly by equity shares** (except cash for fractions); (4) business intended to be continued; (5) assets & liabilities recorded at **book values**.
Here consideration is partly in **cash (₹2/share)** and assets are taken at **revalued ₹40,00,000** — conditions (3) and (5) fail. Hence it is an amalgamation in the **nature of purchase**.

**WN-4 — Goodwill / Capital Reserve (Purchase method) [para 19]:**
Net assets taken over = Assets ₹40,00,000 − (12% Debentures ₹5,50,000 + Sundry liabilities ₹3,00,000) = ₹40,00,000 − ₹8,50,000 = **₹31,50,000**.
Goodwill = PC − Net assets = ₹49,00,000 − ₹31,50,000 = **₹17,50,000 (Goodwill).**

**Answer:** Purchase Consideration = **₹49,00,000**; the amalgamation is in the **nature of purchase**; and A Ltd. records **Goodwill of ₹17,50,000**.

Its **general** borrowings during the year were: 10% loan of ₹40,00,000 and 12% loan of ₹20,00,000. Expenditure incurred on the asset: ₹30,00,000 on 01.04.2025 and ₹24,00,000 on 01.10.2025. General borrowing cost = ₹24,00,000 × 10.67% × 4/12 = ₹24,00,000 × 0.1067 × 0.3333 = **₹85,333** (≈ ₹85,333).

| Source | Computation | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| Specific loan (net of income) | 3,30,000 − 90,000 | 2,40,000 |
| General borrowings | 24,00,000 × 10.67% × 4/12 | 85,333 |

**Answer:** Borrowing cost to be capitalised for FY 2025-26 = **₹3,25,333**. Interest for the 2-month suspension is expensed to P&L.

### Q55. Ch: AS 18 Related Party Disclosures — Related Party & Non-Disclosure Situations (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the meaning of "related party" and "related party transaction" under AS 18. State the situations in which parties are deemed to be related and the cases which are **excluded** from AS 18. When are related-party relationships required to be disclosed even if there are no transactions?

**Answer:**
As per **para 3 of AS 18**, parties are considered to be **related** if at any time during the reporting period one party has the ability to **control** the other party or **exercise significant influence** over the other party in making financial and/or operating decisions. A **related party transaction** (para 10) is a transfer of resources or obligations between related parties, **regardless of whether or not a price is charged**.

**Situations where parties are deemed related [para 3]:** (a) enterprises that directly or indirectly, through intermediaries, control, are controlled by, or are under common control with, the reporting enterprise (holding, subsidiaries and fellow subsidiaries); (b) associates and joint ventures, and the investing party or venturer in respect of which the reporting enterprise is an associate/JV; (c) individuals owning, directly or indirectly, an interest in the voting power that gives them control or significant influence, and their relatives; (d) **key management personnel (KMP)** and their relatives; and (e) enterprises over which any person in (c) or (d) is able to exercise significant influence.

**Cases excluded from AS 18 [para 5]:** (a) two companies merely having a **common director** (unless the director can affect the policies of both in their mutual dealings); (b) a single customer, supplier, franchiser, distributor or **general agent** merely by virtue of the resulting economic dependence; (c) providers of finance, trade unions, public utilities, and government departments in the course of their **normal dealings** with the enterprise; and (d) relationships covered by AS 21, AS 23 and AS 27 to the extent addressed there.

| Related (para 3) | Not related (para 5) |
|---|---|
| Holding, subsidiary, fellow subsidiary | Two companies with only a common director |
| Associates & joint ventures | Bank/financier in normal course |
| KMP and their relatives | Single large customer/supplier (economic dependence) |

**Disclosure without transactions [para 23]:** The **name of the related party and the nature of the relationship** must be disclosed where **control exists**, irrespective of whether or not there have been transactions between the related parties. This ensures the reader is aware of the control relationship even in a dormant period.

**Conclusion:** AS 18 requires disclosure of related-party relationships and transactions based on the ability to control or significantly influence, while specifically excluding relationships arising merely from common directorship, economic dependence or normal financier dealings.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the para 5 exclusions and the para 23 "control-exists" disclosure even without transactions — many students omit both.)*

### Q56. Ch: AS 19 Leases — Finance vs Operating Lease Classification & Lessee's Accounting (Marks: 10) [Problem]
**Question:** On 01.04.2025, Lessee Ltd. took an asset on lease for 4 years. The fair value of the asset is ₹10,00,000 and its useful life is 5 years. Annual lease rentals of ₹3,20,000 are payable at the **end** of each year. The interest rate implicit in the lease is 12%. There is no purchase option; the asset reverts to the lessor at the end of the lease. Determine whether it is a finance or operating lease, and if finance lease, pass the accounting and prepare the finance-charge apportionment schedule and the amount to be depreciated for the year ended 31.03.2026. PV factors at 12%: Yr1 0.893, Yr2 0.797, Yr3 0.712, Yr4 0.636.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Classification [para 8 & 10]:** A lease is a **finance lease** if it transfers substantially all risks and rewards incident to ownership. Indicators include: lease term for the major part of the economic life, and PV of minimum lease payments amounting to substantially all of the fair value.
- Lease term 4 years / useful life 5 years = **80%** of economic life (major part). 
- PV of MLP (see WN-2) = ₹9,72,160 vs fair value ₹10,00,000 = **97%** (substantially all).
Both indicators are met → this is a **finance lease**.

**WN-2 — PV of Minimum Lease Payments:**
₹3,20,000 × (0.893 + 0.797 + 0.712 + 0.636) = ₹3,20,000 × 3.038 = **₹9,72,160**.

**WN-3 — Initial recognition [para 11]:** The lessee records the asset and a liability at the **lower of fair value (₹10,00,000) and PV of MLP (₹9,72,160)** = **₹9,72,160**.

**WN-4 — Finance-charge apportionment schedule (actuarial, 12%):**

| Year | Opening liability (₹) | Interest @12% (₹) | Rental paid (₹) | Closing liability (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2025-26 | 9,72,160 | 1,16,659 | 3,20,000 | 7,68,819 |
| 2026-27 | 7,68,819 | 92,258 | 3,20,000 | 5,41,077 |
| 2027-28 | 5,41,077 | 64,929 | 3,20,000 | 2,86,006 |
| 2028-29 | 2,86,006 | 33,994* | 3,20,000 | 0 |

*Yr-4 interest adjusted for rounding so the liability closes at nil.

**WN-5 — Depreciation [para 23]:** As there is no reasonable certainty that the lessee will obtain ownership (asset reverts to lessor), the asset is depreciated over the **shorter of the lease term (4 years) and useful life (5 years)** = 4 years.
Depreciation for 2025-26 = ₹9,72,160 / 4 = **₹2,43,040**.

**WN-6 — Charge to P&L for 2025-26:** Finance charge ₹1,16,659 + Depreciation ₹2,43,040 = **₹3,59,699**.

**Answer:** It is a **finance lease**; the asset and liability are recorded at **₹9,72,160**; first-year finance charge = **₹1,16,659** and depreciation = **₹2,43,040**, giving a total P&L charge of **₹3,59,699** for the year ended 31.03.2026.

### Q59. Ch: AS 24 Discontinuing Operations — Meaning, Initial Disclosure Event & Disclosures (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Define a "discontinuing operation" under AS 24. When does the "initial disclosure event" occur, and what are the principal disclosures required? Does AS 24 permit deferral of recognition of expected future losses of the discontinuing operation?

**Answer:**
As per **para 3 of AS 24**, a **discontinuing operation** is a **component of an enterprise** (a) that the enterprise, pursuant to a single plan, is disposing of substantially in its entirety, or in a piecemeal manner, or terminating through abandonment; (b) that represents a **separate major line of business or geographical area** of operations; and (c) that can be **distinguished operationally and for financial reporting purposes**.

**Initial disclosure event [para 15]:** Disclosure of a discontinuing operation begins in the financial statements of the period in which the **initial disclosure event** occurs, which is the **earlier** of (a) the enterprise entering into a **binding sale agreement** for substantially all the assets of the operation, or (b) the enterprise's **board of directors (or similar governing body)** having both **approved a detailed formal plan** for the discontinuance and **made an announcement** of the plan.

**Principal disclosures [para 20]:** For each discontinuing operation, the enterprise must disclose: the description of the discontinuing operation; the business or geographical segment in which it is reported under AS 17; the date and nature of the initial disclosure event; the date/period in which the discontinuance is expected to be completed; the **carrying amounts of total assets and total liabilities** to be disposed of; the amounts of **revenue, expenses, pre-tax profit or loss** from ordinary activities attributable to the operation and the related income-tax expense; and the amounts of **net cash flows** attributable to operating, investing and financing activities. Additionally, the amount of pre-tax gain or loss recognised on disposal of assets or settlement of liabilities must be disclosed.

**No deferral / no anticipation of future losses:** AS 24 is a **presentation and disclosure standard only**; it does **not** establish any new recognition or measurement principles. Consequently, an enterprise **cannot** recognise or defer the expected future operating losses of the discontinuing operation merely because a decision to discontinue has been taken. Recognition of impairment (AS 28) and provisions (AS 29) continues to be governed by those standards — a provision is made only when the AS 29 recognition criteria are met.

**Conclusion:** AS 24 requires prominent disclosure of a discontinuing operation from the initial disclosure event onwards, but it does not alter the timing of recognition of gains, losses or provisions, which remain governed by other standards.

*(Full-marks tip: State clearly that AS 24 is disclosure-only — the marks are lost when a student says future losses can be provided for merely on the discontinuance decision.)*

### Q60. Ch: AS 25 Interim Financial Reporting — Recognition Principles & Treatment of Costs (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** State the recognition and measurement principle underlying AS 25 (Interim Financial Reporting). How are the following dealt with in an interim period: (i) a seasonal/cyclical revenue, (ii) a bonus that is contractually mandated, (iii) a major planned repair expected later in the year, and (iv) income-tax expense?

**Answer:**
The fundamental principle of **AS 25** is that an enterprise should apply the **same accounting policies in its interim financial statements as are applied in its annual financial statements** (para 17), and measurements for interim reporting purposes should be made on a **year-to-date basis** (para 28). This is the "**discrete-with-integral-tax**" or **discrete-period view** for most items — each interim period is treated as a discrete accounting period — except that costs incurred unevenly are anticipated or deferred **only if it would be appropriate to anticipate or defer that type of cost at the year-end**.

**(i) Seasonal / cyclical revenue [para 30]:** Revenues that are received seasonally, cyclically or occasionally within a financial year should **not** be anticipated or deferred as of an interim date if anticipation or deferral would **not** be appropriate at the end of the financial year. Such revenue is recognised **when it occurs** — it is not smoothed across interim periods.

**(ii) Contractually mandated bonus [para 31 & Appendix]:** A bonus is **anticipated** for interim reporting purposes only if the bonus is a **legal obligation** or an obligation for which there is a past practice creating a constructive obligation, **and** a reliable estimate can be made. Since the bonus here is contractually mandated, it should be **accrued proportionately** over the interim periods.

**(iii) Major planned repair [para 31]:** A cost that does not meet the definition of a liability at the interim date is **not** provided for merely because it is planned to be incurred later in the year. A **planned but not-yet-incurred** major repair does **not** give rise to a present obligation at the interim date, so it is **not anticipated**; it is recognised when incurred.

**(iv) Income-tax expense [para 29]:** Interim income-tax expense is accrued using the **weighted-average annual effective income-tax rate** expected for the full financial year, applied to the interim period's pre-tax income. This ensures that tax is recognised consistently across interim periods.

**Conclusion:** AS 25 requires the same policies as annual statements, recognises seasonal revenue and unplanned costs when they occur, anticipates only those costs that would be provided for at year-end, and applies the estimated annual effective tax rate to interim income.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner tests the "would it be appropriate at year-end?" test — apply it item by item; the tax question is answered only by "weighted-average annual effective tax rate".)*

### Q62. Ch: AS 27 Financial Reporting of Interests in Joint Ventures — Types of JV & Proportionate Consolidation (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the three forms of joint ventures recognised by AS 27 and the accounting treatment of each. What is "proportionate consolidation" and when does it apply?

**Answer:**
A **joint venture** under **para 3 of AS 27** is a contractual arrangement whereby two or more parties undertake an economic activity that is subject to **joint control** — the contractually agreed sharing of control such that no single venturer can control the activity unilaterally. AS 27 recognises **three forms** of joint ventures:

**(a) Jointly Controlled Operations [para 12–15]:** No separate entity is formed; each venturer uses its own assets and incurs its own expenses and liabilities and raises its own finance. In respect of its interest, a venturer recognises in its **own financial statements** the assets it controls, the liabilities it incurs, and its share of the income and expenses of the joint venture. No adjustment or consolidation procedure is required.

**(b) Jointly Controlled Assets [para 16–20]:** The venturers have joint control (and often joint ownership) of one or more assets but no separate entity is formed. A venturer recognises in its own financial statements its **share of the jointly controlled assets**, any liabilities it has incurred, its share of jointly incurred liabilities, income from the sale/use of its share of output, and its share of the expenses.

**(c) Jointly Controlled Entities [para 24–33]:** A separate entity (company, partnership or other) is established in which each venturer has an interest. In the venturer's **separate financial statements**, the interest is accounted for as an investment under AS 13. In the **consolidated financial statements**, the venturer reports its interest using **proportionate consolidation**.

**Proportionate consolidation [para 28]:** This is a method whereby the venturer's **share of each of the assets, liabilities, income and expenses** of the jointly controlled entity is combined **line-by-line** with the similar items in the venturer's consolidated financial statements (or reported as separate line items). It applies **only to jointly controlled entities** in the consolidated financial statements. It ceases when the venturer loses joint control.

| Form of JV | Separate entity? | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Jointly controlled operations | No | Own assets/expenses recognised |
| Jointly controlled assets | No | Share of assets/liabilities recognised |
| Jointly controlled entity | Yes | AS 13 (separate); proportionate consolidation (CFS) |

**Conclusion:** AS 27 requires the venturer to reflect the substance of its joint control — recognising its own or its share of assets and results for operations and assets, and applying proportionate consolidation for jointly controlled entities in the consolidated financial statements.

*(Full-marks tip: Distinguish the three forms by "separate entity or not", and state that proportionate consolidation is a line-by-line method used ONLY for jointly controlled entities in the CFS.)*

The value in use is estimated from net cash inflows of ₹5,00,000 per annum for 5 years discounted at 10%; the net selling price (fair value less costs to sell) is ₹17,50,000. Compute the impairment loss for the year. In the following year (31.03.2027), the recoverable amount recovers to ₹20,00,000; determine the reversal permitted. ### Q64. Ch: AS 29 Provisions, Contingent Liabilities and Contingent Assets — Provision vs Contingent Liability (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between a "provision" and a "contingent liability" under AS 29 and state the recognition criteria for a provision. For each of the following, state whether a provision, a contingent liability disclosure, or nothing is required: (i) a pending lawsuit where outflow is probable and reliably estimable at ₹5,00,000; (ii) a lawsuit where outflow is only possible; (iii) a guarantee given for a subsidiary's loan where default is remote; (iv) a proposed future operating loss of ₹3,00,000.

**Answer:**
As per **para 10 of AS 29**, a **provision** is a liability which can be measured only by using a **substantial degree of estimation** — i.e. a present obligation of uncertain timing or amount. A **contingent liability** is (a) a **possible obligation** arising from past events whose existence will be confirmed only by the occurrence or non-occurrence of one or more uncertain future events not wholly within the enterprise's control, **or** (b) a **present obligation** that is not recognised because either an outflow of resources is **not probable**, or the amount **cannot be measured reliably**.

**Recognition criteria for a provision [para 14]:** A provision is recognised when **all three** conditions are met: (a) an enterprise has a **present obligation** (legal or, per Indian AS 29, a legal obligation) as a result of a past event; (b) it is **probable** (more likely than not) that an **outflow of resources** embodying economic benefits will be required to settle the obligation; and (c) a **reliable estimate** can be made of the amount. If any condition fails, no provision is recognised — a contingent liability is disclosed instead (unless the possibility of outflow is remote).

| Basis | Provision | Contingent liability |
|---|---|---|
| Obligation | Present obligation (probable outflow) | Possible obligation, or present obligation with non-probable/unmeasurable outflow |
| Recognition | Recognised in the balance sheet | Not recognised — only disclosed by way of note |
| Outflow probability | Probable (> 50%) | Possible; if remote, not even disclosed |

**Application to the cases:**
- **(i)** Present obligation, outflow probable and reliably estimable → **recognise a provision of ₹5,00,000**.
- **(ii)** Outflow only possible (not probable) → **disclose as a contingent liability**; no provision.
- **(iii)** Possibility of default is **remote** → **no provision and no disclosure** (para 68 — remote contingencies need not be disclosed).
- **(iv)** Future operating losses do **not** meet the definition of a present obligation from a past event → **no provision** (para 62 prohibits providing for future operating losses).

**Conclusion:** A provision is recognised only when there is a present obligation with a probable, reliably measurable outflow; otherwise a contingent liability is disclosed, and remote contingencies and future operating losses are neither recognised nor disclosed.

*(Full-marks tip: The three-fold recognition test (present obligation + probable outflow + reliable estimate) plus the rules that remote items are not disclosed and future operating losses are never provided for — that is where the marks sit.)*

### Q68. Ch: Redemption of Preference Shares — Sources & CRR (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the conditions and sources prescribed under Section 55 of the Companies Act, 2013 for redemption of preference shares, and explain the purpose and utilisation of the Capital Redemption Reserve.

**Answer:** Under **Section 55 of the Companies Act, 2013**, a company limited by shares may, if authorised by its Articles, issue redeemable preference shares, and such shares are redeemable subject to the following conditions:

1. **Only fully paid shares can be redeemed** — partly paid preference shares cannot be redeemed.
2. **Sources of redemption:** The shares must be redeemed either (a) out of **profits of the company which would otherwise be available for dividend** (i.e., free reserves), or (b) out of the **proceeds of a fresh issue of shares** made specifically for the purposes of redemption.
3. **Premium on redemption** must be provided for out of the profits of the company or out of the **Securities Premium Account** (before the shares are redeemed).
4. Where shares are redeemed **out of profits**, a sum equal to the **nominal value of the shares redeemed** must be transferred to the **Capital Redemption Reserve (CRR)** account.

**Capital Redemption Reserve (CRR):** The CRR is created to maintain the company's capital base intact when capital is returned to preference shareholders without a corresponding fresh issue — it embodies the doctrine of **capital maintenance** for the protection of creditors. As per Section 55(4), the CRR is treated as if it were paid-up share capital, and it can be utilised **only for the purpose of issuing fully paid bonus shares** to the members. It cannot be used to pay dividends or write off losses.

**Conclusion:** Redemption is permitted only out of divisible profits or a fresh issue, with a matching transfer to CRR when redeemed out of profits, thereby preserving the company's capital.

*(Full-marks tip: Cite Section 55 and the three anchors — fully paid only, two sources, CRR = nominal value redeemed out of profits; state CRR's sole use is bonus shares.)*

### Q70. Ch: Bonus Issue — SEBI Conditions (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the provisions of Section 63 of the Companies Act, 2013 and the relevant SEBI (ICDR) conditions governing the issue of bonus shares.

**Answer:** **Section 63 of the Companies Act, 2013** permits a company to issue **fully paid-up bonus shares** to its members out of (a) its **free reserves**, (b) the **Securities Premium Account**, and (c) the **Capital Redemption Reserve Account**. Bonus shares **cannot** be issued out of reserves created by the **revaluation of assets** (unrealised gains).

The conditions to be satisfied under Section 63(2) are:

1. The issue is **authorised by the Articles of Association**;
2. It has been **recommended by the Board and authorised in general meeting**;
3. The company has **not defaulted** in payment of interest or principal on fixed deposits or debt securities;
4. The company has **not defaulted** in payment of statutory dues of employees (PF, gratuity, bonus);
5. The **partly paid-up shares, if any, are made fully paid-up** before the bonus issue.

Once recommended, a **bonus issue cannot be withdrawn** by the Board. Additionally, under the **SEBI (ICDR) Regulations** (for listed companies): the bonus must be implemented within the stipulated time, must not be made in lieu of dividend, and there must be sufficient reserves after excluding revaluation reserve.

**Conclusion:** A bonus issue capitalises accumulated profits/reserves into share capital without any fresh inflow of cash, and is valid only when the Section 63 and SEBI conditions — especially the bar on revaluation reserves and the requirement of fully paid shares — are met.

### Q73. Ch: Buy-back of Securities — Section 68 Conditions & Maximum Buy-back (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** The following is the summarised Balance Sheet of Orbit Ltd. as on 31st March, 2026. The company proposes to **buy back its equity shares at ₹40 per share**. | Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---|

### Q74. Ch: Buy-back of Securities — Conditions under Section 68 (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Enumerate the conditions and restrictions prescribed under Section 68 of the Companies Act, 2013 which a company must satisfy before it can buy back its own securities.

**Answer:** **Section 68 of the Companies Act, 2013** permits a company to purchase (buy back) its own shares or other specified securities out of (a) its **free reserves**, (b) the **Securities Premium Account**, or (c) the **proceeds of a fresh issue** of any shares or other specified securities — but a buy-back cannot be made out of the proceeds of an **earlier issue of the same kind** of shares.

The conditions to be satisfied are:

1. **Authorisation:** Buy-back must be authorised by the **Articles**; and by a **special resolution** in general meeting. (A Board resolution alone suffices if the buy-back is up to 10% of paid-up equity capital and free reserves.)
2. **Quantum limit [Sec. 68(2)(c)]:** Buy-back in any financial year shall not exceed **25% of the aggregate of paid-up capital and free reserves**; and in respect of equity, not more than **25% of the total paid-up equity capital**.
3. **Debt-Equity Ratio [Sec. 68(2)(d)]:** After buy-back, the ratio of secured and unsecured debt to (paid-up capital + free reserves) shall **not exceed 2 : 1**.
4. **Fully paid securities:** Only **fully paid-up** shares/securities can be bought back.
5. **Solvency & gap:** The company must file a **declaration of solvency**; a further buy-back is permitted only after **one year** (12 months) from the closure of the preceding buy-back.
6. Buy-back must be **completed within one year** of passing the resolution, and shares bought back must be **physically destroyed within 7 days**.

Further, under **Section 69**, a sum equal to the **nominal value** of the shares bought back must be transferred to the **Capital Redemption Reserve** where the buy-back is out of free reserves/securities premium.

**Conclusion:** Buy-back is permissible only when the source, authorisation, 25% quantum, 2:1 debt-equity, and solvency conditions of Section 68 are simultaneously satisfied, with a matching CRR transfer under Section 69.

*(Full-marks tip: Group the answer under source, authorisation, quantum, debt-equity, and completion/CRR — examiners award a mark for each limb correctly stated with the section.)*

### Q77. Ch: ESOP — Concepts & Accounting (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Define an Employee Stock Option Plan (ESOP). Explain the "grant date", "vesting period" and "exercise period", and state how employee compensation cost is measured and accounted for as per the Guidance Note on Accounting for Employee Share-based Payments.

**Answer:** An **Employee Stock Option Plan (ESOP)** is a scheme under which a company grants its employees/directors the **option (a right, not an obligation)** to purchase or subscribe to the company's shares at a **pre-determined exercise price** at a future date, subject to fulfilment of specified conditions such as continued service. It is a form of **share-based payment** used to reward and retain employees and align their interest with shareholders.

Key terms:

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Grant date** | The date on which the company and employee agree to the terms of the scheme (the option is granted). |
| **Vesting period** | The period between the grant date and the date on which all specified vesting conditions are satisfied — i.e., the period the employee must serve before becoming entitled to exercise. |
| **Exercise period** | The period after vesting during which the employee may exercise the vested options by paying the exercise price. |

**Measurement of compensation cost:** Under the **fair value method** (recommended by the Guidance Note), the compensation cost is the **fair value of the options** at the grant date. Under the **intrinsic value method**, it is the excess of the market price of the share over the exercise price on the grant date. The total compensation cost (fair value/intrinsic value × number of options **expected to vest**) is **amortised over the vesting period** on a straight-line basis, and is debited to the **Employee Compensation Expense Account** with a corresponding credit to the **Employee Stock Options Outstanding Account** (shown under Reserves & Surplus). The estimate of options expected to vest is revised at each reporting date for changes in forfeiture.

**Conclusion:** ESOP compensation is measured at grant-date fair (or intrinsic) value of options expected to vest and is spread as an expense over the vesting period.

*(Full-marks tip: Distinguish grant date / vesting / exercise clearly and name the two measurement bases (fair value & intrinsic value) with the amortisation-over-vesting-period rule.)*

### Q80. Ch: Underwriting of Shares & Debentures — Concepts (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the terms "underwriting", "firm underwriting", "marked applications" and "unmarked applications". State the maximum rates of underwriting commission permissible under the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:** **Underwriting** is a contract by which a person (the underwriter), in consideration of a commission, agrees to **subscribe for the shares or debentures of a company** to the extent to which they are **not taken up by the public**. It thus guarantees the minimum subscription and shifts the risk of under-subscription from the company to the underwriter.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Firm underwriting** | A definite commitment by the underwriter to take up a specified number of shares/debentures **irrespective of the public response** — these shares are taken even if the issue is fully subscribed. |
| **Marked applications** | Applications bearing the **stamp/seal of a particular underwriter**, indicating they were procured through that underwriter's efforts; they are credited to that underwriter in computing liability. |

| Security | Maximum rate |
|---|---|
| On **shares** | **5%** of the issue price of the shares (or a lower rate as per Articles) |
| On **debentures** | **2.5%** of the issue price of the debentures |

The commission is payable on the **whole amount underwritten** and may be paid in cash or shares/debentures or a combination.

**Conclusion:** Underwriting insures the company against under-subscription; the liability computation turns on marked, unmarked and firm applications, and the commission is capped at 5% (shares) and 2.5% (debentures).

### Q83. Ch: Redemption of Debentures — DRR Requirement (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** State the provisions of Section 71 of the Companies Act, 2013 read with Rule 18 of the Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules, 2014 relating to the creation of a Debenture Redemption Reserve (DRR) and the maintenance of Debenture Redemption Investment. Which classes of companies are exempt from creating a DRR?

**Answer:** Under **Section 71(4) of the Companies Act, 2013**, where debentures are issued by a company, it is required to **create a Debenture Redemption Reserve (DRR)** out of the **profits of the company available for payment of dividend**, and the amount credited to the DRR shall **not be utilised** by the company **except for the redemption of debentures**.

As per **Rule 18 of the Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules, 2014** (as amended):

1. **Adequacy of DRR:** Companies required to create a DRR must maintain it at **at least 10%** of the outstanding value of debentures.
2. **Debenture Redemption Investment (DRI):** Every company required to create a DRR must, on or before **30th April of each year**, **deposit or invest a sum equal to at least 15%** of the amount of its debentures **maturing during the year ending 31st March next**, in specified securities (e.g., deposits with scheduled banks, Central/State Government securities). This investment must not be used for any purpose other than redemption of debentures maturing during that year, and the amount remaining invested shall not fall below 15% of the debentures maturing during that year.

**Companies exempt from creating DRR:** (i) **All-India Financial Institutions** regulated by the RBI and **Banking Companies** (for both public and privately placed debentures); (ii) **Other Financial Institutions** (NBFCs registered with RBI and HFCs registered with National Housing Bank) — for privately placed debentures; and (iii) **Listed companies** (other than NBFCs/HFCs) issuing debentures on a private placement/public basis, as specified — such companies are required only to maintain the **15% investment (DRI)** and need not create the DRR.

**Conclusion:** A DRR of at least 10% of outstanding debentures must be created out of divisible profits and used solely for redemption, backed by a 15% redemption investment before 30th April, subject to exemptions for banks, financial institutions and specified listed companies.

### Q87. Ch: Internal Reconstruction — Concept and Methods (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the meaning of "Internal Reconstruction" and briefly state its methods. How does it differ from External Reconstruction?

**Answer:** **Internal reconstruction** is the process by which a company, without being liquidated and without forming a new company, reorganises its financial structure — chiefly by reducing or reorganising its share capital under **Sections 61 and 66 of the Companies Act, 2013** — so as to write off accumulated losses, fictitious assets and over-valued assets and present a true and fair financial position. The same legal entity continues; only its capital and liabilities are internally rearranged.

The principal **methods** of internal reconstruction are:
(a) **Alteration of share capital** (Section 61) — consolidation, sub-division, conversion of shares into stock, or cancellation of unissued capital.
(b) **Reduction of share capital** (Section 66) — reducing the paid-up value of shares to write off losses, requiring a special resolution and confirmation by the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT).
(c) **Variation of shareholders' rights** (Section 48) — altering rights attached to a class of shares.
(d) **Compromise / arrangement** (Sections 230–232) — a scheme with creditors and members, sanctioned by the NCLT, under which debenture-holders and creditors sacrifice a part of their claims.
(e) **Surrender of shares** — shareholders surrender a part of their holding, later re-issued to settle creditors.

**Distinction:**

| Basis | Internal Reconstruction | External Reconstruction |
|---|---|---|
| New company | No new company is formed | A new company is formed to take over the old |
| Liquidation | Existing company is **not** wound up | Old company is **liquidated** |
| Legal provisions | Sections 61 & 66 | In the nature of amalgamation (AS 14 / Sections 230–232) |
| Effect | Only capital is reorganised | Business is transferred to the new entity |

**Conclusion:** Internal reconstruction keeps the same company alive by re-writing its capital and settling claims internally, whereas external reconstruction transfers the undertaking to a newly formed company after liquidating the old one.

*(Full-marks tip: Cite Sections 61 and 66 by number and anchor the distinction on "no new company / not wound up" — a vague answer without the statutory references caps the marks.)*

### Q89. Ch: Amalgamation of Companies (AS 14) — Merger vs Purchase (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between "amalgamation in the nature of merger" and "amalgamation in the nature of purchase" as per AS 14, and state the accounting method applicable to each.

**Answer:** **AS 14 "Accounting for Amalgamations"** classifies every amalgamation into two categories on the basis of five conditions specified in **Para 3(e)**. An amalgamation is in the **nature of merger** only if **all** the following five conditions are satisfied:
(i) all the **assets and liabilities** of the transferor company become the assets and liabilities of the transferee company;
(ii) **shareholders holding not less than 90%** of the face value of the equity shares of the transferor company (other than shares already held by the transferee) become equity shareholders of the transferee company;
(iii) the consideration to such equity shareholders is discharged **wholly by the issue of equity shares** of the transferee company, except that **cash may be paid for fractional shares**;
(iv) the **business** of the transferor company is intended to be **carried on** by the transferee company; and
(v) the assets and liabilities taken over are recorded at their **existing book values** (no adjustment except to achieve uniform accounting policies).

If **any one** of these conditions is not met, the amalgamation is in the **nature of purchase**.

| Basis | Nature of Merger | Nature of Purchase |
|---|---|---|
| Accounting method | **Pooling of Interests Method** | **Purchase Method** |
| Recording of assets/liabilities | At existing **book values** | At agreed values / book values |
| Reserves of transferor | Carried forward as they are | Only statutory reserves carried (with amalgamation adjustment reserve); others not carried |
| Goodwill / Capital Reserve | Not arising; difference adjusted in reserves | Excess of PC over net assets = **Goodwill**; shortfall = **Capital Reserve** |
| Goodwill treatment | — | Amortised over its useful life (normally not exceeding 5 years) |

**Conclusion:** The nature of merger uses the pooling-of-interests method preserving book values and reserves, whereas the nature of purchase uses the purchase method recognising goodwill or capital reserve on the difference between purchase consideration and net assets acquired.

*(Full-marks tip: Reproduce all five Para 3(e) conditions and pair each nature with its method (pooling vs purchase) — dropping even one condition or the reserves treatment loses a mark.)*

### Q95. Ch: Departmental Accounts — Allocation of Common Expenses (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the bases generally adopted for the allocation and apportionment of common (indirect) expenses among different departments while preparing departmental accounts.

**Answer:** In departmental accounting, expenses which are **directly identifiable** with a department (e.g., its direct wages, purchases) are **charged (allocated)** to that department, while **common expenses** that benefit all departments are **apportioned** on an equitable basis reflecting the benefit each department derives. The usual bases are:

| Nature of common expense | Basis of apportionment |
|---|---|
| Rent, rates, taxes, repairs & depreciation of building, air-conditioning | **Floor area / space** occupied by each department |
| Lighting and heating | Number of light points or floor area / metered consumption |
| Selling expenses — advertising, salesmen's salaries & commission, bad debts, discount allowed, carriage outward | **Sales (turnover)** of each department |
| Depreciation, insurance & repairs of plant & machinery | **Value of assets** in each department |
| Works manager's / supervisory salary, canteen, labour welfare, PF, ESI | **Number of employees** or wages of each department |
| Power / fuel | **Horse-power** × machine hours, or metered units |
| Carriage inward, discount received | **Purchases** of each department |
| General administration, audit fees, managerial salary | Turnover, or **time devoted** / gross profit of each department |

**Conclusion:** The guiding principle is that each common expense should be apportioned on a basis that most closely reflects the **benefit received or the use made** by each department, so that departmental results are computed fairly; expenses incapable of equitable apportionment (e.g., interest on capital, general manager's remuneration) are usually charged to the **combined/General Profit & Loss Account** rather than to any department.

### Q97. Ch: Consolidated Financial Statements — Concepts (AS 21) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the following in the context of consolidated financial statements under AS 21: (i) Minority Interest; (ii) Goodwill / Capital Reserve on consolidation; (iii) treatment of pre-acquisition and post-acquisition profits.

**Answer:** **AS 21 "Consolidated Financial Statements"** requires a parent that has one or more subsidiaries to present consolidated financial statements in which the financial statements of the parent and its subsidiaries are combined on a **line-by-line** basis by adding together like items of assets, liabilities, income and expenses, after eliminating the cost of the parent's investment against its share of the subsidiary's equity.

**(i) Minority Interest:** Where the parent owns less than 100% of a subsidiary, the **minority (non-controlling) interest** is that part of the net results of operations and of the **net assets of the subsidiary attributable to shareholders other than the parent**. It is computed as the minority's proportion of (share capital + all reserves and surplus) of the subsidiary **as on the balance-sheet date** and is shown separately from the parent's equity and from liabilities in the consolidated balance sheet.

**(ii) Goodwill / Capital Reserve:** On the date of acquisition, the parent's **cost of investment** is compared with its **share of the equity (net assets) of the subsidiary at that date**. The excess of cost over the parent's share of net assets is **Goodwill (cost of control)**, shown as an asset; where the parent's share of net assets exceeds the cost, the difference is a **Capital Reserve**. This is often computed through a "Cost of Control" account.

**(iii) Pre- and post-acquisition profits:** The subsidiary's reserves and profits **existing at the date of acquisition** are **pre-acquisition (capital) profits** — the parent's share is used only to determine goodwill/capital reserve and is **not** available for distribution. Profits earned by the subsidiary **after acquisition** are **post-acquisition (revenue) profits** — the parent's share is added to the **consolidated reserves/Profit & Loss** of the group. The minority's share of both pre- and post-acquisition profits forms part of minority interest.

**Conclusion:** Consolidation eliminates the investment against the subsidiary's equity, capitalising the pre-acquisition portion into goodwill/capital reserve, adding the parent's share of post-acquisition profits to group reserves, and disclosing the outsiders' stake as minority interest.

*(Full-marks tip: Anchor the answer to AS 21 and the "date of acquisition" cut-off — the marks hinge on stating that pre-acquisition profit is capital (feeds goodwill) while post-acquisition profit is revenue (feeds consolidated reserves).)*

### Q99. Ch: Accounting for LLP — Nature and Distinction (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** What is a Limited Liability Partnership (LLP)? Distinguish an LLP from a traditional partnership firm, and state the statements it is required to file annually.

**Answer:** A **Limited Liability Partnership (LLP)** is a body corporate formed and incorporated under the **Limited Liability Partnership Act, 2008**, having a legal existence separate from its partners and **perpetual succession**. It combines the operational flexibility of a partnership with the **limited liability** of a company — the liability of each partner is limited to his agreed contribution, and one partner is not liable for the independent or unauthorised acts of another partner.

| Basis | LLP | Traditional Partnership |
|---|---|---|
| Governing law | LLP Act, 2008 | Indian Partnership Act, 1932 |
| Legal status | **Separate legal entity / body corporate** | No separate legal entity from partners |
| Liability of partners | **Limited** to agreed contribution | **Unlimited**, joint and several |
| Perpetual succession | Yes — unaffected by change of partners | No — affected by admission/retirement/death |
| Maximum partners | **No limit** | 50 (Rule under Companies Act) |
| Registration | **Compulsory** with the Registrar (ROC) | Optional |
| Number of designated partners | Minimum **two** designated partners (one resident in India) | Not applicable |

**Annual filings:** Every LLP must file with the Registrar (i) a **Statement of Account and Solvency in Form 8** (within 30 days from the end of six months of the financial year), and (ii) an **Annual Return in Form 11** (within 60 days of the close of the financial year). An LLP whose turnover exceeds ₹40 lakh or contribution exceeds ₹25 lakh must get its accounts **audited**.

**Conclusion:** An LLP is a hybrid — a separate corporate entity with limited liability and perpetual succession under the LLP Act, 2008 — distinct from a traditional partnership where partners bear unlimited liability and the firm has no separate legal identity.
