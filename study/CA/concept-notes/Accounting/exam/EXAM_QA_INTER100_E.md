### Q1. Ch: AS 2 Inventory — Cost of Purchase, Trade Discount & Duties (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** M/s Vaibhav Traders purchased raw material during the year. From the following particulars, determine the cost of inventory of 1,000 units lying in stock at the year-end as per AS 2 "Valuation of Inventories":

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| List price of 5,000 units purchased | 20,00,000 |
| Trade discount @ 10% on list price | (to be computed) |
| Basic customs duty @ 10% (on price after trade discount) | (to be computed) |
| IGST @ 18% (input credit available) | (to be computed) |
| Freight inward | 45,000 |
| Loading & unloading at supplier premises | 15,000 |
| Cash discount for prompt payment @ 2% | (to be computed) |
| Abnormal storage cost due to flooding | 30,000 |
| Cost of transport to customer (on eventual sale) | 25,000 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Price after trade discount:** Trade discount is deducted to arrive at the purchase price (AS 2, para 7). Net price = ₹20,00,000 − (10% × ₹20,00,000) = ₹20,00,000 − ₹2,00,000 = **₹18,00,000**.

**WN-2 — Customs duty (non-refundable):** Basic customs duty is a non-refundable duty and is **included** in cost. = 10% × ₹18,00,000 = **₹1,80,000**.

**WN-3 — IGST:** IGST of 18% on (₹18,00,000 + ₹1,80,000) = ₹3,56,400 is **recoverable** as input tax credit, hence **excluded** from cost (para 7).

**WN-4 — Exclusions:** Cash discount (₹36,000) is a financing item — **not** deducted from cost. Abnormal storage cost of ₹30,000 is **excluded** (para 13). Transport to customer ₹25,000 is a **selling** cost — excluded (para 13).

**Statement Showing Cost of Purchase (5,000 units)**

| Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Purchase price (net of trade discount) — WN-1 | 18,00,000 |
| Add: Basic customs duty (non-refundable) — WN-2 | 1,80,000 |
| Add: Freight inward | 45,000 |
| Add: Loading & unloading | 15,000 |
| **Total cost of 5,000 units** | **20,40,000** |

Cost per unit = ₹20,40,000 ÷ 5,000 = **₹408 per unit**.
Cost of 1,000 units in stock = 1,000 × ₹408 = **₹4,08,000**.

**Answer:** Value of closing inventory of 1,000 units = **₹4,08,000** (₹408 per unit).

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards correct treatment of each item with a one-line reason and the AS 2 para. Common deduction — deducting cash discount from cost or including recoverable IGST/selling transport in cost.)*

### Q2. Ch: AS 2 Inventory — Valuation of By-Products & Joint Products (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** A chemical plant produces a main product 'Alpha' and a by-product 'Beta' from a common process. During March 2026 the joint (pre-separation) cost was ₹9,60,000. Output was 8,000 kg of Alpha and 1,000 kg of Beta. Beta requires further processing costing ₹40,000 and is then sold at ₹120 per kg; a normal profit margin of 25% on selling price is expected on Beta. Determine the value at which closing stock of 2,000 kg of Alpha should be carried, given the net realisable value of Alpha is ₹150 per kg.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Value credited by by-product (NRV less normal profit):** Where a by-product is not material, AS 2 (para 12) provides that it be measured at net realisable value and this value is **deducted from the cost of the main product**.

Sale value of Beta = 1,000 kg × ₹120 = ₹1,20,000
Less: Further processing cost = ₹40,000
Less: Normal profit (25% of ₹1,20,000) = ₹30,000
Net value attributable to Beta = ₹1,20,000 − ₹40,000 − ₹30,000 = **₹50,000**.

**WN-2 — Joint cost apportioned to Alpha:** Cost of Alpha = Joint cost − value of by-product = ₹9,60,000 − ₹50,000 = **₹9,10,000** for 8,000 kg.
Cost per kg of Alpha = ₹9,10,000 ÷ 8,000 = **₹113.75**.

**WN-3 — Lower of cost and NRV (para 5):** Cost ₹113.75 vs NRV ₹150 → carry at **cost ₹113.75** (cost is lower).

**Statement Showing Value of Closing Stock of Alpha**

| Particulars | Amount |
|---|---|
| Cost per kg of Alpha (WN-2) | ₹113.75 |
| Closing quantity | 2,000 kg |
| **Value of closing stock** | **₹2,27,500** |

**Answer:** Closing stock of 2,000 kg of Alpha = **₹2,27,500** (at cost of ₹113.75 per kg, being lower than NRV of ₹150).

*(Full-marks tip: State that a by-product carried at NRV reduces the main product's cost per para 12, and always apply lower-of-cost-and-NRV to the main product. Deduction if you value Alpha at NRV without comparing to cost.)*

### Q3. Ch: AS 2 Inventory — Cost Formula (Weighted Average) & NRV Write-down (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** From the following record of a trader for the item 'X', compute the value of closing inventory as on 31.03.2026 using the **weighted average** cost formula and state the amount, if any, to be written down as per AS 2. Net realisable value on 31.03.2026 is ₹92 per unit.

| Date | Particulars | Units | Rate (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 01.04.2025 | Opening stock | 500 | 80 |
| 10.06.2025 | Purchase | 1,500 | 84 |
| 20.09.2025 | Sale | 1,200 | — |
| 05.12.2025 | Purchase | 1,000 | 90 |
| 28.02.2026 | Sale | 900 | — |

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Weighted average after 10.06.2025 purchase:**
Units = 500 + 1,500 = 2,000; Value = (500×80) + (1,500×84) = 40,000 + 1,26,000 = ₹1,66,000.
Weighted average rate = ₹1,66,000 ÷ 2,000 = **₹83 per unit**.

**WN-2 — Sale on 20.09.2025:** 1,200 units issued @ ₹83.
Balance = 2,000 − 1,200 = 800 units; value = 800 × ₹83 = ₹66,400.

**WN-3 — Weighted average after 05.12.2025 purchase:**
Units = 800 + 1,000 = 1,800; Value = 66,400 + (1,000×90) = 66,400 + 90,000 = ₹1,56,400.
Weighted average rate = ₹1,56,400 ÷ 1,800 = **₹86.89 per unit** (₹1,56,400/1,800).

**WN-4 — Sale on 28.02.2026:** 900 units issued.
Closing units = 1,800 − 900 = **900 units**.
Cost of closing stock = 900 × ₹86.89 = **₹78,200** (900 × 1,56,400/1,800).

**WN-5 — Lower of cost and NRV:** Cost per unit ₹86.89 vs NRV ₹92 → cost is lower, so **no write-down** required (para 5).

**Statement Showing Value of Closing Inventory (Weighted Average)**

| Particulars | Amount |
|---|---|
| Closing quantity | 900 units |
| Cost per unit (weighted average) | ₹86.89 |
| Cost of closing stock | ₹78,200 |
| NRV (900 × ₹92) | ₹82,800 |
| **Value (lower of the two)** | **₹78,200** |

**Answer:** Closing inventory = **₹78,200**; no write-down needed as cost (₹86.89) is below NRV (₹92).

*(Full-marks tip: Recompute the weighted average after every purchase, not once at year-end. Show the cost-vs-NRV comparison even when no write-down results — examiners award the concluding line.)*

### Q4. Ch: AS 2 Inventory — Fixed & Variable Production Overheads / Normal Capacity (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** A manufacturer gives the following data for the year ended 31.03.2026. Determine the cost of conversion to be included per unit of finished goods and the value of 3,000 units in closing stock as per AS 2.

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Direct material consumed | 24,00,000 |
| Direct labour | 12,00,000 |
| Variable production overheads | 6,00,000 |
| Fixed production overheads | 9,00,000 |
| Normal capacity (units) | 30,000 |
| Actual production (units) | 24,000 |
| Administrative overheads (general) | 3,00,000 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Absorption of fixed production overheads (para 9):** Fixed production overheads are absorbed on **normal capacity**, not actual, when actual is lower. Rate = ₹9,00,000 ÷ 30,000 = ₹30 per unit.
Fixed OH absorbed into 24,000 units = 24,000 × ₹30 = ₹7,20,000.
Unabsorbed fixed OH = ₹9,00,000 − ₹7,20,000 = ₹1,80,000 → charged to **P&L as expense** (not to inventory).

**WN-2 — Variable production overheads (para 8):** Absorbed on **actual** production = ₹6,00,000 ÷ 24,000 = ₹25 per unit.

**WN-3 — Administrative overheads:** General administrative overheads that do not contribute to bringing inventory to its present location and condition are **excluded** (para 13).

**Statement Showing Cost per Unit**

| Element | ₹ per unit |
|---|---|
| Direct material (24,00,000 ÷ 24,000) | 100 |
| Direct labour (12,00,000 ÷ 24,000) | 50 |
| Variable production OH (WN-2) | 25 |
| Fixed production OH at normal capacity (WN-1) | 30 |
| **Cost per unit** | **205** |

Value of 3,000 units in closing stock = 3,000 × ₹205 = **₹6,15,000**.

**Answer:** Cost per unit = **₹205**; closing stock (3,000 units) = **₹6,15,000**. Unabsorbed fixed OH ₹1,80,000 is charged to P&L.

*(Full-marks tip: The trap is absorbing fixed OH on actual (₹37.50) — always use normal capacity when actual < normal. State that the shortfall goes to P&L and exclude general admin OH.)*

### Q5. Ch: AS 2 Inventory — Standard Cost Method & Reversal of Write-down (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** (a) When may the standard cost method or retail method be used to measure inventory under AS 2? (b) A write-down of inventory to net realisable value was made in an earlier year. In the current year the circumstances that caused the write-down no longer exist. How should this be dealt with under AS 2?

**Answer:**
(a) Para 18 of AS 2 permits **techniques for the measurement of cost** — the standard cost method or the retail method — for convenience, **if the results approximate the actual cost**. Standard costs take into account normal levels of consumption of materials and supplies, labour, efficiency and capacity utilisation; they are to be **regularly reviewed** and, if necessary, **revised** in the light of current conditions. The retail method is used in the retail trade for measuring inventories of large numbers of rapidly changing items with similar margins, where cost is determined by reducing the sale value of the inventory by the appropriate percentage gross margin.

(b) Para 24 of AS 2 provides that the assessment of net realisable value is made **in each subsequent period**. When the circumstances that previously caused inventories to be written down below cost **no longer exist**, the amount of the write-down is **reversed** (i.e. the reversal is limited to the amount of the original write-down) so that the new carrying amount is the **lower of the cost and the revised net realisable value**. The reversal reduces the amount of inventories recognised as an expense in the period in which the reversal occurs.

**Conclusion:** Standard/retail cost may be used only if they approximate actual cost and are regularly reviewed; and an earlier write-down is reversed (capped at the original write-down) when the causing circumstances cease.

*(Full-marks tip: Quote para 18 (measurement techniques) and para 24 (reversal). The examiner wants the explicit cap — reversal cannot exceed the original write-down — and "lower of cost and revised NRV".)*

### Q6. Ch: AS 7 Construction Contracts — Foreseeable Loss Recognised Immediately (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** M/s BuildWell Ltd. undertook a fixed-price contract for ₹80,00,000. Details as on 31.03.2026 (end of the 2nd year) are given below. Compute the contract revenue, contract cost and expected loss to be recognised in the Statement of Profit and Loss for the year, applying AS 7.

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Contract price | 80,00,000 |
| Cost incurred up to 31.03.2026 | 60,00,000 |
| Estimated further cost to complete | 30,00,000 |
| Contract revenue recognised up to 31.03.2025 (previous year) | 25,00,000 |
| Contract cost recognised up to 31.03.2025 | 24,00,000 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Stage of completion (cost-to-cost method, para 29):**
Total estimated cost = ₹60,00,000 + ₹30,00,000 = ₹90,00,000.
% completion = ₹60,00,000 ÷ ₹90,00,000 = **66.67%**.

**WN-2 — Total expected loss on contract (para 35):**
Total contract cost (₹90,00,000) exceeds total contract revenue (₹80,00,000).
Expected total loss = ₹80,00,000 − ₹90,00,000 = **(₹10,00,000)**.
Per para 35, when it is probable that total contract costs will exceed total contract revenue, the **expected loss is recognised as an expense immediately**, irrespective of stage of completion.

**WN-3 — Revenue and cost for the current year:**
Revenue to be recognised to date = 66.67% × ₹80,00,000 = ₹53,33,333.
Revenue for current year = ₹53,33,333 − ₹25,00,000 = **₹28,33,333**.

**Statement Showing Amounts Recognised for the year ended 31.03.2026**

| Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Contract revenue for the year (WN-3) | 28,33,333 |
| Cost to be charged for the year (balancing — see below) | 38,33,333 |
| **Loss for the year** | **(10,00,000)** |

Cost to date to be recognised = revenue to date + total foreseeable loss = ₹53,33,333 + ₹10,00,000 = ₹63,33,333.
Cost for the year = ₹63,33,333 − ₹24,00,000 = **₹38,33,333**.
Loss for the year = ₹28,33,333 − ₹38,33,333 = **(₹10,00,000)**.

**Answer:** Contract revenue for the year = **₹28,33,333**; contract cost for the year = **₹38,33,333**; **loss of ₹10,00,000** is recognised immediately in the year ended 31.03.2026.

*(Full-marks tip: Para 35 forces the FULL ₹10,00,000 loss into the current year even though completion is only 66.67%. Deduction if you pro-rate the loss by stage of completion.)*

### Q7. Ch: AS 7 Construction Contracts — Retention Money & Advances / Gross Amount Due (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** For a construction contract, the following information is available as on 31.03.2026. Show the amount to be presented in the Balance Sheet as "gross amount due from/to customers" and the treatment of retention money and advances under AS 7.

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Contract price | 1,00,00,000 |
| Costs incurred to date | 55,00,000 |
| Estimated total costs | 80,00,000 |
| Progress billings raised to date | 50,00,000 |
| Retention money withheld by customer (out of billings) | 5,00,000 |
| Advance received from customer | 8,00,000 |
| Amount received against billings (net of retention) | 42,00,000 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Stage of completion:** = ₹55,00,000 ÷ ₹80,00,000 = **68.75%**.

**WN-2 — Contract revenue and profit recognised to date:**
Total estimated profit = ₹1,00,00,000 − ₹80,00,000 = ₹20,00,000.
Revenue to date = 68.75% × ₹1,00,00,000 = ₹68,75,000.
Cost to date = ₹55,00,000.
Profit to date = ₹68,75,000 − ₹55,00,000 = **₹13,75,000**.

**WN-3 — Gross amount due from customers (para 42):**
= Costs incurred + recognised profits − recognised losses − progress billings
= ₹55,00,000 + ₹13,75,000 − 0 − ₹50,00,000 = **₹18,75,000 (due FROM customer — an asset).**

**WN-4 — Retention money (para 26):** Retention money of ₹5,00,000 is **included in progress billings** and is recognised as a receivable; it is **not** deducted from contract revenue. It remains an amount due from the customer until release.

**WN-5 — Advances (para 27):** The advance of ₹8,00,000 is received before the related work is performed; it is shown as a **liability (advance from customer)** and does **not** form part of contract revenue.

**Statement of Balance Sheet Presentation as on 31.03.2026**

| Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Gross amount due from customers (WN-3) — Asset | 18,75,000 |
| Amount due from customer for retention money (part of billings) — Asset | 5,00,000 |
| Advance from customer — Liability | 8,00,000 |

**Answer:** Gross amount due **from** customers = **₹18,75,000** (asset); retention money ₹5,00,000 shown as a receivable; advance ₹8,00,000 shown as a liability. Revenue of ₹68,75,000 and profit ₹13,75,000 recognised to date.

*(Full-marks tip: Retention is part of billings — not a deduction from revenue — and advances are liabilities. State the para 42 formula (costs + profits − billings) explicitly.)*

### Q8. Ch: AS 7 Construction Contracts — Uncertainty of Recoverability / Cost-based Revenue (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** A contractor is executing a contract of ₹40,00,000. In the early stage the outcome cannot be estimated reliably but costs incurred are expected to be recoverable. As on 31.03.2026: costs incurred ₹12,00,000, of which ₹2,00,000 relates to future activity (materials at site not yet used); costs whose recoverability is **not** probable ₹1,50,000 (included in ₹12,00,000). Determine the contract revenue and profit/loss for the year as per AS 7.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Applicable rule (para 31 & 32):** When the outcome of a contract **cannot be estimated reliably**, revenue is recognised **only to the extent of contract costs incurred of which recovery is probable**, and contract costs are recognised as an expense in the period incurred. No profit is recognised.

**WN-2 — Costs relating to future activity (para 20):** Materials at site of ₹2,00,000 relating to future activity are **excluded** from costs incurred for stage measurement and are carried as an asset (WIP/inventory).

**WN-3 — Recoverable costs incurred to date:**
Costs incurred = ₹12,00,000
Less: costs relating to future activity = (₹2,00,000)
Costs incurred for work performed = ₹10,00,000
Less: costs not probable of recovery (para 33) = (₹1,50,000)
Recoverable costs = **₹8,50,000** → recognised as revenue.

**Statement Showing Amounts for the year ended 31.03.2026**

| Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Contract revenue (= recoverable costs, WN-3) | 8,50,000 |
| Contract cost recognised as expense (work performed) | 10,00,000 |
| **Loss for the year** | **(1,50,000)** |
| Materials at site carried as asset | 2,00,000 |

**Answer:** Contract revenue = **₹8,50,000**, cost recognised = **₹10,00,000**, giving a **loss of ₹1,50,000** (the non-recoverable cost); ₹2,00,000 of materials for future activity is carried forward as an asset.

*(Full-marks tip: In the uncertainty phase revenue = probable recoverable cost, zero profit; the non-recoverable ₹1,50,000 becomes the loss. Deduction if you recognise profit or include future-activity materials in stage of completion.)*

### Q9. Ch: AS 7 Construction Contracts — Combining & Segmenting Contracts (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain when a group of contracts should be treated as a **single** contract and when a single contract should be **segmented** into components, as per AS 7. Also state when construction of an additional asset is treated as a separate contract.

**Answer:**
Under AS 7, the requirements are normally applied separately to each construction contract; however, para 7, 8 and 9 provide for combining and segmenting.

**Segmenting (para 8):** When a contract covers a number of assets, the construction of **each asset** is treated as a **separate contract** when: (i) separate proposals have been submitted for each asset; (ii) each asset has been subject to **separate negotiation** and the contractor and customer have been able to accept or reject that part of the contract relating to each asset; and (iii) the costs and revenues of each asset can be **identified**.

**Combining (para 9):** A group of contracts, whether with a single customer or several customers, is treated as a **single contract** when: (i) the group is negotiated as a **single package**; (ii) the contracts are so closely interrelated that they are, in effect, part of a **single project** with an overall profit margin; and (iii) the contracts are performed **concurrently or in a continuous sequence**.

**Additional asset (para 10):** Construction of an additional asset at the customer's option is treated as a **separate contract** when: (a) the asset **differs significantly** in design, technology or function from the asset(s) covered by the original contract; **or** (b) the price of the asset is negotiated **without regard** to the original contract price.

**Conclusion:** The unit of account under AS 7 is determined by economic substance — segment when assets are separately negotiable and identifiable, combine when contracts form a single interrelated project.

*(Full-marks tip: Give all three conditions under each of para 8, 9 and 10. Half marks are lost if only definitions are stated without the "all conditions" cumulative requirement.)*

### Q10. Ch: AS 9 Revenue Recognition — Completed Service vs Proportionate Completion (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** M/s Prime Consultants renders services under three engagements during the year ended 31.03.2026. Determine the revenue to be recognised in each case under AS 9 and state the method applied.

| Engagement | Nature | Fee (₹) | Status at year-end |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | A single act of service (one-time valuation report delivered on 25.03.2026) | 3,00,000 | Report delivered; fee not yet received |
| B | Annual maintenance over 12 months (started 01.10.2025) | 6,00,000 | 6 of 12 months elapsed; several acts over the period |
| C | Training programme — 40% of sessions conducted; collectability of fee doubtful | 5,00,000 | Uncertainty over ultimate collection |

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Engagement A (completed service contract method):** AS 9 provides that where performance consists of the execution of a **single act**, revenue is recognised when that act is **completed**. The report was delivered on 25.03.2026, so the service is complete. Non-receipt of cash does not defer recognition where ultimate collection is reasonably certain.
Revenue = **₹3,00,000**.

**WN-2 — Engagement B (proportionate completion method):** Where performance consists of the **execution of more than one act**, revenue is recognised proportionately by reference to the performance of each act.
Revenue = ₹6,00,000 × 6/12 = **₹3,00,000**. Balance ₹3,00,000 is recognised as an unearned/advance liability.

**WN-3 — Engagement C (effect of uncertainty, para 9.2):** Where at the time of raising a claim it is **unreasonable to expect ultimate collection**, revenue recognition is **postponed** to the extent of uncertainty. Since collectability is doubtful, revenue is **not recognised** now; it will be recognised when collection becomes reasonably certain.
Revenue = **Nil** (recognition postponed).

**Statement Showing Revenue Recognised — year ended 31.03.2026**

| Engagement | Method | Revenue (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| A | Completed service contract | 3,00,000 |
| B | Proportionate completion | 3,00,000 |
| C | Postponed (uncertainty) | Nil |
| **Total** | | **6,00,000** |

**Answer:** Revenue recognised = **₹6,00,000** (A ₹3,00,000 on completion; B ₹3,00,000 proportionately; C Nil — postponed for uncertainty of collection).

*(Full-marks tip: Match each engagement to the correct method — single act = completed service; multiple acts = proportionate; uncertain collection = postpone. The examiner rewards naming the method and citing para 9.2 for C.)*

### Q11. Ch: AS 9 Revenue Recognition — Effect of Uncertainty & Post-sale Uncertainty (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** State, with reasons under AS 9, the amount of revenue to be recognised in the year ended 31.03.2026 in each of the following independent situations of M/s Trident Ltd.

| # | Situation | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| (i) | Goods sold on 20.03.2026; significant risks & rewards transferred; realisation reasonably certain | 4,00,000 |
| (ii) | Sale made; but the buyer's ability to pay is in serious doubt at the time of sale | 2,50,000 |
| (iii) | Interest on a fixed deposit for the year (time-proportion basis) | 60,000 |
| (iv) | Dividend declared by an investee company on 10.04.2026 (right established after year-end) | 45,000 |
| (v) | Revenue recognised earlier; an uncertainty about collectability arises subsequently on ₹80,000 | 80,000 |

**Solution:**

**(i)** Revenue from the **sale of goods** is recognised when (a) the seller has transferred property/significant risks and rewards to the buyer, and (b) no significant uncertainty exists regarding collection (AS 9, para 11). Both conditions are met → recognise **₹4,00,000**.

**(ii)** Where there is **significant uncertainty as to collectability at the time of sale** (para 9.2), revenue recognition is **postponed**. Recognise **Nil** until collection becomes reasonably certain.

**(iii)** Interest is recognised on a **time proportion basis** taking into account the amount outstanding and the rate applicable (para 13). Recognise **₹60,000**.

**(iv)** Dividends are recognised when the **owner's right to receive payment is established** (para 13). The right was established on 10.04.2026, **after** the year-end, so no revenue in FY 2025-26 → recognise **Nil** for the year.

**(v)** When uncertainty arises **subsequent** to recognition (para 9.4), the amount in respect of which recovery has ceased to be reasonably certain is recognised as an **expense** (not an adjustment of revenue already recognised). Hence ₹80,000 is charged as an **expense**, revenue is not reversed.

**Statement Showing Revenue Recognition**

| Situation | Treatment | Revenue (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| (i) | Recognise | 4,00,000 |
| (ii) | Postpone | Nil |
| (iii) | Recognise (time proportion) | 60,000 |
| (iv) | Recognise next year | Nil |
| (v) | Charge as expense; no reversal | Nil (₹80,000 expense) |
| **Total revenue** | | **4,60,000** |

**Answer:** Revenue recognised = **₹4,60,000**; item (v)'s ₹80,000 is a separate expense, not a reduction of revenue.

*(Full-marks tip: Distinguish uncertainty at time of sale (postpone recognition) from uncertainty arising later (charge as expense — do NOT reverse revenue). This para 9.4 vs 9.2 distinction is the marks-differentiator.)*

### Q12. Ch: AS 9 Revenue Recognition — Consignment, Sale on Approval & Instalment Sales (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** Advise M/s Sunrise Ltd. on the point of revenue recognition in the following independent transactions under AS 9, with the amount recognisable in the year ended 31.03.2026.

| # | Transaction | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| (i) | Goods sent on consignment on 15.03.2026; consignee had not sold any goods by year-end | 3,00,000 |
| (ii) | Goods sent on 'sale or return' on 28.03.2026; buyer accepted goods worth ₹1,20,000 and returned the rest on 03.04.2026 | 2,00,000 |
| (iii) | Instalment sale of a machine on 01.01.2026; ownership passed on delivery; total price ₹6,00,000 collected over 6 instalments (interest element ₹60,000) | 6,00,000 |

**Solution:**

**(i) Consignment:** In a consignment, risks and rewards are **not** transferred on despatch; the consignor retains ownership until the consignee sells to a third party. As nothing was sold by the consignee by year-end, **no revenue** is recognised → **Nil**. The goods remain in the consignor's closing inventory.

**(ii) Sale or return / on approval:** Revenue is recognised when the buyer **formally accepts** the goods or the time for rejection has elapsed. By year-end the buyer had accepted ₹1,20,000; the balance ₹80,000 was returned after year-end. Recognise **₹1,20,000**; the ₹80,000 remains inventory of the seller.

**(iii) Instalment sale:** Under AS 9, when the consideration is receivable in instalments and ownership passes on delivery, the **sale value (excluding interest)** is recognised at the date of sale; the **interest element** is recognised proportionately over the period as it accrues.
Sale value (excluding interest) = ₹6,00,000 − ₹60,000 = ₹5,40,000 → recognised on 01.01.2026 as revenue.
Interest for 3 months (Jan–Mar 2026) recognised on a time-proportion basis (assume ₹60,000 over the collection period; ₹60,000 × 3 months share) — the sale revenue itself is **₹5,40,000**.

**Statement Showing Revenue Recognised — year ended 31.03.2026**

| Transaction | Revenue (₹) |
|---|---|
| (i) Consignment | Nil |
| (ii) Sale or return (accepted portion) | 1,20,000 |
| (iii) Instalment sale (excluding interest) | 5,40,000 |
| **Total sale revenue** | **6,60,000** |

**Answer:** Sale revenue recognised = **₹6,60,000** (Nil on consignment, ₹1,20,000 on approval, ₹5,40,000 on the instalment sale); interest element of instalment sale is recognised separately over time.

*(Full-marks tip: In instalment sales, split the price into sale value (recognised on delivery) and interest (accrued over time). For consignment/approval, recognition hinges on transfer of risks and rewards / formal acceptance.)*

### Q16. Ch: AS 10 Property, Plant & Equipment — Exchange of Assets & Revaluation (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** Answer the following under AS 10 for Crest Ltd.:
(a) On 01.10.2025 Crest Ltd. acquired a new machine in exchange for an old machine (carrying amount ₹6,00,000; fair value ₹7,50,000) plus cash of ₹2,00,000. The exchange has commercial substance and the fair value of the asset given up is more clearly evident. At what value is the new machine recorded, and what is the gain/loss?
(b) Crest Ltd. revalues a class of buildings from a carrying amount of ₹40,00,000 to ₹52,00,000. State the treatment of the revaluation surplus and of the depreciation thereafter.

**Solution:**

**(a) WN-1 — Cost of asset acquired in exchange (para 26–28):** When an item of PP&E is acquired in exchange for another asset and the transaction has **commercial substance**, the cost is measured at the **fair value of the asset given up**, adjusted by cash paid/received, unless the fair value of the asset received is more clearly evident.
Cost of new machine = Fair value of old machine given up ₹7,50,000 + Cash paid ₹2,00,000 = **₹9,50,000**.

**WN-2 — Gain on exchange:** The old machine is derecognised. Gain = Fair value − carrying amount = ₹7,50,000 − ₹6,00,000 = **₹1,50,000** (recognised in P&L).

**(b) Revaluation (para 39 & 40):**
Revaluation surplus = ₹52,00,000 − ₹40,00,000 = ₹12,00,000. When an asset's carrying amount is **increased** on revaluation, the increase is credited **directly to owners' interest under the head "Revaluation Surplus"** (through Other Comprehensive Income / reserve), **not** to P&L — unless it reverses a revaluation decrease of the same asset previously charged to P&L.
Subsequent **depreciation** is charged on the **revalued (increased) carrying amount** over the remaining useful life. An amount equal to the excess depreciation on the revalued amount over depreciation on original cost **may be transferred** from Revaluation Surplus to Retained Earnings (not through P&L).

**Statement of Values**

| Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Cost of new machine (a) | 9,50,000 |
| Gain on exchange to P&L (a) | 1,50,000 |
| Revaluation surplus to reserve (b) | 12,00,000 |

**Answer:** (a) New machine recorded at **₹9,50,000**, gain of **₹1,50,000** to P&L. (b) Revaluation surplus of **₹12,00,000** credited to Revaluation Surplus (owners' interest); future depreciation on the revalued ₹52,00,000, with the excess depreciation transferable from surplus to retained earnings.

*(Full-marks tip: With commercial substance, cost = fair value of asset given up + cash; gain goes to P&L. Revaluation increase goes to reserve, not P&L, and depreciation is on the revalued amount. State the "commercial substance" condition explicitly.)*

### Q17. Ch: AS 10 Property, Plant & Equipment — Recognition Criteria & Cost Components (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** (a) State the two recognition criteria for an item of Property, Plant and Equipment under AS 10. (b) List the elements that comprise the "cost" of an item of PP&E and give two examples of costs that are **not** included in the carrying amount of PP&E.

**Answer:**
(a) **Recognition criteria (para 7):** The cost of an item of property, plant and equipment is recognised as an asset **if, and only if**: (i) it is **probable that future economic benefits** associated with the item will flow to the enterprise; and (ii) the **cost of the item can be measured reliably**.

(b) **Elements of cost (para 16):** The cost of an item of PP&E comprises:
1. its **purchase price**, including import duties and non-refundable purchase taxes, after deducting trade discounts and rebates;
2. any costs **directly attributable** to bringing the asset to the location and condition necessary for it to be capable of operating in the manner intended by management (e.g. site preparation, delivery and handling, installation and assembly, professional fees, costs of testing net of sale proceeds of test items); and
3. the initial estimate of the costs of **dismantling, removing the item and restoring the site**, the obligation for which the enterprise incurs on acquisition or as a consequence of using it.

**Examples of costs NOT included (para 19–20):** costs of opening a new facility (inauguration costs); costs of introducing a new product/service (advertising and promotion); administration and other general overhead costs; **initial operating losses** incurred before the asset reaches planned performance; and costs of relocating or reorganising part of the enterprise's operations.

**Conclusion:** An item is capitalised only when future benefits are probable and cost is measurable; cost includes purchase price + directly attributable costs + decommissioning estimate, but excludes items such as initial operating losses and general overheads.

*(Full-marks tip: Give both recognition tests, all three cost elements of para 16, and at least two exclusions from para 19–20. The decommissioning component in element 3 is frequently missed — include it.)*

### Q19. Ch: AS 5 — Change in Accounting Estimate vs. Change in Accounting Policy (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** For each of the following situations for the year ended 31st March, 2026, state whether it is a **change in accounting policy**, a **change in accounting estimate**, or the **correction of a prior-period error**, and explain the accounting treatment under AS 5.

| # | Situation |
|---|-----------|
| (i) | The company revises the estimated useful life of its plant from 10 years to 8 years on the basis of new technical assessment. |
| (ii) | The company switches its method of valuing inventory from FIFO to weighted-average cost. |
| (iii) | Depreciation of ₹1,20,000 relating to a machine was completely omitted in the accounts of 2024-25 and detected during 2025-26. |
| (iv) | The provision for warranty, earlier computed at 2% of sales, is now computed at 3% of sales following higher observed claim rates. |

**Answer:**
As per **AS 5 "Net Profit or Loss for the Period, Prior Period Items and Changes in Accounting Policies"**, a **change in accounting estimate** results from new information or developments and is applied **prospectively** in the current (and, if relevant, future) periods. A **change in accounting policy** is a change in the specific principles/bases of preparing financial statements; it is permitted only if required by statute, by an Accounting Standard, or for more appropriate presentation, and its effect must be **disclosed** (and quantified where ascertainable). A **prior-period item** is an income or expense arising from an error or omission in the preparation of prior-period statements; it is separately disclosed in the current statement of profit and loss so that its impact on current profit/loss can be perceived.

| # | Classification | Treatment under AS 5 |
|---|---------------|----------------------|
| (i) | Change in accounting **estimate** | Apply **prospectively**; depreciate the remaining carrying amount over the revised remaining life of 8 years. Disclose the effect if material. |
| (ii) | Change in accounting **policy** | Change the basis of inventory valuation; disclose the change and, where ascertainable, the amount of the effect on the current-period profit. |
| (iii) | **Prior-period item** (error/omission) | The omitted depreciation of ₹1,20,000 is charged in 2025-26 and **separately disclosed** as a prior-period item so its effect on current profit is apparent. |
| (iv) | Change in accounting **estimate** | The revised warranty percentage reflects new information about claim experience; apply **prospectively** by charging the current period at 3% of sales. |

Where it is difficult to distinguish a change in policy from a change in estimate, AS 5 requires the change to be treated as a **change in estimate**, with appropriate disclosure.

**Conclusion:** (i) and (iv) are changes in estimate (prospective); (ii) is a change in policy (disclose effect); (iii) is a prior-period item (separately disclosed in current profit and loss).

### Q20. Ch: AS 11 — Recognition of Exchange Difference on Long-Term Foreign Currency Monetary Items (Marks: 6) [Problem]

| Date | Rate |
|------|------|
| 1st July, 2025 (date of loan & acquisition) | 82.00 |
| 31st March, 2026 (year-end) | 86.50 |

The plant is depreciated on straight-line basis over 10 years. Show the accounting treatment of the exchange difference (a) under the **general requirement** of AS 11 and (b) if the company **opts for the option in Para 46A** of AS 11 for long-term foreign currency monetary items. Ignore interest.

Loan is a foreign currency monetary item; it must be restated at the closing rate at year-end.
- Amount on 1st July, 2025 = US $1,00,000 × ₹82.00 = ₹82,00,000
- Amount on 31st March, 2026 = US $1,00,000 × ₹86.50 = ₹86,50,000

Exchange differences arising on the settlement or restatement of monetary items are recognised as **income or expense in the period in which they arise**. Therefore the entire loss of **₹4,50,000 is charged to the Statement of Profit and Loss** for 2025-26. Depreciation on the plant is on its original cost of ₹82,00,000.

| Particulars | ₹ |
|-------------|---|
| Exchange loss charged to P&L | 4,50,000 |
| Depreciation for 9 months (82,00,000 ÷ 10 × 9/12) | 6,15,000 |

- Revised cost of plant = ₹82,00,000 + ₹4,50,000 = **₹86,50,000**

| Particulars | ₹ |

| Revised cost of plant | 86,50,000 |

| Amount charged to P&L as exchange loss | Nil |

**Answer:** Exchange difference = **₹4,50,000 (loss)**. Under the general rule it is charged wholly to the Statement of Profit and Loss for 2025-26; if Para 46A option is exercised, it is capitalised to the plant (revised cost ₹86,50,000) and depreciated over the balance life.

### Q21. Ch: AS 12 — Government Grant in the Nature of Promoter's Contribution (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Helios Ltd. received a grant of **₹40,00,000** from the State Government during the year ended 31st March, 2026. The grant was given as an incentive for setting up an industrial unit in a notified backward area, **by reference to the total investment** in the project and with **no repayment obligation and no relation to any specific fixed asset**. Explain, under AS 12, how this grant should be accounted for and pass the necessary journal entry. Would your answer change if the grant were specifically towards the cost of a machine costing ₹40,00,000?

**Answer:**
As per **AS 12 "Accounting for Government Grants"**, government grants in the nature of **promoter's contribution** are those given with reference to the **total investment in an undertaking** or by way of contribution towards its total capital outlay, for which **no repayment is ordinarily expected** and which are **not related to any specific fixed asset**. Such grants are treated as **capital reserve** which forms part of the shareholders' funds; they are **not** recognised in the Statement of Profit and Loss and are **not** deducted from the cost of any asset.

Since Helios Ltd.'s grant is given by reference to the total investment in the project, carries no repayment obligation, and is not linked to any specific asset, it is a grant in the nature of promoter's contribution and is credited to **Capital Reserve**.

**Journal Entry:**

| Particulars | Dr. (₹) | Cr. (₹) |
|-------------|---------|---------|
| Bank A/c ................................ Dr. | 40,00,000 | |
| &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;To Capital Reserve (Government Grant) A/c | | 40,00,000 |
| *(Being government grant in the nature of promoter's contribution credited to Capital Reserve)* | | |

**If the grant were specifically towards the cost of a machine**, it would be a grant **related to a specific depreciable fixed asset**. AS 12 then permits either (i) deducting the grant from the gross value of the asset (here the machine's book value would become ₹NIL, and depreciation on ₹Nil), or (ii) treating the grant as **deferred income** credited to the Statement of Profit and Loss over the useful life of the asset in proportion to depreciation. Where funds are borrowed generally and used for obtaining a qualifying asset, AS 16 requires the amount of borrowing cost eligible for capitalisation to be determined by applying a **capitalisation rate** = weighted-average of the borrowing costs applicable to the enterprise's general borrowings outstanding during the period.

| Borrowing | Amount (₹) | Rate | Interest (₹) |
|-----------|-----------|------|--------------|
| 12% Term Loan | 40,00,000 | 12% | 4,80,000 |
| 10% Debentures | 60,00,000 | 10% | 6,00,000 |
| **Total** | **1,00,00,000** | | **10,80,000** |

Capitalisation rate = Total interest ÷ Total borrowings = 10,80,000 ÷ 1,00,00,000 = **10.80%**

**WN-2: Weighted-average expenditure on the qualifying asset**

| Date | Expenditure (₹) | Period outstanding | Weighted amount (₹) |
|------|-----------------|--------------------|---------------------|
| 1 Apr 2025 | 20,00,000 | 12/12 | 20,00,000 |
| 1 Oct 2025 | 18,00,000 | 6/12 | 9,00,000 |
| 1 Jan 2026 | 12,00,000 | 3/12 | 3,00,000 |
| **Total** | **50,00,000** | | **32,00,000** |

Borrowing cost to capitalise = Weighted-average expenditure × Capitalisation rate = ₹32,00,000 × 10.80% = **₹3,45,600**

**Answer:** Capitalisation rate = **10.80%**; borrowing cost to be **capitalised = ₹3,45,600**; the balance interest of ₹7,34,400 (10,80,000 − 3,45,600) is charged to the Statement of Profit and Loss.

### Q23. Ch: AS 17 — Primary and Secondary Segment Reporting Formats (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain, under AS 17, how an enterprise determines whether **business segments** or **geographical segments** form its **primary** reporting format, and state the difference in the extent of disclosures required for the primary format versus the secondary format.

**Answer:**
As per **AS 17 "Segment Reporting"**, an enterprise identifies two segment bases — **business segments** (components engaged in providing distinguishable products/services subject to different risks and returns) and **geographical segments** (components operating in a particular economic environment subject to different risks and returns). The dominant source and nature of an enterprise's risks and returns governs which basis is its **primary** reporting format; the other basis becomes the **secondary** format.

The determination is made as follows:

| Source of predominant risks and returns | Primary format | Secondary format |
|------------------------------------------|----------------|------------------|
| Differences in products and services | Business segments | Geographical segments |
| Differences in geographical areas | Geographical segments | Business segments |
| Both strongly influence risks/returns | Business segments (primary) | Geographical segments (secondary) |

The internal organisation and management structure and the internal financial reporting system to the Board/CEO are normally the basis for identifying this predominant source.

**Extent of disclosures:**

| Item | Primary format | Secondary format |
|------|----------------|------------------|
| Segment revenue (external and inter-segment) | Full disclosure required | Limited (external revenue by area/segment where ≥ 10% of total) |
| Segment result | Required | Not required |
| Segment assets | Required | Required |
| Segment liabilities | Required | Not required |
| Cost of acquiring segment fixed assets (capital additions) | Required | Required |
| Depreciation, non-cash expenses, reconciliations | Required | Not required |

Thus the **primary format carries the fuller set of disclosures** (revenue, result, assets, liabilities, capital additions, depreciation and reconciliations), whereas the **secondary format requires only a limited set** (principally external revenue, carrying amount of assets and cost of assets acquired).

**Conclusion:** The basis (business or geographical) that reflects the predominant source of the enterprise's risks and returns is the primary format with full disclosures; the other basis is the secondary format with reduced disclosures.

*(Full-marks tip: Present the risk-and-returns test with the "both influence → business is primary" rule, and tabulate primary-vs-secondary disclosures. Simply defining the two segment types without the primary/secondary determination loses marks.)*

| # | Situation |

### Q25. Ch: AS 19 — Sale and Leaseback Transactions (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** On 1st April, 2025 Cobalt Ltd. sold a building, whose carrying amount was **₹8,00,000** and **fair value ₹10,00,000**, to a leasing company for **₹12,00,000** and immediately took it back on an **operating lease** for 12 years. Explain, under AS 19, how the profit on this sale-and-leaseback transaction should be accounted for. How would your answer differ if the sale price had been (a) equal to the fair value of ₹10,00,000, and (b) ₹9,00,000 (i.e. below fair value)?

**Answer:**
As per **AS 19 "Leases"**, a sale and leaseback transaction involves the sale of an asset by the vendor and the leasing of the same asset back to the vendor. Where the leaseback is an **operating lease** and the transaction is established at **fair value**, any profit or loss is recognised **immediately**. The accounting of profit depends on the relationship between the **sale price**, the **fair value** and the **carrying amount**:

- If the **sale price exceeds fair value**, the excess of sale price over fair value is **deferred and amortised** over the period for which the asset is expected to be used; only the profit up to fair value is recognised immediately.
- If the sale price is at or below fair value, any profit or loss is recognised immediately (subject to the below-fair-value loss rule).

**Given case (Sale price ₹12,00,000 > Fair value ₹10,00,000):**

| Component | Computation | ₹ |
|-----------|-------------|---|
| Total apparent profit (Sale price − Carrying amount) | 12,00,000 − 8,00,000 | 4,00,000 |
| Excess of sale price over fair value → **deferred & amortised over 12 years** | 12,00,000 − 10,00,000 | 2,00,000 |
| Profit up to fair value → **recognised immediately** | 10,00,000 − 8,00,000 | 2,00,000 |

Thus **₹2,00,000 is recognised as profit immediately** and **₹2,00,000 is deferred** and written back to the Statement of Profit and Loss over the 12-year lease term (₹16,667 per annum).

**(a) If sale price = fair value ₹10,00,000:**
The whole profit = ₹10,00,000 − ₹8,00,000 = **₹2,00,000 is recognised immediately**; nothing is deferred, since there is no excess over fair value.

**(b) If sale price = ₹9,00,000 (below fair value):**
Profit = ₹9,00,000 − ₹8,00,000 = **₹1,00,000 recognised immediately** (the transaction is below fair value but still results in a profit, and no future rentals are shown to be below market, so no deferral is warranted).

**Conclusion:** In the given case ₹2,00,000 is recognised at once and ₹2,00,000 is deferred over 12 years; if the sale is at fair value the entire ₹2,00,000 is recognised immediately; if below fair value at ₹9,00,000, ₹1,00,000 is recognised immediately.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the three-way split by comparing sale price, fair value and carrying amount, and correctly deferring only the "sale price − fair value" excess. Deferring the whole ₹4,00,000 profit is the standard mistake.)*

### Q26. Ch: AS 20 — Anti-Dilutive Potential Equity Shares in Diluted EPS (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** The following information relates to **Zenith Ltd.** for the year ended 31st March, 2026:

| Particulars | Amount |
|-------------|--------|
| Net profit after tax (before preference dividend) | ₹60,00,000 |
| Dividend on convertible preference shares | ₹6,00,000 |
| Number of equity shares outstanding | 12,00,000 |
| 10% Convertible Debentures of ₹100 each (₹30,00,000), each convertible into 10 equity shares | 30,000 debentures |
| Convertible preference shares — convertible into equity shares | 50,000 equity shares |
| Applicable tax rate | 30% |

Compute **Basic EPS** and **Diluted EPS** under AS 20, clearly demonstrating the **anti-dilution test** for each class of potential equity shares.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Basic EPS**
Earnings for equity shareholders = Net profit after tax − Preference dividend = ₹60,00,000 − ₹6,00,000 = ₹54,00,000.
Basic EPS = 54,00,000 ÷ 12,00,000 = **₹4.50**

**WN-2: Incremental EPS of each potential equity share (to rank by dilutive effect)**

*(a) 10% Convertible Debentures — conversion into 30,000 × 10 = 3,00,000 equity shares*
- Interest saved = ₹30,00,000 × 10% = ₹3,00,000
- Less tax @ 30% = ₹90,000 → after-tax interest saving = ₹2,10,000
- Incremental EPS = 2,10,000 ÷ 3,00,000 = **₹0.70**

*(b) Convertible Preference Shares — conversion into 50,000 equity shares*
- Preference dividend saved = ₹6,00,000 (dividend is not tax-deductible, so no tax adjustment)
- Incremental EPS = 6,00,000 ÷ 50,000 = **₹12.00**

**WN-3: Ranking (most dilutive first) and sequential test**
Potential shares are included in ascending order of incremental EPS; a potential equity share is **dilutive** only if its inclusion **decreases** EPS from continuing operations. Order: Debentures (₹0.70) → Preference shares (₹12.00).

| Step | Earnings (₹) | Shares | EPS (₹) | Effect |
|------|--------------|--------|---------|--------|
| Basic | 54,00,000 | 12,00,000 | 4.50 | — |
| Add: Convertible Debentures | 54,00,000 + 2,10,000 = 56,10,000 | 12,00,000 + 3,00,000 = 15,00,000 | **3.74** | 3.74 < 4.50 → **Dilutive, include** |

| Particulars | Amount |
|-------------|--------|
| **Basic EPS** | **₹4.50** |
| **Diluted EPS** (after including only the dilutive convertible debentures) | **₹3.74** |

**Answer:** Basic EPS = **₹4.50**; Diluted EPS = **₹3.74**. The convertible preference shares are anti-dilutive (incremental EPS ₹12 > basic EPS) and are excluded from the diluted EPS computation.

### Q27. Ch: AS 22 — Virtual Certainty for Recognition of Deferred Tax Asset (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Under AS 22, a deferred tax asset arising from **unabsorbed depreciation or carry-forward of losses** is recognised only if there is **"virtual certainty"** supported by convincing evidence of realisation. Explain the concept of virtual certainty, how it differs from "reasonable certainty", and the disclosure required.

**Answer:**
As per **AS 22 "Accounting for Taxes on Income"**, deferred tax assets should be recognised and carried forward only to the extent that there is a **reasonable certainty** that sufficient future taxable income will be available against which such deferred tax assets can be realised. However, where the deferred tax asset arises from **unabsorbed depreciation or carry-forward of losses under tax laws**, it is recognised **only to the extent that there is virtual certainty** supported by convincing evidence that sufficient future taxable income will be available against which the asset can be realised. The higher threshold reflects the fact that the very existence of unabsorbed depreciation/losses is itself evidence that future taxable income may not be available.

**Virtual certainty** is not a matter of perception; it must be supported by **convincing evidence** — for example, existing firm sales/purchase contracts, or a favourable turnaround already achieved after restructuring, that make the realisation of the asset **almost certain**. It is a substantially higher degree of assurance than reasonable certainty.

| Basis | Reasonable certainty | Virtual certainty |
|-------|----------------------|-------------------|
| Applies to | Deferred tax asset from ordinary timing differences | DTA from unabsorbed depreciation / carry-forward of losses |
| Degree of assurance | Reasonably assured of future taxable income | Almost certain; supported by convincing objective evidence |
| Evidence | General projections/estimates may suffice | Requires firm, convincing evidence (e.g., binding contracts) |

Mere projections of future profits, or expectations dependent on uncertain future events not within the enterprise's control, do **not** constitute virtual certainty. Where a deferred tax asset is recognised against unabsorbed depreciation/loss, AS 22 requires **disclosure of the nature of the evidence** supporting its recognition. Such assets are **reviewed at each balance sheet date** and written down/written up as appropriate.

**Conclusion:** A DTA on unabsorbed depreciation or carry-forward losses is recognised only when convincing evidence makes realisation virtually certain — a stricter test than the reasonable certainty applied to ordinary timing differences — with disclosure of the supporting evidence.

*(Full-marks tip: Emphasise that virtual certainty requires objective, convincing evidence (not mere profit projections), the disclosure of the nature of that evidence, and the review at each balance sheet date. Treating budgeted future profits as virtual certainty is the common error.)*

### Q28. Ch: AS 24 — Disclosure Requirements for a Discontinuing Operation (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Define a **discontinuing operation** under AS 24 and state the **initial disclosures** that an enterprise must make in respect of a discontinuing operation, along with the point of time at which such disclosures are first made.

**Answer:**
As per **AS 24 "Discontinuing Operations"**, a **discontinuing operation** is a component of an enterprise that (a) the enterprise, pursuant to a **single plan**, is disposing of substantially in its entirety, or disposing of piecemeal, or terminating through abandonment; (b) represents a **separate major line of business or geographical area of operations**; and (c) can be **distinguished operationally and for financial reporting purposes**. AS 24 is a **disclosure** standard — it does not lay down new recognition or measurement principles, which continue to be governed by other applicable Accounting Standards.

**Initial disclosure event:** The enterprise makes the initial disclosures from the **earlier of** the following two dates: (i) the enterprise has entered into a **binding sale agreement** for substantially all of the assets attributable to the discontinuing operation; or (ii) the enterprise's Board of Directors or similar governing body has both **approved a detailed, formal plan** for the discontinuance **and made an announcement** of the plan.

**Initial disclosures required** in the financial statements from that event include:
1. A **description** of the discontinuing operation.
2. The **business or geographical segment(s)** in which it is reported under AS 17.
3. The **date and nature** of the initial disclosure event.
4. The **date or period** in which the discontinuance is expected to be completed, if known.
5. The **carrying amounts**, as of the balance sheet date, of the **total assets and total liabilities** to be disposed of.
6. The amounts of **revenue, expenses, and pre-tax profit or loss** from ordinary activities attributable to the discontinuing operation, and the related **income tax expense**.
7. The amounts of **net cash flows** attributable to the operating, investing and financing activities of the discontinuing operation.

Additionally, when the enterprise disposes of assets or settles liabilities, or enters into binding agreements to do so, it discloses the **gain or loss** recognised and the related income tax, and the **net selling price / expected proceeds** and timing.

**Conclusion:** A discontinuing operation is a separate major line/area being disposed of under a single plan; the enterprise begins the specified disclosures (description, segment, event date, asset/liability carrying amounts, results and cash flows) from the earlier of a binding sale agreement or Board approval-cum-announcement of a formal plan.

### Q32. Ch: AS 29 — Measurement of a Provision at the Best Estimate (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** Solaris Ltd. sells a product carrying a **two-year warranty** against manufacturing defects. During the year ended 31st March, 2026 it sold **1,00,000 units**. Based on past experience the company estimates that: **75%** of the units sold will have **no defect**, **20%** will have **minor defects** costing ₹100 per unit to rectify, and **5%** will have **major defects** costing ₹400 per unit. Compute the **warranty provision** to be recognised at 31st March, 2026 under AS 29, and explain the measurement principle applied.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Measurement principle under AS 29**
As per **AS 29 "Provisions, Contingent Liabilities and Contingent Assets"**, the amount recognised as a provision should be the **best estimate** of the expenditure required to settle the present obligation at the balance sheet date — the amount an enterprise would rationally pay to settle the obligation or transfer it to a third party. Where the provision being measured involves a **large population of items** (as with warranties over many units), the obligation is estimated by weighting **all possible outcomes by their associated probabilities** — the **"expected value"** method. (For a single obligation, the individual **most likely outcome** may be the best estimate.)

**WN-2: Expected cost per unit (expected value)**

| Outcome | Probability | Cost per unit (₹) | Weighted cost (₹) |
|---------|-------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| No defect | 75% (0.75) | 0 | 0 |
| Minor defect | 20% (0.20) | 100 | 20 |
| Major defect | 5% (0.05) | 400 | 20 |
| **Expected cost per unit** | | | **40** |

**WN-3: Warranty provision**
Provision = Units sold × Expected cost per unit = 1,00,000 × ₹40 = **₹40,00,000**

**OR (cross-check by aggregating expected units):**
- Minor defects: 1,00,000 × 20% = 20,000 units × ₹100 = ₹20,00,000
- Major defects: 1,00,000 × 5% = 5,000 units × ₹400 = ₹20,00,000
- Total = ₹20,00,000 + ₹20,00,000 = **₹40,00,000** (agrees)

**Answer:** The warranty provision to be recognised at 31st March, 2026 is **₹40,00,000**, measured as the best estimate using the expected-value (probability-weighted) method appropriate to a large population of warranty obligations.

*(Full-marks tip: State the "best estimate" rule and correctly apply the expected-value method for a large population (contrasting it with the "most likely outcome" for a single obligation). Taking only the most likely outcome — no defect / ₹Nil — or ignoring the probability weighting is the standard error.)*

### Q34. Ch: AS 17 — Identification of Reportable Segments (10% Quantitative Thresholds) (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** Galaxy Ltd. operates through five business segments. The following data (₹ in lakhs) relate to the year ended 31st March, 2026. All revenue shown is external. Identify the **reportable segments** under AS 17, showing the application of each threshold test.

| Segment | Segment revenue | Segment result | Segment assets |
|---------|-----------------|----------------|----------------|

**Solution:**

**WN-1: The three 10% threshold tests (Para 27, AS 17)**
As per **AS 17 "Segment Reporting"**, a business/geographical segment is a **reportable segment** if it satisfies **any one** of the following:
(a) its segment **revenue** (external + inter-segment) is **≥ 10% of total revenue** of all segments;
(b) its segment **result** (profit or loss) is **≥ 10% of the greater**, in absolute amount, of (i) the combined result of all segments in **profit** and (ii) the combined result of all segments in **loss**; or

- Total assets = ₹800 lakh → 10% = **₹80 lakh**

**WN-3: Apply the tests to each segment**

| Segment | Revenue ≥ 60? | Result ≥ 7.5 (abs.)? | Assets ≥ 80? | Reportable? |
|---------|---------------|----------------------|--------------|-------------|
| A | 300 → Yes | 50 → Yes | 400 → Yes | **Yes** |
| B | 120 → Yes | 20 → Yes | 150 → Yes | **Yes** |

| D | 40 → No | 5 → No | 60 → No | **No** |
| E | 60 → Yes | 10 → Yes | 100 → Yes | **Yes** |

### Q35. Ch: AS 3 Cash Flow Statements — Treatment of Interest & Dividend for Financial vs Non-Financial Enterprises (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Under AS 3 "Cash Flow Statements", how are cash flows arising from interest and dividends (both paid and received) to be classified? Explain the treatment separately for a financial enterprise and for a non-financial (other) enterprise, and state the over-riding requirement common to both.

**Answer:**
As per **AS 3 "Cash Flow Statements"**, cash flows from interest and dividends received and paid should each be **disclosed separately** and should be **classified in a consistent manner from period to period** as either operating, investing or financing activities.

The classification differs according to the nature of the enterprise:

| Item | Financial enterprise (bank / NBFC / financial institution) | Non-financial (other) enterprise |
|---|---|---|
| Interest paid | Operating activity (part of its principal revenue-producing operations) | Financing activity (cost of obtaining financial resources) |
| Interest received | Operating activity | Investing activity (return on investments/loans given) |
| Dividend received | Operating activity | Investing activity |
| Dividend paid | Financing activity | Financing activity |

The **rationale** is that for a financial enterprise, interest and dividends form part of its ordinary business of lending and investing, so they are naturally **operating** flows; for other enterprises, interest and dividend received are returns on investments (investing) and interest paid is a cost of raising finance (financing).

The **common over-riding requirement** for both types of enterprises is that **dividend paid is always classified as a financing activity**, and whatever classification is adopted must be applied **consistently** every period, with each item disclosed separately.

**Conclusion:** For a financial enterprise interest/dividend received and interest paid are operating flows; for a non-financial enterprise interest/dividend received is investing and interest paid is financing, while dividend paid is a financing activity for every enterprise.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner rewards the exact four-way split in a table AND the statement that dividend paid is always financing and classification must be consistent; a common deduction is treating "interest received" and "dividend received" identically for a financial enterprise without naming them as operating.)*

### Q40. Ch: Redemption of Preference Shares — Statutory Conditions under Section 55 (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the conditions and sources laid down under **Section 55 of the Companies Act, 2013** (read with the relevant rules) for the redemption of preference shares by a company.

**Answer:**
As per **Section 55 of the Companies Act, 2013**, a company limited by shares may issue preference shares which are **liable to be redeemed** within a period not exceeding **twenty years** from the date of issue (except infrastructure projects, which may exceed twenty years subject to redemption of a minimum percentage annually at the option of the holders). The following conditions govern redemption:

1. **Only fully paid-up shares** can be redeemed [Sec 55(2)(a)]; partly paid preference shares cannot be redeemed.
2. Redemption must be made **either out of the profits of the company which would otherwise be available for dividend, or out of the proceeds of a fresh issue of shares** made for the purpose of redemption [Sec 55(2)(a)].
3. Where redemption is out of **profits**, a sum equal to the **nominal amount** of the shares redeemed must be transferred to the **Capital Redemption Reserve (CRR)** account [Sec 55(2)(c)]; the CRR is to be treated as paid-up capital for all purposes and can be used only to issue fully paid **bonus shares** [Sec 55(4)].
4. Any **premium payable on redemption** must be provided for out of the **profits** of the company or out of the company's **securities premium account** before redemption [Sec 55(2)(d)].

**Conclusion:** Preference shares must be fully paid, and are redeemable only out of distributable profits (with a corresponding transfer to CRR) or the proceeds of a fresh issue, with any premium met from securities premium or profits.

*(Full-marks tip: cite Sec 55(2)(a),(c),(d) and Sec 55(4) by clause; the marks hinge on (a) "fully paid only", (b) the two permitted sources, (c) CRR = nominal value from profits, and (d) premium from securities premium/profits.)*

### Q46. Ch: Buy-back of Securities — Conditions and Prohibitions under Sections 68–70 (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the principal conditions to be fulfilled by a company for **buy-back of its own shares** under **Section 68** of the Companies Act, 2013, and the circumstances in which buy-back is **prohibited** under **Section 70**.

**Answer:**
Under **Section 68 of the Companies Act, 2013**, a company may buy back its own shares or specified securities out of (i) its **free reserves**, (ii) the **securities premium account**, or (iii) the **proceeds of an issue of any shares or specified securities** (but not out of the proceeds of an earlier issue of the **same kind** of shares/securities). The principal conditions are:

1. Buy-back must be **authorised by the Articles** [Sec 68(2)(a)].
2. A **special resolution** in general meeting is required; however, buy-back **up to 10%** of paid-up equity capital and free reserves can be authorised by a **Board resolution** [Sec 68(2)(b)].
3. Buy-back in a financial year shall **not exceed 25%** of the aggregate of **paid-up capital and free reserves**; for equity shares, the 25% is with reference to **paid-up equity capital** in that year [Sec 68(2)(c)].
4. The **debt-equity ratio after buy-back** shall not exceed **2 : 1** (debt ≤ twice the paid-up capital plus free reserves) [Sec 68(2)(d)].
5. All shares/securities bought back must be **fully paid-up** [Sec 68(2)(e)].
6. There must be a gap of at least **one year** between two buy-backs, and the buy-back must be **completed within one year** of the resolution [Sec 68(4)].

Under **Section 70**, a company **shall not** buy back its securities (a) through any **subsidiary** company, (b) through any **investment company** or group of investment companies, or (c) if it is in **default** in repayment of deposits, redemption of debentures/preference shares, payment of dividend, or repayment of term loans (unless the default has been remedied and three years have elapsed), or has defaulted in filing financial statements/annual returns.

**Conclusion:** Buy-back is permitted only from the specified sources subject to the 25% and 2:1 limits, fully-paid condition and Board/special-resolution authority, and is prohibited in the situations listed in Section 70.

*(Full-marks tip: quote Sec 68(2)(a)–(e), 68(4) and Sec 70; the two most-rewarded figures are the "25% of paid-up capital + free reserves" and the "2:1 post-buy-back debt-equity" limits, plus the Sec 70 default-based prohibitions.)*

### Q51. Ch: Buy-back of Securities — Distinction from Redemption of Preference Shares (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between **buy-back of equity shares** (Section 68) and **redemption of preference shares** (Section 55) of the Companies Act, 2013. Also state whether a company can buy back its shares out of the proceeds of an earlier issue of the **same kind** of shares.

**Answer:**
Both buy-back and redemption result in a company returning capital to shareholders and creating a Capital Redemption Reserve, but they differ as follows:

| Basis | Buy-back of equity shares (Sec 68) | Redemption of preference shares (Sec 55) |
|---|---|---|
| Nature of shares | Any shares/specified securities, ordinarily **equity** | Only **preference** shares that are issued as redeemable |
| Terms of issue | Shares are **not** issued as buy-back-able; buy-back is a later decision | Shares are issued as **redeemable** from the outset |
| Authority | **Special resolution** (or Board up to 10%) + Articles [Sec 68] | Terms of issue; no special resolution needed if terms provide |
| Quantitative limits | 25% of capital + free reserves; 2 : 1 post-buy-back debt-equity | No such 25% / 2 : 1 limits |
| Sources | Free reserves, securities premium, proceeds of a fresh issue | Profits available for dividend, or proceeds of a fresh issue |
| CRR | Nominal value bought back (net of fresh issue) → CRR [Sec 69] | Nominal value redeemed out of profits → CRR [Sec 55] |
| Cancellation | Shares bought back are **extinguished** and cannot be re-issued | Preference shares stand redeemed |

**Same-kind proceeds:** As per the **proviso to Section 68(1)**, a company **cannot buy back its shares or specified securities out of the proceeds of an earlier issue of the same kind of shares or same kind of specified securities**. Thus equity shares cannot be bought back out of the proceeds of a fresh issue of equity shares, though they may be bought back out of the proceeds of a fresh issue of preference shares or debentures.

**Conclusion:** Buy-back is a discretionary reduction of (usually equity) capital hedged by the 25% and 2:1 limits, whereas redemption is the honouring of the original redeemable terms of preference shares; and buy-back can never be financed by a fresh issue of the same kind of security.

*(Full-marks tip: a well-structured comparison table plus the proviso to Sec 68(1) about "same kind" proceeds secures full marks; the most-missed point is that buy-back carries the 25%/2:1 limits and cancels the shares, whereas redemption does not carry those quantitative limits.)*

### Q53. Ch: Issue of Bonus Shares — Conditions under Section 63 (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the conditions that a company must satisfy before it can capitalise its profits by way of an issue of fully-paid bonus shares under **Section 63 of the Companies Act, 2013**.

**Answer:**
A company may issue fully-paid bonus shares to its members only out of the sources and subject to the conditions laid down in **Section 63 of the Companies Act, 2013**, read with **Rule 14 of the Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules, 2014**.

1. **Permitted sources [Sec 63(1)]:** Bonus shares may be issued only out of (i) its **free reserves**, (ii) the **securities premium account**, or (iii) the **capital redemption reserve account**.
2. **Prohibited source:** No issue of bonus shares shall be made by **capitalising reserves created by the revaluation of assets** [Sec 63(1) proviso], because such reserves are unrealised.
3. **Authorisation:** The bonus issue must be **authorised by the articles** of the company; it must be **recommended by the Board** and thereafter **authorised in general meeting** [Sec 63(2)(a) & (b)].
4. **No default:** The company must **not have defaulted** in payment of interest or principal on **fixed deposits or debt securities**, nor in payment of **statutory dues of employees** (PF, gratuity, bonus) [Sec 63(2)(c) & (d)].
5. **Partly-paid shares made fully paid:** The **partly paid-up shares, if any, outstanding on the date of allotment, must be made fully paid-up** before the bonus issue [Sec 63(2)(e)].
6. **Not in lieu of dividend:** Bonus shares shall **not be issued in lieu of dividend** [Rule 14].
7. **Irrevocable:** Once the Board recommends and members approve a bonus issue, the decision **cannot be withdrawn** [Sec 63(3)].

**Conclusion:** A bonus issue that meets the source test, the no-default test and the procedural/partly-paid conditions of Section 63 is valid; capitalisation of a revaluation reserve or issue in lieu of dividend is void.

*(Full-marks tip: quoting the exact clause numbers of Section 63(2)(a)–(e) and the revaluation-reserve prohibition is what separates a 4/4 from a 2/4.)*

### Q55. Ch: Rights Issue — Theoretical ex-rights price and value of a right (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** **Palash Ltd.** offers a rights issue of **one new share for every four shares held** at a subscription price of **₹30 per share**. The shares are currently quoted **cum-rights at ₹50 per share**. Compute (a) the theoretical ex-rights price, (b) the value of a right, and (c) show the effect on a shareholder holding **400 shares** who (i) exercises the rights, (ii) sells the rights, and (iii) allows the rights to lapse.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Theoretical Ex-Rights Price (TERP)**
Take the smallest representative parcel = 4 existing + 1 new = 5 shares.

TERP = (Existing shares × cum-rights price + New shares × issue price) ÷ Total shares
= (4 × ₹50 + 1 × ₹30) ÷ 5 = (200 + 30) ÷ 5 = **₹46 per share**.

**WN-2 — Value of a right**
Value of the right attaching to **one existing share** = Cum-rights price − TERP = ₹50 − ₹46 = **₹4 per share held**.
*Cross-check* (per new share): TERP − Issue price = ₹46 − ₹30 = ₹16, i.e., ₹16 ÷ 4 existing shares = **₹4 per share held** — consistent.

**WN-3 — Effect on a holder of 400 shares** (entitlement = 400 ÷ 4 = 100 new shares)

| Course of action | Working | Wealth (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| Wealth before issue | 400 × ₹50 | 20,000 |
| **(i) Exercise** | 500 shares × ₹46 = 23,000; less cash paid 100 × ₹30 = 3,000 | 20,000 |
| **(ii) Sell the rights** | 400 shares × ₹46 = 18,400 + rights sold 400 × ₹4 = 1,600 | 20,000 |
| **(iii) Let rights lapse** | 400 shares × ₹46 = 18,400 (nothing received for rights) | 18,400 |

**Answer:** TERP = **₹46**, value of a right = **₹4 per share held**. Exercising or selling the rights preserves the shareholder's wealth at ₹20,000; letting the rights lapse causes a **loss of ₹1,600** (400 × ₹4).

*(Full-marks tip: examiners award the last mark for demonstrating that "sell" and "exercise" leave wealth unchanged while "lapse" destroys value equal to the right — the whole economic point of a rights issue.)*

### Q57. Ch: Rights Issue — Statutory provisions under Section 62(1)(a) (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the provisions of **Section 62(1)(a) of the Companies Act, 2013** relating to a further issue of capital to existing equity shareholders (rights issue).

**Answer:**
Where at any time a company having a share capital proposes to increase its subscribed capital by a **further issue of shares**, **Section 62(1)(a)** requires that such shares be **offered first to the existing equity shareholders** in proportion to their paid-up holdings, subject to the following conditions:

1. **Proportionate offer [Sec 62(1)(a)]:** The offer must be made to persons who are equity shareholders **in proportion, as nearly as circumstances admit, to the paid-up share capital** on those shares as on the date of the offer.
2. **Letter of offer and time limit [Sec 62(1)(a)(i)]:** The offer is made by a **notice (letter of offer)** specifying the number of shares offered and giving the shareholder **not less than fifteen days and not more than thirty days** to accept, failing which the offer is deemed **declined**.
3. **Right of renunciation [Sec 62(1)(a)(ii)]:** Unless the articles provide otherwise, the offer is **deemed to include a right to renounce** the shares offered in favour of any other person.
4. **Disposal of shares not taken up [Sec 62(1)(a)(iii)]:** After the expiry of the time, or on receipt of an earlier intimation of decline, the **Board may dispose of the un-accepted shares in such manner which is not disadvantageous to the shareholders and the company**.
5. **Exceptions:** The rights procedure is **not mandatory** where shares are issued to employees under an **ESOP** [Sec 62(1)(b)] or to any persons by a **special resolution** on a preferential/private basis [Sec 62(1)(c)].

**Conclusion:** A rights issue protects the existing shareholders' proportionate ownership; the company may go outside the members only after they decline or after the 15–30 day window lapses.

*(Full-marks tip: the "15 to 30 days" window and the "not disadvantageous" disposal power are the two facts examiners look for; also mention the ESOP and special-resolution carve-outs to show the full picture.)*

### Q59. Ch: Employee Stock Option Plans — Fair value vs. intrinsic value method (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between the **fair value method** and the **intrinsic value method** of accounting for employee stock options, and explain the meaning of *grant date, vesting period* and *exercise period*.

**Answer:**
Under the **Guidance Note on Accounting for Employee Share-based Payments**, an enterprise recognises the services received from employees as an expense over the vesting period, measured either by the fair value method or the intrinsic value method.

- **Grant date:** the date on which the enterprise and the employees agree to the terms of the share-based payment arrangement.
- **Vesting period:** the period between the grant date and the date on which the employee becomes **unconditionally entitled** to the options (i.e., all vesting conditions are satisfied). The compensation cost is spread over this period.
- **Exercise period:** the period, **after vesting**, within which the vested options may be exercised; options not exercised within it **lapse**.

| Basis | Fair Value Method | Intrinsic Value Method |
|---|---|---|
| Measure of cost | Fair value of the option (an option-pricing model, e.g., Black–Scholes) | Excess of market price of the share over the exercise price at the measurement date |
| Recognition of time value | Captures the option's **time value** as well | Ignores time value; only the "in-the-money" element |
| Amount of expense | Generally **higher and more complete** | Often lower, may be **nil** for at-the-money options |
| Preference | **Recommended/preferred** method | Permitted, but the P&L impact of fair value must then be **disclosed** by way of a pro-forma note |

**Conclusion:** Both methods amortise the option cost over the vesting period, but the fair value method reflects the full economic cost of the option and is the preferred basis; an enterprise using the intrinsic value method must disclose the fair-value impact.

*(Full-marks tip: the disclosure requirement attaching to the intrinsic value method — pro-forma fair-value effect on profit and EPS — is the point most candidates forget.)*

### Q62. Ch: Underwriting of Shares & Debentures — Concepts and commission (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the terms **underwriting, marked and unmarked applications, firm underwriting**, and state the **maximum underwriting commission** payable under the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:**
**Underwriting** is a contract by which the **underwriter agrees to take up the shares or debentures not subscribed for by the public**, in return for a commission, thereby guaranteeing the company a minimum subscription.

- **Marked applications:** applications that **bear the stamp/name of a particular underwriter**, showing that they were procured through that underwriter's efforts; they are credited to that specific underwriter.
- **Unmarked applications:** applications received **directly by the company** without any underwriter's stamp; in a fully underwritten issue they are distributed among all underwriters in their **gross-liability ratio**.
- **Firm underwriting:** a definite commitment by the underwriter to **take up a stated number of shares/debentures irrespective of the public response**, over and above the liability arising from the underwriting agreement.

**Maximum commission [Section 40(6) read with Rule 13 of the Companies (Prospectus and Allotment of Securities) Rules, 2014]:**

| Security | Maximum rate | Base |
|---|---|---|
| Shares | **5%** (or the rate in the articles, whichever is less) | Price at which shares are issued |
| Debentures | **2.5%** (or the rate in the articles, whichever is less) | Price at which debentures are issued |

Commission may be paid only if authorised by the articles and disclosed in the prospectus, and is payable only on securities **actually offered to the public for subscription**.

**Conclusion:** Underwriting shifts the risk of under-subscription to the underwriter; marked/unmarked/firm classifications drive the allocation of the shortfall, and the commission is capped at 5% (shares) and 2.5% (debentures).

*(Full-marks tip: state both the percentage caps and the "or as per articles, whichever is less" qualification — examiners deduct a mark for quoting the flat 5%/2.5% without it.)*

### Q63. Ch: Underwriting of Shares — Partial underwriting with commission (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** ** Satpura Ltd.** issued **2,00,000 equity shares of ₹10 each at par**. The issue was underwritten as to: **X — 60%** and **Y — 20%**; the balance **20%** was **not underwritten** and is retained by the company. Applications received were: **marked** — X 90,000, Y 25,000; **unmarked** — 35,000. Underwriting commission is **5%**. Determine the net liability of each underwriter and the net cash payable to/receivable from each.

**Solution:** As the issue is **only partially underwritten**, the company itself is treated as the underwriter for the un-underwritten 20% (40,000 shares). By convention, in a partial underwriting the benefit of **unmarked applications is credited to the company**, not to the underwriters.

**WN-1 — Gross liability**
X = 60% × 2,00,000 = 1,20,000; Y = 20% × 2,00,000 = 40,000; Company = 20% × 2,00,000 = 40,000.

**WN-2 — Total applications and shortfall**
Marked 90,000 + 25,000 + Unmarked 35,000 = 1,50,000 → shortfall = **50,000 shares**.

**WN-3 — Statement of net liability** *(in shares)*

| Particulars | X | Y | Company | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gross liability | 1,20,000 | 40,000 | 40,000 | 2,00,000 |
| Less: Unmarked (credited to company) | — | — | 35,000 | 35,000 |
| Less: Marked applications | 90,000 | 25,000 | — | 1,15,000 |
| **Net liability (shortfall borne)** | **30,000** | **15,000** | **5,000** | **50,000** |

**WN-4 — Underwriting commission (5% on gross shares underwritten, at ₹10)**
X = 5% × (1,20,000 × ₹10) = ₹60,000; Y = 5% × (40,000 × ₹10) = ₹20,000. (No commission on the company's own 20%.)

**WN-5 — Net cash from each underwriter**

| Particulars | X (₹) | Y (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| Value of shares to be taken (net liability × ₹10) | 3,00,000 | 1,50,000 |
| Less: Underwriting commission | 60,000 | 20,000 |
| **Net cash payable by underwriter to company** | **2,40,000** | **1,30,000** |

**Answer:** Net liabilities — **X 30,000 shares, Y 15,000 shares, Company 5,000 shares**. X pays the company **₹2,40,000** and Y pays **₹1,30,000** (each after adjusting commission).

*(Full-marks tip: the crux of a partial underwriting is that **unmarked applications go to the company** (its un-underwritten portion), not shared among underwriters — using them to reduce X and Y's liability is the classic error that unravels the whole answer.)*

### Q67. Ch: Redemption of Debentures — DRR and Debenture Redemption Investment (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the provisions relating to the creation of a **Debenture Redemption Reserve (DRR)** and the **Debenture Redemption Investment** under **Section 71(4) of the Companies Act, 2013** read with **Rule 18(7)** of the Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules, 2014 (as amended in 2019).

**Answer:**
Under **Section 71(4)**, where debentures are issued by a company, it must **create a Debenture Redemption Reserve account out of the profits available for dividend**, and the amount so credited shall be utilised only for the redemption of debentures. **Rule 18(7)** (as amended by the Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Amendment Rules, 2019) prescribes:

1. **DRR quantum:**
   - **Listed companies, All-India Financial Institutions, banking companies, and NBFCs/HFCs registered with the RBI/NHB** — **no DRR** is required to be created.
   - **Other (unlisted) companies** — DRR of **at least 10% of the outstanding value of debentures** to be created.
2. **Debenture Redemption Investment (the "15% rule"):** Every company **required to create DRR**, and additionally **listed NBFCs/HFCs and other listed companies** issuing debentures through a **public issue**, must, on or before **30th April of each year**, deposit or invest a sum **not less than 15% of the amount of debentures maturing during the year ending 31st March next following**, in the specified securities (bank deposits, government securities, etc.).
3. **Ring-fencing:** The investment so made must **not fall below 15%** of the debentures maturing during the year and can be used only to redeem the debentures maturing that year.

**Conclusion:** Post-2019, DRR (10% of outstanding value) is confined largely to **unlisted non-exempt companies**, while the **15% redemption investment** by 30th April operates for the categories specified in Rule 18(7).

*(Full-marks tip: after the 2019 amendment, listed companies need **no DRR**; quoting the pre-amendment "25%/DRR for all" attracts a deduction. Mention both the 10% DRR base and the 15% investment base clearly.)*

### Q71. Ch: Investment Accounts — Accounting for Rights Shares: Subscribed vs Renounced (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the accounting treatment, in the books of an investor, of **rights shares** offered by a company (i) when the rights are subscribed, (ii) when the rights are renounced (sold), and (iii) when part is subscribed and part renounced, distinguishing the position for pre-acquisition and post-acquisition situations.

**Answer:**
A **rights issue** is an offer of further shares to existing shareholders in proportion to their holding, at a price usually below market. Its treatment follows the cost principle of **AS 13, Accounting for Investments**.

(i) **Where the rights are subscribed** — the amount paid on subscription is a genuine acquisition of additional shares and is **added to the cost (carrying amount) of the investment**. The nominal value and the cost columns of the Investment Account are both increased.

(ii) **Where the rights are renounced (sold in the market)** — the shareholder does not acquire the shares; he only realises the value of the right attached to his existing holding. The **sale proceeds of the renounced rights are credited to the Profit & Loss Account** and are **not** entered in the cost column of the Investment Account, because no additional investment cost has been incurred.

(iii) **Where part is subscribed and part renounced** — the two limbs are treated separately: the subscription price of the part taken up is added to cost, while the proceeds of the part sold are credited to P&L.

**Exception — pre-acquisition situation:** if the rights relate to shares **acquired cum-right shortly before the record date** and the market price falls appreciably below cost immediately after the rights (indicating that part of the price paid was really for the right), it is appropriate to **apply the sale proceeds of the right to reduce the carrying amount** of the investment rather than credit them to income.

**Conclusion:** Subscription money increases cost; proceeds of renounced rights are ordinarily P&L income, save where they represent a recovery of a pre-acquisition cost.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner looks for the explicit "credit to P&L for renounced rights" rule plus the pre-acquisition exception. Common deduction: treating renounced-rights proceeds as a reduction of cost in all cases.)*

### Q73. Ch: Investment Accounts — Reclassification of Investments under AS 13 (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** M Ltd. holds the following investments. During the year it decides to reclassify them. Determine the value at which each transfer should be recorded and pass the necessary treatment, citing **AS 13**.

| Case | Investment | Original cost (₹) | Fair value / carrying amount on date of transfer (₹) | Reclassification |
|---|---|---:|---:|---|
| A | 5,000 shares of R Ltd. | 1,00,000 | Fair value 85,000 | Long-term → Current |
| B | 4,000 shares of S Ltd. | 60,000 | Fair value 92,000 | Long-term → Current |
| C | 2,000 shares of T Ltd. | 40,000 | Carrying amount 34,000 (already written down) | Current → Long-term |
| D | 3,000 shares of U Ltd. | 50,000 | Carrying amount (cost) 50,000; fair value 63,000 | Current → Long-term |

**Transfer from long-term to current investments** — recorded at the **lower of cost and carrying amount** at the date of transfer.
- **Case A:** carrying amount = cost ₹1,00,000 (a long-term investment is carried at cost unless there is a permanent diminution). Lower of cost (₹1,00,000) and carrying amount (₹1,00,000) = **₹1,00,000**. (The fall to ₹85,000 is a temporary market fall and does not reduce a long-term carrying amount.)

### Q76. Ch: Internal Reconstruction — Internal vs External Reconstruction (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between **internal reconstruction** and **external reconstruction**.

**Answer:**
**Reconstruction** is the process of reorganising a company's capital structure to eliminate accumulated losses and over-valued assets and to place it on a sound financial footing. It may be internal or external.

| Basis | Internal Reconstruction | External Reconstruction |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Reorganisation of the **same company's** capital without winding it up | The existing company is **liquidated** and a **new company** is formed to take over its business |
| New company | No new company is formed | A new company comes into existence |
| Legal process | Carried out through a scheme under **Sections 66 / 230–232 of the Companies Act, 2013** (capital reduction / compromise & arrangement) | Involves **amalgamation/absorption** — accounting per **AS 14** |
| Effect on entity | Legal entity continues unchanged | Old entity ceases; a new legal entity continues the business |
| Approvals | Requires approval of members/creditors and confirmation of the Tribunal (NCLT) | Requires approval of shareholders of both companies and the Tribunal |
| Loss write-off | Accumulated losses written off against the surplus (Capital Reduction A/c) | Losses of the old company do not pass to the new company's books |
| Goodwill/Capital Reserve | Not relevant; surplus goes to Capital Reserve | Difference between purchase consideration and net assets = goodwill / capital reserve |

**Conclusion:** Internal reconstruction keeps the same company alive by reducing/rearranging its capital, whereas external reconstruction transfers the business to a newly formed company.

*(Full-marks tip: the pivotal distinction the examiner marks is "same entity continues vs new company formed." Common deduction: describing only the accounting and omitting the statutory route — Sec 66 vs AS 14.)*

### Q78. Ch: Internal Reconstruction — Treatment of Reconstruction Surplus & Crystallising Contingent Liability (Marks: 5) [Application]

| Less: Damages settled (contingent liability crystallised) | (90,000) |
| Less: Provision for bad debt | (20,000) |

**Conclusion:** The reconstruction surplus is a capital profit taken to Capital Reserve; the ₹90,000 and ₹20,000 are absorbed against it, leaving **₹1,40,000** to Capital Reserve.

### Q79. Ch: Amalgamation of Companies (AS 14) — Merger vs Purchase Method (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between the **Pooling of Interests Method** (amalgamation in the nature of merger) and the **Purchase Method** (amalgamation in the nature of purchase) under **AS 14**, with reference to recording of assets/liabilities, reserves and goodwill.

**Answer:**
**AS 14, Accounting for Amalgamations**, prescribes two methods depending on whether the amalgamation is in the nature of **merger** or **purchase**.

| Basis | Pooling of Interests (Merger) | Purchase Method |
|---|---|---|
| When applied | When **all five conditions** of an amalgamation in the nature of merger are satisfied | When any one condition is not satisfied — i.e. amalgamation in the nature of purchase |
| Recording of assets & liabilities | Recorded at their **existing book (carrying) values** in the transferor's books | Recorded either at existing book values or at **revalued (agreed) amounts** |
| Reserves of transferor | **All reserves** (general reserve, statutory reserves, P&L balance) are carried forward and appear in the transferee's books in the same form | Only **statutory reserves** are carried (via Amalgamation Adjustment Reserve); other reserves are **not** carried forward |
| Difference on amalgamation | Difference between purchase consideration and transferor's share capital is **adjusted in reserves** | Difference between purchase consideration and net assets taken over is **Goodwill (if PC higher) or Capital Reserve (if PC lower)** |
| Goodwill | **No goodwill** arises | Goodwill may arise, and is **amortised over its useful life** (not exceeding five years, per AS 14) |
| Nature | A genuine **uniting/continuation** of two businesses and shareholder groups | Acquisition of one company by another |

**Conclusion:** The pooling method preserves book values and reserves as a continuation of interests, whereas the purchase method treats the transaction as an acquisition, recognising goodwill or capital reserve.

*(Full-marks tip: the two examiner-critical contrasts are (i) reserves carried forward vs not, and (ii) no goodwill vs goodwill/capital reserve. Common deduction: saying purchase method always uses revalued figures — book values are also permissible.)*

### Q83. Ch: Amalgamation of Companies (AS 14) — Conditions for Amalgamation in the Nature of Merger (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the **five conditions** that must be satisfied under **AS 14** for an amalgamation to be treated as an **amalgamation in the nature of merger**.

**Answer:**
Under **AS 14, Accounting for Amalgamations**, an amalgamation is treated as being **in the nature of merger** only if **all** of the following five conditions are satisfied:

1. **All the assets and liabilities** of the transferor company become, after amalgamation, the assets and liabilities of the transferee company.

2. **Shareholders holding not less than 90% of the face value of the equity shares** of the transferor company (other than shares already held by the transferee, or its subsidiaries or their nominees) become **equity shareholders of the transferee** company.

3. The **consideration** payable to the equity shareholders of the transferor company (who agree to become equity shareholders of the transferee) is **discharged wholly by the issue of equity shares** of the transferee company, **except** that cash may be paid in respect of **fractional shares**.

4. The **business** of the transferor company is **intended to be carried on**, after the amalgamation, by the transferee company.

5. **No adjustment is intended** to be made to the **book values** of the assets and liabilities of the transferor company when they are incorporated in the financial statements of the transferee, except to ensure uniformity of accounting policies.

**Conclusion:** If any one of these conditions is not met, the amalgamation is treated as being **in the nature of purchase** and the purchase method applies.

*(Full-marks tip: the 90% threshold, the "wholly by equity shares (except fractions)" condition, and "no adjustment to book values" are the three most frequently mis-stated. Common deduction: writing "75%" instead of "90%", or omitting the fractional-share cash exception.)*

### Q84. Ch: Amalgamation of Companies (AS 14) — Inter-Company Owings & Unrealised Profit on Stock (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** H Ltd. takes over the business of S Ltd. by way of amalgamation in the nature of purchase. At the date of amalgamation the following inter-company items exist: (a) S Ltd. owed H Ltd. ₹30,000 on trade account; (b) H Ltd. had accepted a bill of ₹10,000 drawn by S Ltd., which S Ltd. still holds; (c) the inventory of H Ltd. includes goods purchased from S Ltd. for ₹25,000, on which S Ltd. had earned a profit of **₹5,000**. Explain the adjustments required in the books of H Ltd. after the amalgamation, and pass the necessary entries.

**Answer:**
When two companies amalgamate into a single entity, **mutual owings and unrealised profit on inter-company stock must be eliminated**, because a company cannot owe money to, or make a profit out of, itself. These adjustments are made **after** the assets and liabilities of the transferor (S Ltd.) have been incorporated in H Ltd.'s books.

**(a) Inter-company trade balance (₹30,000):** On takeover, H Ltd. records S Ltd.'s trade receivable of ₹30,000, but H Ltd. also carries a trade payable of ₹30,000 to S Ltd. These are **mutual balances** that must be **cancelled**:
> Trade Payables (Sundry Creditors) A/c ... Dr. ₹30,000
> &nbsp;&nbsp;To Trade Receivables (Sundry Debtors) A/c ₹30,000

**(b) Bills payable / bills receivable (₹10,000):** After amalgamation the ₹10,000 **Bills Payable** in H Ltd.'s books and the ₹10,000 **Bills Receivable** taken over from S Ltd. are held by the same entity and must be **cancelled**:
> Bills Payable A/c ... Dr. ₹10,000
> &nbsp;&nbsp;To Bills Receivable A/c ₹10,000

**(c) Unrealised profit on stock (₹5,000):** The goods bought from S Ltd. remain unsold in H Ltd.'s inventory; the ₹5,000 profit loaded by S Ltd. is **unrealised** from the combined entity's viewpoint. It is eliminated by creating a **Stock Reserve** (charged against Goodwill/Capital Reserve, or against P&L/General Reserve as appropriate) and reducing the value of the stock:
> Goodwill / Capital Reserve (or P&L) A/c ... Dr. ₹5,000
> &nbsp;&nbsp;To Stock Reserve A/c ₹5,000
> *(Being unrealised profit on inter-company stock eliminated; the Stock Reserve is deducted from Inventory in the Balance Sheet.)*

**Conclusion:** After amalgamation, mutual trade balances (₹30,000) and bills (₹10,000) are cancelled against each other, and the ₹5,000 unrealised profit is removed by a Stock Reserve, so that the amalgamated Balance Sheet reflects only genuine external assets, liabilities and realised profits.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner rewards recognising that these adjustments come AFTER incorporation and that unrealised profit is on the UNSOLD portion only (here the whole ₹5,000, as all goods remain in stock). Common deduction: netting the debt inside the purchase consideration, or eliminating the full ₹25,000 stock value instead of only the ₹5,000 profit element.)*

### Q87. Ch: Branch Accounts — Debtors System vs Stock & Debtors System (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between the **Debtors System** and the **Stock and Debtors System** of maintaining accounts of a dependent branch.

**Answer:**
A dependent branch does not keep its own double-entry books; the Head Office records all branch transactions. Two methods are commonly used to ascertain branch profit and to exercise control over branch stock.

Under the **Debtors System**, a single **Branch Account** (nominal-cum-personal) is prepared. All opening balances, goods sent, expenses and closing balances are entered in this one account and the **balancing figure gives the branch profit or loss**. It is simple and suited to small branches, but it does not by itself reveal any shortage or surplus of stock.

Under the **Stock and Debtors System**, the Head Office opens several accounts — **Branch Stock A/c, Branch Debtors A/c, Branch Expenses A/c, Branch Adjustment A/c (for loading) and Branch Profit & Loss A/c**. The Branch Stock Account is maintained at selling price and any difference between the book balance and physical balance discloses **stock shortage or surplus**, giving stronger internal control.

| Basis | Debtors System | Stock and Debtors System |
|---|---|---|
| Number of accounts | One (Branch A/c) | Several (Stock, Debtors, Adjustment, Expenses, P&L) |
| Profit ascertainment | Balancing figure of Branch A/c | Through Branch Adjustment & Branch P&L A/c |
| Stock control | Shortage/surplus not disclosed | Shortage/surplus disclosed by Branch Stock A/c |
| Suitability | Small branches, limited transactions | Large branches, tighter control needed |
| Treatment of loading | Adjusted within the single account | Routed through Branch Adjustment A/c |

**Conclusion:** Both methods yield the same branch profit; the Stock and Debtors System is preferred where the Head Office needs continuous control over branch stock and disclosure of shortages.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the point that only the Stock & Debtors System discloses stock shortage/surplus. Common deduction: describing accounts without stating the control advantage.)*

### Q90. Ch: Departmental Accounts — Departmental Accounts vs Branch Accounts (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain how **Departmental Accounts** differ from **Branch Accounts**, and state any two situations that make departmental accounting desirable.

**Answer:**
**Departmental accounts** are prepared where a single business is carried on under **one roof** but is divided into several departments dealing in different lines, and the management wishes to know the trading result of each department separately. **Branch accounts** are prepared where a business operates through units situated at **different locations** (branches), and the profitability and control of each geographically separate unit is to be assessed.

| Basis | Departmental Accounts | Branch Accounts |
|---|---|---|
| Location | All departments under one roof | Branches at different places |
| Accounting records | One set of books; results split department-wise | Branch may or may not keep its own books |
| Basis of allocation | Common expenses apportioned on suitable bases | Expenses usually identified branch-wise |
| Inter-unit transfers | Inter-departmental transfers at cost/transfer price | Goods sent to branch at cost/invoice price |
| Currency/translation | Not involved | Foreign branch needs AS 11 conversion |
| Purpose | Compare relative efficiency of departments | Control over remote units and remittances |

**Desirability of departmental accounting:** (i) to judge the relative profitability and efficiency of each line of activity so that unprofitable departments can be closed or improved; (ii) to compute departmental commission/remuneration payable to departmental managers on a fair basis.

**Conclusion:** Both aim at unit-wise profit measurement, but departmental accounting works within one entity through apportionment, whereas branch accounting deals with physically separate units and may require currency translation.

*(Full-marks tip: The comparison table and the two "desirability" points together fetch full marks. Common deduction: giving only definitions without contrasting the two on stated bases.)*

### Q93. Ch: Consolidated Financial Statements — Meaning of "Control" & Consolidation Procedure (AS 21) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the concept of **"control"** under AS 21 and state the principal procedures to be followed in preparing consolidated financial statements. When may a subsidiary be **excluded** from consolidation?

**Answer:**
As per **AS 21 "Consolidated Financial Statements,"** a **subsidiary** is an enterprise that is controlled by another enterprise (the parent). **Control** means either (a) ownership, directly or indirectly through subsidiary(ies), of **more than one-half of the voting power** of an enterprise; or (b) **control of the composition of the board of directors** (governing body) so as to obtain economic benefits from its activities.

The **consolidation procedure** requires the parent to combine its financial statements with those of its subsidiaries on a **line-by-line basis** by adding together like items of assets, liabilities, income and expenses. In doing so:
- The **cost of the parent's investment** in the subsidiary is eliminated against the parent's portion of equity of the subsidiary at the date of investment; any excess is **goodwill** and any deficiency is **capital reserve** (para 13).
- **Minority interest** in the net income and net assets of subsidiaries is presented separately (paras 21–24).
- **Intra-group balances and intra-group transactions**, and the resulting **unrealised profits**, are eliminated in full (para 16).
- Financial statements used should ordinarily be drawn up to the **same reporting date** and using **uniform accounting policies** (paras 18–20).

A subsidiary is **excluded** from consolidation where (i) **control is intended to be temporary** because the subsidiary is acquired and held exclusively with a view to its subsequent disposal in the near future; or (ii) the subsidiary operates under **severe long-term restrictions** that significantly impair its ability to transfer funds to the parent. Investments in such subsidiaries are accounted for as per **AS 13**.

**Conclusion:** Consolidation gives a true and fair view of the group as a single economic entity, achieved by combining line-by-line, eliminating the investment against equity, and separately showing minority interest.

*(Full-marks tip: State both limbs of "control" (voting power AND board composition) and the two exclusion grounds. Common deduction: mentioning only the >50% voting-power test.)*

### Q96. Ch: Accounting for LLP — LLP vs Traditional Partnership Firm (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between a **Limited Liability Partnership (LLP)** and a **traditional partnership firm**, and state why LLP accounting broadly follows partnership accounting.

**Answer:**
A **Limited Liability Partnership** is a body corporate formed and registered under the **LLP Act, 2008**, whereas a **traditional partnership firm** is governed by the **Indian Partnership Act, 1932**. Although both are vehicles for carrying on business collectively, they differ materially in legal status and liability.

| Basis | Limited Liability Partnership (LLP Act, 2008) | Partnership Firm (Partnership Act, 1932) |
|---|---|---|
| Legal status | Separate legal entity / body corporate | Not a separate legal entity from partners |
| Liability of partners | Limited to agreed contribution | Unlimited, joint and several |
| Perpetual succession | Exists; unaffected by change in partners | No perpetual succession |
| Registration | Compulsory with Registrar of Companies | Optional under the 1932 Act |
| Minimum/maximum partners | Minimum 2; no maximum limit | Minimum 2; maximum 50 (Rule, Companies Act) |
| Designated partners | At least two, one resident in India | No such concept |
| Governing document | LLP Agreement | Partnership Deed |

**Why LLP accounting follows partnership accounting:** An LLP earns and distributes profits among partners in an agreed ratio, maintains **capital and current accounts** for each partner, and on reconstitution (admission, retirement, dissolution) makes adjustments for goodwill, revaluation and accumulated reserves — **exactly as in a partnership firm**. Hence the substance of accounting entries is identical; only the legal framework and disclosure/regulatory filings differ.

**Conclusion:** An LLP combines the operational flexibility of a partnership with the limited liability and perpetual succession of a company, yet its internal accounting for partners follows established partnership principles.

### Q97. Ch: Company Financial Statements — Schedule III Division I: Presentation & Disclosure (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** State the **General Instructions** governing the preparation of the Balance Sheet under **Schedule III, Division I** of the Companies Act, 2013, and the key disclosure requirements a company must observe.

**Answer:**
**Division I of Schedule III** prescribes the form of the Balance Sheet and Statement of Profit & Loss for a company whose financial statements are prepared in accordance with the **Accounting Standards (AS)**. Its General Instructions require the following:

1. **Vertical form only** — the Balance Sheet must be presented in the prescribed **vertical format**; the horizontal form is not permitted.
2. **Current / non-current classification** — every asset and liability must be classified as **current or non-current** based on the criteria of realisation/settlement within the **operating cycle** or within **twelve months** after the reporting date; where the operating cycle cannot be identified, it is assumed to be **twelve months**.
3. **Rounding off** — figures may be rounded off based on **"Total Income"**: where total income is **less than ₹100 crore**, to the nearest hundreds, thousands, lakhs or millions; where **₹100 crore or more**, to the nearest lakhs, millions or crores. The rounding-off unit must be **used uniformly**.
4. **Comparatives** — corresponding figures for the **immediately preceding reporting period** must be disclosed for all items.
5. **Notes to accounts** — each item on the face of the Balance Sheet must be **cross-referenced to the related note**, and any additional information material to an understanding must be disclosed in the notes.
6. **No netting off** — an asset and a liability, or income and expense, must **not be set off** unless permitted by an Accounting Standard.

**Key disclosures** include, among others: the **classes of share capital** with reconciliation of shares outstanding, rights attaching to each class, and shares held by holding/subsidiary companies and by shareholders holding more than 5%; a break-up of **Reserves & Surplus**; the terms of **long-term borrowings** and details of any default; separate disclosure of **trade payables** and, where applicable, dues to **Micro and Small Enterprises**; and the reconciliation of the **gross and net carrying amount of Property, Plant and Equipment**.

**Conclusion:** Division I imposes a standardised vertical format with mandatory current/non-current classification, uniform rounding-off, comparatives and extensive note disclosures, ensuring comparability and a true and fair view.

*(Full-marks tip: The rounding-off rule is now keyed to "Total Income" (₹100 crore threshold) — quoting the older turnover slabs loses marks. Common deduction: omitting the current/non-current classification requirement, which is the heart of Schedule III.)*

### Q99. Ch: Company Financial Statements — Current vs Non-Current Classification (Schedule III) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** A manufacturing company with a normal **operating cycle of 18 months** seeks your advice on classifying the following items in its Balance Sheet as on 31st March 2026 under **Schedule III, Division I**. State, with reasons, whether each is **current or non-current**.

| # | Item |
|---|---|
| (i) | Trade receivable expected to be realised in 15 months (within the operating cycle) |
| (ii) | Installment of a term loan falling due within 12 months of the reporting date |
| (iii) | Investment in shares intended to be held for 2 years |
| (iv) | Provision for warranty expected to be settled in 8 months |
| (v) | Advance paid for purchase of machinery |

**Answer:**
As per Schedule III, an **asset is current** if it is expected to be realised in, or is held primarily for trading in, the company's **normal operating cycle**, or is expected to be realised **within twelve months** after the reporting date, or is cash/cash equivalent; **all other assets are non-current**. A **liability is current** on the corresponding tests; **all other liabilities are non-current**. Where the operating cycle exceeds twelve months, the **operating cycle** governs items connected with operations.

| # | Classification | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| (i) | **Current asset** (trade receivable) | Realised within the 18-month operating cycle; the 12-month test is not applied to operating items when the cycle is longer. |
| (ii) | **Current liability** — shown as "Current maturities of long-term debt" under Other current liabilities | Due for settlement within 12 months of the reporting date. |
| (iii) | **Non-current investment** | Held for 2 years — beyond both the operating cycle and 12 months; not held for trading. |
| (iv) | **Current liability** (short-term provision) | Expected to be settled within 12 months. |
| (v) | **Non-current asset** — Capital advance under "Long-term loans and advances" | It relates to acquisition of a fixed asset, not to the operating cycle, and is not expected to be realised as cash. |

**Conclusion:** Items (i), (ii) and (iv) are current; items (iii) and (v) are non-current.

*(Full-marks tip: The operating-cycle test (not merely 12 months) decides operating items such as trade receivables — a longer cycle keeps them current even beyond a year. Common deduction: classifying the capital advance as a current asset because "advances" sound short-term.)*

### Q100. Ch: Branch Accounts — Integral vs Non-Integral Foreign Operation & Exchange Differences (AS 11) (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Sterling Ltd. has two foreign branches. **Branch A** merely sells goods imported from the Indian Head Office and remits all proceeds to it. **Branch B** operates as an autonomous unit — it purchases and sells locally, borrows in local currency and retains its earnings abroad. Advise the company, with reasons under **AS 11 (Revised)**, on how each branch should be classified, the exchange rates to be used for translation, and the treatment of exchange differences.

**Answer:**
**AS 11 "The Effects of Changes in Foreign Exchange Rates"** classifies every foreign operation as either an **integral foreign operation** or a **non-integral foreign operation**, and the method of translation depends on this classification.

**Branch A — Integral foreign operation:** It carries on business as though it were an **extension of the Head Office**, selling imported goods and remitting proceeds; its cash flows directly affect those of the Head Office. It should therefore be translated using the **temporal method** — non-monetary items (fixed assets, and stock at cost) at **historical rates**, monetary items (debtors, creditors, cash) at the **closing rate**, and income/expense items at rates on the dates of transactions (or a suitable **average rate**). The resulting **exchange difference is recognised immediately in the Profit & Loss Account**.

**Branch B — Non-integral foreign operation:** It **accumulates cash and other items, generates income, incurs expenses and arranges borrowings substantially in its local currency**, operating with a degree of autonomy. It should be translated using the **closing-rate method** — **all assets and liabilities** (monetary and non-monetary) at the **closing rate**, and income/expense items at rates on transaction dates (or average rate). The exchange difference arising is **accumulated in a Foreign Currency Translation Reserve (FCTR)** under equity and is **not** recognised in profit until the net investment is disposed of.

| Basis | Branch A (Integral) | Branch B (Non-integral) |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Extension of Head Office | Autonomous unit |
| Assets & liabilities | Temporal method (historical/closing) | All at closing rate |
| Income & expenses | Transaction/average rate | Transaction/average rate |
| Exchange difference | To Profit & Loss Account | To FCTR (equity) |

**Conclusion:** Branch A is an integral operation (exchange differences to P&L); Branch B is a non-integral operation (exchange differences to FCTR).

*(Full-marks tip: Link the classification to the indicators in AS 11 — degree of autonomy and local financing — before choosing the translation method. Common deduction: routing a non-integral branch's translation difference through the Profit & Loss Account instead of the FCTR.)*
