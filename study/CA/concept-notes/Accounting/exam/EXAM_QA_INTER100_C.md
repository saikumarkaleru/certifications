### Q1. Ch: AS 2 Inventory — Measurement using Standard Cost & treatment of variances (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** Zenith Auto Components Ltd. values its inventory of a machined part using the standard cost technique as a matter of convenience. The following data relate to the closing inventory of 8,000 units of Part "GX-9" as on 31st March, 2026:

| Particulars | Standard cost per unit (₹) | Actual cost incurred (for the year's production of 40,000 units) |
|---|---|---|
| Direct material | 60 | ₹26,40,000 |
| Direct labour | 25 | ₹9,60,000 |
| Variable production overhead | 15 | ₹6,40,000 |
| Fixed production overhead (normal capacity 40,000 units) | 20 | ₹7,60,000 |

The material price rose sharply mid-year on account of a general market shortage; the purchase manager confirms the higher rate will persist. Labour and overhead variances arose purely from normal operating fluctuations. Determine the value at which the closing stock of 8,000 units should be carried under AS 2.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Reasonableness test of standard cost (AS 2, para 8).**
AS 2 permits the standard-cost method only if the standards are *regularly reviewed and, if necessary, revised in the light of current conditions* so that the result approximates actual cost. Variances that are *normal and immaterial* may be ignored; variances that are *material or persistent* must be re-absorbed so that inventory reflects actual cost.

**WN-2: Variance analysis (per unit, on 40,000 units produced).**

| Element | Std cost (₹) | Actual per unit (₹) | Variance per unit (₹) | Nature |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Material | 60 | 26,40,000 ÷ 40,000 = 66.00 | 6.00 (A) | Persistent / material — must adjust |
| Labour | 25 | 9,60,000 ÷ 40,000 = 24.00 | 1.00 (F) | Normal fluctuation — ignore |
| Variable OH | 15 | 6,40,000 ÷ 40,000 = 16.00 | 1.00 (A) | Normal / immaterial — ignore |
| Fixed OH | 20 | 7,60,000 ÷ 40,000 = 19.00 | 1.00 (F) | Capacity at normal — ignore |

**WN-3: Revised valuation rate.**
Only the material price variance is persistent and material (it reflects a durable change in current conditions), so the standard for material is revised to actual (₹66). Other elements are retained at standard because the variances are minor and offsetting.

Revised cost per unit = 66 (material) + 25 (labour) + 15 (variable OH) + 20 (fixed OH) = **₹126**

**Statement Showing Valuation of Closing Inventory of Part GX-9**

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Number of units in closing stock | 8,000 units |
| Revised cost per unit (WN-3) | ₹126 |
| **Value of closing inventory** | **₹10,08,000** |

**Cross-check (OR):** Value at unadjusted standard = 8,000 × ₹120 = ₹9,60,000; add persistent material variance 8,000 × ₹6 = ₹48,000 → ₹10,08,000. Reconciled.

**Answer:** The closing stock of 8,000 units of Part GX-9 is carried at **₹10,08,000** (₹126 per unit).

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the explicit AS 2 para 8 test — standard cost is acceptable only if it approximates actual, and only material/persistent variances are re-loaded. Blindly valuing at ₹120 (pure standard) or at full actual ₹125 loses marks; you must justify adjusting material alone.)*

### Q2. Ch: AS 2 Inventory — Joint products, reverse-cost (NRV) apportionment (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** In a single process, Krishna Refiners Ltd. produces three joint products — Alpha, Beta and Gamma — from a common input. During March 2026, joint (pre-separation) cost was ₹18,60,000. Data for the month:

| Product | Units produced | Selling price at split-off per unit (₹) | Further processing cost per unit (₹) | Final selling price per unit (₹) | Selling expense per unit (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Alpha | 6,000 | 90 | 40 | 150 | 6 |
| Beta | 4,000 | 120 | — (sold at split-off) | 120 | 4 |
| Gamma | 5,000 | 50 | 30 | 95 | 5 |

Closing stock: Alpha 1,000 units (fully processed), Beta 800 units, Gamma 1,200 units. Alpha and Gamma are further processed; Beta is sold at split-off. Value the closing stock of each joint product under AS 2 using the net realisable value (reverse-cost) method for apportioning joint cost.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Net realisable value (NRV) at split-off for each product.**
NRV at split-off = (Final selling price − further processing cost − selling expense) × units. For Beta (no further processing), NRV = (SP − selling expense) × units.

| Product | Units | Net realisable value per unit (₹) | Total NRV (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alpha | 6,000 | 150 − 40 − 6 = 104 | 6,24,000 |
| Beta | 4,000 | 120 − 4 = 116 | 4,64,000 |
| Gamma | 5,000 | 95 − 30 − 5 = 60 | 3,00,000 |
| **Total** | | | **13,88,000** |

**WN-2: Apportionment of joint cost of ₹18,60,000 in ratio of total NRV.**

| Product | NRV (₹) | Share ratio | Joint cost apportioned (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alpha | 6,24,000 | 6,24,000/13,88,000 | 8,36,196 |
| Beta | 4,64,000 | 4,64,000/13,88,000 | 6,21,844 |
| Gamma | 3,00,000 | 3,00,000/13,88,000 | 4,01,960 |
| **Total** | 13,88,000 | | **18,60,000** |

**WN-3: Total cost and cost per unit of each product.**

| Product | Joint cost (₹) | Further processing cost (₹) | Total cost (₹) | Units | Cost per unit (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Alpha | 8,36,196 | 6,000 × 40 = 2,40,000 | 10,76,196 | 6,000 | 179.37 |
| Beta | 6,21,844 | — | 6,21,844 | 4,000 | 155.46 |
| Gamma | 4,01,960 | 5,000 × 30 = 1,50,000 | 5,51,960 | 5,000 | 110.39 |

**WN-4: NRV per unit as a ceiling (AS 2 — lower of cost and NRV).**
NRV per unit (final SP − yet-to-be-incurred selling exp; processing already done for closing stock) = Alpha 150 − 6 = 144; Beta 120 − 4 = 116; Gamma 95 − 5 = 90.

| Product | Cost per unit (₹) | NRV per unit (₹) | Lower value (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alpha | 179.37 | 144.00 | 144.00 (NRV lower) |
| Beta | 155.46 | 116.00 | 116.00 (NRV lower) |
| Gamma | 110.39 | 90.00 | 90.00 (NRV lower) |

**Statement Showing Valuation of Closing Inventory of Joint Products**

| Product | Closing units | Valuation rate — lower of cost & NRV (₹) | Closing stock value (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alpha | 1,000 | 144.00 | 1,44,000 |
| Beta | 800 | 116.00 | 92,800 |
| Gamma | 1,200 | 90.00 | 1,08,000 |
| **Total** | | | **3,44,800** |

**Answer:** Closing inventory is valued at **₹3,44,800** (Alpha ₹1,44,000 + Beta ₹92,800 + Gamma ₹1,08,000), each product written down to NRV as it is below apportioned cost.

*(Full-marks tip: Two-layer solution — (1) apportion joint cost on NRV, then (2) apply the lower-of-cost-and-NRV rule product-by-product. Students who stop after step 1 and value at cost lose the AS 2 write-down marks. Note joint cost is allocated for costing, but the AS 2 measurement ceiling is still NRV.)*

### Q3. Ch: AS 2 Inventory — Cost of inventories of a service provider (Marks: 4) [Problem]
**Question:** M/s Sharp & Associates, a firm of consulting engineers, is preparing its financial statements for the year ended 31st March, 2026. On that date three assignments were incomplete and no bill had been raised. The firm asks you to compute the value of "work-in-progress (unbilled services)" carried as inventory under AS 2. Data for the incomplete assignments:

| Cost element (total for the three jobs) | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Salaries and wages of engineers directly engaged on the jobs | 8,40,000 |
| Salary of partners supervising the jobs (partners' remuneration) | 3,00,000 |
| Attributable technical overheads (drawing office, software licences) | 1,60,000 |
| General administration overhead (not attributable to jobs) | 1,20,000 |
| Selling and marketing costs relating to winning the jobs | 90,000 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Applicable rule.** AS 2, para 19: the cost of inventories of a *service provider* consists primarily of the labour and other costs of personnel *directly engaged* in providing the service, including supervisory personnel, and *attributable overheads*. It **excludes**: (a) profit margins, (b) **non-attributable (general administration) overheads**, and (c) **selling costs** — these are expensed. Para 5A specifically bars including profit margins/non-attributable overheads in the inventory of a service provider.

**WN-2: Partners' remuneration.** Salaries of personnel directly engaged, including supervisory staff, are includible. However, the portion representing partners' own *profit share* is excluded. Here ₹3,00,000 is stated as *supervisory remuneration for the jobs* (a labour cost, not profit distribution), so it is includible as supervisory labour.

**Statement Showing Cost of Unbilled Work-in-Progress (Service Inventory) under AS 2**

| Particulars | Included? | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| Salaries and wages of engineers directly engaged | Yes — direct labour | 8,40,000 |
| Supervisory partners' remuneration on the jobs | Yes — supervisory labour | 3,00,000 |
| Attributable technical overheads | Yes — attributable OH | 1,60,000 |
| General administration overhead | No — not attributable | — |
| Selling and marketing costs | No — selling cost | — |
| **Value of service WIP inventory** | | **13,00,000** |

**Answer:** The unbilled service work-in-progress is carried at **₹13,00,000**. General administration overhead (₹1,20,000) and selling/marketing costs (₹90,000) are charged to the Statement of Profit and Loss and are not part of inventory.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner looks for the three explicit exclusions for service-provider inventory under AS 2 para 19 — profit margin, non-attributable overhead, selling cost. Wrongly capitalising general admin or selling cost is the classic error.)*

### Q4. Ch: AS 2 Inventory — Fixed overhead absorption on normal capacity & abnormal wastage (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** Vaibhav Ceramics Ltd. gives the following data for the year ended 31st March, 2026 for its tile division:

| Particulars | Amount / Quantity |
|---|---|
| Normal production capacity | 1,00,000 tiles |
| Actual production during the year | 70,000 tiles |
| Direct material and labour (variable) | ₹98,00,000 |
| Fixed production overhead for the year | ₹30,00,000 |
| Abnormal spoilage (included in actual production above) | 4,000 tiles |
| Closing stock (out of good units) | 12,000 tiles |

Compute the value of closing stock of 12,000 good tiles under AS 2, and state the accounting treatment of the under-absorbed fixed overhead and the abnormal spoilage.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Fixed overhead absorption rate (AS 2, para 12).** Fixed production overhead is absorbed on the basis of **normal capacity**, not actual output, in a period of low production.
Absorption rate = ₹30,00,000 ÷ 1,00,000 normal units = **₹30 per tile.**

**WN-2: Good production and abnormal loss.**
Good tiles produced = 70,000 − 4,000 abnormal spoilage = 66,000 tiles.
Variable cost per tile = ₹98,00,000 ÷ 70,000 = ₹140 per tile (variable cost attaches to all units produced including spoilage before segregation).

**WN-3: Cost per good tile.**
Fixed OH absorbed on good output = 66,000 × ₹30 = ₹19,80,000.
Total absorbable cost of good tiles = variable cost of good tiles + fixed OH on good tiles.
Variable cost of good tiles = 66,000 × ₹140 = ₹92,40,000.
Total cost of good tiles = 92,40,000 + 19,80,000 = ₹1,12,20,000.
Cost per good tile = 1,12,20,000 ÷ 66,000 = **₹170.**

**WN-4: Treatment of unabsorbed fixed OH and abnormal spoilage.**
- Fixed OH absorbed (on 66,000 good) = ₹19,80,000; total fixed OH = ₹30,00,000; **unabsorbed = ₹10,20,000** — charged to P&L as expense of the period (not to inventory), because output is below normal capacity.
- Abnormal spoilage cost = 4,000 × ₹140 variable = ₹5,60,000 → charged to P&L as abnormal loss (excluded from inventory).

**Statement Showing Valuation of Closing Stock**

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Closing stock (good tiles) | 12,000 units |
| Cost per good tile (WN-3) | ₹170 |
| **Value of closing stock** | **₹20,40,000** |

**Answer:** Closing stock = **₹20,40,000** (12,000 × ₹170). Unabsorbed fixed overhead ₹10,20,000 and abnormal spoilage ₹5,60,000 are charged to the Statement of Profit and Loss.

*(Full-marks tip: AS 2 para 12/13 rewards two things — absorbing fixed OH on normal capacity (so the shortfall hits P&L, not stock) and excluding abnormal spoilage from cost. Absorbing fixed OH on actual 70,000 units, or loading spoilage into stock, are the two common deductions.)*

### Q5. Ch: AS 7 Construction Contracts — Incentive payments and claims in contract revenue (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain, with reference to AS 7 "Construction Contracts", the conditions under which (a) an incentive payment and (b) a claim raised against the customer may be included in contract revenue. Why does AS 7 treat these two items differently from the basic agreed contract price?

**Answer:**
Under **AS 7 "Construction Contracts"**, contract revenue comprises (i) the initial amount of revenue agreed in the contract and (ii) **variations in contract work, claims and incentive payments** to the extent that it is probable they will result in revenue and they are capable of being **reliably measured** (AS 7, para 10). Because items (a) and (b) are inherently uncertain, AS 7 subjects them to stricter recognition tests than the basic agreed price.

**(a) Incentive payments.** An incentive payment is an additional amount payable to the contractor if specified performance standards are met or exceeded (e.g. early completion). Per **AS 7 para 15**, incentive payments are included in contract revenue when **both** conditions are satisfied:
(i) the contract is sufficiently **advanced** that it is probable the specified performance standards will be met or exceeded; and
(ii) the amount of the incentive payment can be **measured reliably**.

**(b) Claims.** A claim is an amount the contractor seeks to collect from the customer as reimbursement for costs not included in the contract price (e.g. customer-caused delays, errors in specification, disputed variations). Per **AS 7 para 14**, a claim is included in contract revenue only when:
(i) negotiations have reached an **advanced stage** such that it is probable the customer **will accept** the claim; and
(ii) the amount that will probably be accepted can be **measured reliably**.

| Basis | Basic contract price | Incentive payment | Claim |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nature | Agreed, certain | Reward for over-performance | Reimbursement sought from customer |
| Recognition trigger | Automatic on the contract | Probable standards met + reliably measurable | Probable customer acceptance + reliably measurable |
| Uncertainty | Low | Moderate | High (disputed) |

**Conclusion:** The agreed price is recognised as a matter of course, whereas incentives and claims are recognised only when their realisation becomes **probable** and they can be **measured reliably** — a prudence-driven distinction that prevents recognising revenue that may never materialise.

*(Full-marks tip: Cite the exact paras — 14 for claims, 15 for incentives — and give the twin "probable + reliably measurable" test for each. A comparative table showing why the disputed claim carries the highest recognition threshold earns the top band.)*

### Q6. Ch: AS 7 Construction Contracts — Change in estimate of total contract cost (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** Sundaram Infra Ltd. undertook a bridge contract for a fixed price of ₹960 lakh, to be completed over three years. The company uses the percentage-of-completion method based on the proportion of contract costs incurred to estimated total contract cost. Data:

| Particulars | Year 1 (₹ lakh) | Year 2 (₹ lakh) | Year 3 (₹ lakh) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Costs incurred up to end of year (cumulative) | 210 | 560 | 840 |
| Estimated further costs to complete | 490 | 280 | — |
| Progress billings during the year | 200 | 380 | 380 |
| Amounts received during the year | 180 | 350 | 410 |

At the start of Year 2, on account of a design revision, the total estimated cost rose (as reflected above). Compute the contract revenue, contract cost and profit to be recognised in **each** of the three years, and show how the change in estimate is dealt with under AS 7.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Total estimated cost and percentage of completion (cost-to-cost).**

| Particulars | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cost incurred to date (₹ lakh) | 210 | 560 | 840 |
| Estimated further cost (₹ lakh) | 490 | 280 | — |
| **Total estimated cost (₹ lakh)** | 700 | 840 | 840 |
| **Stage of completion = cost to date ÷ total est. cost** | 210/700 = 30% | 560/840 = 66.67% | 840/840 = 100% |

**WN-2: Cumulative contract revenue recognised (stage % × contract price ₹960 lakh).**

| | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cumulative revenue (₹ lakh) | 30% × 960 = 288 | 66.67% × 960 = 640 | 100% × 960 = 960 |

**WN-3: Change in estimate treatment (AS 7, para 30).** A change in the estimate of total contract cost is a **change in accounting estimate**, accounted for **prospectively** — the effect is recognised in the period of change and future periods; **prior-year figures are not restated.** Hence the revised total cost of ₹840 lakh is applied from Year 2 onward, without disturbing Year 1's recognised profit.

**Statement Showing Contract Revenue, Cost and Profit Recognised Each Year**

| Particulars | Year 1 (₹ lakh) | Year 2 (₹ lakh) | Year 3 (₹ lakh) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cumulative revenue (WN-2) | 288 | 640 | 960 |
| Less: Revenue recognised in earlier years | — | (288) | (640) |
| **Revenue for the year** | **288** | **352** | **320** |
| Cumulative cost recognised (stage % × total est. cost, i.e. cost to date) | 210 | 560 | 840 |
| Less: Cost recognised in earlier years | — | (210) | (560) |
| **Cost for the year** | **210** | **350** | **280** |
| **Profit for the year** | **78** | **2** | **40** |

**WN-4: Cross-check total profit.** Total contract profit = 960 − 840 = ₹120 lakh; recognised 78 + 2 + 40 = ₹120 lakh. Reconciled.

**Answer:** Recognised profit — **Year 1 ₹78 lakh, Year 2 ₹2 lakh, Year 3 ₹40 lakh.** The upward revision of total cost is treated prospectively (AS 7 para 30); Year 1 is not restated, and the effect of the higher cost is absorbed as reduced profit in Year 2.

*(Full-marks tip: The trap is retrospectively re-computing Year 1 at 30% on the revised ₹840 (= 210/840 = 25%) — wrong. AS 7 change-in-estimate is prospective, so Year 1's 30% and ₹78 lakh profit stand; the squeeze shows up as Year 2's thin ₹2 lakh. Cost-for-year equals incremental cost incurred; state that explicitly.)*

### Q7. Ch: AS 7 Construction Contracts — Expected loss with a probable claim (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** Girish Constructions Ltd. is executing a contract with an agreed price of ₹500 lakh. As at 31st March, 2026:

| Particulars | ₹ lakh |
|---|---|
| Costs incurred to date | 340 |
| Estimated further costs to complete | 260 |
| Progress billings raised to date | 300 |

Owing to a delay caused entirely by the customer's late handing-over of the site, the company has lodged a claim of ₹80 lakh. Negotiations are at an advanced stage and the customer's engineer has, in writing, admitted liability for ₹50 lakh; the balance is disputed. Determine the amount of loss (if any) to be recognised in the year ended 31st March, 2026, under AS 7.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Includible claim (AS 7, para 14).** A claim is added to contract revenue only when acceptance is probable and the amount is reliably measurable. Here ₹50 lakh has been admitted in writing (probable + measurable) and is includible; the disputed ₹30 lakh fails the test and is excluded.

Revised contract revenue = 500 + 50 = **₹550 lakh.**

**WN-2: Total estimated contract cost.**
Total estimated cost = 340 (to date) + 260 (to complete) = **₹600 lakh.**

**WN-3: Expected outcome of the contract.**
Expected total revenue ₹550 lakh − expected total cost ₹600 lakh = **expected loss ₹50 lakh.**

**WN-4: Recognition of foreseeable loss (AS 7, para 35).** When it is probable that total contract costs will exceed total contract revenue, the **expected loss is recognised as an expense immediately**, irrespective of the stage of completion and of whether work has commenced. The whole ₹50 lakh is therefore recognised now, not spread over the stage of completion.

**Statement Showing Loss to be Recognised**

| Particulars | ₹ lakh |
|---|---|
| Total contract revenue (incl. admitted claim ₹50) | 550 |
| Total estimated contract cost | 600 |
| **Foreseeable loss on the contract** | **50** |
| Loss to be recognised immediately (AS 7 para 35) | **50** |

**Answer:** A foreseeable loss of **₹50 lakh** must be recognised as an expense in the year ended 31st March, 2026. The admitted claim of ₹50 lakh is included in revenue; the disputed ₹30 lakh is not, as acceptance is not yet probable.

*(Full-marks tip: Two AS 7 rules combine here — para 14 (only the admitted ₹50 lakh claim enters revenue) and para 35 (whole expected loss booked at once, ignoring % completion). Recognising only the completion-weighted portion of the loss, or including the disputed ₹30 lakh, are the mark-losers.)*

### Q8. Ch: AS 7 Construction Contracts — Incentive payment and outcome estimation (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** Meghraj Builders Ltd. has a contract of agreed price ₹720 lakh with a clause that an incentive of ₹60 lakh will be paid if the project is completed two months ahead of schedule. As at 31st March, 2026:

| Particulars | ₹ lakh |
|---|---|
| Costs incurred to date | 396 |
| Estimated further costs to complete | 264 |
| Progress billings raised | 420 |

By the balance-sheet date the work is well advanced and the project management office confirms early completion is now highly probable; the ₹60 lakh incentive is contractually fixed and hence reliably measurable. Compute the contract revenue, cost and profit to be recognised for the year, and the amount to be shown as "due from / due to customer".

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Includible incentive (AS 7, para 15).** Both conditions are met — the contract is sufficiently advanced that early completion is probable, and the incentive of ₹60 lakh is reliably measurable. Hence it is included in contract revenue.
Total contract revenue = 720 + 60 = **₹780 lakh.**

**WN-2: Total estimated cost and stage of completion.**
Total estimated cost = 396 + 264 = ₹660 lakh.
Stage of completion = 396 ÷ 660 = **60%.**

**WN-3: Revenue, cost and profit for the year.**

**Statement Showing Recognition (Percentage-of-Completion Method)**

| Particulars | ₹ lakh |
|---|---|
| Contract revenue recognised = 60% × 780 | 468 |
| Contract cost recognised (cost to date) | 396 |
| **Profit recognised for the year** | **72** |

**WN-4: Amount due from/to customer (AS 7, para 41–42).**
Gross amount due from customers = Costs incurred + Recognised profits − Progress billings
= 396 + 72 − 420 = **₹48 lakh due FROM the customer** (an asset).

**WN-5: Cross-check of total contract profit.** Total = 780 − 660 = ₹120 lakh; at 60% completion, recognised profit = 60% × 120 = ₹72 lakh. Reconciled.

**Answer:** Recognise **revenue ₹468 lakh, cost ₹396 lakh, profit ₹72 lakh** for the year; **₹48 lakh** is disclosed as amount **due from the customer** (gross amount due from customers).

*(Full-marks tip: Add the incentive to revenue only after clearing the AS 7 para 15 twin test, then include it in the % calculation. The "due from/due to" line (cost + profit − billings) is frequently omitted — showing it, with the correct sign, secures the last 2 marks.)*

### Q9. Ch: AS 9 Revenue Recognition — Sale of goods with a right of return (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** Fabricmart Ltd. sells garments to retailers on a "sale-or-return" basis under which any unsold stock may be returned within 90 days. On 20th March, 2026 it despatched goods invoiced at ₹40,00,000 (cost ₹28,00,000) to various retailers. Based on a consistent 5-year history, the company reliably estimates that **15%** of such despatches are returned. Up to 31st March, 2026 no goods had been returned and the return period had not expired. Advise, with reasons under AS 9, how much revenue Fabricmart Ltd. should recognise for the year ended 31st March, 2026, and pass the necessary treatment.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Applicable principle (AS 9, para 6.1 and para 11).** For a sale of goods, revenue is recognised when the seller has transferred the property in the goods (and significant risks and rewards of ownership) to the buyer and no significant uncertainty exists regarding the amount of consideration. Where goods are sold subject to a **right of return**, and the seller can **reliably estimate** future returns from past experience, revenue is recognised at the time of sale for the portion **not expected to be returned**, and a provision is made for the estimated returns.

**WN-2: Revenue recognisable.**
Goods likely to be retained = 100% − 15% = 85%.
Recognised sale value = 85% × ₹40,00,000 = **₹34,00,000.**
Value of goods expected to be returned (not recognised as revenue) = 15% × ₹40,00,000 = ₹6,00,000; corresponding cost = 15% × ₹28,00,000 = ₹4,20,000 to be retained in inventory.

**Statement Showing Revenue and Balance-Sheet Effect**

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Total goods despatched (invoice value) | 40,00,000 |
| Less: Estimated returns (15%) — revenue deferred | (6,00,000) |
| **Revenue recognised for the year** | **34,00,000** |
| Inventory carried for expected returns (at cost) | 4,20,000 |

**OR (alternative view):** If the entity were *unable* to reliably estimate returns, AS 9 would require recognition to be **postponed until the goods are formally accepted or the return period expires**; the entire ₹40,00,000 would then not be recognised in FY 2025-26. As reliable estimation exists here, the 85% approach applies.

**Answer:** Revenue of **₹34,00,000** is recognised for the year ended 31st March, 2026; the estimated 15% expected to be returned (₹6,00,000 sale / ₹4,20,000 cost) is not treated as sale, and the related goods remain in inventory.

*(Full-marks tip: The pivot is "reliable estimate of returns" — state both branches of AS 9 (estimate reliable → recognise net of expected returns; not reliable → defer until period expires). Recognising the full ₹40 lakh without adjusting for the 15% return probability is the standard error.)*

### Q10. Ch: AS 9 Revenue Recognition — Installation fees and "goods sold subject to installation" (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** With reference to AS 9, state when revenue is recognised in each of the following cases and give the reason: (i) installation fees where installation is a significant part of the contract; (ii) sale of goods where the goods are sold subject to installation and inspection, the installation being simple; (iii) advertising commission earned by an advertising agency; (iv) subscription received in advance for a monthly journal.

**Answer:**
**AS 9 "Revenue Recognition"** provides that revenue from the sale of goods is recognised when the seller has transferred to the buyer the property in the goods and significant risks and rewards of ownership and no significant uncertainty exists as to collectability, and revenue from services is recognised as the service is performed (by the completed service or proportionate completion method).

| Case | When recognised | Reason (AS 9 basis) |
|---|---|---|
| (i) Installation fees — installation significant | Recognised **only when the installation is completed** and accepted by the buyer | Installation is a significant act; the service is not complete, and hence the earnings process is not complete, until installation is done |
| (ii) Goods sold subject to installation & inspection, installation simple | Recognised at the point of **sale/delivery** (installation and inspection being a minor/formal act) | When installation is a minor formality, transfer of significant risks and rewards effectively occurs on delivery; recognition need not await installation |
| (iii) Advertising agency's commission | Recognised **when the related advertisement appears** before the public | The service (media placement) is rendered when the advertisement is published/telecast; production commission is recognised when the project is completed |
| (iv) Subscription received in advance for a monthly journal | Recognised **over the subscription period as each issue is despatched** (spread on a straight-line basis where items are of similar value) | Revenue is earned progressively as the goods/service are delivered; the advance is a liability until then |

**Conclusion:** Under AS 9, the timing turns on **when the significant act is completed** and the earnings process is substantially complete — hence significant installation defers recognition, whereas a trivial installation does not.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner wants the contrast between (i) and (ii) — the *significance* of the installation act decides whether recognition waits. Quote AS 9's "completion of the significant act" test and give the reason column, not just the timing.)*

### Q12. Ch: AS 9 Revenue Recognition — Postponement of recognition and disclosure (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** "Recognition of revenue requires that the revenue is measurable and that at the time of sale or rendering of service it would not be unreasonable to expect ultimate collection." Explain, with reference to AS 9, (a) the circumstances in which recognition of revenue is postponed, and (b) the disclosure required when revenue recognition is postponed.

**Answer:**
**AS 9 "Revenue Recognition"** lays down that revenue from sales or service transactions should be recognised only when **(i) the requisite performance has been achieved** (transfer of significant risks and rewards for goods; completion/proportionate performance for services) and **(ii) no significant uncertainty exists** regarding the amount of consideration and its collectability.

**(a) Postponement of recognition.** Per **AS 9 paras 9.1–9.2**, where at the time of raising a claim it is **unreasonable to expect ultimate collection**, revenue recognition is **postponed**. Two situations are distinguished:
1. If the **uncertainty as to collectability arises subsequent to the time of sale** (the sale was properly recognised), a **separate provision** is made to reflect the uncertainty rather than adjusting the revenue originally recorded.
2. If **uncertainty as to collectability exists at the time of the sale or the rendering of the service itself** (e.g. collectability is doubtful from the outset), revenue recognition is **postponed** and revenue is recognised only when it becomes reasonably certain that the consideration will be collected.
Similarly, where the ability to assess the ultimate collection **with reasonable certainty is lacking** — for instance, escalation of price subject to government approval — recognition is postponed to the extent of uncertainty.

**(b) Disclosure.** Per **AS 9 para 14**, where revenue recognition is postponed due to the effect of uncertainties, an enterprise should **disclose the circumstances** in which revenue recognition has been postponed pending the resolution of significant uncertainties.

**Conclusion:** AS 9 permits recognition only when collection is reasonably assured; where it is not, recognition is deferred (uncertainty at inception) or a provision is created (uncertainty arising later), and the postponement must be disclosed.

*(Full-marks tip: Distinguish the two cases — uncertainty at the time of sale (postpone recognition) versus uncertainty arising later (recognise, then provide). Add the para 14 disclosure requirement; students routinely forget the disclosure limb.)*

### Q14. Ch: AS 10 Property, Plant & Equipment — Capitalisation of spare parts (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** With the revision of AS 10, the treatment of spare parts, stand-by equipment and servicing equipment has changed. Explain the current position under AS 10 "Property, Plant and Equipment" regarding when spare parts are recognised as PP&E and when they are treated as inventory. Illustrate with the treatment of a major spare part costing ₹9,00,000 expected to be used only when the main plant is overhauled after 5 years.

**Answer:**
**AS 10 "Property, Plant and Equipment"** deals with spare parts under the **recognition principle in para 8**. The classification no longer depends on the *value* of the spare or on whether it is a "major" spare; it depends on whether the item **meets the definition of PP&E**.

**Rule:** Items such as **spare parts, stand-by equipment and servicing equipment are recognised as PP&E** when they meet the **definition of property, plant and equipment** — i.e. they are held for use in production/supply of goods or services, for rental to others, or for administrative purposes, and are **expected to be used during more than one period** (para 8). If they do **not** meet this definition, they are classified as **inventory** and dealt with under AS 2, being charged to profit and loss on consumption.

A spare that meets the PP&E definition is:
(i) **capitalised** and depreciated. Its depreciation is provided from the date it is **available for use**, i.e. over a period **not exceeding the useful life of the asset to which it relates** (for a spare usable only on the specific main asset).

**Application to the ₹9,00,000 spare:** The major spare costs ₹9,00,000 and is held for use in connection with the plant, expected to be used only after 5 years but usable **over more than one period** in the enterprise's operations. It therefore meets the definition of PP&E and is **capitalised** (not expensed as inventory). It is depreciated over the shorter of its own useful life and the remaining useful life of the main plant, commencing when it is available for use.

**Conclusion:** Under the current AS 10, the test for a spare part is the **PP&E definition (use in more than one period)**, not its cost. The ₹9,00,000 spare is capitalised as PP&E and depreciated, rather than being carried as inventory and written off on issue.

*(Full-marks tip: The key change to state is that the old "value-based / major spare" test is gone — the criterion is now purely whether the item meets the PP&E definition (used over more than one period). Mention that depreciation on a dedicated spare cannot exceed the main asset's useful life.)*

### Q18. Ch: AS 4 — Adjusting vs Non-adjusting Events after the Balance Sheet Date (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** ABC Ltd. closes its books on 31st March 2025. The financial statements are approved by the Board on 20th June 2025. | Sl. | Event (all occur between 01.04.2025 and 20.06.2025) | Amount (₹) |
|-----|------|------|
| (i) | A debtor of ₹8,00,000 (outstanding at 31.03.2025) is declared insolvent on 12.05.2025; ₹2,00,000 finally realisable | 8,00,000 |
| (ii) | A fire in the finished-goods godown on 05.05.2025 destroys stock; the stock existed and was intact on 31.03.2025 | 15,00,000 |
| (iii) | The Board proposes a dividend of 12% on 20.06.2025 for FY 2024-25 | 24,00,000 |
| (iv) | Sale of an investment on 18.06.2025 confirms that its 31.03.2025 diminution in value was permanent | 3,50,000 |

As per **AS 4 (Revised) "Contingencies and Events Occurring After the Balance Sheet Date"**, events occurring after the balance sheet date are those that occur between the balance sheet date and the date on which the financial statements are approved by the approving authority. Such events are classified as **adjusting events** (those providing further evidence of conditions that existed at the balance sheet date) and **non-adjusting events** (those concerning conditions that arose after the balance sheet date).

**(i) Insolvency of debtor — Adjusting event.** The insolvency confirms a condition (the debtor's inability to pay) that existed at the balance sheet date. Therefore, an additional provision for doubtful debts of ₹6,00,000 (₹8,00,000 − ₹2,00,000 realisable) must be recognised in the accounts for the year ended 31.03.2025.

**(ii) Fire destroying stock — Non-adjusting event.** The stock physically existed and was intact on 31.03.2025; the loss arose from a condition (fire) that occurred wholly after the balance sheet date. No adjustment is made to the accounts. However, since the amount is material and destruction of a significant part of inventory may affect the going-concern assessment, disclosure of the nature of the event and an estimate of its financial effect is required.

**(iii) Proposed dividend — Non-adjusting event (post-amendment treatment).** Consequent to the amendment of AS 4, dividends proposed after the balance sheet date but before approval of the financial statements are **not recognised as a liability** at the balance sheet date, since no obligation exists on that date. The proposed dividend of ₹24,00,000 is only **disclosed in the notes**.

**(iv) Sale confirming permanent diminution — Adjusting event.** The subsequent sale provides further evidence of the value of the investment as at the balance sheet date. The diminution of ₹3,50,000 is treated as an adjusting event and the carrying amount of the investment is written down in the 2024-25 accounts.

**Conclusion:** Items (i) and (iv) are adjusting events requiring adjustment in the accounts; items (ii) and (iii) are non-adjusting events requiring disclosure only.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner rewards the explicit "condition existing on the balance sheet date" test for each item and the correct post-amendment treatment of proposed dividend as disclosure-only. ### Q19. Ch: AS 5 — Change in Accounting Estimate vs Change in Accounting Policy (Marks: 4) [Application]
**Question:** During the year ended 31st March 2025, XYZ Ltd. made the following three changes. Advise, under **AS 5**, whether each is a change in accounting **policy**, a change in accounting **estimate**, or the correction of a **prior period item**, and state the accounting treatment.

| Sl. | Change made in FY 2024-25 |
|-----|------|
| (i) | Revised the estimated useful life of a plant from 10 years to 7 years based on a technical assessment |
| (ii) | Switched the method of valuing inventory from FIFO to Weighted Average |
| (iii) | Discovered that sales of ₹6,00,000 pertaining to FY 2023-24 were omitted, being posted twice as a reversal |

**Answer:**
As per **AS 5 "Net Profit or Loss for the Period, Prior Period Items and Changes in Accounting Policies"**, the treatment depends on the nature of the change.

**(i) Revision of useful life — Change in accounting estimate.** An estimate may require revision if changes occur in the circumstances on which it was based, or as a result of new information or more experience. A change in useful life is a change in estimate (not a policy). Its effect is recognised **prospectively** by spreading the unamortised depreciable amount over the revised remaining useful life. No restatement of prior periods is made.

**(ii) FIFO to Weighted Average — Change in accounting policy.** The cost formula for inventory is an accounting policy. A change in policy should be made only if required by statute, an accounting standard, or if it results in a more appropriate presentation. The effect (the amount by which any item in the financial statements is affected) must be **disclosed** to the extent ascertainable; if not ascertainable, that fact is disclosed. AS 5 requires only disclosure, not retrospective restatement of comparatives.

**(iii) Omitted sales of FY 2023-24 — Prior period item.** This is an income arising in the current period as a result of an error in the preparation of the prior period's financial statements. It is included in the determination of net profit/loss for the current period, but shown **separately** so that its impact on current profit can be perceived.

**Conclusion:** (i) is a change in estimate — prospective; (ii) is a change in policy — disclose the effect; (iii) is a prior period item — separately disclosed in current profit and loss.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner looks for the "prospective vs disclosure vs separate presentation" distinction. A frequent error is treating the change in useful life as a change in policy — depreciation method change is a policy/estimate hybrid, but a bare change in useful life is always an estimate.)*

**Question:** Sterling Exports Ltd. (₹ functional currency) entered into a forward exchange contract on **1st February 2025** to buy **USD 1,00,000** maturing on **31st May 2025** to hedge a firm liability. The contract is **not for trading or speculation**. Relevant rates are:

| Particulars | Rate (₹/USD) |
|-----|------|
| Spot rate on 01.02.2025 (inception) | 83.50 |
| Forward rate for 31.05.2025 (contracted) | 84.30 |
| Spot rate on 31.03.2025 (year-end) | 83.90 |

The company follows a 31st March year-end. Compute the amounts to be recognised in the Statement of Profit and Loss for the year ended 31st March 2025 in respect of this forward contract, and pass the relevant journal entries (narration not required).

**WN-1: Forward premium and its amortisation.**
As per **AS 11**, for a forward contract not intended for trading/speculation and not to hedge a firm commitment/highly probable forecast transaction, the **premium or discount at inception** (difference between forward rate and spot rate on the date of the contract) is **amortised as expense or income over the life of the contract**.

Premium = (Forward rate − Spot rate at inception) × Contract amount
= (84.30 − 83.50) × 1,00,000 = ₹0.80 × 1,00,000 = **₹80,000**

Life of contract = 1 Feb 2025 to 31 May 2025 = **4 months**; premium per month = 80,000 ÷ 4 = ₹20,000.
Months expired up to 31.03.2025 = **2 months** (Feb, Mar).
Premium to be amortised to P&L for FY 2024-25 = 2 × ₹20,000 = **₹40,000 (expense).**

**WN-2: Exchange difference on the contract for the period.**
Exchange difference = (Spot rate at year-end − Spot rate at inception) × Contract amount
= (83.90 − 83.50) × 1,00,000 = ₹0.40 × 1,00,000 = **₹40,000 (gain/income)** recognised in P&L for the year.

**Statement showing amounts recognised in P&L for the year ended 31.03.2025**

| Particulars | ₹ | Nature |
|-----|------:|------|
| Amortisation of forward premium (WN-1) | 40,000 | Expense (Dr to P&L) |
| Exchange difference on contract (WN-2) | 40,000 | Income (Cr to P&L) |
| **Net effect on P&L** | **Nil** | — |

**Journal Entries (FY 2024-25):**

| Date | Particulars | Dr (₹) | Cr (₹) |
|-----|------|------:|------:|
| 01.02.2025 | Forward Contract Premium (Prepaid) A/c Dr | 80,000 | |
| | To Bank / Forward Contract Liability A/c | | 80,000 |
| 31.03.2025 | Profit & Loss A/c Dr | 40,000 | |
| | To Forward Contract Premium (Prepaid) A/c | | 40,000 |
| 31.03.2025 | Forward Contract Receivable A/c Dr | 40,000 | |
| | To Profit & Loss A/c (exchange difference) | | 40,000 |

**Answer:** For the year ended 31.03.2025, premium of **₹40,000** is charged as expense and exchange gain of **₹40,000** is credited to the Statement of Profit and Loss; the unamortised premium of **₹40,000** is carried forward to be recognised in FY 2025-26.

*(Full-marks tip: examiners award marks for keeping the premium (forward-minus-spot at inception) and the exchange difference (spot-minus-spot) as two SEPARATE streams. ### Q21. Ch: AS 12 — Refund of Government Grant Related to a Specific Fixed Asset (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** On 1st April 2022, Nirman Ltd. purchased a machine for **₹50,00,000** (useful life 10 years, SLM, nil residual value) and received a government grant of **₹10,00,000** relating to it. On **31st March 2025** (i.e., after 3 years), the grant became **refundable in full** because the company breached an export condition attached to the grant. Show the accounting treatment of the refund under **both permitted methods** of AS 12 and state the amount charged to the Statement of Profit and Loss (if any) on refund.

**Solution:**
As per **AS 12 "Accounting for Government Grants"**, a grant that becomes refundable is treated as an **extraordinary item / change in estimate** and accounted for by reversing the earlier treatment. Two methods are permitted for a grant relating to a specific fixed asset.

**WN-1: Method I — Grant deducted from the cost of the asset.**
- Asset recorded at 50,00,000 − 10,00,000 = **₹40,00,000**; depreciation = 40,00,000 ÷ 10 = **₹4,00,000 p.a.**
- WDV at 31.03.2025 (after 3 years) = 40,00,000 − (4,00,000 × 3) = **₹28,00,000.**
- On refund, the amount refundable of ₹10,00,000 is **added back to the book value of the asset**: revised book value = 28,00,000 + 10,00,000 = **₹38,00,000.**
- This revised book value is depreciated **prospectively** over the remaining useful life of 7 years: revised depreciation = 38,00,000 ÷ 7 = **₹5,42,857 p.a.**
- **No amount is charged to P&L on the date of refund** (the effect flows through higher future depreciation).

**WN-2: Method II — Grant treated as deferred income.**
- Deferred grant income initially = ₹10,00,000, credited to P&L over 10 years = ₹1,00,000 p.a.
- Amount credited to P&L in 3 years = ₹3,00,000; **unamortised deferred income at 31.03.2025 = ₹7,00,000.**
- On refund of ₹10,00,000: first adjust against unamortised deferred income of ₹7,00,000; the **excess of ₹3,00,000 (10,00,000 − 7,00,000) is charged immediately to the Statement of Profit and Loss.**

**Statement showing effect of refund under the two methods**

| Particulars | Method I (deduct from asset) | Method II (deferred income) |
|-----|------:|------:|
| Amount refunded | 10,00,000 | 10,00,000 |
| Adjusted against unamortised deferred income | — | 7,00,000 |
| Added to book value of asset | 10,00,000 | — |
| **Charged to P&L on refund** | **Nil** | **3,00,000** |
| Depreciation p.a. after refund | 5,42,857 (over 7 yrs) | 5,00,000 (unchanged: 50,00,000÷10) |

**Answer:** Under **Method I** no amount hits P&L on refund; the revised carrying amount of ₹38,00,000 is depreciated at ₹5,42,857 p.a. over the remaining 7 years. Under **Method II**, ₹7,00,000 is set off against deferred income and **₹3,00,000 is charged to the Statement of Profit and Loss** on refund.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner rewards showing that under the "deferred income" method the excess of refund over the unamortised balance is expensed, whereas under the "reduction from cost" method the refund raises the asset's carrying amount and is recovered through prospective depreciation. Mixing the two treatments loses marks.)*

### Q23. Ch: AS 17 — Identification of Reportable Segments (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** Prism Ltd. operates through five business segments. The following data (₹ in lakhs) relate to the year ended 31st March 2025. Identify the **reportable segments** under **AS 17** and verify whether the 75% requirement is satisfied.

| Segment | Segment Revenue | Segment Result (Profit/(Loss)) | Segment Assets |
|-----|------:|------:|------:|
| A | 300 | 40 | 250 |
| B | 150 | (30) | 120 |
| C | 80 | 10 | 60 |
| D | 40 | 5 | 30 |
| E | 30 | (5) | 20 |
| **Total** | **600** | — | **480** |

**Solution:**
As per **AS 17 "Segment Reporting"**, a business/geographical segment is a **reportable segment** if it satisfies **any one** of three 10% thresholds — revenue, result, or assets — computed as follows.

**WN-1: Revenue threshold** = 10% of total revenue = 10% × 600 = **₹60 lakhs.**

**WN-2: Result threshold.**
Combined profit of profit-making segments = 40 + 10 + 5 = **₹55 lakhs.**
Combined loss of loss-making segments = 30 + 5 = **₹35 lakhs.**
Threshold = 10% of the **greater absolute amount** = 10% × 55 = **₹5.5 lakhs.**

**WN-3: Assets threshold** = 10% of total segment assets = 10% × 480 = **₹48 lakhs.**

**WN-4: Applying the tests (segment is reportable if it crosses any one threshold)**

| Segment | Revenue ≥ 60? | Result (abs) ≥ 5.5? | Assets ≥ 48? | Reportable? |
|-----|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| A | 300 ✓ | 40 ✓ | 250 ✓ | **Yes** |
| B | 150 ✓ | 30 ✓ | 120 ✓ | **Yes** |
| C | 80 ✓ | 10 ✓ | 60 ✓ | **Yes** |
| D | 40 ✗ | 5 ✗ | 30 ✗ | No |
| E | 30 ✗ | 5 ✗ | 20 ✗ | No |

**WN-5: 75% test.**
AS 17 requires that reportable segments constitute **at least 75% of total external (enterprise) revenue**; otherwise additional segments are identified as reportable even if below 10%.
External revenue of reportable segments (A + B + C) = 300 + 150 + 80 = **₹530 lakhs.**
Percentage = 530 ÷ 600 = **88.33% ≥ 75%.** Requirement satisfied.

**Answer:** The reportable segments are **A, B and C**; segments D and E are not individually reportable and may be aggregated into an "all other segments" reconciling category. The 75% requirement is satisfied (88.33%).

### Q24. Ch: AS 18 — Related Party Relationships and Key Management Personnel Disclosure (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** With reference to **AS 18 "Related Party Disclosures"**, answer the following for Meridian Ltd. for FY 2024-25:
(i) Define **Key Management Personnel (KMP)** and state the disclosure required in respect of KMP compensation.
(ii) Mr. R is a **non-executive director** who attends Board meetings and draws only sitting fees, with no authority over planning, directing or controlling the entity's activities. Is he KMP?
(iii) Ms. S is the **Managing Director** and her brother's proprietary firm supplied raw material worth ₹40,00,000 during the year. State whether, and what, disclosure is required.

**Answer:**
As per **AS 18**, **Key Management Personnel** are those persons who have the **authority and responsibility for planning, directing and controlling the activities of the reporting enterprise** — for example, a whole-time director or managing director. Mere possession of a title is not the test; the test is authority and responsibility over the enterprise's activities.

**(i) Disclosure of KMP compensation.** Where there have been transactions between the enterprise and its KMP, AS 18 requires disclosure of the **name of the related party, the nature of the relationship, the nature of transactions, the volume (amount or appropriate proportion), amounts outstanding at the balance sheet date, and provisions for doubtful debts and amounts written off** in respect of those amounts. Remuneration paid to KMP is a related party transaction requiring disclosure.

**(ii) Status of Mr. R (non-executive director).** A non-executive director who **merely attends Board meetings and draws sitting fees**, without the authority and responsibility for planning, directing and controlling the enterprise's activities, is **not** Key Management Personnel under AS 18. Hence no KMP disclosure is triggered by his sitting fees.

**(iii) Ms. S (MD) and her brother's firm.** Ms. S, being the Managing Director, is KMP and hence a related party. **Relatives of KMP** are also related parties, and an **enterprise over which a relative of KMP is able to exercise significant influence** (here, her brother's proprietary concern) is a related party. The purchase of raw material of ₹40,00,000 is a related party transaction and must be disclosed, giving the name of the related party, the relationship, the nature and amount of the transaction, and any amount outstanding at year-end.

**Conclusion:** KMP is defined by authority/responsibility, not title; Mr. R is not KMP; the ₹40,00,000 purchase from the MD's brother's firm is a disclosable related party transaction.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner rewards the "authority and responsibility" test (not designation) for KMP, and the correct identification of a relative's concern as a related party. A common error is disclosing the amount but omitting the required particulars — name, relationship, nature, volume and outstanding balances.)*

### Q25. Ch: AS 19 — Sale and Leaseback Resulting in a Finance Lease (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** On 1st April 2024, Coastal Ltd. **sold** a plant with a carrying amount of **₹40,00,000** to a leasing company for **₹50,00,000** and immediately **leased it back** under a **finance lease** for a non-cancellable period of **5 years**. Explain the treatment under **AS 19** of the excess of sale price over carrying amount, show the amount recognised in the Statement of Profit and Loss for the year ended 31st March 2025, and pass the entry for the sale.

**Solution:**
As per **AS 19 "Leases"**, in a **sale and leaseback transaction that results in a finance lease**, the excess of sale proceeds over the carrying amount **should not be immediately recognised as income** in the financial statements of the seller-lessee. Instead, it is **deferred and amortised over the lease term** in proportion to the depreciation of the leased asset. This is because a sale-and-finance-leaseback is, in substance, a means by which the lessor provides finance to the lessee with the asset as security — a genuine sale has not occurred.

**WN-1: Excess (profit) to be deferred.**
Sale price − Carrying amount = 50,00,000 − 40,00,000 = **₹10,00,000 (deferred gain).**

**WN-2: Amortisation of deferred gain.**
Amortised over the lease term of 5 years = 10,00,000 ÷ 5 = **₹2,00,000 per annum** credited to the Statement of Profit and Loss.
Amount recognised in P&L for FY 2024-25 = **₹2,00,000**; deferred balance carried forward = **₹8,00,000.**

**Journal Entry on 1st April 2024 (sale):**

| Particulars | Dr (₹) | Cr (₹) |
|-----|------:|------:|
| Bank A/c Dr | 50,00,000 | |
| To Plant A/c (carrying amount) | | 40,00,000 |
| To Deferred Income (Sale & Leaseback) A/c | | 10,00,000 |

**On 31st March 2025 (amortisation):**

| Particulars | Dr (₹) | Cr (₹) |
|-----|------:|------:|
| Deferred Income (Sale & Leaseback) A/c Dr | 2,00,000 | |
| To Profit & Loss A/c | | 2,00,000 |

The asset is simultaneously brought back into the books at the leased amount and depreciated, and a finance lease liability is recognised — the deferred gain is released in proportion to that depreciation.

**Answer:** The excess of **₹10,00,000** is deferred and amortised over 5 years; **₹2,00,000** is recognised in the Statement of Profit and Loss for FY 2024-25, and **₹8,00,000** remains as deferred income.

*(Full-marks tip: examiners reward the reasoning that in a sale-and-finance-leaseback the "profit" is not real income but deferred and amortised over the lease term. Immediately crediting the ₹10,00,000 to P&L is the classic error. Contrast this with a sale-and-operating-leaseback at fair value, where profit IS recognised immediately.)*

### Q26. Ch: AS 20 — Diluted Earnings Per Share with Convertible Debentures (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** From the following information of Vertex Ltd. for the year ended 31st March 2025, compute **Basic EPS and Diluted EPS** under **AS 20** and state whether the potential equity shares are dilutive.

| Particulars | Amount |
|-----|------:|
| Net profit attributable to equity shareholders | ₹50,00,000 |
| Weighted average number of equity shares outstanding | 10,00,000 |
| 12% Convertible Debentures (₹100 each) | 10,000 debentures |
| Terms of conversion | Each debenture convertible into 8 equity shares |
| Tax rate | 30% |

**Solution:**
As per **AS 20 "Earnings Per Share"**, **basic EPS** is net profit attributable to equity shareholders divided by the weighted average number of equity shares. **Diluted EPS** adjusts both the numerator and denominator for the effects of all **dilutive potential equity shares** — the numerator is increased by the post-tax interest that would be saved on conversion, and the denominator by the number of shares issuable on conversion.

**WN-1: Basic EPS.**
Basic EPS = 50,00,000 ÷ 10,00,000 = **₹5.00 per share.**

**WN-2: Adjustment to earnings on assumed conversion.**
Interest on debentures = 10,000 × ₹100 × 12% = ₹1,20,000.
Post-tax interest saved (added back) = 1,20,000 × (1 − 0.30) = **₹84,000.**
Adjusted earnings = 50,00,000 + 84,000 = **₹50,84,000.**

**WN-3: Potential equity shares on conversion.**
Shares issuable = 10,000 × 8 = **80,000 shares.**
Adjusted number of shares = 10,00,000 + 80,000 = **10,80,000.**

**WN-4: Dilution test.**
Incremental EPS from the potential shares = 84,000 ÷ 80,000 = **₹1.05 per share.**
Since ₹1.05 < Basic EPS of ₹5.00, the conversion **reduces EPS** — the potential equity shares are **dilutive** and must be included.

**WN-5: Diluted EPS.**
Diluted EPS = 50,84,000 ÷ 10,80,000 = **₹4.71 per share.**

**Statement of EPS — Year ended 31.03.2025**

| Particulars | Earnings (₹) | Shares | EPS (₹) |
|-----|------:|------:|------:|
| Basic | 50,00,000 | 10,00,000 | 5.00 |
| Effect of convertible debentures | 84,000 | 80,000 | — |
| **Diluted** | **50,84,000** | **10,80,000** | **4.71** |

**Answer:** Basic EPS = **₹5.00**; Diluted EPS = **₹4.71**. The convertible debentures are dilutive (incremental EPS ₹1.05 < basic EPS ₹5.00).

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner insists on the dilution test — a potential share is included in diluted EPS ONLY if its incremental EPS is less than basic EPS; anti-dilutive shares are ignored. Forgetting to add back the interest net of tax, or omitting the dilution check, is the usual deduction.)*

### Q27. Ch: AS 22 — Deferred Tax Asset on Carry-forward Loss and Unabsorbed Depreciation (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** For the year ended 31st March 2025, Horizon Ltd. has a carry-forward **business loss of ₹30,00,000** and **unabsorbed depreciation of ₹10,00,000** under the Income-tax Act, 1961. The applicable tax rate is **30%**. The management is confident of future profits. Explain, under **AS 22**, the conditions for recognising a deferred tax asset (DTA) on these items, compute the DTA, and state how the recognition test differs from that for other timing differences.

**Answer:**
As per **AS 22 "Accounting for Taxes on Income"**, a deferred tax asset arises from **deductible timing differences**, including carry-forward of losses and unabsorbed depreciation. However, AS 22 imposes a **stricter recognition test** for DTA arising from unabsorbed depreciation or carry-forward of losses: such a DTA is recognised **only to the extent that there is virtual certainty supported by convincing evidence** that sufficient future taxable income will be available against which the DTA can be realised.

For **other deductible timing differences** (e.g., provision for doubtful debts disallowed under tax), the lower threshold of **reasonable certainty** of future taxable income is sufficient.

**WN-1: Amount of DTA.**
Deductible amount = Carry-forward business loss + Unabsorbed depreciation = 30,00,000 + 10,00,000 = **₹40,00,000.**
DTA = 40,00,000 × 30% = **₹12,00,000.**

**Distinction — recognition threshold under AS 22**

| Nature of DTA | Recognition condition |
|-----|------|
| Carry-forward loss / unabsorbed depreciation | **Virtual certainty** supported by convincing evidence of future taxable income |
| Other deductible timing differences | **Reasonable certainty** of sufficient future taxable income |

Mere **confidence** or optimism of management is not "convincing evidence". Virtual certainty requires evidence such as firm sales/export orders in hand or binding contracts that make future taxable income virtually certain. In the absence of such convincing evidence, the DTA of ₹12,00,000 **should not be recognised**, and the position should be re-assessed at each balance sheet date.

**Conclusion:** The DTA of ₹12,00,000 may be recognised only if **virtual certainty supported by convincing evidence** of future taxable income exists; otherwise it is not recognised. Management's general confidence alone is insufficient.

*(Full-marks tip: examiners specifically test the "virtual certainty + convincing evidence" threshold for losses/unabsorbed depreciation versus "reasonable certainty" for other items — stating both thresholds and the need for objective evidence earns full marks. Recognising the DTA on bare management confidence is the classic error.)*

### Q28. Ch: AS 24 — Discontinuing Operations: Initial Disclosure Event (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain, under **AS 24 "Discontinuing Operations"**, (i) what constitutes a "discontinuing operation", (ii) the meaning of the **"initial disclosure event"**, and (iii) two key items required to be disclosed in respect of a discontinuing operation.

**Answer:**
As per **AS 24 "Discontinuing Operations"**, a **discontinuing operation** is a component of an enterprise that (a) the enterprise, pursuant to a **single plan**, is disposing of substantially in its entirety, or disposing of piecemeal, or terminating through abandonment; (b) **represents a separate major line of business or geographical area of operations**; and (c) can be **distinguished operationally and for financial reporting purposes**. Merely deciding to phase out a product model or shift some production is not, by itself, a discontinuing operation.

**(ii) Initial disclosure event.** The initial disclosure event is the **earlier of**:
(a) the enterprise entering into a **binding sale agreement** for substantially all of the assets attributable to the discontinuing operation; or
(b) the enterprise's **Board of Directors (or similar governing body) both approving a detailed, formal plan** for the discontinuance **and making an announcement of the plan**.
The disclosures required by AS 24 commence in the financial statements of the period in which the initial disclosure event occurs.

**(iii) Key disclosures.** In respect of a discontinuing operation, the enterprise must disclose, among others: the **description of the discontinuing operation, the business/geographical segment in which it is reported, the date and nature of the initial disclosure event, the expected timing and manner of disposal**, and the **carrying amounts of total assets and total liabilities** to be disposed of. It must also disclose the **pre-tax profit or loss from ordinary activities** attributable to the discontinuing operation, the related income tax, and the **net cash flows** attributable to operating, investing and financing activities.

**Conclusion:** A discontinuing operation is a separate major line/area disposed of under a single plan; disclosure begins at the initial disclosure event (earlier of a binding sale agreement or Board approval-and-announcement of a formal plan).

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner rewards the precise "earlier of binding sale agreement OR Board approval AND announcement" wording for the initial disclosure event, and the three-part test for what qualifies as a discontinuing operation. Confusing AS 24 with treatment as an extraordinary item loses marks.)*

### Q30. Ch: AS 26 — Research vs Development Expenditure on an Intangible Asset (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** During FY 2024-25, Innova Ltd. incurred the following expenditure on an internal project to develop a new production process. | Sl. | Expenditure | ₹ |

| (iv) | Initial operating losses incurred while demand built up | 3,00,000 |

As per **AS 26 "Intangible Assets"**, expenditure on an internal project is split into a **research phase** and a **development phase**. **No intangible asset arising from research (or the research phase) shall be recognised** — such expenditure is charged to the Statement of Profit and Loss as incurred. Expenditure in the **development phase** is recognised as an intangible asset **only if the enterprise can demonstrate all of the following**: technical feasibility of completing the asset, its intention and ability to complete and use/sell it, the manner in which it will generate probable future economic benefits, the availability of adequate resources to complete it, and the ability to measure the expenditure reliably.

Applying these principles:

| Sl. | Nature | Treatment | ₹ |
|-----|------|------|------:|
| (i) | Research phase (before feasibility established) | Charge to P&L | 6,00,000 |
| (ii) | Development phase (all recognition criteria met) | Capitalise | 14,00,000 |
| (iii) | Selling/admin overheads not directly attributable | Charge to P&L (cannot be part of cost) | 2,00,000 |
| (iv) | Initial operating losses | Charge to P&L (expressly excluded from cost) | 3,00,000 |

**Amount to be capitalised as intangible asset = ₹14,00,000** (item ii only).
**Amount charged to the Statement of Profit and Loss = ₹6,00,000 + ₹2,00,000 + ₹3,00,000 = ₹11,00,000.**

**Conclusion:** Only ₹14,00,000 (development-phase cost satisfying all recognition criteria) is capitalised; ₹11,00,000 is expensed. AS 26 specifically excludes selling/administrative overheads and initial operating losses from the cost of an internally generated intangible asset.

Capitalising research or start-up losses is the classic error.)*

### Q31. Ch: AS 28 — Impairment Loss and Allocation across a Cash-Generating Unit (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** Apex Ltd. has a cash-generating unit (CGU) comprising the following assets at 31st March 2025. Owing to a downturn, the recoverable amount of the CGU as a whole is estimated at **₹32,00,000**. Compute the **impairment loss** and show its **allocation** to the individual assets under **AS 28**, and give the revised carrying amounts.

| Asset | Carrying amount (₹) |
|-----|------:|
| Goodwill | 5,00,000 |
| Building | 21,00,000 |
| Plant & Machinery | 14,00,000 |
| **Total carrying amount** | **40,00,000** |

**Solution:**
As per **AS 28 "Impairment of Assets"**, an impairment loss for a CGU is recognised when its **recoverable amount** (higher of value in use and net selling price) is **less than its carrying amount**. The loss is allocated **first to goodwill** of the unit, and **then to the other assets of the unit pro rata on the basis of their carrying amounts**. However, no individual asset is written down below the highest of its net selling price, value in use, or zero.

**WN-1: Total impairment loss.**
Impairment loss = Carrying amount − Recoverable amount = 40,00,000 − 32,00,000 = **₹8,00,000.**

**WN-2: Allocation — Step 1 (to goodwill first).**
Goodwill of ₹5,00,000 is fully written off. Balance loss to be allocated = 8,00,000 − 5,00,000 = **₹3,00,000.**

**WN-3: Allocation — Step 2 (pro rata to other assets).**
Ratio of carrying amounts, Building : Plant = 21,00,000 : 14,00,000 = **3 : 2.**
- Building = 3,00,000 × 21/35 = **₹1,80,000.**
- Plant & Machinery = 3,00,000 × 14/35 = **₹1,20,000.**

**Statement showing allocation of impairment loss and revised carrying amounts**

| Asset | Carrying amount (₹) | Impairment allocated (₹) | Revised carrying amount (₹) |
|-----|------:|------:|------:|
| Goodwill | 5,00,000 | 5,00,000 | Nil |
| Building | 21,00,000 | 1,80,000 | 19,20,000 |
| Plant & Machinery | 14,00,000 | 1,20,000 | 12,80,000 |
| **Total** | **40,00,000** | **8,00,000** | **32,00,000** |

**Answer:** The impairment loss is **₹8,00,000**, allocated ₹5,00,000 to goodwill (fully written off) and the balance ₹3,00,000 to Building (₹1,80,000) and Plant & Machinery (₹1,20,000) in the ratio 3:2. Revised carrying amounts: Building ₹19,20,000 and Plant & Machinery ₹12,80,000, aggregating to the recoverable amount of ₹32,00,000.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner insists on the two-step order — goodwill first, then pro rata on carrying amounts — and on the floor rule that no asset is reduced below its own recoverable amount. Allocating the whole ₹8,00,000 pro rata, or ignoring goodwill's priority, is the usual deduction.)*

### Q32. Ch: AS 29 — Provision, Contingent Liability and Contingent Asset (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** For the year ended 31st March 2025, examine each of the following situations of Sentinel Ltd. and state, with reasons, the correct treatment under **AS 29 "Provisions, Contingent Liabilities and Contingent Assets"** — whether to recognise a **provision**, disclose a **contingent liability**, or make **no disclosure**.

| Sl. | Situation |
|-----|------|
| (i) | A customer has filed a suit for ₹12,00,000; the company's lawyers advise that it is probable the company will lose ₹8,00,000 |
| (ii) | The company has given a guarantee for a loan taken by its associate; as at 31.03.2025 the associate is financially sound and default is not probable |
| (iii) | The company has itself filed a claim of ₹5,00,000 against a supplier and, based on legal advice, recovery is virtually certain |

**Answer:**
As per **AS 29**, a **provision** is recognised only when (a) an enterprise has a **present obligation** as a result of a past event, (b) it is **probable** that an outflow of resources embodying economic benefits will be required to settle it, and (c) a **reliable estimate** can be made of the obligation. A **contingent liability** is a possible obligation, or a present obligation whose outflow is not probable or cannot be reliably measured, and is **only disclosed, not provided**. A **contingent asset** is **not recognised**; it is disclosed only where the inflow of economic benefits is probable, and if realisation is virtually certain, it ceases to be contingent and the related asset is recognised.

**(i) Customer's suit.** There is a present obligation from a past event, outflow of ₹8,00,000 is **probable**, and the amount can be reliably estimated. Hence a **provision of ₹8,00,000 is recognised**; the balance ₹4,00,000 (possible but not probable) is disclosed as a contingent liability.

**(ii) Guarantee for associate's loan.** This is a possible obligation depending on the associate's default. As default is **not probable** (associate is financially sound), no provision is made; it is **disclosed as a contingent liability** (with the estimated financial effect), and reviewed at each balance sheet date.

**(iii) Company's own claim against supplier.** This is a **contingent asset**. Since recovery is **virtually certain** (based on legal advice), it is **no longer contingent** and the asset and related gain of ₹5,00,000 **should be recognised** in the financial statements.

**Conclusion:** (i) recognise a provision of ₹8,00,000 (disclose ₹4,00,000 as contingent liability); (ii) disclose a contingent liability; (iii) recognise the asset of ₹5,00,000, virtual certainty having removed the contingency.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner rewards the sharp distinction between "probable" (provide) and "possible" (disclose), and the rule that a contingent asset is recognised only when the inflow becomes virtually certain. Providing for the full claim in (i) or recognising the guarantee as a provision in (ii) loses marks.)*

### Q33. Ch: AS 29 — Restructuring Provision: Items Includible and Excludible (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** On 15th March 2025, the Board of Meghna Ltd. approved and announced a detailed formal plan to close a division, raising a **valid expectation** in those affected. The estimated costs are given below. Determine the amount of the **restructuring provision** to be recognised at 31st March 2025 under **AS 29**, giving reasons for including or excluding each item.

| Sl. | Estimated cost of restructuring | ₹ |
|-----|------|------:|
| (i) | Redundancy/retrenchment compensation to employees being terminated | 20,00,000 |
| (ii) | Cost of retraining and relocating **continuing** staff | 6,00,000 |
| (iii) | Contractual termination penalty on cancelling a lease of the division's premises | 4,00,000 |
| (iv) | Expected future operating losses of the division up to the closure date | 5,00,000 |
| (v) | Investment in new systems and distribution networks for the continuing business | 8,00,000 |

**Answer:**
As per **AS 29**, a provision for restructuring is recognised only when the general recognition criteria are met and the enterprise has a **constructive obligation** — arising when it has a **detailed formal plan** identifying the business/part concerned, the location and number of employees affected, the expenditure to be undertaken and the timing, **and** has raised a **valid expectation** in those affected by starting to implement the plan or announcing its main features. Here, Board approval and announcement on 15.03.2025 have raised a valid expectation, so a constructive obligation exists.

A restructuring provision includes **only the direct expenditures arising from the restructuring**, which are those both **necessarily entailed** by the restructuring and **not associated with the ongoing activities** of the enterprise. Specifically excluded are costs of retraining or relocating continuing staff, marketing, and investment in new systems, as these relate to the future conduct of the business. **Future operating losses are not provided for** (they do not arise from a past obligating event).

| Sl. | Item | Treatment | ₹ |

| (i) | Retrenchment compensation to terminated staff | Include (direct, entailed by restructuring) | 20,00,000 |
| (ii) | Retraining/relocating continuing staff | Exclude (relates to ongoing activities) | — |
| (iii) | Lease termination penalty | Include (direct cost necessarily entailed) | 4,00,000 |
| (iv) | Future operating losses up to closure | Exclude (AS 29 prohibits provision for future operating losses) | — |
| (v) | Investment in new systems/networks | Exclude (relates to future conduct of business) | — |

**Restructuring provision = ₹20,00,000 + ₹4,00,000 = ₹24,00,000.**

**Conclusion:** A restructuring provision of **₹24,00,000** is recognised at 31.03.2025, comprising only the retrenchment compensation and the lease termination penalty. Retraining/relocation costs, future operating losses and new investment are excluded.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner rewards the "detailed formal plan + valid expectation" test for a constructive obligation, and the rule that only costs necessarily entailed by and not associated with ongoing activities are provided. Including future operating losses or retraining costs is the classic error.)*

### Q34. Ch: AS 20 — EPS Adjustment for a Rights Issue (Theoretical Ex-Rights Price) (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** Nayara Ltd. had **5,00,000 equity shares** outstanding as on 1st April 2024. On **1st October 2024**, it made a **rights issue of 1 new share for every 4 held at ₹15 per share**, when the fair value of the share immediately before the rights issue was **₹25**. Net profit for the year ended 31st March 2025 was **₹27,50,000** and for the year ended 31st March 2024 was **₹22,00,000**. Compute the **Basic EPS for FY 2024-25** and the **restated Basic EPS for FY 2023-24** (comparative), under **AS 20**.

**Solution:**
As per **AS 20**, a **rights issue at a price below fair value contains a bonus element**. Therefore the number of equity shares outstanding **before** the rights issue is **adjusted for the bonus element** by multiplying by an **adjustment factor**, so that EPS across periods is comparable. The adjustment factor is:

Adjustment factor = Fair value per share immediately before the exercise of rights ÷ **Theoretical ex-rights fair value per share (TERP)**.

**WN-1: Theoretical ex-rights price (TERP).**
Consider 4 existing shares plus 1 rights share (ratio 1:4):

| Particulars | Shares | ₹ |
|-----|------:|------:|
| Fair value of shares before rights (4 × ₹25) | 4 | 100 |
| Proceeds from rights share (1 × ₹15) | 1 | 15 |
| **Total** | **5** | **115** |

TERP = 115 ÷ 5 = **₹23 per share.**

**WN-2: Adjustment (bonus) factor.**
Adjustment factor = Fair value before rights ÷ TERP = 25 ÷ 23 = **1.0870.**

**WN-3: Weighted average number of equity shares for FY 2024-25.**
Rights shares issued = 5,00,000 ÷ 4 = 1,25,000; total after rights = **6,25,000.**

| Period | Shares | Adjustment | Time weight | Weighted shares |
|-----|------:|------:|------:|------:|
| 01.04.2024 – 30.09.2024 (6 months) | 5,00,000 | × 25/23 | × 6/12 | 2,71,739 |
| 01.10.2024 – 31.03.2025 (6 months) | 6,25,000 | — | × 6/12 | 3,12,500 |
| **Weighted average number of shares** | | | | **5,84,239** |

**WN-4: Basic EPS for FY 2024-25.**
Basic EPS = 27,50,000 ÷ 5,84,239 = **₹4.71 per share.**

**WN-5: Restated Basic EPS for FY 2023-24 (comparative).**
The prior-year EPS is restated by dividing by the adjustment factor (i.e., multiplying by TERP ÷ fair value):
Original 2023-24 EPS = 22,00,000 ÷ 5,00,000 = ₹4.40.
Restated 2023-24 EPS = 4.40 × (23 ÷ 25) = **₹4.05 per share.**

**Statement of Basic EPS**

| Year | Net profit (₹) | Weighted avg shares | Basic EPS (₹) |
|-----|------:|------:|------:|
| 2024-25 | 27,50,000 | 5,84,239 | 4.71 |
| 2023-24 (restated) | 22,00,000 | (5,00,000 × 25/23) = 5,43,478 | 4.05 |

**Answer:** Basic EPS for FY 2024-25 is **₹4.71** and the restated comparative Basic EPS for FY 2023-24 is **₹4.05**.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner rewards (a) computing TERP correctly, (b) applying the bonus adjustment factor of 25/23 only to the pre-rights period, and (c) restating the previous year's EPS. Treating the rights issue as a simple time-weighted issue without the bonus element is the classic error.)*

### Q35. Ch: AS 3 Cash Flow Statements — Cash & Cash Equivalents and Foreign Currency Balances (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State, with reasons under AS 3 (Revised), how each of the following should be treated while preparing a Cash Flow Statement for the year ended 31st March, 2025: (i) a bank overdraft of ₹3,50,000 repayable on demand which forms an integral part of the company's cash management; (ii) 91-day Treasury Bills purchased 20 days before the balance-sheet date; (iii) equity shares of a listed company held as a short-term marketable investment; (iv) US $ 12,000 lying in a foreign-currency current account. Also state when cash flows may be reported on a **net** basis.

**Answer:**
As per **AS 3 (Revised), Cash Flow Statements**, "Cash" comprises cash on hand and demand deposits, and "Cash equivalents" are short-term, highly liquid investments that are readily convertible into known amounts of cash and which are subject to an insignificant risk of changes in value.

(i) **Bank overdraft repayable on demand** — Although the standard normally treats bank borrowings as financing activities, where a bank overdraft is repayable on demand and forms an **integral part of an enterprise's cash management**, it is included as a **component of cash and cash equivalents** and the net balance of cash is disclosed accordingly. Hence the ₹3,50,000 overdraft is netted against cash and cash equivalents.

(ii) **91-day Treasury Bills purchased 20 days before year-end** — An investment normally qualifies as a cash equivalent only when it has a **short maturity of, say, three months or less from the date of acquisition**. Since these T-Bills were acquired only 20 days before the balance-sheet date (remaining maturity well under three months) and carry insignificant risk of value change, they qualify as **cash equivalents**.

(iii) **Equity shares held as marketable investment** — Investments in **equity shares are excluded** from cash equivalents because they carry a **significant risk of change in value**; they are treated as **investing activities**. (Preference shares acquired shortly before their redemption date are an exception.)

(iv) **Foreign-currency balance of US $12,000** — This is cash held in foreign currency. It is reported at the **exchange rate at the balance-sheet date**; the unrealised gain/loss on such balance is **not a cash flow**, but the effect of exchange-rate changes on cash held is reported **separately** to reconcile opening and closing cash.

**Net-basis reporting:** Cash flows may be reported on a net basis for (a) receipts/payments made on behalf of customers where flows reflect the customer's activity, and (b) items where turnover is quick, amounts are large and maturities are short.

**Conclusion:** Items (i) and (ii) form part of cash and cash equivalents; (iii) is an investing activity; (iv) is cash held in foreign currency with the exchange effect shown separately.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the exact AS 3 phrase "integral part of cash management" for overdraft and "acquisition date maturity of three months or less" for cash equivalents; a common deduction is treating equity shares as cash equivalents.)*

### Q39. Ch: AS 3 Cash Flow Statements — Activity Classification of Special Items (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State, giving reasons under AS 3, under which activity (Operating / Investing / Financing) each of the following cash flows of a manufacturing company should be classified: (i) cash paid to acquire goodwill of another business; (ii) insurance claim received on account of loss of stock by fire; (iii) cash advance/loan given by the company to another entity (the company is not a financial enterprise); (iv) capital-gains tax paid on sale of a factory building.

**Answer:**
Under **AS 3 (Revised)**, cash flows are classified into operating, investing and financing activities according to the nature of the underlying transaction; where a single item straddles more than one activity, it is **apportioned** appropriately.

(i) **Cash paid to acquire goodwill** — Goodwill is an intangible **long-term asset**; its acquisition is an **Investing Activity**.

(ii) **Insurance claim for loss of stock by fire** — Since the underlying item (stock/inventory) relates to the principal revenue-producing activities, the claim received is an **Operating Activity** cash inflow. (Had the claim related to a fixed asset, it would be investing.)

(iii) **Loan/advance given by a non-financial company** — Making loans is not this company's principal activity; therefore both the loan given and its recovery are **Investing Activities**. (For a financial enterprise, such loans would be operating.)

(iv) **Capital-gains tax on sale of a factory building** — Taxes on income are generally operating; however, where the tax can be **specifically identified** with an investing transaction (sale of building), that portion of tax is classified with **Investing Activities**.

**Conclusion:** (i) Investing, (ii) Operating, (iii) Investing, (iv) Investing.

### Q42. Ch: Redemption of Preference Shares — Section 55 Conditions & Sources (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the conditions and permissible sources for the redemption of preference shares under **Section 55 of the Companies Act, 2013**. Also state whether Capital Redemption Reserve can be used for issuing bonus shares, and how the **premium payable on redemption** is to be provided.

**Answer:**
**Section 55 of the Companies Act, 2013** governs the issue and redemption of preference shares. The key conditions are:

1. A company limited by shares may, if so authorised by its **articles**, issue redeemable preference shares, but **no shares can be irredeemable**; every issue must be redeemable within a period **not exceeding 20 years** (except infrastructure projects, which may exceed 20 years subject to redemption of a minimum percentage annually).
2. **Only fully paid-up** preference shares can be redeemed.
3. Redemption must be made **either** out of **profits of the company which would otherwise be available for dividend**, **or** out of the **proceeds of a fresh issue of shares** made for the purpose of redemption.
4. Where redemption is out of **profits**, a sum equal to the **nominal amount of the shares redeemed** must be transferred to the **Capital Redemption Reserve (CRR)** account. The CRR is treated as if it were paid-up share capital.

**Use of CRR:** The CRR may be used **only** for issuing **fully paid bonus shares** to members [Sec 55(3) / Sec 69 principle]; it cannot be distributed as dividend or used to write off any other loss.

**Premium on redemption:** The premium payable on redemption is to be provided **out of the profits** of the company **or out of the Securities Premium** account before the shares are redeemed. (Under the current framework, companies are expected to provide the premium out of Securities Premium first where available, otherwise out of divisible profits.)

| Basis | Redemption out of profits | Redemption out of fresh issue |
|---|---|---|
| CRR required | Yes, = nominal value redeemed | No (to the extent of fresh issue) |
| Effect on capital | Capital maintained via CRR | New capital replaces old |

**Conclusion:** Redemption is permitted only from divisible profits or fresh-issue proceeds, only for fully paid shares, with CRR equal to the nominal value redeemed out of profits, and the redemption premium provided from Securities Premium/profits.

*(Full-marks tip: State the "20-year" ceiling and that CRR equals the *nominal* value redeemed *out of profits* — not the total redemption amount; naming Sec 55 explicitly is expected.)*

### Q46. Ch: Buy-back of Securities — Sections 68/69/70 Sources & CRR (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the permissible **sources of funds** for buy-back of shares and the **requirement of Capital Redemption Reserve** under Sections 68 and 69 of the Companies Act, 2013. Also state, under Section 70, the sources and circumstances in which a company is **prohibited** from buying back its securities.

**Answer:**
**Section 68** permits a company to purchase its own shares or other specified securities (buy-back) **out of**:
(a) its **free reserves**; or
(b) its **securities premium account**; or
(c) the **proceeds of the issue of any shares or other specified securities** (but not from the proceeds of an earlier issue of the *same kind* of shares/securities).

**Capital Redemption Reserve — Section 69:** Where a company buys back its own shares out of **free reserves or securities premium**, a sum equal to the **nominal value of the shares bought back** must be transferred to the **Capital Redemption Reserve (CRR)** account, and the details of such transfer are disclosed in the balance sheet. The CRR may be applied in paying up unissued shares as **fully paid bonus shares**. (Where buy-back is out of the proceeds of a fresh issue, no CRR is required to that extent.)

**Section 70 — Prohibitions:** No company shall buy back its securities:
1. out of the proceeds of an **earlier issue of the same kind** of shares/specified securities;
2. through any **subsidiary** company including its own subsidiaries;
3. through any **investment company** or group of investment companies; or
4. if the company is in **default** in repayment of deposits/interest, redemption of debentures/preference shares, payment of dividend, or repayment of any term loan/interest to a bank/financial institution (unless the default has been remedied and three years have elapsed).
Further, no buy-back is allowed if the company has not complied with **Sections 92 (annual return), 123 (dividend), 127 (payment of dividend) and 129 (financial statements)**.

**Conclusion:** Buy-back may be financed only from free reserves, securities premium, or fresh-issue proceeds, with CRR equal to the nominal value bought back where the first two sources are used; Section 70 bars buy-back through subsidiaries/investment companies, from the same-kind issue proceeds, and while certain defaults subsist.

*(Full-marks tip: Distinguish clearly between Sec 68 (sources), Sec 69 (CRR) and Sec 70 (prohibitions); listing the four Section 70 defaults and the "same kind of shares" bar earns full marks.)*

### Q51. Ch: Buy-back of Securities — Post-Buy-back Restrictions & Time Limits (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the important **procedural conditions and restrictions** that a company must observe **on and after a buy-back** of its shares under the Companies Act, 2013, relating to (i) extinguishment of shares, (ii) declaration of solvency, (iii) further issue of shares, and (iv) maintenance of a register.

**Answer:**
The following conditions apply on and after a buy-back under **Section 68 read with related provisions**:

(i) **Extinguishment of shares [Sec 68(7)]:** Where a company buys back its own shares/securities, it shall **extinguish and physically destroy** the shares so bought back **within 7 days** of the last date of completion of the buy-back.

(ii) **Declaration of solvency [Sec 68(6)]:** Before making the buy-back, the company must file with the Registrar (and SEBI, where applicable) a **declaration of solvency** signed by at least two directors (one being the managing director, if any), verified by an affidavit stating that the Board has made a full enquiry into the affairs of the company and that it is capable of meeting its liabilities and will not be rendered insolvent within one year.

(iii) **Restriction on further issue [Sec 68(8)]:** After completion of a buy-back, the company shall **not make a further issue of the same kind of shares** (including allotment of new shares) within a period of **six months**, except by way of a **bonus issue** or in discharge of subsisting obligations such as conversion of warrants, stock option schemes, sweat equity or conversion of preference shares/debentures into equity.

(iv) **Register of buy-back [Sec 68(9)]:** The company shall maintain a **register of shares/securities bought back**, recording the consideration paid, the date of cancellation, the date of extinguishing and physically destroying the shares, and such other particulars as prescribed.

Additionally, the buy-back must be **completed within one year** from the date of the special resolution (or Board resolution), and a **return of buy-back** in the prescribed form must be filed with the Registrar within 30 days of completion.

**Conclusion:** A company must destroy the bought-back shares within 7 days, file a declaration of solvency beforehand, refrain from a same-kind further issue for six months, maintain the statutory register, and complete the buy-back within one year.

*(Full-marks tip: The specific numbers — 7 days (extinguishment), 6 months (no further issue), 1 year (completion), 30 days (return) — are the marks; listing the bonus-issue exception to the six-month bar shows precision.)*

### Q52. Ch: Bonus Issue — Sources & Statutory Conditions under Sec 63 (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** "A company may capitalise any reserve for issuing bonus shares." Examine the correctness of this statement with reference to Section 63 of the Companies Act, 2013. Specifically state (i) which reserves may and may not be used, and (ii) whether Securities Premium and Capital Redemption Reserve can be used to convert partly-paid shares into fully-paid shares by way of bonus.

**Answer:**
The statement is **not fully correct**. The issue of fully paid-up bonus shares is governed by **Section 63 of the Companies Act, 2013** read with **Rule 14 of the Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules, 2014**.

**(a) Permitted sources — Section 63(1).** A company may issue fully paid-up bonus shares to its members out of only three sources: (i) its **free reserves**; (ii) the **securities premium account** (Sec 52); and (iii) the **capital redemption reserve account** (Sec 55).

**(b) Reserves that cannot be used.** A reserve created by the **revaluation of assets** is expressly excluded because Section 2(43) defines "free reserves" so as to exclude any unrealised/notional gain. Hence revaluation reserve, and any reserve not available for distribution as dividend, cannot be capitalised for bonus.

**(c) Statutory conditions — Section 63(2).** Bonus can be issued only if (i) it is **authorised by the Articles**; (ii) it is **recommended by the Board and authorised in general meeting**; (iii) the company has **not defaulted** in payment of interest/principal on fixed deposits or debt securities; (iv) it has **not defaulted** in payment of statutory dues of employees (PF, gratuity, bonus); and (v) **partly paid-up shares, if any, are made fully paid-up** before the bonus. Also, per Section 63(3), bonus shares **shall not be issued in lieu of dividend**.

**(d) Partly-paid to fully-paid restriction.** Securities Premium and Capital Redemption Reserve can be used **only to issue fully paid-up bonus shares** — they **cannot** be applied to make **existing partly-paid shares fully paid-up**. Such conversion (which is also a form of bonus) can be met **only out of free reserves / general reserve / surplus in P&L**.

**Conclusion:** Only free reserves, securities premium and CRR may be capitalised; revaluation reserve cannot; and for converting partly-paid shares to fully-paid, only free reserves — not securities premium/CRR — may be used.

*(Full-marks tip: examiners reward the express citing of Sec 63(1)/(2)/(3), Sec 52 and Sec 55, and the specific "SP & CRR only for fully-paid bonus" restriction. Merely listing sources without the partly-paid nuance loses a mark.)*

### Q55. Ch: Rights Issue — Section 62(1)(a) Pre-emptive Right (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the statutory framework of a **rights issue** under Section 62(1)(a) of the Companies Act, 2013. State the conditions relating to the offer, the right of renunciation, and the treatment of shares declined by existing shareholders.

**Answer:**
A **rights issue** is a further issue of shares offered to **existing equity shareholders in proportion to their existing holding**, governed by **Section 62(1)(a) of the Companies Act, 2013**, which preserves the shareholder's **pre-emptive right** so that his proportionate stake is not diluted without consent.

**Conditions of the offer:**
1. The offer must be made to persons who are **holders of equity shares** as on the record date, **in proportion to the paid-up capital** on those shares.
2. The offer is made by a **letter of offer** containing the right to **renounce** the shares in favour of any other person, and this right of renunciation must be stated in the offer.
3. The offer must specify the **number of shares offered** and give a period **not less than 15 days and not more than 30 days** from the date of the offer within which it must be accepted; if not accepted within this period it is **deemed to be declined** [Sec 62(2)].
4. **Shares declined/not taken up** may be **disposed of by the Board** in a manner **not disadvantageous** to the shareholders and the company [Sec 62(1)(a)(iii)].

**Renunciation:** Unless the Articles provide otherwise, the shareholder may accept in full/part, **renounce** in favour of another, or decline. A **listed company** must additionally comply with **SEBI (ICDR) Regulations, 2018**.

**Conclusion:** A rights issue under Sec 62(1)(a) protects the existing shareholder's proportionate ownership, gives a renounceable, time-bound offer (15–30 days), and lets the Board deal advantageously with declined shares.

*(Full-marks tip: cite the 15–30 day window under Sec 62(2) and the "not disadvantageous" disposal of declined shares — these two specifics separate a full-mark answer from a generic one.)*

### Q57. Ch: ESOP — Recognition & Methods under the Guidance Note (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the accounting treatment of **Employee Stock Options** as per the ICAI Guidance Note on Accounting for Employee Share-based Payments (aligned with Ind AS 102). Explain the meaning of grant date, vesting period and exercise period, and how the employee compensation cost is measured and recognised.

**Answer:**
An **employee share-based payment** is a transaction in which an enterprise receives services from employees as consideration for its **equity instruments (stock options)**. It is dealt with under the **ICAI Guidance Note on Accounting for Share-based Payments** (consistent with **Ind AS 102**).

**Key dates:**
- **Grant date** — the date the enterprise and employee agree to the terms; the option's fair value is measured here.
- **Vesting period** — the period between grant date and the date the option **vests** (becomes unconditionally exercisable on fulfilment of service/performance conditions).
- **Exercise period** — the period after vesting within which the employee may exercise the vested options.

**Measurement.** Under the **fair value method** (preferred), the compensation cost = **fair value of the option at grant date** × number expected to vest. Under the **intrinsic value method** (permitted where fair value cannot be reliably estimated), cost = **market price − exercise price** at grant.

**Recognition.** The total cost is recognised as **"Employee Compensation Expense"** in the Statement of P&L, spread **on a straight-line basis over the vesting period**, with the corresponding credit to **"Stock Options Outstanding Account"** (shown under Reserves & Surplus / a separate line). The estimate of options expected to vest is **revised each period** for expected/actual **forfeitures** (a true-up), but the grant-date fair value is **not re-measured** for market movements.

**On exercise**, the Stock Options Outstanding balance for the exercised options is transferred to share capital/securities premium; on **lapse** after vesting, the balance is transferred to a **General Reserve** (not to P&L).

**Conclusion:** ESOP cost = grant-date fair value of options expected to vest, expensed over the vesting period with forfeiture true-ups, credited to Stock Options Outstanding.

*(Full-marks tip: state that forfeiture is trued-up each year but grant-date fair value is frozen, and that post-vesting lapse goes to General Reserve, not back to P&L — these are the two facts examiners test.)*

### Q60. Ch: Underwriting of Shares & Debentures — Concepts & Commission Limits (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between **marked, unmarked and firm underwriting applications**, and state how each is treated in determining an underwriter's net liability. Also state the **maximum underwriting commission** payable under Section 40(6) of the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:**
**Underwriting** is a contract by which the underwriter agrees, for a **commission**, to take up the shares/debentures **not subscribed by the public**, thereby guaranteeing the minimum subscription.

| Term | Meaning | Treatment in liability |
|---|---|---|
| **Marked applications** | Applications bearing an underwriter's **stamp/seal**, traceable to a particular underwriter | Credited to the **individual** underwriter concerned |
| **Unmarked applications** | Applications received **directly** by the company with no underwriter's stamp | In **full/complete** underwriting, distributed among all underwriters in their **gross-liability ratio**; in **partial** underwriting, credited to the **company** (for the portion it self-underwrites) |
| **Firm underwriting** | Shares the underwriter agrees to **subscribe irrespective** of the public response | Treated as **marked** (credited to that underwriter) and then **added back**, since he must take them in addition to his net liability |

**Commission — Section 40(6) read with the Companies (Prospectus and Allotment of Securities) Rules, 2014:** commission is payable only if authorised by the Articles, and must not exceed **5% of the issue price of shares** (or the rate in the Articles, whichever is less) and **2½% of the issue price of debentures** (or the Articles' rate, whichever is less). Commission is computed on the **gross liability** (whole amount underwritten), regardless of how many shares the underwriter ultimately takes.

**Conclusion:** Marked → individual; unmarked → ratio (full) or company (partial); firm → marked + added back; commission ≤ 5% (shares) / 2½% (debentures) on the amount underwritten.

*(Full-marks tip: state that commission is on the amount UNDERWRITTEN, not on shares actually taken by the underwriter, and give both the 5%/2½% caps with the "or Articles, whichever less" qualifier.)*

### Q64. Ch: Redemption of Debentures — DRR & DRI under Rule 18(7) (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the current requirements regarding **Debenture Redemption Reserve (DRR)** and **Debenture Redemption Investment/Deposit (DRI)** under Rule 18(7) of the Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules, 2014 (as amended in 2019). Also list the methods by which debentures may be redeemed.

**Answer:**
Redemption of debentures is governed by **Section 71(4) of the Companies Act, 2013** read with **Rule 18(7) of the Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules, 2014** (as amended by the 2019 amendment).

**(A) Debenture Redemption Reserve (DRR).** DRR is a reserve created out of **profits available for dividend**, to be utilised only for redemption. Post-2019 position:
- **Listed companies** and **NBFCs/HFCs registered with RBI/NHB** (whether listed or unlisted) → **DRR not required.**
- **Unlisted companies (other than NBFC/HFC)** → **DRR = 10% of the outstanding value of debentures.**

**(B) Debenture Redemption Investment/Deposit (DRI).** Every company required to create DRR, **and** every listed NBFC/HFC and other listed company, must, **on or before 30th April of each year, invest or deposit a sum of at least 15% of the amount of debentures maturing during the year ending 31st March of the next year** in prescribed securities/deposits (e.g., bank deposits, Central/State Govt securities). This amount is **used only for redemption** and must not fall below 15% of the debentures maturing that year.

**(C) Methods of redemption:** (i) **payment in lump sum** on maturity; (ii) **payment in annual instalments by draw of lots**; (iii) **purchase of own debentures in the open market** for immediate cancellation or as investment; and (iv) **conversion** into shares/new debentures.

**Conclusion:** Unlisted non-NBFC companies create DRR of 10% of outstanding debentures; DRI of ≥15% of debentures maturing next year is set aside by 30 April; redemption may be in lump sum, by instalments/draw of lots, by open-market purchase, or by conversion.

*(Full-marks tip: quote the exact figures — DRR 10% (unlisted non-NBFC), DRI 15% by 30 April — and the post-2019 exemptions for listed companies and NBFC/HFC. Stating the old "25% DRR" rule is now wrong and is penalised.)*

### Q69. Ch: Investment Accounts — Cum-interest vs Ex-interest quotations (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** An examinee, while preparing an Investment Account for fixed-income securities, is confused about how a "cum-interest" purchase differs from an "ex-interest" purchase, and how each affects the *cost* posted to the capital (principal) column. Explain the distinction and the accounting treatment under the accepted principles of AS 13 for interest-bearing investments.

**Answer:** (theory)
Interest on a fixed-income security (debentures, government stock, bonds) accrues on a day-to-day basis, but is *paid* only on the fixed half-yearly (or annual) interest dates. Consequently, whenever such a security changes hands *between* two interest dates, the price paid by the buyer necessarily contains an element of interest that has accrued from the last interest date up to the date of purchase. The manner of quotation tells us whether that accrued interest is *inside* or *outside* the quoted price.

**Cum-interest quotation:** The quoted (gross) price *includes* the interest accrued from the last interest date up to the date of the transaction. The buyer therefore pays a single composite amount. To arrive at the *capital cost* of the investment (the figure posted to the principal column of the Investment Account), the accrued interest must be **deducted** from the cum-interest price; the accrued interest so separated is posted to the **interest column** (debit) because the buyer, in effect, buys the right to the next interest and will recover it on the following interest date.

**Ex-interest quotation:** The quoted price is the *clean capital price* and does **not** include accrued interest. Here the buyer pays the ex-interest price *plus* the accrued interest separately. The ex-interest price is posted straight to the principal column, and the accrued interest is again posted to the interest column (debit).

| Basis | Cum-interest | Ex-interest |
|---|---|---|
| What the quoted price contains | Capital value **+** accrued interest | Capital value **only** |
| Capital column (cost) | Quoted price **less** accrued interest | Quoted price (as is) |
| Interest column (Dr, on purchase) | Accrued interest separated out of the price | Accrued interest paid over and above the price |
| Total cash paid by buyer | Quoted price | Ex-price **+** accrued interest |

In **both** cases the amount ultimately capitalised as cost of the investment is the same, and the accrued interest never forms part of cost — it is recovered against the next interest receipt so that only the *post-acquisition* interest is recognised as income (AS 13, para 9.2 — interest accrued and included in the cost is not treated as part of cost).

**Conclusion:** The distinction lies only in *where* the accrued interest sits relative to the quoted price; the accrued interest paid on purchase is invariably excluded from cost and routed through the interest column, so that only interest earned for the holding period is taken to the Profit & Loss Account.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner rewards the explicit statement that "accrued interest is never part of cost." A frequent deduction is treating the cum-interest price itself as cost, which overstates the capital column and understates interest income.)*

### Q72. Ch: Investment Accounts — Classification and carrying amount under AS 13 (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State how investments are **classified** under AS 13 "Accounting for Investments" and the **basis of carrying amount** for each class. Also explain how a "reclassification" from one category to the other is measured.

**Answer:** (theory)
AS 13 governs the accounting for investments by enterprises not required to comply with Ind AS. It classifies investments into two categories on the basis of the **intended holding period**:

**(a) Current investments** — an investment that is by its nature *readily realisable* and is intended to be held for **not more than one year** from the date of acquisition. Per AS 13, para 14, a current investment is carried in the financial statements at the **lower of cost and fair value**, determined either on an individual-investment basis or by category, but not on a global basis. Any reduction to fair value (and any reversal) is charged/credited to the Profit & Loss Account.

**(b) Long-term investments** — every investment *other than* a current investment. Per AS 13, para 17, a long-term investment is carried at **cost**; however, a provision for **diminution** is made to recognise a **decline, other than temporary (permanent)**, in its value, determined for each investment individually. Temporary falls are ignored.

**Reclassification (AS 13, para 23):**

| Transfer | Carrying amount transferred at |
|---|---|
| Long-term → Current | **Lower** of cost and carrying amount at date of transfer |
| Current → Long-term | **Lower** of cost and fair value at date of transfer |

**Conclusion:** Current investments are stated at the *lower of cost and fair value* (temporary falls recognised); long-term investments are stated at *cost less permanent diminution* (temporary falls ignored) — the two treatments reflecting the different holding intent.

*(Full-marks tip: the word "**other than temporary**" for long-term investments is the marks-carrying phrase; a common deduction is writing that long-term investments are marked to fair value.)*

### Q74. Ch: Internal Reconstruction — Internal vs External reconstruction (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between **internal reconstruction** and **external reconstruction**, and list the principal methods by which internal reconstruction is carried out.

**Answer:** (theory)
Both are devices to reorganise the capital structure of a company whose accounts are burdened by accumulated losses and over-valued assets, but they differ fundamentally in whether the existing company survives.

| Basis | Internal Reconstruction | External Reconstruction |
|---|---|---|
| Formation of new company | No new company is formed | A **new company** is formed to take over the old one |
| Continuity of legal entity | The **same** company continues | The old company is **liquidated** |
| Governing provision | Reduction of share capital under **Sec 66 of the Companies Act, 2013** (Tribunal-confirmed) | An amalgamation/absorption governed by **AS 14** |
| Approvals | Special resolution + confirmation of the **National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT)** | Scheme of amalgamation sanctioned by NCLT |
| Purchase consideration | Does **not** arise | A **purchase consideration** is computed and discharged |

**Methods of internal reconstruction:** (i) **Alteration of share capital** under Sec 61 (consolidation, sub-division, conversion, cancellation of unissued capital); (ii) **Reduction of share capital** under Sec 66 (writing off paid-up capital lost or unrepresented by assets, or returning surplus capital); (iii) **Variation of shareholders' rights** under Sec 48; and (iv) **compromise/arrangement** with creditors and members under Secs 230–232.

**Conclusion:** Internal reconstruction re-organises capital *within* the surviving company under Sec 66, whereas external reconstruction winds up the old company and floats a new one to which its business is transferred.

*(Full-marks tip: naming Sec 66 for internal and AS 14 for external, plus "no new company is formed," secures the distinguishing marks.)*

### Q77. Ch: Internal Reconstruction — Legal basis, Capital Reduction Account and contingent liabilities (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the statutory basis of a reduction of share capital in an internal reconstruction, the working and disposal of the balance of the **Capital Reduction (Reconstruction) Account**, and how **contingent liabilities** are dealt with in a reconstruction scheme.

**Answer:** (theory)
**Statutory basis.** A company limited by shares may, if authorised by its articles, reduce its share capital by passing a **special resolution** and obtaining the confirmation of the **National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT)** under **Section 66 of the Companies Act, 2013**. The reduction may take the form of (i) extinguishing/reducing liability on partly-paid shares, (ii) cancelling paid-up capital that is **lost or unrepresented by available assets**, or (iii) paying off capital that is in excess of the company's needs. An internal reconstruction typically uses form (ii).

**Capital Reduction (Reconstruction) Account.** This is a temporary account opened to collect the reorganisation adjustments. It is **credited** with all the sacrifices — the reduction in the paid-up value of equity and preference capital, amounts forgone by debenture-holders and creditors, and any upward revaluation of assets. It is **debited** with all the losses to be absorbed — the accumulated debit balance of Profit & Loss, fictitious/intangible assets like goodwill and preliminary expenses written off, the write-down of over-valued assets, and provisions newly created. The account must **not** be left with a debit balance (the scheme is designed so that sacrifices at least equal the losses); any resulting **credit surplus is a capital profit** and is transferred to a **Capital Reserve**, which is not available for the distribution of dividend.

**Contingent liabilities.** A contingent liability (e.g., a pending suit, a claim under a guarantee) is not, until it crystallises, recorded in the books. If, under the scheme, such a liability is **expected to become an actual obligation**, it must be **provided for** — this is a fresh loss and is **debited** to the Capital Reduction Account, with a corresponding credit to a provision (or bank, if paid). Contingent liabilities that lapse or are cancelled (such as arrears of cumulative preference dividend that are waived) require **no entry** and are merely disclosed.

**Conclusion:** The reduction operates under Sec 66 with NCLT sanction; the Capital Reduction Account nets sacrifices against losses, the surplus going to Capital Reserve, while crystallising contingent liabilities are provided for as an additional debit to that account.

*(Full-marks tip: "surplus → Capital Reserve, not distributable" and "crystallising contingent liability → debit Capital Reduction" are the two conceptual marks the examiner isolates.)*

### Q79. Ch: Amalgamation of Companies (AS 14) — Merger vs Purchase (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Under AS 14, an amalgamation is classified either as an "amalgamation in the nature of **merger**" or an "amalgamation in the nature of **purchase**." State the **five conditions** for the nature of merger and bring out the key differences between the **pooling of interests method** and the **purchase method**.

**Answer:** (theory)
**Conditions for amalgamation in the nature of merger (AS 14, para 3(e)).** All five must be satisfied:
1. **All** the assets and liabilities of the transferor company become, after amalgamation, the assets and liabilities of the transferee company.
2. Shareholders holding **not less than 90%** of the face value of the equity shares of the transferor company (other than shares already held by the transferee or its subsidiaries or their nominees) become equity shareholders of the transferee company.
3. The consideration to the equity shareholders who agree to become shareholders of the transferee company is discharged by the transferee **wholly by the issue of equity shares**, except that cash may be paid in respect of fractional shares.
4. The **business** of the transferor is intended to be carried on by the transferee after the amalgamation.
5. **No adjustment** is intended to the book values of the assets and liabilities of the transferor, except to ensure **uniformity of accounting policies**.

Any amalgamation that does not satisfy *all* the above is an amalgamation in the nature of **purchase**.

| Basis | Pooling of Interests (merger) | Purchase method |
|---|---|---|
| Recording of assets/liabilities | At existing **book values** | At **agreed values** (may differ from book) |
| Reserves of transferor | Carried forward and **aggregated** (identity preserved) | Only **statutory reserves** preserved (via Amalgamation Adjustment Reserve); others not carried |
| Difference on consideration | Adjusted against **reserves** | Recorded as **Goodwill** (if PC > net assets) or **Capital Reserve** (if PC < net assets) |
| Goodwill | Does not arise | Amortised over a period **not exceeding 5 years** |

**Conclusion:** Where all five conditions are met, the pooling of interests method applies and continuity of book values and reserves is preserved; otherwise the purchase method applies, recognising assets at agreed values and goodwill/capital reserve.

*(Full-marks tip: the "90% of face value of equity shares, excluding shares already held by the transferee" wording is precise — dropping "face value" or the exclusion clause is a common deduction.)*

Shares: (20,000 ÷ 2) × 3 = 30,000 equity shares of S Ltd issued at ₹12 = **₹3,60,000** (capital ₹3,00,000 + securities premium ₹60,000).

1,000 × ₹100 × 110% = **₹1,10,000** (in 9% preference shares of S Ltd).

**WN-3 — Total purchase consideration (net payments method).**
₹3,60,000 + ₹1,00,000 + ₹1,10,000 = **₹5,70,000**.

| Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Equity shares — 30,000 × ₹12 | 3,60,000 |

### Q82. Ch: Amalgamation of Companies (AS 14) — Goodwill and statutory reserves (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Under AS 14, explain the treatment of (i) **goodwill** arising on an amalgamation, and (ii) **statutory reserves** of the transferor company where the amalgamation is in the nature of **purchase**.

**Answer:** (theory)
**(i) Goodwill on amalgamation.** Goodwill arises under the *purchase method* when the purchase consideration **exceeds** the net assets taken over. Because goodwill acquired on amalgamation represents a payment made in anticipation of future income, AS 14 (para 19–20) requires that it be **amortised on a systematic basis over its useful life, ordinarily not exceeding five years**, unless a longer period can be justified. This is a specific over-ride of the general position that purchased goodwill need not be amortised.

**(ii) Statutory reserves in a purchase-method amalgamation.** Ordinarily, in the purchase method the reserves of the transferor (other than statutory reserves) are **not** carried forward. However, certain reserves — e.g. the **Development Allowance Reserve, Investment Allowance Reserve or Export Profit Reserve** — must be maintained for a statutory period to retain a tax/legal benefit. To keep the identity of such reserves alive, AS 14 (para 18) requires that they be **recorded in the transferee's books** by:
- crediting the **statutory reserve**, and
- debiting a corresponding **"Amalgamation Adjustment Reserve"** (shown separately on the *assets* side / as a negative reserve under Reserves & Surplus).

When the statutory requirement lapses, **both** accounts are reversed and eliminated.

**Conclusion:** Goodwill on amalgamation is amortised over a period not exceeding five years, while statutory reserves are preserved even under the purchase method through the Amalgamation Adjustment Reserve, which is written back once the legal obligation ends.

*(Full-marks tip: naming the "**Amalgamation Adjustment Reserve**" and the 5-year amortisation limit are the two scoring points; do not confuse this with the pooling method, where *all* reserves are carried forward.)*

### Q87. Ch: Branch Accounts — Treatment of In-Transit Items & Stock Reserve on Consolidation (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** When a Head Office (HO) prepares a consolidated Balance Sheet incorporating an **independent branch**, explain the accounting treatment of (i) goods-in-transit, (ii) cash-in-transit, and (iii) the rationale and computation of the **Stock Reserve**.

**Answer:**
**(i) Goods-in-transit.** Goods dispatched by HO but not yet received by the branch at the year-end cause the Branch Current Account (in HO books) and HO Current Account (in branch books) to differ. On consolidation, the goods-in-transit are shown as a separate asset ("Goods-in-transit") on the assets side of the consolidated Balance Sheet at their **cost to the group** (i.e., after removing any inter-branch loading), and the two current accounts are reconciled by treating the transit goods as received. This ensures the item is neither omitted nor double-counted.

**(ii) Cash-in-transit.** Similarly, cash/cheques remitted by the branch (or by HO) but not yet received are shown as a separate current asset "Cash-in-transit" in the consolidated Balance Sheet, and the current accounts are reconciled by treating the remittance as received by the payee. After incorporating both transit items, the Branch Current Account and HO Current Account become equal and **contra out** on consolidation, so neither appears in the final Balance Sheet.

**(iii) Stock Reserve.** Where HO invoices goods to the branch at a price above cost (cost plus a mark-up), any such goods still lying **unsold in the branch's closing stock (and in goods-in-transit)** carry an element of **unrealised inter-branch profit**, because from the group's viewpoint no sale to an outsider has occurred. To eliminate this, a **Stock Reserve** is created and deducted from the value of branch stock in the consolidated Balance Sheet. It is computed as: *Stock Reserve = Invoice value of HO-supplied unsold stock × (mark-up ÷ (100 + mark-up))*. Only the **increase** in the reserve over the opening balance is charged to the consolidated Profit & Loss Account.

**Conclusion:** In-transit items are separately disclosed to make the current accounts agree and eliminate on consolidation, while the Stock Reserve removes unrealised profit so that group stock and group profit are stated at true cost to the enterprise.

*(Full-marks tip: State clearly that transit items are shown at cost to the group and that only the increase in stock reserve hits P&L. Merely saying "adjust the difference" without explaining the separate-asset disclosure loses marks.)*

### Q89. Ch: Departmental Accounts — Bases of Apportionment of Common Expenses (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** When preparing departmental accounts, common (joint) expenses must be apportioned among departments on an equitable basis. State the appropriate basis of apportionment for any **six** common expenses, and explain the treatment of **inter-departmental transfers**.

**Answer:** Expenses that cannot be directly identified with a particular department are apportioned on a basis that reflects the benefit derived by, or the cause traceable to, each department. The commonly accepted bases are:

| Common Expense | Basis of Apportionment |
|---|---|
| Rent, rates, repairs & depreciation of building | Floor area / space occupied by each department |
| Lighting and heating | Number of light points or floor area / metered consumption |
| Selling expenses, discount, bad debts, carriage outward | Sales (turnover) of each department |
| Insurance and depreciation of stock/plant | Value of stock / value of assets in each department |
| Works manager's / supervisory salary | Time devoted to, or wages of, each department |
| Carriage inward, discount received, purchase-office expenses | Purchases of each department |
| Labour welfare, canteen, PF, ESI, workmen compensation | Number of employees / wages of each department |
| Power / fuel | Horse-power × machine hours worked in each department |

**Treatment of inter-departmental transfers.** Goods (or services) supplied by one department to another are recorded at the agreed transfer price — cost, or cost plus a mark-up. In the columnar Departmental Trading Account, the transfer is credited to the *supplying* department (like a sale) and debited to the *receiving* department (like a purchase), so that each department's true trading result is measured. Where transfers are made at a price **above cost**, any such goods remaining **unsold at the year-end** carry unrealised profit, which must be eliminated by creating a **Stock Reserve** (charged to the combined Profit & Loss Account), so that inventory and overall profit are stated at cost to the firm.

**Conclusion:** Fair apportionment on a cause-and-benefit basis and elimination of unrealised profit on inter-departmental transfers together ensure that each department's profitability and the firm's overall profit are correctly reported.

*(Full-marks tip: Give the basis paired to each expense — a bare list of expenses without the matching basis earns half marks. Always mention that transfers above cost require a stock reserve.)*

|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Equity Share Capital (₹10 each) | 10,00,000 | 5,00,000 | 3,00,000 |
| Reserves on 1.4.2024 (acquisition) | — | 1,00,000 | 60,000 |
| Reserves on 31.3.2026 | 6,00,000 | 2,60,000 | 1,40,000 |
| Cost of investment: H in S | 5,20,000 | — | — |
| Cost of investment: S in SS | — | 2,40,000 | — |

Compute for the consolidated Balance Sheet as on 31.3.2026: (a) Goodwill / Capital Reserve, (b) Minority Interest, and (c) Consolidated Reserves.

**Solution:** In a chain holding, the sub-subsidiary (SS) is consolidated first into S, then S is consolidated into H.

**WN-1: Analysis of SS Ltd's reserves (S's subsidiary).**
- Post-acquisition reserves of SS = 1,40,000 − 60,000 = **₹80,000**.
- S's share (60%) of post-acquisition reserves = 80,000 × 60% = **₹48,000** (revenue profit, added to S).

**WN-2: Goodwill on S's acquisition of SS.**
- Net assets of SS at acquisition = 3,00,000 + 60,000 = 3,60,000.
- S's share (60%) = 3,60,000 × 60% = 2,16,000; Cost = 2,40,000.
- Goodwill = 2,40,000 − 2,16,000 = **₹24,000**.

**WN-3: Goodwill on H's acquisition of S.**
- Net assets of S at acquisition = 5,00,000 + 1,00,000 = 6,00,000.
- H's share (80%) = 6,00,000 × 80% = 4,80,000; Cost = 5,20,000.
- Goodwill = 5,20,000 − 4,80,000 = **₹40,000**.
- **Total Goodwill = 40,000 + 24,000 = ₹64,000.**

**WN-4: Minority Interest.**
- *In SS Ltd (40% direct):* Net assets at 31.3.2026 = 3,00,000 + 1,40,000 = 4,40,000; MI = 4,40,000 × 40% = **₹1,76,000**.
- *In S Ltd (20%):* S's consolidated net assets = Capital 5,00,000 + own reserves 2,60,000 + share of SS post-acq reserves 48,000 (WN-1) = 8,08,000; MI = 8,08,000 × 20% = **₹1,61,600**.
- **Total Minority Interest = 1,76,000 + 1,61,600 = ₹3,37,600.**

**WN-5: Consolidated Reserves.**
- Post-acquisition reserves available at S level = S's own post-acq reserves (2,60,000 − 1,00,000 = 1,60,000) + share of SS post-acq (48,000) = 2,08,000.
- H's share (80%) = 2,08,000 × 80% = 1,66,400.
- Consolidated Reserves = H's own reserves 6,00,000 + 1,66,400 = **₹7,66,400.**

**Summary for Consolidated Balance Sheet**

| Item | ₹ |
|---|---:|
| Goodwill | 64,000 |
| Minority Interest | 3,37,600 |
| Consolidated Reserves | 7,66,400 |

**Answer:** Goodwill **₹64,000**; Minority Interest **₹3,37,600**; Consolidated Reserves **₹7,66,400**.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner specifically checks that the minority of S also gets its share of SS's post-acquisition profit (the ₹48,000 flows into S before applying the 20% minority). Treating H's effective interest as a flat 48% and ignoring the two-level minority is the classic error that loses most of the marks.)*

### Q91. Ch: Consolidated Financial Statements — Investment in Associate (AS 23, Equity Method) (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** P Ltd acquired **30%** of the equity of A Ltd on **1st April, 2025** for ₹8,00,000, thereby acquiring significant influence. On that date A Ltd's equity comprised Share Capital ₹15,00,000 and Reserves ₹5,00,000. During the year ended 31.3.2026, A Ltd earned a profit after tax of ₹6,00,000 and paid a dividend of ₹2,00,000. During the year, A Ltd sold goods to P Ltd, and ₹50,000 of unrealised profit remains in P Ltd's closing stock in respect of these goods. Applying **AS 23**, compute (a) the goodwill included in the cost of investment, (b) the carrying amount of the investment in the Consolidated Balance Sheet as on 31.3.2026, and (c) the amount taken to the Consolidated Statement of Profit & Loss.

**Solution:** Under **AS 23 "Accounting for Investments in Associates in Consolidated Financial Statements"**, the investment is initially recorded at cost and thereafter adjusted for the investor's share of post-acquisition profits/losses (equity method).

**WN-1: Goodwill within the cost of investment.**
- Net assets of A Ltd at acquisition = 15,00,000 + 5,00,000 = 20,00,000.
- P's share (30%) = 20,00,000 × 30% = 6,00,000; Cost = 8,00,000.
- **Goodwill (included in carrying amount) = 8,00,000 − 6,00,000 = ₹2,00,000.**

**WN-2: Share of post-acquisition profit and dividend.**
- Share of profit for the year = 6,00,000 × 30% = **₹1,80,000** (added to carrying amount and credited to consolidated P&L).
- Dividend received = 2,00,000 × 30% = **₹60,000** (reduces carrying amount; not income under equity method).

**WN-3: Elimination of unrealised profit (AS 23).** Unrealised profits on transactions between P and A are eliminated to the extent of P's interest: 50,000 × 30% = **₹15,000** — deducted from the share of profit and from the carrying amount.

**Statement of Carrying Amount of Investment in Associate as on 31.3.2026**

| Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---:|
| Cost of investment (incl. goodwill ₹2,00,000) | 8,00,000 |
| Add: Share of post-acquisition profit (WN-2) | 1,80,000 |
| Less: Dividend received (WN-2) | (60,000) |
| Less: Unrealised profit eliminated (WN-3) | (15,000) |
| **Carrying amount of investment** | **9,05,000** |

**Amount taken to Consolidated Statement of Profit & Loss** = Share of profit 1,80,000 − Unrealised profit 15,000 = **₹1,65,000**.

**Answer:** Goodwill in cost **₹2,00,000**; carrying amount of investment in associate **₹9,05,000**; share of profit recognised in consolidated P&L **₹1,65,000**.

*(Full-marks tip: Two AS-23 judgements earn the marks — dividend from an associate is NOT income (it reduces the carrying amount), and unrealised profit is eliminated only to the extent of the investor's 30% interest. Treating the ₹60,000 dividend as income is the standard mistake.)*

### Q92. Ch: Consolidated Financial Statements — Bonus Shares Issued by Subsidiary (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** H Ltd acquired **75%** of the 80,000 equity shares of ₹10 each of S Ltd on **1st April, 2025** for ₹9,60,000. On that date S Ltd's equity comprised Share Capital ₹8,00,000 and General Reserve ₹4,00,000. During the year, S Ltd issued **bonus shares in the ratio of 1 : 4** (i.e., ₹2,00,000). Show the effect on **Goodwill/Cost of Control, Minority Interest and Consolidated Revenue Reserves** in each of the following independent situations: (a) bonus issued out of **pre-acquisition** profits, and (b) bonus issued out of **post-acquisition** profits.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Goodwill before the bonus issue.**
- Net assets of S at acquisition = 8,00,000 + 4,00,000 = 12,00,000.
- H's share (75%) = 9,00,000; Cost = 9,60,000.
- **Goodwill = 9,60,000 − 9,00,000 = ₹60,000.**
- Minority Interest (25%) = 12,00,000 × 25% = **₹3,00,000**.

**Bonus amount** = 1/4 × 80,000 shares × ₹10 = 20,000 shares × ₹10 = **₹2,00,000**.

**Case (a): Bonus out of pre-acquisition profits.**
The bonus merely transfers ₹2,00,000 from pre-acquisition reserves to share capital; **total net assets (₹12,00,000) are unchanged** and both amounts are pre-acquisition. Therefore:

| Item | Before | After (Case a) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Capital | 8,00,000 | 10,00,000 |
| Pre-acq reserve | 4,00,000 | 2,00,000 |
| Net assets | 12,00,000 | 12,00,000 |
| Goodwill (Cost − 75% NA) | 60,000 | **60,000 (no change)** |
| Minority Interest (25%) | 3,00,000 | **3,00,000 (no change)** |

**No effect** on goodwill, minority interest or consolidated reserves.

**Case (b): Bonus out of post-acquisition profits.**
Here ₹2,00,000 of **revenue (post-acquisition) profit is capitalised** into share capital. From the group's viewpoint the paid-up capital in which H holds a pre-acquisition-type interest rises, so H's share of the capitalised amount (75% × 2,00,000 = **₹1,50,000**) is transferred out of consolidated revenue reserves and into cost of control, **increasing Goodwill by ₹1,50,000**:

| Item | Effect (Case b) |
|---|---|
| Goodwill | 60,000 + 1,50,000 = **₹2,10,000** |
| Consolidated Revenue Reserves | Reduced by **₹1,50,000** (H's 75% share of capitalised profit) |
| Minority Interest | **Unchanged at ₹3,00,000** (net assets are the same) |

**Answer:** If the bonus is from pre-acquisition profits, there is no effect. If from post-acquisition profits, **goodwill rises by ₹1,50,000 to ₹2,10,000** and consolidated revenue reserves fall by ₹1,50,000, while minority interest stays at ₹3,00,000 in both cases.

*(Full-marks tip: The distinction rewarded is that a bonus from pre-acquisition profits is neutral, whereas a bonus from post-acquisition profits converts revenue profit into capital profit — inflating goodwill and depleting distributable consolidated reserves by the holding company's share. Minority interest never changes because net assets are unaffected.)*

### Q93. Ch: Consolidated Financial Statements — Definition of Control & Exclusion of a Subsidiary (AS 21) (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** With reference to **AS 21 "Consolidated Financial Statements"**, explain the meaning of "control" and state the circumstances in which a subsidiary is **excluded** from consolidation, along with the accounting treatment of such an excluded subsidiary.

**Answer:**
**Meaning of control (AS 21).** A **subsidiary** is an enterprise that is controlled by another enterprise (the parent). **Control** exists where the parent has either:
(a) the ownership, directly or indirectly through subsidiary(ies), of **more than one-half of the voting power** of an enterprise; or
(b) **control of the composition of the Board of Directors** (in the case of a company) or of the corresponding governing body, so as to obtain economic benefits from its activities.
Where control exists, the parent must consolidate the subsidiary's financial statements on a line-by-line basis.

**Circumstances of exclusion.** A subsidiary is **excluded** from consolidation in the following two situations:
(i) where **control is intended to be temporary** because the subsidiary is acquired and held exclusively with a view to its **subsequent disposal in the near future**; and
(ii) where the subsidiary operates under **severe long-term restrictions** which significantly impair its ability to transfer funds to the parent.

**Treatment of an excluded subsidiary.** An investment in a subsidiary that is excluded from consolidation on the above grounds is accounted for in accordance with **AS 13 "Accounting for Investments"** (i.e., as a current or long-term investment, as appropriate). The reasons for not consolidating must be disclosed in the consolidated financial statements.

**Conclusion:** Consolidation is mandatory wherever control (as defined) subsists, except for a subsidiary held temporarily for disposal or subject to severe long-term restrictions, which is instead carried as an investment under AS 13.

*(Full-marks tip: State BOTH limbs of control (voting power AND board composition) and BOTH exclusion grounds, and specifically name AS 13 for the excluded subsidiary. Omitting the "intended to be temporary" wording is a frequent deduction.)*

### Q96. Ch: Accounting for LLP — Distinction between an LLP and a Traditional Partnership Firm (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between a **Limited Liability Partnership (LLP)** governed by the LLP Act, 2008 and a **traditional partnership firm** governed by the Indian Partnership Act, 1932, on any six points.

**Answer:**

| Basis | LLP (LLP Act, 2008) | Partnership Firm (Partnership Act, 1932) |
|---|---|---|
| Legal status | A **separate legal entity** distinct from its partners; a body corporate. | **Not a separate legal entity**; the firm and partners are the same in law. |
| Liability of partners | **Limited** to the agreed contribution; partners are not liable for the wrongful acts of other partners. | **Unlimited**, joint and several; each partner is liable for the acts of the others. |
| Perpetual succession | Enjoys **perpetual succession**; its existence is unaffected by change/death of partners. | **No perpetual succession**; may dissolve on death, insolvency or retirement of a partner. |
| Registration | **Compulsory** registration with the Registrar of Companies (ROC). | Registration is **optional** (though an unregistered firm has procedural disabilities). |
| Number of partners | Minimum **two**, with **no maximum** limit. | Minimum two; maximum **50** (Companies Rules). |
| Maintenance of accounts / audit | Must maintain books and file annual statements; **audit mandatory** if turnover > ₹40 lakh or contribution > ₹25 lakh. | No statutory requirement to maintain prescribed books or to be audited. |
| Ownership of property | Property is owned by the **LLP in its own name**. | Property is held jointly by the **partners**. |

**Conclusion:** An LLP combines the operational flexibility of a partnership with the **limited liability and separate corporate personality** of a company, and is therefore a distinct legal form from a traditional partnership firm.

*(Full-marks tip: Present the distinction in tabular form with both columns filled — the examiner awards marks per contrasted point. The two highest-value points are "separate legal entity/perpetual succession" and "limited liability".)*

- Grand total = 2,70,000 + 76,000 + 33,000 + 21,000 = **₹4,00,000**, which equals the total trade payables (90,000 + 2,90,000 + 4,000 + 16,000).

**Answer:** Total Trade Payables disclosed in the ageing schedule = **₹4,00,000**, split across the four ageing buckets and four categories as above.

*(Full-marks tip: Schedule III mandates the four-row split — MSME, Others, Disputed MSME, Disputed Others — against the four ageing buckets measured from the due date (not the invoice date). A separate "Unbilled dues / Not due" line is added where applicable. Merging MSME with Others, or measuring from the transaction date, loses the disclosure marks.)*

### Q99. Ch: Company Financial Statements (Schedule III) — Additional Regulatory Disclosures (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** The Ministry of Corporate Affairs amended **Schedule III** (effective 1st April, 2021) to introduce several **additional regulatory disclosures** in the notes to the financial statements. State any **eight** such additional disclosures now required of a company.

**Answer:** The amended Schedule III (Division I) requires, among others, the following additional disclosures in the notes to accounts:

1. **Title deeds of immovable property not held in the name of the company** — a schedule giving the description, gross carrying value, and the name of the holder, with reasons.
2. **Ageing schedule of Trade Receivables and Trade Payables** — split into prescribed periods (less than 6 months / 1 year, 1–2 years, 2–3 years, more than 3 years) and by category (including disputed dues and MSME/others).
3. **Ageing schedule of Capital Work-in-Progress and Intangible Assets under Development**, and disclosure of projects **overdue or exceeding their cost** compared to the original plan.
4. **Loans or advances granted to promoters, directors, KMPs and related parties** (repayable on demand or without specified terms) — amount and percentage to total loans.
5. **Details of Benami property** held and any proceedings initiated under the Benami Transactions (Prohibition) Act, 1988.
6. **Reconciliation and reasons for material discrepancies** where quarterly returns/statements of current assets filed with banks (against sanctioned working-capital limits) differ from the books.
7. Whether the company has been declared a **wilful defaulter** by any bank/financial institution.
8. **Relationship with companies struck off** under section 248 of the Companies Act, 2013 (or section 560 of the 1956 Act).
9. **Registration/satisfaction of charges** with the ROC that are pending beyond the statutory period.
10. Compliance with the **number of layers** of companies prescribed under section 2(87).
11. **Financial ratios** (e.g., current ratio, debt-equity, debt-service coverage, return on equity, inventory/receivables/payables turnover, net profit ratio, return on capital employed) with explanations for a variance of more than 25%.
12. Disclosures relating to **utilisation of borrowed funds/share premium** (funds advanced or invested in "intermediaries" and "ultimate beneficiaries"), **undisclosed income** surrendered under the Income-tax Act, and dealings in **Crypto currency / Virtual digital assets**.

**Conclusion:** These disclosures substantially enhance transparency on asset ownership, related-party funding, regulatory compliance and financial health, and any **eight** of the above satisfy the requirement.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner looks for genuinely *new* items introduced by the 2021 amendment — title-deed schedule, promoter loans, wilful defaulter, struck-off companies, ratios with 25% variance, benami and crypto. Listing pre-existing Schedule III heads (like share capital notes) does not count.)*

### Q100. Ch: Company Financial Statements (Schedule III) — Classification of Items (Marks: 4) [Application]
**Question:** State the **major head** and **sub-head** under which each of the following items will be shown in the Balance Sheet of a company prepared as per **Schedule III, Division I**:
(a) Calls-in-arrears; (b) Securities Premium; (c) Provision for warranty (expected to be settled within 12 months); (d) Current maturities of long-term debt; (e) Unclaimed/unpaid dividend; (f) Loose tools; (g) Public deposits repayable after 3 years; (h) Capital advances.

**Answer:**

| Item | Major Head | Sub-head / Line item |
|---|---|---|
| (a) Calls-in-arrears | Shareholders' Funds | **Share Capital** — deducted from "Subscribed but not fully paid" |
| (b) Securities Premium | Shareholders' Funds | **Reserves and Surplus** |
| (c) Provision for warranty (< 12 months) | Current Liabilities | **Short-term Provisions** |
| (d) Current maturities of long-term debt | Current Liabilities | **Other Current Liabilities** |
| (e) Unclaimed / unpaid dividend | Current Liabilities | **Other Current Liabilities** |
| (f) Loose tools | Current Assets | **Inventories** |
| (g) Public deposits repayable after 3 years | Non-current Liabilities | **Long-term Borrowings** |
| (h) Capital advances | Non-current Assets | **Long-term Loans and Advances** |

**Note:** The **current maturities of long-term debt** are specifically classified under "Other Current Liabilities" and **not** under "Short-term Borrowings"; capital advances are treated as non-current (they will yield a fixed asset, not realise in cash within 12 months).

**Conclusion:** Correct classification turns on the operating-cycle/12-month test of Schedule III — items expected to be settled or realised within 12 months are current, the rest non-current.

*(Full-marks tip: The two items most often mis-classified are "current maturities of long-term debt" — which go to *Other Current Liabilities*, never Short-term Borrowings — and "capital advances", which are *Long-term Loans & Advances*, not part of PPE. Loose tools sit under Inventories, not PPE.)*
