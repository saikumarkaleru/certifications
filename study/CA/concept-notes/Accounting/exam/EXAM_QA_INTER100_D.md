### Q1. Ch: AS 2 Valuation of Inventories — Inventory of a Service Provider (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** M/s Insight Advisory LLP, a management consultancy firm, is engaged on a large assignment that is only partly completed as on 31st March, 2026. The following costs have been incurred up to the year-end in respect of the unbilled (work-in-progress) portion of the assignment:

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Salaries of consultants directly engaged on the assignment | 8,00,000 |
| Salary of the supervisory partner directly attributable to the assignment | 1,50,000 |
| Other overheads directly attributable to rendering the service | 1,00,000 |
| General administration overheads (firm-wide) | 2,00,000 |
| Selling and business-development costs | 50,000 |
| Notional profit margin the firm expects to earn | 3,00,000 |

Determine the value at which the work-in-progress (inventory of services) should be carried as on 31st March, 2026 in accordance with AS 2.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Principle for measuring a service provider's inventory (AS 2, para 19)**
In the case of a service provider, inventories are measured at the **costs of production**, which consist primarily of the labour and other costs of **personnel directly engaged in providing the service, including supervisory personnel, and attributable overheads.** Labour and other costs relating to sales and general administrative personnel are **not included** but are recognised as expenses in the period in which they are incurred. AS 2 also **prohibits including any profit margin or non-attributable overheads** in the cost of a service provider's inventory.

**WN-2: Classification of the costs incurred**

| Particulars | Includible? | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| Salaries of consultants directly engaged | Yes — personnel directly rendering service | 8,00,000 |
| Salary of supervisory partner (directly attributable) | Yes — supervisory personnel are specifically included | 1,50,000 |
| Other directly attributable overheads | Yes — attributable overhead | 1,00,000 |
| General administration overheads | No — general/administrative personnel excluded | — |
| Selling and business-development costs | No — selling costs excluded | — |
| Notional profit margin | No — profit margin cannot be included | — |

**Statement Showing Value of Inventory of Services (Work-in-Progress) as on 31.03.2026**

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Salaries of consultants directly engaged | 8,00,000 |
| Salary of supervisory partner (attributable) | 1,50,000 |
| Other attributable overheads | 1,00,000 |
| **Cost of inventory of services (WIP)** | **10,50,000** |

**Answer:** The work-in-progress (inventory of services) should be carried at **₹10,50,000** as on 31st March, 2026. The general administration cost (₹2,00,000), selling cost (₹50,000) and notional profit (₹3,00,000) are charged to the Statement of Profit and Loss / not recognised.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the explicit statement that a service provider's inventory includes supervisory personnel but excludes selling, general-admin and profit margin. The most common deduction is wrongly loading the profit margin or general overheads into WIP.)*

### Q2. Ch: AS 2 Valuation of Inventories — Allocation of Fixed Production Overheads on Normal Capacity (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** Precision Forgings Ltd. manufactures a single product. Its fixed production overheads for the year are ₹6,00,000 and its normal capacity is 20,000 units per annum. Variable production overhead is ₹10 per unit and direct material plus direct labour together cost ₹90 per unit. Compute the amount of fixed overhead to be absorbed into the cost of inventory and the per-unit valuation under each of the following independent situations, and state the accounting treatment of any unabsorbed amount:
(a) Actual production during the year is 15,000 units.
(b) Actual production during the year is 24,000 units.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Governing rule (AS 2, para 10)**
The allocation of **fixed production overheads** to the costs of conversion is based on the **normal capacity** of the production facilities. The amount of fixed overhead allocated to each unit is **not increased as a consequence of low production or idle plant**; unallocated overheads are recognised as an **expense in the period** in which they are incurred. In periods of **abnormally high production**, the amount of fixed overhead allocated to each unit is **decreased** (i.e. actual level of production is used) so that inventories are not measured above cost.

Fixed overhead rate on normal capacity = ₹6,00,000 ÷ 20,000 units = **₹30 per unit**.

**Situation (a): Actual production 15,000 units (below normal)**

| Particulars | Amount |
|---|---|
| Fixed OH absorbed into inventory (15,000 units × ₹30) | ₹4,50,000 |
| Fixed OH treated as period expense (₹6,00,000 − ₹4,50,000) | ₹1,50,000 |

Per-unit cost of production = Material & labour ₹90 + Variable OH ₹10 + Fixed OH ₹30 = **₹130 per unit.** The unallocated ₹1,50,000 is charged directly to the Statement of Profit and Loss and does **not** inflate the per-unit rate.

**Situation (b): Actual production 24,000 units (above normal)**

Fixed OH per unit is reduced to the actual level: ₹6,00,000 ÷ 24,000 units = **₹25 per unit.**

Per-unit cost of production = ₹90 + ₹10 + ₹25 = **₹125 per unit.** The entire ₹6,00,000 fixed overhead is absorbed (24,000 × ₹25) and no amount remains unallocated; inventory is thereby not carried above cost.

**Answer:** (a) Fixed OH absorbed ₹4,50,000; ₹1,50,000 expensed; cost/unit **₹130**. (b) Full ₹6,00,000 absorbed at reduced rate ₹25/unit; cost/unit **₹125**.

*(Full-marks tip: State the "not increased on low production / decreased on high production" rule verbatim. Losing marks usually happens by spreading full ₹6,00,000 over 15,000 units — that wrongly capitalises idle-capacity cost into inventory.)*

### Q3. Ch: AS 2 Valuation of Inventories — Item-by-Item Cost vs NRV and Materials Write-down (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** From the following information relating to the closing inventory of Vardhan Manufacturers as on 31st March, 2026, determine the value at which each category of inventory should be stated and the total value of inventory as per AS 2. Raw Material-A is used exclusively in producing Finished Good-X and Raw Material-B is used exclusively in producing Finished Good-Y.

| Item | Quantity (units) | Cost per unit (₹) | Net Realisable Value / Replacement cost per unit (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Finished Good-X | 500 | 200 | NRV 180 |
| Finished Good-Y | 300 | 150 | NRV 170 |
| Raw Material-A (for X) | 400 | 120 | Replacement 110 |
| Raw Material-B (for Y) | 250 | 100 | Replacement 90 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Rules applied (AS 2, paras 5, 24)**
Inventories are valued at the **lower of cost and net realisable value**, and this comparison is ordinarily made on an **item-by-item basis**. Materials held for use in production are **not written down below cost if the finished products in which they will be incorporated are expected to be sold at or above cost.** However, where a decline in the price of materials indicates that the cost of the finished products will exceed NRV, the materials are written down to NRV, and the **replacement cost of the materials may be the best available measure of their NRV.**

**WN-2: Finished goods valuation (lower of cost and NRV)**
- Finished Good-X: lower of ₹200 and ₹180 = **₹180** (sold at a loss).
- Finished Good-Y: lower of ₹150 and ₹170 = **₹150** (sold at a profit).

**WN-3: Raw materials valuation**
- Raw Material-A: since Finished Good-X will be sold **below cost**, Material-A is written down; replacement cost ₹110 is taken as its NRV. Value = **₹110** per unit.
- Raw Material-B: since Finished Good-Y will be sold **at a profit** (above cost), Material-B is **not written down** despite replacement cost falling; it is carried at cost ₹100 per unit.

**Statement Showing Value of Closing Inventory as on 31.03.2026**

| Item | Quantity | Rate (₹) | Value (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Finished Good-X | 500 | 180 | 90,000 |
| Finished Good-Y | 300 | 150 | 45,000 |
| Raw Material-A | 400 | 110 | 44,000 |
| Raw Material-B | 250 | 100 | 25,000 |
| **Total value of inventory** | | | **2,04,000** |

**Answer:** Closing inventory is valued at **₹2,04,000**.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner looks for the logic that a raw material is written down only when its finished good is loss-making. Marking loss: writing Material-B down to ₹90 merely because replacement cost fell, ignoring that Good-Y is profitable.)*

### Q4. Ch: AS 2 Valuation of Inventories — Costs Excluded from Cost of Inventories (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State, with reasons, the items that are **excluded** from the cost of inventories under AS 2 and the treatment of storage costs and interest/borrowing costs in valuing inventory.

**Answer:**
AS 2 "Valuation of Inventories" provides that the cost of inventories comprises the costs of purchase, costs of conversion and other costs incurred in bringing the inventories to their **present location and condition**. Certain costs, however, are **excluded** from the cost of inventories and are recognised as **expenses in the period in which they are incurred**:

| Excluded cost | Reason for exclusion |
|---|---|
| Abnormal amounts of wasted materials, labour or other production costs | They are not incurred in bringing inventory to its present location and condition |
| **Storage costs**, unless those costs are **necessary in the production process before a further production stage** | Ordinary storage of finished goods adds no further value; only storage that is a necessary part of production (e.g. maturing/ageing) is includible |
| Administrative overheads that do not contribute to bringing inventories to their present location and condition | Not attributable to production |
| Selling and distribution costs | Incurred after the inventory is ready for sale |

**Interest and borrowing costs:** As a general rule, interest and other borrowing costs are **not** included in the cost of inventories; however, in accordance with AS 16, in limited circumstances borrowing costs may be included where the inventory is a **qualifying asset** (one that necessarily takes a substantial period of time to get ready for its intended sale, e.g. inventories that require a substantial period to mature such as wine or timber).

**Conclusion:** Abnormal wastage, non-productive storage costs, administrative overheads not contributing to production, and selling and distribution costs are excluded from inventory cost and charged to the Statement of Profit and Loss in the period incurred.

*(Full-marks tip: State the "present location and condition" test and the storage-cost exception precisely — that is the point ICAI rewards.)*

### Q5. Ch: AS 7 Construction Contracts — Contract Revenue with Variation and Incentive; Stage by Surveys (Marks: 10) [Problem]
**Question:** Sterling Infra Ltd. is executing a construction contract. The following particulars (all figures in ₹ lakh) are available as on 31st March, 2026:

| Particulars | ₹ lakh |
|---|---|
| Initial agreed contract revenue | 300 |
| Variation in contract work — approved by customer and reliably measurable | 30 |
| Incentive payment for early completion — completion of milestone makes it probable and it can be measured reliably | 15 |
| Estimated total contract costs | 260 |
| Costs incurred up to 31.03.2026 | 108 |
| Progress billings raised on customer | 120 |
| Amounts actually collected from customer | 100 |

The stage of completion is determined on the basis of **surveys of work performed**, which certify the contract to be **40% complete** as on 31st March, 2026. You are required to compute the contract revenue, contract costs and profit to be recognised for the year, and the amount due from/to the customer to be presented in the Balance Sheet.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Total contract revenue (AS 7, paras 10–16)**
Contract revenue includes the initial amount agreed, plus **variations** to the extent it is probable they will result in revenue and are capable of being reliably measured, plus **incentive payments** where the contract is sufficiently advanced that it is probable the specified standards will be met and the incentive can be reliably measured.

| Component | ₹ lakh |
|---|---|
| Initial contract revenue | 300 |
| Add: Approved variation | 30 |
| Add: Incentive payment (probable and measurable) | 15 |
| **Total contract revenue** | **345** |

**WN-2: Stage of completion**
Stage of completion is measured by **surveys of work performed** = **40%** (this method overrides the cost-to-cost proportion; costs incurred to date are used only for presentation, not to measure stage here).

**WN-3: Recognition for the year**

| Particulars | Computation | ₹ lakh |
|---|---|---|
| Contract revenue recognised | 345 × 40% | 138.00 |
| Contract costs recognised | 260 × 40% | 104.00 |
| **Profit recognised for the year** | 138 − 104 | **34.00** |

**WN-4: Gross amount due from/to customer (AS 7, para 41)**
Gross amount due from customers = Costs incurred + Recognised profits − Recognised losses − Progress billings.

| Particulars | ₹ lakh |
|---|---|
| Costs incurred to date | 108 |
| Add: Recognised profit | 34 |
| Less: Progress billings | (120) |
| **Amount due FROM customer (asset)** | **22** |

**Statement of Amounts Recognised — Year ended 31.03.2026**

| Particulars | ₹ lakh |
|---|---|
| Contract revenue | 138.00 |
| Contract costs | 104.00 |
| Contract profit | 34.00 |
| Due from customers (gross amount) | 22.00 |
| Retention (billed but uncollected 120 − 100) shown as receivable | 20.00 |

**Answer:** Recognise **Revenue ₹138 lakh, Costs ₹104 lakh and Profit ₹34 lakh** for the year; the Balance Sheet shows **₹22 lakh due from the customer** as an asset, and the unpaid billing of ₹20 lakh is a trade receivable.

*(Full-marks tip: Add the incentive/variation into contract revenue only after stating the probability + reliable-measurement test, and apply the survey % of 40% — not the cost ratio 108/260 = 41.5%. The gross-amount-due formula is a standard ICAI mark-earner.)*

### Q6. Ch: AS 7 Construction Contracts — Recognition of an Expected (Foreseeable) Loss (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** Horizon Builders Ltd. has a fixed-price construction contract with a total contract revenue of ₹200 lakh. The estimated total contract cost has risen to ₹230 lakh. The contract is **25% complete** as on 31st March, 2026 (measured on a cost-to-cost basis) and costs of ₹57.50 lakh have been incurred to date. Show the amounts of revenue, cost and loss to be recognised for the year in accordance with AS 7.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Governing rule (AS 7, paras 35–36)**
When it is probable that **total contract costs will exceed total contract revenue, the expected loss is recognised as an expense immediately**, irrespective of the stage of completion of the contract activity.

**WN-2: Foreseeable loss**

| Particulars | ₹ lakh |
|---|---|
| Total contract revenue | 200 |
| Total contract cost (estimated) | 230 |
| **Total foreseeable loss** | **30** |

**WN-3: Amounts recognised in the Statement of Profit and Loss**

| Particulars | Computation | ₹ lakh |
|---|---|---|
| Contract revenue recognised | 200 × 25% | 50.00 |
| Contract cost to be recognised (revenue + full loss) | 50 + 30 | 80.00 |
| **Loss recognised for the year** | 50 − 80 | **(30.00)** |

**WN-4: Provision for expected loss**

| Particulars | ₹ lakh |
|---|---|
| Cost to be recognised in P&L | 80.00 |
| Less: Costs actually incurred to date | 57.50 |
| **Provision for expected loss (liability)** | **22.50** |

**Answer:** Recognise **Revenue ₹50 lakh** and **Costs ₹80 lakh**, giving a **loss of ₹30 lakh** in the year; ₹22.50 lakh of that (being the cost not yet incurred) is carried as a **provision for foreseeable loss.**

*(Full-marks tip: The whole ₹30 lakh loss must be booked now, not 25% of it. Show that the balancing figure of cost (₹80 lakh) exceeds costs incurred (₹57.5 lakh), with the difference set up as a provision.)*

### Q7. Ch: AS 7 Construction Contracts — Stage of Completion and Uncertain Outcome (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the methods that may be used under AS 7 to determine the stage of completion of a construction contract, and state how contract revenue and costs are recognised when the **outcome of a contract cannot be estimated reliably.**

**Answer:**
Under AS 7 "Construction Contracts", when the outcome of a construction contract can be estimated reliably, contract revenue and costs are recognised by reference to the **stage of completion** of the contract activity at the reporting date (the **percentage-of-completion method**). AS 7 permits the stage of completion to be determined by any of the following methods, depending on the nature of the contract:

| Method | Basis |
|---|---|
| Cost-to-cost method | The proportion that contract costs incurred for work performed to date bear to the estimated total contract costs |
| Surveys of work performed | Physical certification / measurement of the work carried out |
| Completion of a physical proportion | The proportion of the physical work completed (e.g. metres of road laid) |

Progress payments and advances received from customers do **not** necessarily reflect the work performed and are therefore not, by themselves, a basis for measuring the stage of completion.

**When the outcome cannot be estimated reliably:** contract revenue is recognised **only to the extent of contract costs incurred that are probable of being recovered**, and contract costs are recognised as an **expense in the period in which they are incurred**. Consequently, **no profit is recognised**. As soon as the uncertainties that prevented reliable estimation cease to exist, revenue and costs are recognised under the normal percentage-of-completion approach.

**Conclusion:** Stage of completion may be measured by the cost, survey or physical-proportion method; where the outcome is not reliably estimable, revenue is limited to recoverable costs so that no profit results.

*(Full-marks tip: Naming all three stage-of-completion methods and the "revenue to the extent of recoverable costs / no profit" rule for uncertain outcomes is what earns full marks.)*

### Q8. Ch: AS 7 Construction Contracts — Change in Estimate and Gross Amount Due to Customer (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Meridian Constructions Ltd. gives the following particulars of a contract as on 31st March, 2026 (₹ in lakh):

| Particulars | ₹ lakh |
|---|---|
| Contract revenue agreed | 500 |
| Costs incurred to date | 390 |
| Estimated further costs to complete | 110 |
| Progress billings raised | 430 |

During the year, on account of a revised design the estimated total cost increased from ₹460 lakh to ₹500 lakh. Compute the stage of completion, the profit/loss to be recognised, and the amount due to/from the customer to be shown in the Balance Sheet. State how the change in estimate is dealt with.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Estimated total cost and total profit**

| Particulars | ₹ lakh |
|---|---|
| Costs incurred to date | 390 |
| Add: Estimated further costs | 110 |
| **Estimated total contract cost** | **500** |
| Total contract revenue | 500 |
| **Estimated total profit** | **Nil** |

**WN-2: Stage of completion (cost-to-cost)** = 390 ÷ 500 = **78%**.

**WN-3: Recognition — treatment of change in estimate (AS 7, para 21)**
A change in the estimate of contract revenue or costs is accounted for as a **change in accounting estimate** and is used in the determination of revenue and expenses in the **period of change and in subsequent periods** (prospective effect; no retrospective restatement). Since total estimated profit is now **nil**, the revenue and cost recognised are equal.

| Particulars | Computation | ₹ lakh |
|---|---|---|
| Contract revenue recognised | 500 × 78% | 390 |
| Contract costs recognised | 500 × 78% | 390 |
| **Profit recognised** | | **Nil** |

**WN-4: Gross amount due to/from customer**

| Particulars | ₹ lakh |
|---|---|
| Costs incurred + recognised profit (390 + 0) | 390 |
| Less: Progress billings | (430) |
| **Amount due TO customer (liability)** | **40** |

**Answer:** The contract is **78% complete**; **no profit** is recognised (estimated total profit is nil after the cost revision, treated prospectively as a change in estimate); and **₹40 lakh is shown as amount due to the customer (a liability).**

*(Full-marks tip: Treat the cost increase prospectively — never restate prior periods — and note that when billings exceed costs plus recognised profits the balance is a liability "due to customer," not a receivable.)*

### Q9. Ch: AS 9 Revenue Recognition — Sale on Approval / Sale-or-Return Basis (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Novena Traders sent goods to customers on a **sale-on-approval** basis during March 2026 at invoice prices totalling ₹5,00,000. The firm sells goods at a uniform gross margin such that **cost is 80% of the invoice price**. It had, however, recorded the entire ₹5,00,000 as sales in its books. By 31st March, 2026 the position was:
- Goods worth ₹3,00,000 (invoice price) had been **approved/accepted** by customers;
- Goods worth ₹1,00,000 (invoice price) had been **returned/rejected**;
- Goods worth ₹1,00,000 (invoice price) were lying with customers with the **approval period not yet expired**.

Compute the correct sales figure and the adjustment to closing inventory as per AS 9.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Recognition principle (AS 9, para 6.1 and Appendix)**
In a sale on approval / sale-or-return transaction, revenue is recognised only when the goods have been **formally accepted by the buyer or the buyer has done an act adopting the transaction, or the time period for rejection has elapsed** and no rejection has been intimated. Until then, no sale is recognised and the goods remain the seller's inventory.

**WN-2: Correct revenue**

| Category | Invoice value (₹) | Recognise as sale? |
|---|---|---|
| Approved by customers | 3,00,000 | Yes |
| Returned / rejected | 1,00,000 | No — goods back with seller |
| Approval period not expired | 1,00,000 | No — sale not yet complete |
| **Sales recorded originally** | 5,00,000 | — |

Correct sales = **₹3,00,000.** Sales recorded in excess = ₹5,00,000 − ₹3,00,000 = **₹2,00,000 to be reversed.**

**WN-3: Adjustment to closing inventory (at cost = 80% of invoice)**
- Goods lying with customers, approval pending (₹1,00,000 × 80%) = **₹80,000** to be added to closing inventory.
- Returned goods (₹1,00,000 × 80% = ₹80,000) are physically back and included in inventory on receipt.

**Statement of Adjustments**

| Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Reduce recorded sales by | 2,00,000 |
| Add to closing inventory: goods on approval pending (at cost) | 80,000 |
| Add to closing inventory: goods returned (at cost) | 80,000 |

**Answer:** Recognise sales of **₹3,00,000 only**; reverse ₹2,00,000 of wrongly-booked sales, and **add ₹1,60,000 (at cost) of unsold goods back to closing inventory** (₹80,000 on approval + ₹80,000 returned).

*(Full-marks tip: Convert unsold goods to cost (80% of invoice) before adding to inventory — a frequent error is adding them back at invoice price, which double-counts the profit.)*

### Q10. Ch: AS 9 Revenue Recognition — Interest, Royalty and Dividend Income (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** Compute the amount of income to be recognised by Anchor Investments Ltd. for the year ended 31st March, 2026, from the following, stating the basis for each:

| Particulars | Details |
|---|---|
| Investment in 9% Debentures of ₹10,00,000, purchased on 1st July, 2025 | Interest at 9% p.a. |
| Royalty receivable under a licensing agreement | 20,000 units sold during the year at ₹5 per unit royalty |
| Dividend from B Ltd. declared on 15th April, 2026 | ₹60,000 |
| Dividend from C Ltd. declared on 20th March, 2026 (received 10th April, 2026) | ₹40,000 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Recognition rules (AS 9, para 13)**
Revenue arising from the use by others of enterprise resources is recognised as follows: **interest** on a **time proportion basis** taking into account the amount outstanding and the rate applicable; **royalties** on an **accrual basis** in accordance with the terms of the relevant agreement; and **dividends** from investments in shares **when the owner's right to receive payment is established** (i.e. when declared).

**WN-2: Computation of each item**

| Item | Basis | Computation | ₹ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Interest on 9% Debentures | Time proportion (1 Jul 2025 – 31 Mar 2026 = 9 months) | 10,00,000 × 9% × 9/12 | 67,500 |
| Royalty | Accrual per agreement | 20,000 units × ₹5 | 1,00,000 |
| Dividend — B Ltd. | Right established only on declaration 15.04.2026 (after year-end) | Not recognised in FY 2025-26 | Nil |
| Dividend — C Ltd. | Declared 20.03.2026 — right to receive established before year-end | Recognised though received later | 40,000 |
| **Total income for the year** | | | **2,07,500** |

**Answer:** Income to be recognised = **₹2,07,500** (Interest ₹67,500 + Royalty ₹1,00,000 + Dividend from C Ltd. ₹40,000). The B Ltd. dividend of ₹60,000, declared after the year-end, is recognised only in the next year.

*(Full-marks tip: The distinguishing point is the dividend "right to receive" test — the date of declaration governs, not the date of receipt. Recognise C Ltd. (declared before year-end) and exclude B Ltd. (declared after).)*

### Q11. Ch: AS 9 Revenue Recognition — Postponement of Recognition under Uncertainty (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the circumstances in which the recognition of revenue is **postponed** under AS 9, and distinguish between uncertainty relating to **measurement** of revenue and uncertainty relating to its **collectability**.

**Answer:**
AS 9 "Revenue Recognition" provides that revenue from sales or service transactions is recognised when the requirements as to performance are satisfied, **provided that at the time of performance it is not unreasonable to expect ultimate collection.** Where, at the time of raising a claim, it is **unreasonable to expect ultimate collection, revenue recognition is postponed.**

The two forms of uncertainty are treated differently:

| Nature of uncertainty | Treatment under AS 9 |
|---|---|
| **Uncertainty as to measurement / determination of the amount** (e.g. the consideration is not determinable within reasonable limits) | Revenue recognition is **postponed** — revenue is recognised only when it becomes reasonably determinable |
| **Uncertainty as to collectability** arising **after** the sale/service has been completed and revenue already recognised | Revenue is **still recognised**; the uncertainty about collection is provided for as a **separate expense (e.g. provision for doubtful debts)** rather than by adjusting the revenue originally recorded |

Thus, where the very ability to measure the revenue is in doubt at the time of performance, recognition itself waits; but where doubt about collection arises only later, the sale is not reversed — a charge is made against profit for the doubtful amount.

**Conclusion:** Revenue is postponed only when significant uncertainty exists about the **amount or its ultimate collection at the time of the transaction**; uncertainty about collection arising afterwards is dealt with by a provision, not by reversing recognised revenue.

*(Full-marks tip: The key contrast ICAI expects: measurement uncertainty postpones recognition, whereas later collection uncertainty is met by a provision without disturbing recognised revenue.)*

### Q12. Ch: AS 9 Revenue Recognition — Application to Various Transactions (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** State, with reasons, the point at which revenue should be recognised in each of the following independent situations as per AS 9:
(a) An electronics dealer sells an air-conditioner for ₹40,000 that requires installation, which is a significant part of the contract, completed after the year-end.
(b) An advertising agency has placed advertisements for a client during March 2026; the advertisements appear in the media in April 2026.
(c) A magazine publisher receives ₹12,000 in advance in January 2026 for a 12-month subscription commencing February 2026.
(d) A firm sells goods worth ₹5,00,000 with a "money-back if not satisfied within 30 days" guarantee; past experience shows returns of about 2%.

**Answer:**
AS 9 recognises revenue from the sale of goods when the **seller has transferred to the buyer the property in the goods (or the significant risks and rewards of ownership)** and no significant uncertainty exists as to the amount of consideration and its collectability; revenue from services is recognised as the service is performed, either on the **proportionate completion** or **completed service contract** method.

| Case | Recognition point and reason |
|---|---|
| (a) A/c requiring significant installation | Revenue is recognised **only when the installation is complete and accepted by the buyer**, since installation is a significant part of the contract. As it is completed after year-end, **no revenue is recognised in FY 2025-26**. |
| (b) Advertising agency | For **advertising commissions, revenue is recognised when the advertisement appears before the public.** As the advertisements appear in April 2026, revenue is recognised in the **next year**, not on mere placement. |

| (d) Sale with return guarantee | Revenue for the **full ₹5,00,000 is recognised at the point of sale**, since risks and rewards pass to the buyer and returns can be **reliably estimated**; a **provision of 2% (₹10,000)** is made for expected returns. |

**Conclusion:** Revenue is recognised in (c) to the extent of ₹2,000 and in (d) at ₹5,00,000 (less a ₹10,000 provision); in (a) and (b) recognition is deferred to the next year.

*(Full-marks tip: Each answer must name the trigger — installation acceptance, appearance before the public, time-proportion, and passing of risks/rewards with a returns provision. Reasoned application, not just a yes/no, earns the marks.)*

### Q17. Ch: AS 10 Property, Plant and Equipment — Subsequent Costs, Componentisation and Spare Parts (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** With reference to AS 10, explain the treatment of (i) subsequent costs (day-to-day servicing versus replacement of parts), (ii) componentisation of an item of PPE, and (iii) major spare parts and stand-by equipment.

**Answer:**
AS 10 "Property, Plant and Equipment" applies the general recognition principle — that a cost is added to the carrying amount of an item of PPE only when it is **probable that future economic benefits will flow to the enterprise and the cost can be measured reliably** — to both initial and subsequent costs.

| Aspect | Treatment under AS 10 |
|---|---|
| **(i) Day-to-day servicing** | Costs of day-to-day servicing (repairs and maintenance, primarily labour, consumables and minor parts) do **not** meet the recognition criteria and are **charged to the Statement of Profit and Loss** as incurred. |
| **(i) Replacement of parts / major inspection** | Where parts of an item are **replaced at intervals**, or a **regular major inspection/overhaul** is performed, the cost is **capitalised** if the recognition criteria are met, and the **carrying amount of the replaced part (or previous inspection) is derecognised.** |
| **(ii) Componentisation** | Each part of an item of PPE with a **cost that is significant in relation to the total cost** of the item is **depreciated separately** (e.g. the engine and airframe of an aircraft), so that different components with different useful lives are accounted for individually. |
| **(iii) Spare parts / stand-by equipment** | Items such as **spare parts, stand-by equipment and servicing equipment are recognised as PPE** when they meet the **definition of property, plant and equipment** (i.e. are expected to be used during more than one period); otherwise they are classified as **inventory** and charged to the Statement of Profit and Loss on consumption. |

**Conclusion:** Routine servicing is expensed; replacement/major-inspection costs are capitalised with derecognition of the replaced part; significant components are depreciated separately; and major spares/stand-by equipment that satisfy the PPE definition are capitalised rather than treated as inventory.

*(Full-marks tip: The 2016-revised distinction that major spares meeting the PPE definition are PPE — not inventory — is the freshest examinable point here, along with separate depreciation of significant components.)*

### Q18. Ch: AS 4 — Proposed Dividend and Events Occurring After the Balance Sheet Date (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** While finalising the accounts of Meridian Ltd. for the year ended 31st March, 2026, the following matters came to notice. Explain the treatment required under AS 4 in each case, with reasons:

| Sl. | Event | Details |
|-----|-------|---------|
| (i) | Proposed dividend | The Board, at its meeting on 20th May 2026, recommended a dividend of ₹18,00,000 for FY 2025-26. |
| (ii) | Insolvency of a debtor | A debtor owing ₹6,50,000 as on 31.03.2026 was declared insolvent on 25th April 2026; recovery estimated at 20%. |
| (iii) | Fire in godown | A fire on 12th May 2026 destroyed uninsured finished goods costing ₹9,00,000 (goods manufactured in April 2026). |
| (iv) | Litigation settled | A suit pending on 31.03.2026 for ₹4,00,000 was settled on 30th April 2026 for ₹2,80,000. |

**Answer:**
As per **AS 4 "Contingencies and Events Occurring After the Balance Sheet Date"**, events occurring after the balance sheet date are those that occur between the balance sheet date and the date on which the financial statements are approved by the Board. They are of two kinds — **adjusting events** (which provide further evidence of conditions existing at the balance sheet date) and **non-adjusting events** (which are indicative of conditions arising after the balance sheet date).

**(i) Proposed dividend:** As per the amendment to AS 4 (consequent to the Companies (Accounting Standards) Amendment Rules and the omission of the requirement in Schedule III), dividends proposed after the balance sheet date but before approval of the financial statements are **not a liability** as at the balance sheet date, because there is no present obligation on the reporting date (declaration by the shareholders is a subsequent act). Hence **no provision** is to be made for the proposed dividend of ₹18,00,000; it is only **disclosed in the notes** to accounts.

**(ii) Insolvency of a debtor:** The insolvency of a debtor after the balance sheet date is an **adjusting event**, as it confirms that the debt was doubtful of recovery on the balance sheet date itself. Provision for the irrecoverable portion i.e. ₹6,50,000 × 80% = **₹5,20,000 must be made** and the debtor written down accordingly.

**(iii) Fire in godown:** The goods destroyed were manufactured in April 2026 and hence did **not exist** on 31.03.2026. This is a **non-adjusting event** arising out of conditions after the balance sheet date. No adjustment is made; however, if material and affecting going concern/understanding, its **nature and financial effect are disclosed**.

**(iv) Litigation settled:** The suit was pending on the balance sheet date and its settlement provides further evidence of the obligation existing on that date. It is an **adjusting event**; the liability must be recognised at the settled amount of **₹2,80,000** (in place of any earlier estimate).

**Conclusion:** Only items (ii) and (iv) are adjusting events requiring adjustment; the proposed dividend (i) is merely disclosed and the fire (iii) is a non-adjusting event disclosed for its nature and effect.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards correctly stating that post-2016 proposed dividend is NOT provided for — a common deduction is students still creating a provision. Always classify each item as adjusting/non-adjusting before prescribing treatment.)*

### Q19. Ch: AS 5 — Ordinary Activities, Extraordinary Items and Prior Period Items (Marks: 4) [Application]
**Question:** State, with reasons, how the following should be presented in the Statement of Profit and Loss of Novena Ltd. for the year ended 31st March, 2026, under AS 5:
(a) Loss of ₹15,00,000 on account of an earthquake that destroyed a plant;
(b) Profit of ₹7,00,000 on sale of a long-held investment in the ordinary course;
(c) Bad debts of ₹1,20,000 relating to sales of FY 2024-25 that were wrongly omitted last year;
(d) A ₹90,000 reduction in the provision for warranty made in an earlier year, arising from a fresh estimate.

**Answer:**
**AS 5 "Net Profit or Loss for the Period, Prior Period Items and Changes in Accounting Policies"** requires that all items of income and expense recognised in a period be included in the determination of net profit or loss, splitting the net profit or loss into (a) profit or loss from **ordinary activities** and (b) **extraordinary items**, with prior period items and changes in estimates separately dealt with.

**(a)** An earthquake is clearly **distinct from ordinary activities** and not expected to recur frequently or regularly. The loss of ₹15,00,000 is an **extraordinary item**, disclosed separately on the face of the Statement of Profit and Loss (with its nature and amount) so that the impact on current profit can be perceived.

**(b)** Sale of investments, though not the primary business, is an **activity incidental to and in furtherance of ordinary activities**. Since it is ordinary in nature, the profit of ₹7,00,000 is part of profit from ordinary activities; being of a size/nature relevant to explaining performance, it is disclosed as a **separate line item within ordinary activities**, not as extraordinary.

**(c)** The bad debts of ₹1,20,000 arise from an **error/omission in the prior period's** financial statements. This is a **prior period item**, included in the current statement but **disclosed separately** so that its impact on current profit is known.

**(d)** A revision of the warranty provision on fresh estimate is a **change in accounting estimate**, not a prior period item or change in policy. The ₹90,000 write-back is recognised in the **current period's** profit from ordinary activities (prospective effect); it is disclosed if material.

**Conclusion:** (a) extraordinary; (b) ordinary with separate disclosure; (c) prior period item disclosed separately; (d) change in estimate, current-year effect.

*(Full-marks tip: Distinguish "extraordinary" (a & not common) from "separate disclosure within ordinary" (unusual size but ordinary nature) — the sale of investments trap catches students. Also never confuse a change in estimate with a prior period item.)*

**Question:** Sunrise Ltd. has a foreign operation whose classification is under debate. Answer the following:

| Item | Foreign Currency (FC) | Rate applied |
|------|----------------------|--------------|
| Fixed assets (acquired when rate ₹60) | 1,00,000 | Closing rate ₹66 |
| Inventory (at average) | 40,000 | Average rate ₹64 |
| Trade receivables | 30,000 | Closing rate ₹66 |
| Trade payables | 25,000 | Closing rate ₹66 |
| Opening net investment translated last year | — | ₹58,00,000 |
| Net FC assets (1,00,000+40,000+30,000−25,000) | 1,45,000 | — |

| Basis | Integral foreign operation | Non-integral foreign operation |
|-------|---------------------------|-------------------------------|
| Nature | Carries on business as if an extension of the reporting enterprise | Accumulates cash and other items, generates income largely on its own |
| Cash flows | Directly and immediately affect the cash flows of the reporting enterprise | Insulated from the reporting enterprise's cash flows |
| Financing | Financed mainly by the head office | Financed mainly from its own operations/local borrowings |

- Represented by: capital/opening plus current-year movements. The balancing exchange difference on retranslation of the opening net investment and the difference between average and closing rates is taken to FCTR.
- Exchange difference to FCTR (illustrative) = Closing translated net assets − (opening net investment ₹58,00,000 + current period results translated at average).

### Q21. Ch: AS 12 — Government Grant Related to a Non-Depreciable Asset (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Zephyr Ltd. received the following in FY 2025-26. Explain the accounting treatment under AS 12:
(a) A grant of freehold **land** (a non-depreciable asset) from the State, free of cost, fair value ₹40,00,000, with a condition that the company construct and operate a factory on it for at least 8 years.
(b) A cash grant of ₹25,00,000 towards a plot of non-depreciable land, with **no** conditions attached.
(c) State how the treatment differs if the grant in (a) had carried **no** conditions.

**Answer:**
**AS 12 "Accounting for Government Grants"** provides that grants related to non-depreciable assets are ordinarily credited to **capital reserve**, unless the grant is given subject to the fulfilment of certain obligations; in that case the grant is credited to income **over the same period over which the cost of meeting the obligation is charged** to income.

**(a) Grant of land with an 8-year operating condition:** The grant is related to a **non-depreciable asset** (land) but carries an obligation (operate the factory for 8 years). Therefore the fair value of ₹40,00,000 is **not** taken to capital reserve immediately; it is treated as **deferred income** and recognised in the Statement of Profit and Loss **over 8 years** — the period over which the cost of meeting the obligation is incurred i.e. ₹40,00,000 ÷ 8 = **₹5,00,000 per annum**. Where a non-monetary asset is given free, it may be recorded at a nominal value; if recorded at fair value, both the asset and the deferred grant are recognised at ₹40,00,000.

**(b) Cash grant of ₹25,00,000 for land, no conditions:** As this grant relates to a **non-depreciable asset and requires no fulfilment of obligations**, it is credited **directly to Capital Reserve** and is not routed through the Statement of Profit and Loss.

**(c) If grant in (a) had no conditions:** The land grant would then be credited **directly to Capital Reserve** (like (b)), because absent any obligation there is no period over which to spread the grant.

**Conclusion:** Conditional grant on a non-depreciable asset → deferred income spread over the obligation period (₹5,00,000 p.a. for 8 years); unconditional grant on a non-depreciable asset → straight to Capital Reserve.

### Q22. Ch: AS 16 — Capitalisation of Borrowing Costs: Specific and General Borrowings (Marks: 8) [Problem]

|--------|-----------|------|---------|

### Q24. Ch: AS 18 — Related Party Disclosures (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Examine whether the following are "related parties" of Falcon Ltd. under AS 18 for FY 2025-26, and state what is disclosable:
(a) Mr. X, a director of Falcon Ltd., and his brother's proprietorship firm to which Falcon sold goods worth ₹12,00,000;
(b) A supplier from whom Falcon buys 80% of its raw material, but with whom there is no ownership or control link;
(c) A company in which Falcon holds 22% equity and exercises significant influence (an associate);
(d) A retirement benefit trust of Falcon's employees.

**Answer:**
**AS 18 "Related Party Disclosures"** applies where one party has the ability to **control** the other party or exercise **significant influence** over it in making financial and/or operating decisions. Parties are considered related if during the reporting period one party had such ability over the other, or both were subject to common control/significant influence.

**(a) Director and his brother's firm:** A director is **key management personnel (KMP)**, and hence a related party. His brother is a **relative** of KMP (relative = spouse, son, daughter, brother, sister, father, mother who may be expected to influence, or be influenced by, that person). A firm in which a relative of KMP has significant interest is a related party. Hence transactions with the brother's firm (₹12,00,000) are **related party transactions and disclosable**.

**(b) 80% supplier:** A single customer, supplier, franchiser, distributor or general agent with whom significant volume of business is transacted is **specifically excluded** from being a related party merely by virtue of the resulting economic dependence, in the absence of control or significant influence. Hence **not a related party**; no disclosure under AS 18.

**(c) 22% associate:** Holding of 22% equity ordinarily confers **significant influence** (presumed at ≥ 20%). An associate is a related party; transactions and balances are **disclosable**.

**(d) Employees' retirement benefit trust:** Post-employment benefit plans/trusts for the benefit of employees are a related party of the reporting enterprise. Contributions and transactions with the trust are **disclosable**.

**Conclusion:** (a), (c) and (d) are related parties requiring disclosure of the nature of relationship, transactions and outstanding balances; (b) is not a related party.

### Q27. Ch: AS 22 — MAT Credit Entitlement and Deferred Tax (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Vega Ltd. is subject to Minimum Alternate Tax (MAT). For FY 2025-26, its tax on book profits under MAT is ₹28,00,000 whereas its normal income-tax liability is ₹19,00,000. The management is confident, on the basis of approved projections, that the company will pay normal tax (higher than MAT) within the next four years. Explain (a) whether the MAT credit of ₹9,00,000 is a deferred tax asset under AS 22, (b) how it should be recognised and presented, and (c) how it differs from a deferred tax asset.

**Answer:**
**AS 22 "Accounting for Taxes on Income"** deals with the accounting of **timing differences** between accounting income and taxable income. **MAT credit** does not arise out of a timing difference — it is the excess of tax paid under Section 115JB over the normal tax, which is available for **set-off against future normal tax** under the Income-tax Act, 1961. Hence it is **not** a deferred tax asset under AS 22.

**(a) Nature:** The MAT credit of ₹9,00,000 (₹28,00,000 − ₹19,00,000) is an **asset in the nature of a tax credit / prepaid tax**, governed by the **Guidance Note on Accounting for Credit Available in respect of MAT**, not by AS 22.

**(b) Recognition and presentation:** MAT credit entitlement should be recognised as an asset **only when and to the extent there is convincing evidence** that the company will pay normal income-tax during the specified period (the period for which the credit is allowed to be carried forward and set off). It is recognised by crediting the Statement of Profit and Loss (as a reduction of current tax) and debiting **"MAT Credit Entitlement"**, presented on the **assets side** under **Loans and Advances / Other non-current or current assets**. It must be **reviewed at each balance sheet date** and written down to the extent it is no longer probable it can be utilised.

**(c) Distinction from deferred tax asset:**

| Basis | MAT Credit Entitlement | Deferred Tax Asset (AS 22) |
|-------|------------------------|----------------------------|
| Origin | Excess of MAT over normal tax paid | Deductible timing differences / unabsorbed losses |
| Governing pronouncement | Guidance Note on MAT | AS 22 |
| Recognition test | Convincing evidence of normal tax payment in carry-forward period | Reasonable certainty (virtual certainty if losses) of future taxable income |
| Presentation | Loans and Advances / Other assets | Deferred Tax Asset (net of DTL) |

**Conclusion:** The ₹9,00,000 MAT credit is **not a deferred tax asset**; it is recognised as "MAT Credit Entitlement" under the Guidance Note when there is convincing evidence of future normal-tax payment, and disclosed separately from deferred tax.

*(Full-marks tip: The key mark-earner is explicitly stating MAT credit is NOT covered by AS 22 (no timing difference) and is governed by the Guidance Note with the "convincing evidence" test — do not merge it with deferred tax.)*

### Q28. Ch: AS 24 — Discontinuing Operations (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Define a "discontinuing operation" under AS 24. State when the **initial disclosure event** occurs and list the disclosures required at that stage. Also state the treatment when a plan to discontinue is subsequently **abandoned**.

**Answer:**
**AS 24 "Discontinuing Operations"** defines a **discontinuing operation** as a component of an enterprise (a) that the enterprise, pursuant to a single plan, is **disposing of substantially in its entirety** (by sale or otherwise), or is disposing of **piecemeal**, or is **terminating through abandonment**; (b) that represents a **separate major line of business or geographical area** of operations; and (c) that can be **distinguished operationally and for financial reporting** purposes.

**Initial disclosure event:** Disclosure of a discontinuing operation begins from the earlier of (i) the enterprise entering into a **binding sale agreement** for substantially all of the assets of the operation, or (ii) the enterprise's **board of directors (or similar governing body) approving and announcing a detailed formal plan** for the discontinuance.

**Disclosures at the initial disclosure event** include:
1. a **description** of the discontinuing operation;
2. the **business or geographical segment(s)** in which it is reported under AS 17;
3. the **date and nature** of the initial disclosure event;
4. the **date or period** in which the discontinuance is expected to be completed, if known or determinable;
5. the **carrying amounts**, as at the balance sheet date, of the **total assets and total liabilities** to be disposed of;
6. the **amounts of revenue, expenses, pre-tax profit or loss** from ordinary activities attributable to the discontinuing operation and the related **income-tax expense**; and
7. the amounts of **net cash flows** attributable to operating, investing and financing activities of the discontinuing operation.

**Abandonment of the plan:** If an enterprise **abandons or withdraws** from a plan that was previously reported as a discontinuing operation, that fact, the reasons for it, and its **effect** are to be **disclosed**, and the operation ceases to be reported as a discontinuing operation. AS 24 is a **disclosure standard** — it does not establish any new recognition or measurement principles; recognition of provisions/impairment continues to be governed by AS 29 and AS 28 respectively.

**Conclusion:** A discontinuing operation is a distinguishable major component being disposed of/terminated under a single plan; disclosures commence at the initial disclosure event and are reversed with explanation if the plan is abandoned.

*(Full-marks tip: State clearly that AS 24 is a DISCLOSURE standard only (no new recognition/measurement) and that the initial disclosure event is the EARLIER of binding sale agreement and board-approved announced plan — both are classic examiner points.)*

### Q29. Ch: AS 25 — Interim Financial Reporting: Recognition Principles (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Explain, with reasons under AS 25, how the following are to be dealt with in the interim financial report of Halcyon Ltd. for the quarter ended 30th June, 2025:
(a) An annual bonus of ₹24,00,000 that is contractually payable and reliably estimable;
(b) A major planned advertising campaign of ₹8,00,000 incurred wholly in the first quarter but benefiting the full year;
(c) A cyclical revenue where 60% of annual sales occur in the last quarter;
(d) A once-a-year property tax of ₹12,00,000 relating to the calendar of operations for the whole year.

**Answer:**
**AS 25 "Interim Financial Reporting"** requires that an enterprise apply the **same accounting policies** in its interim statements as in its annual statements, and that revenues and costs be recognised on a **year-to-date** basis. The frequency of reporting does not affect the measurement of annual results; income should not be anticipated or deferred unless such anticipation/deferral would be appropriate at the year end.

**(a) Annual bonus:** A bonus is anticipated for interim reporting **only if** it is a **legal/constructive obligation** and a reliable estimate can be made. Here it is contractual and reliably estimable, so a **proportionate charge** is recognised: ₹24,00,000 × 3/12 = **₹6,00,000** for the quarter.

**(b) Advertising campaign:** Costs are recognised as expense when incurred; a cost that does **not** meet the definition of an asset at the year end cannot be deferred at an interim date merely because it benefits later interim periods. Since advertising does not create an asset, the entire **₹8,00,000 is expensed in Q1** (it would be expensed at year end too).

**(c) Cyclical/seasonal revenue:** Revenues that are received seasonally or cyclically **should not be anticipated or deferred** at an interim date if such treatment would not be appropriate at the year end. Hence revenue is recognised **only as it is earned** — the interim report shows only the actual Q1 sales, not a smoothed 25%.

**(d) Once-a-year property tax:** A cost incurred unevenly during the year that is **anticipated or deferred at the year end** (because it relates to the whole year's operations) should be similarly anticipated or deferred in interim reports. The ₹12,00,000 is therefore **spread**: ₹12,00,000 × 3/12 = **₹3,00,000** charged in Q1.

**Conclusion:** Recognise bonus ₹6,00,000 and property tax ₹3,00,000 on a time-proportion basis; expense the full advertising ₹8,00,000 in Q1; recognise cyclical revenue only as actually earned.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner tests the "same treatment as at year end" principle — anticipate/defer an item interim-wise ONLY if it would be anticipated/deferred annually. Seasonal revenue must NOT be smoothed — a frequent error.)*

### Q30. Ch: AS 26 — Recognition of Internally Generated Intangible Assets (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** State the conditions under AS 26 for capitalising costs incurred in the **development phase** of an internally generated intangible asset. Also state (a) why internally generated **goodwill, brands and mastheads** cannot be recognised, and (b) the treatment of **research phase** expenditure.

**Answer:**
**AS 26 "Intangible Assets"** requires that to assess whether an internally generated intangible asset meets the recognition criteria, the generation of the asset be classified into a **research phase** and a **development phase**.

**Development-phase recognition — the six conditions:** An intangible asset arising from development is recognised **if, and only if,** the enterprise can **demonstrate all** of the following:
1. the **technical feasibility** of completing the intangible asset so that it will be available for use or sale;
2. its **intention to complete** the intangible asset and use or sell it;
3. its **ability to use or sell** the intangible asset;
4. how the intangible asset will generate **probable future economic benefits** (e.g. existence of a market for its output, or usefulness of the asset if for internal use);
5. the availability of **adequate technical, financial and other resources** to complete the development and to use or sell the asset; and
6. its **ability to measure reliably the expenditure** attributable to the intangible asset during its development.

Only expenditure meeting all six conditions, incurred **from the date the conditions are first met**, is capitalised; expenditure earlier recognised as an expense **cannot** be reinstated as part of the asset's cost.

**(a) Internally generated goodwill, brands, mastheads, publishing titles, customer lists and items similar in substance are NOT recognised** because they are **not identifiable resources** controlled by the enterprise that can be measured reliably at cost; expenditure on them cannot be distinguished from the cost of developing the business as a whole. Internally generated goodwill is not an identifiable asset and hence is not recognised.

**(b) Research-phase expenditure:** No intangible asset arising from research (or the research phase) is to be recognised. **Research expenditure is recognised as an expense** when incurred, because in the research phase an enterprise cannot demonstrate that an intangible asset exists that will generate probable future economic benefits.

**Conclusion:** Development costs are capitalised only when all six demonstrable conditions are satisfied; research costs and internally generated goodwill/brands/mastheads are expensed/not recognised.

### Q31. Ch: AS 28 — Reversal of an Impairment Loss (Marks: 8) [Problem]

On 31st March, 2024, the recoverable amount of the machine fell to ₹9,00,000 and an impairment loss was recognised. On 31st March, 2026, favourable market conditions raised the recoverable amount to ₹11,00,000. Compute (a) the impairment loss in 2023-24, (b) depreciation thereafter, and (c) the reversal of impairment loss to be recognised in 2025-26, in accordance with AS 28.

**Solution:**
As per **AS 28 "Impairment of Assets"**, an impairment loss is the excess of carrying amount over recoverable amount. A reversal is recognised when there is a change in the estimates used to determine recoverable amount, but the increased carrying amount **must not exceed** the carrying amount that would have been determined (net of depreciation) had **no impairment loss** been recognised in prior years.

**WN-1: Carrying amount and impairment loss on 31.03.2024**
- Annual depreciation (pre-impairment) = ₹20,00,000 ÷ 10 = ₹2,00,000
- Carrying amount on 31.03.2024 (after 3 years) = 20,00,000 − (3 × 2,00,000) = **₹14,00,000**
- Recoverable amount = ₹9,00,000
- **Impairment loss (2023-24) = 14,00,000 − 9,00,000 = ₹5,00,000**

- Revised carrying amount = ₹9,00,000; remaining life = 10 − 3 = 7 years
- Depreciation per year = 9,00,000 ÷ 7 = **₹1,28,571**
- Carrying amount on 31.03.2026 (after 2 years) = 9,00,000 − (2 × 1,28,571) = **₹6,42,858**

**WN-3: Carrying amount had there been NO impairment (ceiling)**
- On 31.03.2026 = 20,00,000 − (5 × 2,00,000) = **₹10,00,000**

**WN-4: Reversal of impairment loss (2025-26)**
- Recoverable amount on 31.03.2026 = ₹11,00,000
- Capped at the "no-impairment" carrying amount = ₹10,00,000 (lower of 11,00,000 and 10,00,000)
- Reversal = Ceiling − present carrying amount = 10,00,000 − 6,42,858 = **₹3,57,142**

**Statement Showing Reversal of Impairment**

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|-------------|-----------:|
| Carrying amount on 31.03.2026 (with impairment) | 6,42,858 |
| Recoverable amount | 11,00,000 |
| Ceiling: carrying amount had no impairment occurred | 10,00,000 |
| Revised carrying amount (restricted to ceiling) | 10,00,000 |
| **Reversal credited to Statement of Profit and Loss** | **3,57,142** |

**Answer:** Impairment loss in 2023-24 = **₹5,00,000**; reversal of impairment in 2025-26 = **₹3,57,142**; revised carrying amount after reversal = **₹10,00,000** (restricted to the no-impairment carrying amount).

*(Full-marks tip: The decisive step is the CEILING — reversal cannot raise the asset above its would-have-been depreciated cost (₹10,00,000), even though recoverable amount is ₹11,00,000. Students who reverse up to ₹11,00,000 lose marks.)*

### Q32. Ch: AS 29 — Onerous Contract and Restructuring Provision (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Advise Pinnacle Ltd. on the following, applying AS 29, for the year ended 31st March, 2026:
(a) Pinnacle has a non-cancellable contract to purchase 10,000 units of a component at ₹500 each. Owing to a product redesign the components are now worthless to Pinnacle; cancelling the contract attracts a penalty of ₹18,00,000. Should a provision be made and, if so, for how much?
(b) On 15th March, 2026 the Board approved and publicly announced a detailed plan to close a division, involving redundancy costs of ₹40,00,000, staff retraining/relocation of continuing employees ₹6,00,000, and expected future operating losses of the division of ₹9,00,000. Which of these may be provided for?

**Answer:**
**AS 29 "Provisions, Contingent Liabilities and Contingent Assets"** requires a provision to be recognised when (i) an enterprise has a **present obligation** as a result of a past event, (ii) it is **probable** that an outflow of resources will be required to settle it, and (iii) a **reliable estimate** can be made of the amount.

**(a) Onerous contract:** A contract becomes **onerous** when the unavoidable costs of meeting the obligations exceed the economic benefits expected to be received. The **unavoidable cost** is the **lower of** the cost of fulfilling the contract and any compensation/penalty arising from failure to fulfil it.
- Cost of fulfilling = 10,000 × ₹500 = ₹50,00,000 (components worthless, so benefit = nil → net cost ₹50,00,000)
- Cost of exiting (penalty) = ₹18,00,000
- Unavoidable cost = lower of the two = **₹18,00,000**

A **provision of ₹18,00,000** should be recognised (the least net cost of an onerous contract). *(Note: under AS 29 a specific onerous-contract requirement applies; the present obligation exists because the contract is non-cancellable.)*

**(b) Restructuring provision:** A provision for restructuring is recognised only when there is a **constructive obligation**, which arises when the enterprise has (i) a **detailed formal plan** identifying the business/location/employees affected, and (ii) raised a **valid expectation** in those affected by starting to implement the plan or announcing its main features. Here the plan is Board-approved and **publicly announced** by 31.03.2026 — a constructive obligation exists.

A restructuring provision includes **only direct expenditures necessarily entailed** by the restructuring and **not associated with the ongoing activities** of the enterprise. Therefore:

| Item | Provide? | Reason |
|------|:--------:|--------|
| Redundancy costs ₹40,00,000 | Yes | Direct cost of restructuring, not related to ongoing activities |
| Retraining/relocation of continuing staff ₹6,00,000 | No | Relates to the **future conduct of the business** (continuing employees) |
| Future operating losses ₹9,00,000 | No | **Future operating losses are not provided** unless relating to an onerous contract |

**Restructuring provision = ₹40,00,000** only.

**Conclusion:** (a) provide ₹18,00,000 for the onerous contract (lower of fulfilment cost and penalty); (b) provide ₹40,00,000 for redundancy only — retraining of continuing staff and future operating losses are excluded.

*(Full-marks tip: For onerous contracts, take the LOWER of fulfilment cost and exit penalty. For restructuring, the two exclusions — costs of ongoing activities (retraining continuing staff) and future operating losses — are exactly what the examiner checks.)*

### Q33. Ch: AS 20 — Diluted Earnings Per Share (Marks: 6) [Problem]

| Particulars | Details |
|-------------|---------|

| 12% Convertible Debentures outstanding | ₹1,00,00,000 (each ₹100 debenture convertible into 8 equity shares) |

**Solution:**

= ₹50,00,000 ÷ 20,00,000 = **₹2.50 per share**

- Adjusted profit = 50,00,000 + 8,40,000 = **₹58,40,000**

**WN-3: Potential equity shares on conversion**

### Q36. Ch: AS 3 Cash Flow Statements — Finance Lease Treatment (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** During FY 2025-26, Nimbus Ltd. acquired plant costing ₹12,00,000 under a **finance lease**. The company paid lease rentals of ₹2,80,000 during the year, of which ₹1,15,000 represented finance charge (interest) and ₹1,65,000 the principal repayment. Explain, with reasons, how each element of this transaction is presented in the Cash Flow Statement prepared as per AS 3. State also the required disclosure for the non-cash portion.

**Answer:**
As per **AS 3 "Cash Flow Statements"**, a cash flow statement reports only actual inflows and outflows of cash and cash equivalents; the acquisition of an asset under a finance lease is treated as follows.

The **acquisition of the plant under the finance lease** (₹12,00,000) does **not** involve any movement of cash at the point of acquisition — the asset and the corresponding lease liability are recognised without cash changing hands. AS 3 expressly states that investing and financing transactions that do not require the use of cash (such as the acquisition of an asset by assuming directly related liabilities, i.e., a finance lease) are **excluded from the cash flow statement** and instead **disclosed elsewhere in the financial statements** (by way of a note) so that all relevant information about such transactions is provided.

The **finance-charge component of ₹1,15,000** is interest. AS 3 permits interest paid to be classified under **financing activities** (for a non-financial enterprise), and it must be shown as an outflow of **₹1,15,000 under Financing Activities** (disclosed separately, whether capitalised or charged to P&L).

The **principal-repayment component of ₹1,65,000** represents repayment of the amount borrowed under the lease and is a **financing outflow of ₹1,65,000 under Financing Activities**.

| Element | Amount (₹) | Treatment under AS 3 |
|---|---:|---|
| Asset acquired under finance lease | 12,00,000 | Non-cash — excluded; disclosed by note |
| Finance charge (interest) paid | 1,15,000 | Financing activities — outflow |
| Principal repayment of lease liability | 1,65,000 | Financing activities — outflow |

**Conclusion:** Only the cash rentals of ₹2,80,000 (₹1,15,000 interest + ₹1,65,000 principal) appear as financing outflows; the ₹12,00,000 asset acquisition is a non-cash transaction disclosed separately by way of a note.

*(Full-marks tip: State explicitly that the non-cash acquisition is disclosed by a note — students routinely mention exclusion but forget the mandated separate disclosure, which is where a mark is lost.)*

### Q37. Ch: AS 3 Cash Flow Statements — Interest & Dividend Classification (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** "The classification of interest and dividends, received and paid, in a cash flow statement depends on whether the enterprise is a financial enterprise." Discuss the classification requirements of AS 3 for a **non-financial (other) enterprise** in respect of (i) interest paid, (ii) interest and dividends received, and (iii) dividends paid, and state the guiding principle.

**Answer:**
As per **AS 3 "Cash Flow Statements"**, cash flows arising from interest and dividends must each be **disclosed separately** and classified in a **consistent manner from period to period** as either operating, investing or financing activities. For an enterprise **other than a financial enterprise**, the classification is as follows.

**(i) Interest paid** is classified as a **Financing Activity**, because it is the cost of obtaining financial resources.

**(ii) Interest received and dividends received** are classified as **Investing Activities**, because they are returns on investments made by the enterprise.

**(iii) Dividends paid** are classified as a **Financing Activity**, because they represent a cost of obtaining (equity) financial resources.

| Item | Non-financial enterprise |
|---|---|
| Interest paid | Financing |
| Interest received | Investing |
| Dividends received | Investing |
| Dividends paid | Financing |

The **guiding principle** is that, for a non-financial enterprise, interest and dividends **received** are returns on investments (investing), while interest and dividends **paid** are costs of raising finance (financing); each amount is disclosed separately and classified consistently across periods.

**Conclusion:** For a non-financial enterprise, interest and dividends received are investing cash flows, whereas interest paid and dividends paid are financing cash flows.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner looks for the qualifier "for a non-financial enterprise" and the words "disclosed separately" and "consistent from period to period" — omitting these loses marks even if the four items are placed correctly.)*

### Q39. Ch: AS 3 Cash Flow Statements — Cash & Cash Equivalents / Bank Overdraft (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** State how each of the following would be dealt with while preparing a Cash Flow Statement as per AS 3, giving the reason in each case:
1. Investment in a 2-month treasury bill acquired one month before maturity, ₹5,00,000.
2. Equity shares of a listed company held as a short-term investment, ₹3,00,000.
3. Bank overdraft repayable on demand forming an integral part of cash management.
4. Movement between cash on hand and a 60-day fixed deposit.
5. A foreign-currency cash balance restated at the closing rate giving an exchange gain of ₹18,000.

**Answer:**
As per **AS 3**, "cash equivalents" are short-term, highly liquid investments that are readily convertible into known amounts of cash and which are subject to an **insignificant risk of changes in value**; an investment normally qualifies only when it has a **short maturity of, say, three months or less from the date of acquisition**.

1. **Treasury bill (₹5,00,000):** Since only one month remains to maturity from the date of acquisition, it is readily convertible with insignificant risk of value change and qualifies as a **cash equivalent**. It is included in cash and cash equivalents and its purchase is **not** shown as an investing cash flow.

2. **Equity shares held short-term (₹3,00,000):** Equity investments are **excluded** from cash equivalents (except redeemable preference shares acquired close to redemption) because they carry a significant risk of change in value. Their purchase/sale is an **investing** cash flow.

3. **Bank overdraft repayable on demand** that is an integral part of cash management is **included as a component of cash and cash equivalents**; its movement is not shown as a financing flow.

4. **Cash ↔ 60-day fixed deposit:** A transfer between items that both qualify as cash/cash equivalents (movement between components) is **not a cash flow**; it is excluded.

5. **Exchange gain of ₹18,000** on restating a foreign-currency cash balance is **not a cash flow**, but is reported in the statement **separately** as the "effect of exchange rate changes" to reconcile opening and closing cash and cash equivalents.

**Conclusion:** Items 1, 3 and 4 relate to cash/cash equivalents themselves (no cash flow / component classification); item 2 is an investing flow; item 5 is a separately-reported non-cash reconciling adjustment.

*(Full-marks tip: The "three-month from date of acquisition" test and the overdraft-integral-to-cash-management rule are the two markers examiners look for. Do not treat the exchange gain as an operating add-back — it is a separate reconciling line.)*

### Q43. Ch: Redemption of Preference Shares — Sources Permissible / Theory (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Under **Section 55 of the Companies Act, 2013**, state the conditions and permissible sources for redemption of preference shares. Also explain how the premium payable on redemption is to be provided.

**Answer:**
As per **Section 55 of the Companies Act, 2013**, a company limited by shares may, if authorised by its articles, issue preference shares which are liable to be redeemed within a period **not exceeding twenty years** from the date of issue (with a specific exception for infrastructure projects). The redemption is subject to the following conditions.

**Conditions for redemption [Sec 55(2)]:**
1. **No preference shares shall be redeemed unless they are fully paid.**
2. Preference shares shall be redeemed **only** out of (a) the **profits of the company which would otherwise be available for dividend**, or (b) out of the **proceeds of a fresh issue of shares** made for the purposes of such redemption.
3. Where the shares are redeemed **out of profits**, a sum equal to the **nominal amount** of the shares so redeemed must be transferred to the **Capital Redemption Reserve (CRR)** account, and the CRR is treated as if it were paid-up share capital.

**Premium on redemption:** The premium payable on redemption must be provided for. Under the Act read with the applicable provisions, the premium on redemption is to be provided out of the **company's Securities Premium** (to the extent available) or out of the **profits of the company**, before the shares are redeemed.

| Aspect | Position under Sec 55 |
|---|---|
| Shares must be | Fully paid |
| Redeem out of | Divisible profits OR proceeds of fresh issue |
| CRR transfer | Nominal value redeemed, less nominal value of fresh issue used |
| Premium on redemption from | Securities Premium, else profits |
| Use of CRR | Only to issue fully paid bonus shares |

**Conclusion:** Only fully paid preference shares can be redeemed, financed by divisible profits and/or fresh-issue proceeds, with a CRR transfer to protect capital, and premium met out of securities premium or profits.

*(Full-marks tip: State both permissible sources AND the fully-paid precondition; examiners deduct where the CRR-equals-nominal rule or the "twenty years" cap is omitted.)*

### Q47. Ch: Buy-back of Securities — Open-Market Route & Conditions (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Distinguish the buy-back of shares through the **tender offer** route from the **open-market (stock exchange)** route, and state any four **conditions/restrictions** applicable to a buy-back under Section 68 of the Companies Act, 2013 that a company must satisfy regardless of route.

**Answer:**
As per **Section 68 of the Companies Act, 2013**, a company may buy back its own shares out of its free reserves, securities premium account, or the proceeds of an earlier issue of a different class of shares, subject to prescribed conditions and by any of the permitted routes.

**Tender offer vs Open-market route**

| Basis | Tender offer route | Open-market route |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | Company invites existing shareholders to tender (offer) their shares at a fixed price | Company buys shares from the stock exchange / book-building at prevailing market prices |
| Price | Fixed, uniform price to all | Varies with market; a maximum price is fixed |
| Shareholders covered | All shareholders proportionately (on record date) | Whoever sells in the market |
| Certainty of quantity | Higher — up to the offer size | Depends on market availability during offer period |

**Four conditions/restrictions under Section 68 (route-independent):**
1. The buy-back must be **authorised by the articles** of the company.
2. A **special resolution** must be passed in general meeting authorising the buy-back (a board resolution alone suffices only where the buy-back is **10% or less** of paid-up equity capital and free reserves).
3. The buy-back shall **not exceed 25%** of the aggregate of **paid-up capital and free reserves**; and in a financial year it shall not exceed 25% of the **paid-up equity capital**.
4. The ratio of **debt (secured + unsecured) to (capital + free reserves) after buy-back shall not be more than 2:1**; and all the shares for buy-back must be **fully paid-up**.
*(Further: buy-back must be completed within one year of the special/board resolution; and no further buy-back within one year.)*

**Conclusion:** Whichever route is used, the 25% ceiling, the 2:1 post-buy-back debt-equity test, the fully-paid requirement and the special-resolution/authorisation conditions of Section 68 must all be satisfied.

*(Full-marks tip: State the 10%-board-resolution vs 25%-special-resolution distinction and the 2:1 debt-equity cap; these two carry marks even in a theory question.)*

### Q50. Ch: Buy-back of Securities — Prohibitions & Time Limits (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the circumstances in which a company is **prohibited from buying back its own shares**, and the key **time-limit conditions** governing buy-back, under Sections 68 and 70 of the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:**
As per **Section 70 of the Companies Act, 2013**, a company shall **not** directly or indirectly purchase its own shares:
1. Through any **subsidiary company** including its own subsidiaries;
2. Through any **investment company or group of investment companies**; or
3. If a **default** is subsisting in the repayment of deposits or interest thereon, redemption of debentures or preference shares, payment of dividend, or repayment of any term loan or interest to a financial institution/bank (buy-back is allowed if the default is remedied and three years have elapsed).

A company shall **not** buy back unless it has **complied with Sections 92 (annual return), 123 (declaration of dividend), 127 (payment of dividend) and 129 (financial statements)**.

**Time-limit conditions under Section 68:**
1. Every buy-back must be **completed within one year** from the date of passing the special resolution (or Board resolution, as applicable).
2. After completion of a buy-back, the company shall **not make a further buy-back within one year** from the date of completion of the previous buy-back.
3. Where shares are bought back, the company must **extinguish and physically destroy** the shares so bought back within **seven days** of the last date of completion of buy-back.
4. After buy-back, the company shall not issue the same kind of shares within **six months** (except by way of bonus, or discharge of subsisting obligations such as conversion, warrants, etc.).

**Conclusion:** Buy-back is prohibited through subsidiaries/investment companies or during a subsisting default, and is bound by the one-year completion, one-year cooling-off, seven-day extinguishment and six-month re-issue restrictions.

*(Full-marks tip: The seven-day extinguishment rule and the one-year cooling-off between buy-backs are the frequently-forgotten points that distinguish a full-marks answer.)*

### Q52. Ch: Bonus Issue — Bonus vs Stock Dividend & the Bar under Section 63(3) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** "A company facing a cash crunch may capitalise its reserves and, instead of paying a cash dividend, hand over fully paid bonus shares to its members — this is the same thing as a stock dividend." Examine the correctness of this statement in the light of Section 63 of the Companies Act, 2013. Also state whether a company may make its partly paid-up shares fully paid-up by way of a bonus.

**Answer:** (theory)
A bonus issue is a capitalisation of a company's accumulated **free reserves, securities premium or capital redemption reserve** whereby fully paid bonus shares are issued to existing members without any fresh inflow of cash, governed by **Section 63 of the Companies Act, 2013** read with **Rule 14 of the Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules, 2014**.

The statement that a bonus issue is merely a "stock dividend" and can be used *in lieu of* a cash dividend is **incorrect**. **Section 63(3)** expressly provides that **"the bonus shares shall not be issued in lieu of dividend."** A dividend is a distribution of divisible profits that leaves the company's paid-up capital unchanged, whereas a bonus issue permanently converts distributable reserves into non-distributable share capital; a company therefore cannot dress up a dividend as a bonus to escape the discipline of the dividend rules.

The permissible **sources** of a bonus issue under **Section 63(1)** are limited to (i) free reserves, (ii) the securities premium account, and (iii) the capital redemption reserve. A reserve created by **revaluation of assets** cannot be capitalised for a bonus issue [Section 63(1) proviso].

The **conditions** in **Section 63(2)** must be satisfied: the bonus is authorised by the articles; recommended by the Board and authorised in general meeting; the company has not defaulted in payment of interest/principal on fixed deposits or debt securities; it has not defaulted in payment of statutory dues of employees (PF, gratuity, bonus); and **the partly paid-up shares outstanding on the date of allotment are made fully paid-up** [Section 63(2)(e)]. A bonus, once recommended, cannot be withdrawn.

On the second issue — the sources usable for **making partly paid shares fully paid**: only **free reserves** may be so applied. The **securities premium account** and the **capital redemption reserve** can be applied *only* in paying up **unissued shares** to be issued as **fully paid bonus shares** [Section 52(2) and Section 55]; they **cannot** be used to convert existing partly paid shares into fully paid shares.

| Basis | Bonus Issue | Cash Dividend / "Stock Dividend" |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Capitalisation of reserves | Distribution of profit |
| Effect on capital | Paid-up capital increases | Paid-up capital unchanged |
| Cash outflow | Nil | Outflow (cash dividend) |
| Governing law | Section 63 | Section 123 |
| In lieu of dividend? | Prohibited — Sec 63(3) | It *is* the dividend |

**Conclusion:** A bonus issue is **not** a substitute for dividend; issuing bonus shares in lieu of dividend is barred by Section 63(3), and partly paid shares can be made fully paid only out of free reserves, never out of securities premium or CRR.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the explicit citation of Section 63(3) for the "in lieu of dividend" bar and the source-restriction under Section 52/55. Losing marks is common when candidates list revaluation reserve as a valid source or say securities premium can convert partly paid shares to fully paid.)*

### Q55. Ch: Rights Issue — Value of a Right and Cum-/Ex-Rights Price (Marks: 5) [Problem]

Compute (a) the theoretical **ex-rights (market) price**, (b) the **value of a right** attaching to one existing share, and (c) the value attaching to one *new* share, and reconcile the two.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — New shares and funds raised**
Rights shares = 4,00,000 × 1/4 = **1,00,000 shares** at ₹30 = **₹30,00,000**.

Formula: Ex-rights price = [(Existing shares × cum-rights price) + (Rights shares × issue price)] ÷ Total shares after issue

= [(4,00,000 × ₹50) + (1,00,000 × ₹30)] ÷ 5,00,000
= (₹2,00,00,000 + ₹30,00,000) ÷ 5,00,000
= ₹2,30,00,000 ÷ 5,00,000 = **₹46 per share**.

|---|---|

| Theoretical ex-rights price | ₹46 |
| Value of right per existing share | ₹4 |
| Value of right per new share (×4) | ₹16 |

**Answer:** Ex-rights price **₹46**; value of right **₹4 per existing share** (₹16 per new share). The two reconcile because 4 existing shares' rights (4 × ₹4 = ₹16) are required to subscribe to one new share.

### Q56. Ch: Rights Issue — Statutory Framework under Section 62(1)(a) (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the conditions a company must comply with under **Section 62(1)(a)** of the Companies Act, 2013 when it proposes to increase its subscribed capital by a further issue of shares to existing equity shareholders (rights issue).

**Answer:** (theory)
Where a company having a share capital proposes to increase its subscribed capital by a further issue of shares, **Section 62(1)(a)** of the Companies Act, 2013 requires that such shares be **offered first to existing equity shareholders** in proportion to the paid-up capital held by them, subject to the following conditions:

1. **Letter of offer** — the offer shall be made by notice specifying the number of shares offered and giving a period of **not less than 15 days and not more than 30 days** from the date of the offer within which it must be accepted; if not accepted within that period it is deemed declined [Section 62(1)(a)(i)]. *(For a private company, a shorter period is allowed if 90% of members agree.)*
2. **Right of renunciation** — unless the articles otherwise provide, the offer shall include a right to **renounce** the shares in favour of any other person [Section 62(1)(a)(ii)].
3. **Disposal on non-acceptance** — after the offer period expires, or on receipt of intimation of declining, the Board may **dispose of the unsubscribed shares** in a manner **not disadvantageous to the shareholders and the company** [Section 62(1)(a)(iii)].
4. The notice of offer must be **despatched through registered post, speed post, courier or electronic mode** to all existing shareholders at least **three days before the opening** of the issue.

**Conclusion:** A rights issue under Section 62(1)(a) protects the pre-emptive right of existing shareholders through a proportionate, renounceable offer kept open for 15–30 days, with unsubscribed shares disposed of only in a manner beneficial to the company and its members.

*(Full-marks tip: The "15 to 30 days" window and the renunciation right are the two facts examiners always want; state the exact section clause references to secure full marks.)*

### Q58. Ch: ESOP — Equity-Settled Options, Cash-Settled SARs, Reload and Repricing (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** With reference to the ICAI **Guidance Note on Accounting for Share-based Payments**, distinguish between an **equity-settled** employee stock option and a **cash-settled Stock Appreciation Right (SAR)**, and briefly explain the accounting treatment of a **reload option** and of the **repricing** of options.

**Answer:** (theory)
Under the ICAI **Guidance Note on Accounting for Share-based Payments**, an entity that receives services from employees in exchange for its equity instruments (or amounts based on their price) recognises those services as an **expense over the vesting period** with a corresponding credit to equity (equity-settled) or to a liability (cash-settled).

An **equity-settled** ESOP gives the employee the right to acquire the company's **shares** at an exercise price. It is measured at the **fair value of the option on the grant date**, and that grant-date fair value is **not subsequently remeasured**; only the number of options expected to vest is revised. The credit is to a **"Stock Options Outstanding" account** within equity, transferred to share capital and securities premium on exercise.

A **cash-settled SAR** entitles the employee to a **cash payment** equal to the appreciation in the share price over a base price. Because it creates an **obligation to pay cash**, it is recognised as a **liability**, measured at fair value and **remeasured at every reporting date and at settlement**, with all changes taken to the Statement of Profit & Loss.

| Basis | Equity-settled ESOP | Cash-settled SAR |
|---|---|---|
| Settlement | Shares | Cash |
| Credit is to | Equity (SOO account) | Liability |
| Remeasurement | No (grant-date FV frozen) | Yes, every period end & settlement |
| Effect on capital | Share capital increases | No change; cash outflow |

**Reload option:** A reload feature grants **new (reload) options automatically** when the employee exercises the original option by tendering already-owned shares. The reload feature is **not** taken into account when measuring the original grant; instead, a reload option, **if and when granted**, is accounted for as a **new grant of options** on its own grant date.

**Repricing (modification):** If a company **reduces the exercise price** of options that are "out of the money," it has modified the grant. The original grant-date fair value **continues** to be recognised over the remaining vesting period, and in addition the **incremental fair value** (fair value of the repriced option less fair value of the original option, both measured at the modification date) is recognised **over the remaining vesting period** (or immediately if already vested). A modification cannot reduce the expense below the original grant-date fair value.

**Conclusion:** Equity-settled options are frozen at grant-date fair value with an equity credit, cash-settled SARs are remeasured liabilities; a reload option is a fresh grant, and repricing adds the incremental fair value to the continuing original charge.

*(Full-marks tip: The "no remeasurement for equity-settled vs remeasure every period for cash-settled" contrast and the "incremental fair value" rule for repricing are the scoring statements. Do not say the original charge is cancelled on repricing — it continues.)*

### Q63. Ch: Underwriting — Statutory Commission Limits under Section 40 and Rule 13 (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the maximum rates and the conditions for payment of **underwriting commission** under **Section 40(6)** of the Companies Act, 2013 read with **Rule 13** of the Companies (Prospectus and Allotment of Securities) Rules, 2014.

**Answer:** (theory)
**Section 40(6)** permits a company to **pay commission to any person** in connection with the subscription (absolute or conditional) to its securities, subject to the conditions prescribed in **Rule 13** of the Companies (Prospectus and Allotment of Securities) Rules, 2014:

1. The **payment of commission is authorised by the articles** of the company.
2. The commission **does not exceed**:
   - in the case of **shares**, **5% of the price** at which the shares are issued, **or** the rate authorised by the articles, **whichever is less**; and
   - in the case of **debentures**, **2.5% of the price** at which the debentures are issued, **or** the rate authorised by the articles, **whichever is less**.
3. The commission rate/amount is **disclosed in the prospectus** (or in the statement in lieu of prospectus / a filed statement).
4. The **number of shares or debentures** which persons have agreed to subscribe absolutely or conditionally is **disclosed**.
5. There is **no payment of commission** to any person on shares or debentures which are **not offered to the public for subscription**.
6. A **copy of the underwriting contract** is delivered to the Registrar at the time of delivery of the prospectus for registration.

**Conclusion:** Underwriting commission is capped at **5% for shares and 2.5% for debentures** (or the lower articled rate), payable only when authorised by the articles, disclosed in the prospectus, and confined to securities offered to the public.

*(Full-marks tip: The "5% shares / 2.5% debentures, whichever is less than the articled rate" limit is the key markable fact; also mention that commission cannot be paid on securities not offered to the public.)*

### Q65. Ch: Redemption of Debentures — Redemption Out of Profits vs Out of Capital and DRR (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between redemption of debentures **"out of profits"** and **"out of capital,"** and state the requirement to create a **Debenture Redemption Reserve (DRR)** and to invest in specified securities under **Rule 18(7)** of the Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules, 2014.

**Answer:** (theory)
Redemption of debentures is governed by **Section 71** of the Companies Act, 2013 read with **Rule 18(7)** of the Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules, 2014.

Redemption is said to be **"out of profits"** when an amount equal to the profits used for redemption is **transferred to a Debenture Redemption Reserve (DRR)** out of the divisible profits that would otherwise be available for dividend. The effect is that profits equal to the nominal value of debentures redeemed are **retained in the business** and are **not available for distribution as dividend**, thereby protecting the company's capital.

Redemption is said to be **"out of capital"** when **no such appropriation** of profits to DRR is made (or only a partial DRR is created); the divisible profits remain free for distribution, and the redemption is financed effectively out of capital.

| Basis | Out of profits | Out of capital |
|---|---|---|
| DRR created | Yes (to the extent of redemption) | No / partial |
| Divisible profits | Reduced / locked in | Remain available for dividend |
| Protection of capital | Higher | Lower |

**DRR and investment requirement under Rule 18(7):**
- **Listed companies** (and NBFCs/HFCs registered with RBI/NHB) — **not required** to create a DRR.
- **Unlisted companies** (other than NBFCs/HFCs) — required to create a DRR of **at least 10% of the outstanding value of debentures**.
- **Debenture Redemption Investment (DRI):** every company **required to create DRR** must, on or before **30th April** of each year, **invest or deposit** a sum of **not less than 15% of the amount of debentures maturing during the year ending 31st March of the next year** in specified securities/deposits, to be used only for redemption.
- The DRR is transferred to General Reserve after the debentures are fully redeemed.

**Conclusion:** Redemption out of profits locks profits into a DRR (protecting capital), while redemption out of capital does not; the extent of DRR (10% for unlisted non-NBFC companies) and the 15% DRI requirement under Rule 18(7) determine the minimum statutory cushion.

*(Full-marks tip: Quote the 10% DRR (unlisted) and the 15% DRI (by 30 April) figures with Rule 18(7). Stating that all companies must create a 25% DRR is outdated — that reflects the pre-2019 rule and loses marks.)*

### Q70. Ch: Investment Accounts — Classification & Measurement of Investments (AS 13) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain, as per **AS 13 "Accounting for Investments"**, (a) the classification of investments, (b) the measurement of a current investment and a long-term investment at the balance sheet date, and (c) the accounting treatment on reclassification of an investment from long-term to current and vice versa.

**Answer:**
**(a) Classification.** AS 13 classifies investments into two categories. A **current investment** is an investment that is by its nature readily realisable and is intended to be held for **not more than one year** from the date on which it is made. A **long-term investment** is an investment other than a current investment, i.e., one intended to be held for more than one year. The classification is based on the **intention** of management at the time of acquisition, not merely on the physical form of the investment.

**(b) Measurement at the balance sheet date.**

| Basis | Current Investment | Long-term Investment |
|---|---|---|
| Carrying amount | **Lower of cost and fair value** (fair value = net realisable value / market value) | **At cost** |
| Valuation approach | May be determined on an individual investment basis (or category basis) | Cost, reduced for a **decline other than temporary** |
| Reduction in value | Any reduction to fair value is charged to the Profit & Loss Statement | Only a **permanent (other than temporary) diminution** is provided for; temporary falls are ignored |

**(c) Reclassification.** Where a **long-term investment is reclassified as current**, the transfer is made at the **lower of cost and carrying amount** at the date of transfer. Where a **current investment is reclassified as long-term**, the transfer is made at the **lower of cost and fair value** at the date of transfer.

**Conclusion:** Thus, current investments are carried conservatively at the lower of cost and fair value with all declines routed through P&L, whereas long-term investments are carried at cost and written down only for a permanent diminution; reclassifications are always effected at the lower of the two relevant amounts prescribed by AS 13.

*(Full-marks tip: State the exact "lower of" rule for BOTH directions of reclassification — students routinely swap "fair value" and "carrying amount". Mention that classification rests on intention, and that only a *permanent* diminution is provided for long-term investments.)*

### Q72. Ch: Investment Accounts — Treatment of Bonus & Right Shares (AS 13) (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the accounting treatment, in the books of an investor, of (a) **bonus shares** received on shares held as an investment, and (b) **right shares** where the investor (i) subscribes to the rights, (ii) renounces (sells) the rights in the market, and (iii) subscribes to a part and renounces the balance.

**Answer:**
**(a) Bonus shares.** Bonus shares are issued by the investee company free of cost out of its accumulated profits/reserves. In the investor's Investment Account, **only the number of shares (nominal value) is increased; no amount is added to the cost column**, because no consideration passes. The effect is that the **average cost per share falls**, since the same total cost is now spread over a larger number of shares (AS 13).

**(b) Right shares.**

| Situation | Treatment in the Investment Account |
|---|---|
| (i) Rights **subscribed** | The **amount paid** on the right shares is **added to the cost** of the investment (both nominal value and cost columns increase). |
| (ii) Rights **renounced / sold** | The **sale proceeds of the rights are credited to the Profit & Loss Account** as income; cost of the existing holding is not disturbed, provided there is no diminution in the value of the shares retained. |
| (iii) **Part subscribed, part renounced** | The paid portion is added to cost (as in (i)); the proceeds of the renounced portion are taken to P&L (as in (ii)). |

**Exception:** Where the right shares are acquired on a **cum-right basis shortly after purchase** and the market price of the original shares falls below cost after the rights are declared (a diminution), the sale proceeds of the rights should first be applied to **reduce the carrying cost** to that extent, and only the balance is taken to P&L.

**Conclusion:** Bonus increases quantity but not cost; subscribed rights increase cost; and proceeds of renounced rights are ordinarily revenue income, subject to the diminution exception.

*(Full-marks tip: Naming the diminution exception for renounced rights lifts a routine answer to full marks. Do not confuse bonus (no cost) with rights (cost added when subscribed).)*

### Q75. Ch: Internal Reconstruction — Legal Framework & Methods (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between **internal reconstruction** and **external reconstruction**, and state the methods by which a company may carry out an internal reconstruction, referring to the relevant sections of the Companies Act, 2013.

**Answer:**
**Meaning.** **Internal reconstruction** is the re-arrangement of a company's capital structure — by reducing/altering share capital and, where required, the claims of creditors — **without liquidating the company or forming a new company**. **External reconstruction** is the winding-up of an existing company and the transfer of its business to a **newly formed company** (accounted for as an amalgamation in the nature of purchase).

| Basis | Internal Reconstruction | External Reconstruction |
|---|---|---|
| New company | Not formed | A new company is formed |
| Liquidation | Existing company continues | Existing (transferor) company is liquidated |
| Accounting | Capital Reduction / alteration entries | Amalgamation (AS 14) entries |

**Methods of internal reconstruction:**
1. **Alteration of share capital** under **Section 61** — consolidation, sub-division, conversion of shares into stock, and cancellation of unissued capital (no NCLT approval needed).
2. **Reduction of share capital** under **Section 66** — reducing the paid-up value of shares to write off accumulated losses/over-valued assets; requires a **special resolution and confirmation by the NCLT (Tribunal)**.
3. **Variation of shareholders' rights** (Section 48) and **compromise/arrangement with creditors and members** under **Sections 230–232**, whereby creditors and other stakeholders sacrifice a part of their claims.

**Conclusion:** Internal reconstruction reorganises the capital of a continuing company under Sections 61 and 66 (and Sections 230–232), whereas external reconstruction dissolves the company and vests its business in a new company.

*(Full-marks tip: Cite the exact sections — Sec 61 (alteration, no Tribunal), Sec 66 (reduction, needs Tribunal), Sec 230–232 (arrangements). Stating that reduction needs NCLT confirmation but alteration does not is a frequent examiner check.)*

### Q77. Ch: Internal Reconstruction — Treatment of Capital Reduction Balance & Expenses (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** In a scheme of internal reconstruction, explain the accounting treatment of (a) the **balance remaining in the Capital Reduction Account** after all write-offs, (b) a situation where the **write-offs exceed** the sacrifices available, and (c) **reconstruction expenses**. Also state the treatment of any **excess provision** (e.g., a contingent liability that later crystallises for a lower amount).

**Answer:**
**(a) Surplus in the Capital Reduction Account.** After using the sacrifices to write off the accumulated losses, fictitious assets and over-valued assets, any **credit balance left in the Capital Reduction (or Reconstruction) Account is a capital profit** and is transferred to a **Capital Reserve**, which is not available for distribution as dividend. This is because the surplus arises out of a capital re-arrangement, not from trading.

**(b) Write-offs exceeding sacrifices.** The scheme must be so drawn that the total sacrifices are **at least equal to** the losses and asset write-downs to be absorbed. If the debits would exceed the credits, the Capital Reduction Account would show a debit balance, which is **not permissible** — it would mean the scheme is inadequate. In practice, the shareholders/creditors are asked to sacrifice more, or a smaller amount of assets is written down, so that the account **at least balances or leaves a small credit**.

**(c) Reconstruction expenses.** Expenses incurred to give effect to the scheme (legal, professional, Tribunal costs) are **debited to the Capital Reduction Account** (being a cost of the reconstruction) and credited to Bank, since they are not a normal revenue expense of the continuing business.

**(d) Excess provision / crystallisation at a lower amount.** Where a liability provided in the scheme (e.g., a contingent liability or a penalty) is finally settled for a **lower amount**, the excess provision is a gain arising out of the scheme and is **credited to the Capital Reduction / Capital Reserve Account**, not to the Profit & Loss Account.

**Conclusion:** All gains and costs peculiar to the reconstruction pass through the Capital Reduction Account; the net surplus is a non-distributable Capital Reserve, and the scheme must be self-balancing.

*(Full-marks tip: Emphasise that the residual is a *non-distributable* Capital Reserve and that reconstruction expenses are a debit to Capital Reduction (not revenue). Mentioning that any excess provision released also goes to Capital Reserve shows depth.)*

### Q80. Ch: Amalgamation of Companies (AS 14) — Merger vs Purchase (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** State the **five conditions** which must ALL be satisfied for an amalgamation to be treated as an "amalgamation in the nature of merger" under **AS 14**, and distinguish the **Pooling of Interests Method** from the **Purchase Method**.

**Answer:**
**Conditions for amalgamation in the nature of merger (AS 14).** All the following five conditions must be satisfied:
1. **All the assets and liabilities** of the transferor company become, after amalgamation, the assets and liabilities of the transferee company.
2. **Shareholders holding not less than 90%** of the face value of the equity shares of the transferor company (other than shares already held by the transferee) become equity shareholders of the transferee company.
3. The consideration payable to those equity shareholders is discharged by the transferee company **wholly by the issue of its equity shares**, except that cash may be paid for fractional shares.
4. The **business** of the transferor company is intended to be **carried on** by the transferee company.
5. **No adjustment** is intended to be made to the book values of the assets and liabilities of the transferor company, except to ensure uniformity of accounting policies.

If any one condition is not met, the amalgamation is **in the nature of purchase**.

| Basis | Pooling of Interests Method (Merger) | Purchase Method (Purchase) |
|---|---|---|
| Recording of assets/liabilities | At **existing book values** | At **agreed / revalued values** |
| Reserves of transferor | **Carried forward** (identity preserved) | Only **statutory reserves** carried (via Amalgamation Adjustment A/c); others not carried |
| Difference on consideration | Adjusted against **reserves** | Recorded as **Goodwill** (if PC > net assets) or **Capital Reserve** (if net assets > PC) |
| Goodwill | Does not arise | May arise and is **amortised** (ordinarily over ≤ 5 years) |

**Conclusion:** A merger uses the pooling method (book values and full reserve carry-forward), whereas a purchase uses the purchase method (agreed values, goodwill/capital reserve, and only statutory reserves retained).

*(Full-marks tip: List ALL five conditions verbatim — the examiner allots a mark for the 90% and "wholly by equity shares" conditions specifically. The table earns the distinction marks.)*

### Q82. Ch: Amalgamation of Companies (AS 14) — Statutory Reserves & Amalgamation Adjustment Reserve (Marks: 5) [Application]

| Particulars | Dr (₹) | Cr (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| Amalgamation Adjustment Reserve A/c … Dr. / To Investment Allowance Reserve A/c / To Development Rebate Reserve A/c | 1,00,000 | 60,000 / 40,000 |

### Q84. Ch: Amalgamation of Companies (AS 14) — Consideration, Goodwill & Disclosures (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** With reference to **AS 14**, (a) define "consideration", (b) state the treatment of goodwill arising on an amalgamation in the nature of purchase, and (c) list the disclosures to be made for the first financial statements following an amalgamation.

**Answer:**
**(a) Consideration.** Under AS 14, "**consideration**" for an amalgamation means the **aggregate of the shares and other securities issued and the payment made in the form of cash or other assets by the transferee company to the shareholders of the transferor company**. It specifically **excludes** any amount paid to discharge the **debentures, creditors or other outside liabilities** of the transferor company, since those are settled directly and are not part of the consideration to shareholders.

**(b) Goodwill (purchase method).** Where the amount of consideration **exceeds** the value of the net assets acquired, the excess is recorded as **Goodwill**; where the net assets exceed the consideration, the difference is credited to **Capital Reserve**. Goodwill arising on amalgamation should be **amortised on a systematic basis over its useful life, ordinarily not exceeding five years**, unless a longer period can be justified.

**(c) Disclosures.** In the first financial statements after amalgamation, the following are disclosed for **both** methods: names and general nature of business of the amalgamating companies; the **effective date** of amalgamation; the **method of accounting** used (pooling of interests / purchase); and particulars of the scheme sanctioned. Additionally, for a **merger**: the description and number of shares issued and the amount of any difference adjusted against reserves; and for a **purchase**: the consideration and its components, and the amount of any **goodwill/capital reserve** together with its **amortisation/treatment**.

**Conclusion:** Consideration is confined to payments to shareholders; the excess over net assets is goodwill (amortised over ≤ 5 years) or, if net assets are higher, a capital reserve; and the prescribed method-specific disclosures must accompany the first post-amalgamation accounts.

*(Full-marks tip: Stress that consideration EXCLUDES payments to debenture holders/creditors, and quote the "amortise goodwill over a period not exceeding five years" rule — both are frequent one-mark checks.)*

### Q87. Ch: Branch Accounts — Integral vs Non-Integral Foreign Operation (AS 11) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between an **"integral foreign operation"** and a **"non-integral foreign operation"** as defined in **AS 11 (The Effects of Changes in Foreign Exchange Rates)**, and state how the translation method and the treatment of the exchange difference differ between the two.

**Answer:** As per **AS 11**, an **integral foreign operation (IFO)** is a foreign operation whose activities are an **integral part of the operations of the reporting enterprise** — it carries on business as though it were an extension of the head office (e.g. it only sells goods imported from the H.O. and remits the proceeds back). A **non-integral foreign operation (NFO)** is one that **accumulates cash and other monetary items, incurs expenses, generates income and arranges borrowings substantially in its local currency**, and thus operates with a significant degree of autonomy from the reporting enterprise.

The classification governs the translation method as follows:

| Basis | Integral Foreign Operation | Non-Integral Foreign Operation |
|---|---|---|
| **Governing method** | Treated as the reporting enterprise's own transactions (temporal method) | Net investment / closing rate method |
| **Monetary items** | Closing rate | Closing rate |
| **Non-monetary items at historical cost** (fixed assets, opening stock) | Historical (transaction-date) rate | Closing rate |
| **Income & expenses** | Actual / average rate | Actual / average rate |
| **Exchange difference** | Recognised in the **Statement of Profit & Loss** in the period it arises | Accumulated in a **Foreign Currency Translation Reserve (FCTR)** under equity |
| **Reserve on disposal** | Not applicable | FCTR is transferred to P&L when the net investment is disposed of |

A change in classification (e.g. from integral to non-integral) is applied **from the date of the change**, and the translated amount of a non-monetary item at that date is treated as its historical cost.

**Conclusion:** The essence is autonomy — an IFO's exchange differences hit the P&L immediately, whereas an NFO's differences are deferred in FCTR until disposal, because they do not affect the present cash flows of the reporting enterprise.

*(Full-marks tip: State the "substantially in its local currency" test verbatim and give the FCTR-on-disposal rule — examiners look for both the classification indicators and the differing P&L vs reserve treatment.)*

### Q89. Ch: Departmental Accounts — Basis of Apportionment of Common Expenses (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the **basis on which the following common (joint) expenses** should be apportioned among departments, and explain the principle governing selection of the basis. Give the basis for: (i) Rent, rates and taxes; (ii) Lighting and heating; (iii) Depreciation of plant; (iv) Works manager's salary; (v) Discount allowed and carriage outward; (vi) Provident fund contribution.

**Answer:** The guiding principle of departmental accounting is that a joint expense should be apportioned to departments on an **equitable basis that reflects the benefit derived by, or the burden created by, each department** — i.e., a **cause-and-effect (or ability-to-benefit) relationship** between the expense and the departments. Expenses that can be directly identified with a department are charged directly; only genuinely common expenses are apportioned.

| Common Expense | Equitable Basis of Apportionment |
|---|---|
| (i) Rent, rates and taxes | Floor **area / space occupied** by each department |
| (ii) Lighting and heating | Number of **light points** or floor area / metered consumption |
| (iii) Depreciation of plant | **Value of plant & machinery** used in each department |
| (iv) Works manager's salary | **Time devoted** to each department (or wages/output) |
| (v) Discount allowed & carriage outward | **Net sales** (turnover) of each department |
| (vi) Provident fund contribution | **Wages / salaries** of each department |

**Conclusion:** The basis chosen must bear a rational relationship to the incurrence of the expense; where no logical basis exists, the expense (e.g. general manager's salary, interest on capital, audit fees) is treated as a **general charge** and shown only in the **combined (general) Profit & Loss Account**, not apportioned.

*(Full-marks tip: Mention that expenses incapable of equitable apportionment are NOT split but debited to the general P&L — that distinction earns the last mark.)*

### Q93. Ch: Consolidated Financial Statements — Concepts under AS 21 (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** With reference to **AS 21 (Consolidated Financial Statements)**, answer the following: (a) When is an enterprise treated as a **subsidiary**? (b) State any two situations where a subsidiary is **excluded** from consolidation. (c) How is **goodwill / capital reserve** arising on consolidation determined and presented?

**Answer:**
**(a) Definition of a subsidiary:** Under **AS 21**, a **subsidiary** is an enterprise that is **controlled by another enterprise (the parent)**. **Control** means either (i) the **ownership, directly or indirectly through subsidiary(ies), of more than one-half of the voting power** of an enterprise, or (ii) **control of the composition of the board of directors** (in case of a company) or of the governing body (in case of any other enterprise) so as to obtain economic benefits from its activities.

**(b) Exclusion from consolidation:** A subsidiary is **excluded** from consolidation when:
1. **Control is intended to be temporary** because the subsidiary is acquired and held **exclusively with a view to its subsequent disposal in the near future**; or
2. The subsidiary **operates under severe long-term restrictions** which **significantly impair its ability to transfer funds** to the parent. In such a case the investment is accounted for as per **AS 13**. (Note: the erstwhile "dissimilar activities" ground is NOT a valid reason for exclusion.)

**(c) Goodwill / Capital Reserve on consolidation:** It is the **difference between the cost to the parent of its investment in the subsidiary and the parent's portion of equity (net assets) of the subsidiary at the date of investment**. An **excess of cost over the parent's share of net assets = Goodwill** (shown as an asset); a **deficit (parent's share of net assets over cost) = Capital Reserve**. Where the investment is acquired in stages, the comparison is made **stage by stage**. Goodwill is presented under non-current assets and Capital Reserve under reserves in the Consolidated Balance Sheet.

**Conclusion:** Consolidation is driven by the substance of **control**, and the goodwill/capital reserve captures the premium or bargain paid over the acquired net-asset share at the acquisition date.

*(Full-marks tip: Give BOTH limbs of "control" (voting power AND board composition) and note that dissimilar activities is not a ground for exclusion — that nuance is frequently tested.)*

### Q95. Ch: Accounting for LLP — Nature, Advantages & Distinction from Partnership (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** A **Limited Liability Partnership (LLP)** is often described as a **"hybrid of a company and a partnership."** In the light of the **Limited Liability Partnership Act, 2008**, explain this statement and distinguish an LLP from a traditional partnership firm on any four bases.

**Answer:** A **Limited Liability Partnership** governed by the **LLP Act, 2008** is a body corporate that combines the **organisational flexibility of a partnership** with the **limited liability and perpetual succession of a company** — hence the description "hybrid." Under **Section 3** of the Act, an LLP is a **body corporate** formed and incorporated under the Act, a **legal entity separate from its partners**, having **perpetual succession**; a change in partners does not affect its existence, rights or liabilities. The liability of each partner is **limited to his agreed contribution** (Section 26 onwards), except in cases of fraud.

| Basis | Traditional Partnership | Limited Liability Partnership |
|---|---|---|
| **Governing law** | Indian Partnership Act, 1932 | LLP Act, 2008 |
| **Legal status** | Not a separate legal entity | Separate legal entity / body corporate (Sec. 3) |
| **Liability of partners** | Unlimited, joint and several | Limited to agreed contribution (Sec. 26) |
| **Perpetual succession** | No — dissolved on death/insolvency of a partner | Yes — unaffected by change in partners |
| **Maximum partners** | 50 (as per Rules under Companies Act) | No maximum limit |
| **Registration** | Optional | Compulsory with the Registrar (Sec. 11–12) |

**Conclusion:** Because an LLP enjoys a separate legal personality, limited liability and perpetual succession — features drawn from company law — while retaining the internal governance freedom of a partnership agreement, it is aptly called a hybrid form of business organisation.

*(Full-marks tip: Cite Section 3 for separate legal entity/perpetual succession and Section 26 for limited liability — statutory references convert a good answer into a full-marks answer.)*

### Q98. Ch: Company Financial Statements — Schedule III Ratio Disclosures (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** Schedule III (Division I) requires disclosure of certain **financial ratios** in the Notes to Accounts. | Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Current Assets | 24,00,000 |
| Current Liabilities | 10,00,000 |

**WN-4 — Inventory Turnover Ratio** = Cost of Goods Sold ÷ Average Inventory = 42,00,000 ÷ 7,00,000 = **6 times**.

### Q99. Ch: Company Financial Statements — Schedule III Structure & Disclosures (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the following requirements of **Schedule III to the Companies Act, 2013 (Division I)**: (a) the **format** it prescribes and its overriding effect; (b) the classification of assets and liabilities into **current and non-current**; and (c) the rule on **rounding off** of figures.

**Answer:**
**(a) Format and overriding effect:** **Schedule III, Division I** prescribes the **format of the Balance Sheet (vertical form only)** and the **Statement of Profit and Loss** for companies (other than those to whom Ind AS or specific regulators apply). Where compliance with the requirements of the **Act, including the Accounting Standards**, requires any change in the disclosures, **the requirements of Schedule III stand modified accordingly** — i.e., the Accounting Standards and the Act **override** Schedule III in case of conflict. The disclosure requirements of Schedule III are **in addition to**, and not in substitution of, those of the Accounting Standards.

**(b) Current vs Non-current classification:** An asset is classified as **current** when it satisfies any of these: it is **expected to be realised in, or is intended for sale/consumption in, the company's normal operating cycle**; it is **held primarily for trading**; it is **expected to be realised within 12 months** after the reporting date; or it is **cash or a cash equivalent** not restricted. **All other assets are non-current.** A liability is **current** if it is expected to be settled in the normal operating cycle, is held for trading, is due within 12 months, or the company has no unconditional right to defer settlement beyond 12 months; **all others are non-current.** The **operating cycle** is the time between acquisition of assets for processing and their realisation in cash; where it cannot be identified, it is assumed to be **12 months**.

**(c) Rounding off:** Depending on **turnover**, figures may be rounded off — where turnover is **less than ₹100 crore**, to the **nearest hundreds, thousands, lakhs or millions** (or decimals thereof); where turnover is **₹100 crore or more**, to the **nearest lakhs, millions or crores** (or decimals thereof). Once a unit of measurement is adopted, it must be used **uniformly** in the financial statements.

**Conclusion:** Schedule III standardises presentation, mandates the current/non-current split around the operating cycle, and enforces consistent rounding — but always yields to the Accounting Standards and the Act.

*(Full-marks tip: State that Schedule III is subordinate to the Accounting Standards, give the four current-asset tests, and quote the turnover-based rounding thresholds (₹100 crore boundary) — each is a distinct mark.)*
