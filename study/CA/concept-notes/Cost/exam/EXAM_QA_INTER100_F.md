### Q1. Ch: Material Cost — JIT Purchasing and its Effect on Stock Levels (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the concept of "Just-in-Time (JIT) Purchasing." How does the adoption of a JIT purchasing system affect the various stock levels (Re-order level, Maximum level, Minimum level and average inventory) maintained by a manufacturing concern? State any two pre-conditions essential for JIT purchasing to succeed.

**Answer:**
Just-in-Time (JIT) purchasing is a system of buying materials in such a manner that they are received by the purchasing organisation only when they are actually needed for production or sale, and not before. Under this system the materials arrive in small lots, at frequent intervals, and are taken straight to the shop floor rather than into stores, so that raw-material and component inventories are reduced almost to nil.

The effect of JIT purchasing on the stock levels is explained below:

| Stock parameter | Effect under JIT purchasing | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| **Re-order level** (Max. consumption × Max. re-order period) | Falls sharply | The lead time (re-order period) is drastically compressed because the supplier delivers frequently and reliably, and safety allowance is minimised. |
| **Maximum level** | Falls sharply | Order quantities are small and frequent, so the peak inventory that can accumulate is very low. |
| **Minimum (safety) level** | Reduced towards nil | Reliable, frequent supply removes the need for a large buffer against stock-out. |
| **Average inventory** and carrying cost | Substantially reduced | Small lot sizes keep average holding low, cutting storage, insurance, interest and obsolescence costs. |

Thus JIT purchasing shifts the burden of holding stock from the buyer to a small number of dependable suppliers and slashes the buyer's investment locked up in inventory.

**Two pre-conditions essential for success:** (i) A few reliable, quality-assured suppliers located near the factory who can deliver defect-free material on time (so that no incoming inspection or buffer is needed); and (ii) A stable, predictable production schedule and long-term supply contracts (blanket orders) that let the supplier plan frequent small deliveries.

**Conclusion:** JIT purchasing pulls down the re-order, maximum and minimum levels and the average inventory, thereby minimising carrying cost, provided dependable suppliers and a smooth production plan are assured.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards a level-by-level statement of the EFFECT (not just a definition of JIT). Students commonly lose marks by describing JIT manufacturing generally and forgetting to link it specifically to each stock level and to carrying cost.)*

### Q2. Ch: Material Cost — Computation of Stock Levels (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** From the following data relating to component "BX-7", calculate (i) Re-order Level, (ii) Minimum Level, (iii) Maximum Level, (iv) Average Stock Level and (v) Danger Level.

| Particulars | Data |
|---|---|
| Re-order quantity | 4,000 units |
| Maximum consumption | 400 units per day |
| Minimum consumption | 200 units per day |
| Normal consumption | 300 units per day |
| Maximum re-order period | 12 days |
| Minimum re-order period | 6 days |
| Normal re-order period | 9 days |
| Emergency delivery period (for danger level) | 3 days |

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Re-order Level (ROL)**
ROL = Maximum consumption × Maximum re-order period = 400 × 12 = **4,800 units**

**WN-2 — Minimum Level**
Minimum Level = ROL − (Normal consumption × Normal re-order period)
= 4,800 − (300 × 9) = 4,800 − 2,700 = **2,100 units**

**WN-3 — Maximum Level**
Maximum Level = ROL + Re-order Quantity − (Minimum consumption × Minimum re-order period)
= 4,800 + 4,000 − (200 × 6) = 8,800 − 1,200 = **7,600 units**

**WN-4 — Average Stock Level**
Average Level = Minimum Level + ½ × Re-order Quantity = 2,100 + ½ × 4,000 = 2,100 + 2,000 = **4,100 units**
*(OR by the alternative formula: (Maximum Level + Minimum Level) ÷ 2 = (7,600 + 2,100) ÷ 2 = 4,850 units.)*

**WN-5 — Danger Level**
Danger Level = Normal (Average) consumption × Emergency delivery period = 300 × 3 = **900 units**

| Statement Showing Stock Levels of Component BX-7 | Units |
|---|---|
| Re-order Level | 4,800 |
| Minimum Level | 2,100 |
| Maximum Level | 7,600 |
| Average Stock Level | 4,100 |
| Danger Level | 900 |

**Answer:** ROL = 4,800 units; Minimum = 2,100 units; Maximum = 7,600 units; Average = 4,100 units; Danger = 900 units.

*(Full-marks tip: State each formula before substituting. A frequent deduction is using normal consumption in the Maximum-level formula — it requires MINIMUM consumption and MINIMUM period. Mention both methods of the average-stock level to secure the mark.)*

### Q3. Ch: Material Cost — EOQ vis-à-vis a JIT Delivery Proposal (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** A manufacturer consumes 1,20,000 units of a raw material evenly throughout the year. The purchase price is ₹50 per unit, the cost of placing an order is ₹300, and the annual carrying cost is ₹8 per unit. A supplier under a JIT arrangement offers to deliver the material in small daily lots, so that the buyer's ordering cost and inventory-carrying cost on this material are eliminated altogether; in return the supplier will charge ₹50.15 per unit. 

(i) Compute the Economic Order Quantity (EOQ) and the total annual relevant cost (ordering + carrying) under the existing system. (ii) Evaluate the JIT proposal and advise whether it should be accepted.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Economic Order Quantity**
EOQ = √(2 × A × O ÷ C), where A = 1,20,000 units, O = ₹300, C = ₹8
= √(2 × 1,20,000 × 300 ÷ 8) = √(7,20,00,000 ÷ 8) = √(90,00,000) = **3,000 units**

**WN-2 — Number of orders and relevant costs (existing system)**
Number of orders = 1,20,000 ÷ 3,000 = 40 orders
Ordering cost = 40 × ₹300 = ₹12,000
Carrying cost = (EOQ ÷ 2) × C = (3,000 ÷ 2) × ₹8 = 1,500 × ₹8 = ₹12,000

| Statement of Total Annual Relevant Cost — Existing System | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Ordering cost (40 orders × ₹300) | 12,000 |
| Carrying cost (1,500 units × ₹8) | 12,000 |
| **Total relevant (ordering + carrying) cost** | **24,000** |

**WN-3 — Extra purchase price payable under JIT**
Increase in price = ₹50.15 − ₹50.00 = ₹0.15 per unit
Extra purchase cost = 1,20,000 units × ₹0.15 = **₹18,000**

**WN-4 — Evaluation (Incremental analysis)**

| Statement Showing Comparative Cost | Existing (₹) | JIT (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| Ordering cost | 12,000 | Nil |
| Carrying cost | 12,000 | Nil |
| Extra price paid to supplier | — | 18,000 |
| **Total incremental cost** | **24,000** | **18,000** |

Net saving under JIT = ₹24,000 − ₹18,000 = **₹6,000 per annum**.

**Answer:** EOQ = 3,000 units; existing relevant cost = ₹24,000; the JIT proposal costs only ₹18,000, giving a net saving of ₹6,000 per annum. **The JIT proposal should be accepted** (subject to the supplier's reliability of quality and delivery).

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner looks for INCREMENTAL reasoning — only the ordering cost, carrying cost and the price differential are relevant; the base purchase cost of ₹50 is common to both and is correctly excluded. Compare like with like and give a clear recommendation.)*

### Q4. Ch: Material Cost — Treatment of Normal and Abnormal Loss of Material in Stores (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between "normal loss" and "abnormal loss" of material arising in stores, and state the cost-accounting treatment of each.

**Answer:**
Losses of material occurring while it is held in stores (through evaporation, shrinkage, breakage, spoilage, pilferage, etc.) are classified as normal or abnormal according to whether they are inherent and unavoidable or exceptional and avoidable.

| Basis | Normal Loss of Material | Abnormal Loss of Material |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Loss that is inherent in the nature of the material or the storage process and is unavoidable within acceptable limits (e.g., evaporation of a volatile liquid, natural shrinkage of grain). | Loss in excess of the normal, arising from an abnormal or avoidable cause (e.g., fire, flood, theft, careless handling, defective storage). |
| Controllability | Uncontrollable / expected | Controllable / avoidable |
| Cost treatment | The cost of the lost quantity is **absorbed by the good units remaining** in stock — i.e., the value of the balance is spread over the reduced quantity, raising the issue rate per unit. It is treated as part of material cost. | The cost of the abnormally lost quantity is **charged to the Costing Profit and Loss Account** (or Abnormal Loss Account). It is **not** allowed to inflate the cost of good units, so that product cost is not distorted. |
| Effect on issue rate | Increases the per-unit rate of the balance | No effect on the rate of good units |

**Conclusion:** Normal storage loss is inventoriable and is borne by the surviving good units, whereas abnormal storage loss is non-inventoriable and is written off to the Costing Profit and Loss Account so that it does not vitiate the cost of production.

*(Full-marks tip: The key distinction the examiner rewards is that normal loss RAISES the issue price of good units while abnormal loss goes to Costing P&L. Merely defining the two without stating the treatment loses half the marks.)*

### Q5. Ch: Material Cost — Stores Ledger (FIFO) with Return to Vendor and Normal Loss (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** Prepare the Stores Ledger Account of material "M-20" for March 2026 under the **First-In-First-Out (FIFO)** method from the following transactions. Note the treatment of the return to the vendor and of the normal storage loss.

| Date (Mar 2026) | Transaction |
|---|---|
| 1 | Opening balance 1,000 units @ ₹25 per unit |
| 5 | Purchased 2,000 units @ ₹26 per unit |
| 8 | Issued 1,500 units to production |
| 12 | Purchased 1,000 units @ ₹28 per unit |
| 15 | Returned 200 units to the vendor out of the purchase of 12 March (found defective) |
| 20 | Issued 1,800 units to production |
| 25 | Normal loss due to evaporation 50 units |

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Basis of valuation**
Under FIFO, issues are priced at the cost of the earliest lots in stock. A **return to the vendor** is a reversal of a specific purchase, hence it is valued at the price at which that lot was bought (₹28, from the 12-March purchase), and it is shown on the issue (credit) side. A **normal loss** carries no separate value; the quantity is written off but the total value of the balance is retained, so the rate of the surviving good units rises.

**WN-2 — Stores Ledger Account (FIFO)**

| Date | Receipts (Units × ₹ = ₹) | Issues / Returns (Units × ₹ = ₹) | Balance (Units, ₹) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mar 1 | — | — | 1,000 @ 25 = 25,000 |
| Mar 5 | 2,000 × 26 = 52,000 | — | 1,000 @ 25 + 2,000 @ 26 = 77,000 |
| Mar 8 | — | 1,000 × 25 = 25,000; 500 × 26 = 13,000 (Total 1,500 = 38,000) | 1,500 @ 26 = 39,000 |
| Mar 12 | 1,000 × 28 = 28,000 | — | 1,500 @ 26 + 1,000 @ 28 = 67,000 |
| Mar 15 | — | Return to vendor 200 × 28 = 5,600 | 1,500 @ 26 + 800 @ 28 = 61,400 |
| Mar 20 | — | 1,500 × 26 = 39,000; 300 × 28 = 8,400 (Total 1,800 = 47,400) | 500 @ 28 = 14,000 |
| Mar 25 | — | Normal loss 50 units (value nil) | 450 units = 14,000 (revised rate ₹31.11) |

**WN-3 — Revised rate after normal loss (25 March)**
Balance before loss = 500 units, value ₹14,000. After writing off 50 units (normal), value unchanged at ₹14,000, quantity 450 units.
Revised rate = ₹14,000 ÷ 450 = **₹31.11 per unit**. The cost of the 50 lost units is thus absorbed by the 450 good units.

**Answer:** Closing stock as on 31 March 2026 = **450 units valued at ₹14,000** (₹31.11 per unit). The return of 200 units to the vendor is credited at ₹28 (₹5,600); the 50-unit normal loss is absorbed by the good units, lifting the rate from ₹28 to ₹31.11.

*(Full-marks tip: Two things earn the marks here — valuing the vendor return at the SPECIFIC lot price (₹28), not at a FIFO issue price; and NOT valuing the normal loss separately but re-computing the rate on the balance. Charging the normal loss to P&L would be wrong.)*

### Q6. Ch: Material Cost — Abnormal Loss of Material (Fire) (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** On 28 March 2026, a fire in the store of ABC Ltd. destroyed 300 units of material "M-20". Immediately before the fire the stock stood at 500 units valued at ₹28 per unit (total ₹14,000). The insurance company admitted a claim of ₹6,000. Show how the loss will be dealt with in cost accounts and state the value of the closing stock. Also state the treatment had the loss been a normal one.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Nature of the loss**
A fire is an abnormal, avoidable event; the loss of 300 units is therefore an **abnormal loss**. Its cost must be charged to the Costing Profit and Loss Account and must NOT be loaded on to the remaining good units.

**WN-2 — Value of the abnormal loss**
Value of 300 units destroyed = 300 × ₹28 = **₹8,400**

**WN-3 — Net loss to be written off**
| Statement of Abnormal Loss | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Cost of 300 units destroyed (300 × ₹28) | 8,400 |
| Less: Insurance claim admitted | 6,000 |
| **Net abnormal loss charged to Costing P&L A/c** | **2,400** |

**WN-4 — Closing stock**
Units remaining = 500 − 300 = 200 units; rate unchanged at ₹28 (abnormal loss does not affect the rate of good units).
Closing stock value = 200 × ₹28 = **₹5,600**.

**Answer:** The abnormal loss of ₹8,400 is removed from stores at cost; ₹6,000 is recovered from insurance and the net ₹2,400 is charged to the Costing Profit and Loss Account. Closing stock = 200 units @ ₹28 = ₹5,600.
*(Contrast: had the 300 units been a NORMAL loss, no amount would go to P&L; the ₹14,000 would simply be spread over the surviving 200 units, giving a revised rate of ₹70 per unit.)*

*(Full-marks tip: Show the loss at COST, deduct any insurance recovery, and route the net to Costing P&L. The examiner specifically rewards the note that abnormal loss leaves the good-unit rate unchanged, unlike normal loss.)*

### Q7. Ch: Material Cost — Consumption and Inventory Turnover (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** From the following information relating to material "P" for the year ended 31 March 2026, compute (i) the cost of materials consumed, (ii) the Inventory (Raw-material) Turnover Ratio, and (iii) the average number of days for which the stock was held. Comment briefly on the result. Assume 365 days in the year.

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Opening stock of material P | 90,000 |
| Purchases during the year | 6,00,000 |
| Closing stock of material P | 1,10,000 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Cost of materials consumed**
Consumption = Opening stock + Purchases − Closing stock
= 90,000 + 6,00,000 − 1,10,000 = **₹5,80,000**

**WN-2 — Average stock held**
Average stock = (Opening stock + Closing stock) ÷ 2 = (90,000 + 1,10,000) ÷ 2 = **₹1,00,000**

**WN-3 — Inventory Turnover Ratio**
Inventory Turnover Ratio = Cost of materials consumed ÷ Average stock
= ₹5,80,000 ÷ ₹1,00,000 = **5.8 times**

**WN-4 — Average holding period**
Average number of days stock held = 365 ÷ Inventory Turnover Ratio
= 365 ÷ 5.8 = **62.93 days ≈ 63 days**

| Statement Showing Inventory Turnover of Material P | Value |
|---|---|
| Cost of materials consumed | ₹5,80,000 |
| Average stock | ₹1,00,000 |
| Inventory Turnover Ratio | 5.8 times |
| Average holding period | ≈ 63 days |

**Answer:** Consumption = ₹5,80,000; Inventory Turnover Ratio = 5.8 times; average holding period ≈ 63 days.
**Comment:** A turnover of 5.8 times (about a two-month holding) indicates a moderately fast-moving material; a rising ratio would signal more efficient use of working capital, whereas a falling ratio would warn of over-stocking or slow-moving/obsolete stock.

*(Full-marks tip: Use cost of CONSUMPTION (not purchases or sales) in the numerator and AVERAGE stock in the denominator. A one-line interpretation of what a high/low ratio signifies secures the comment mark.)*

### Q8. Ch: Material Cost — Treatment of Specific Items in Material Cost (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State, with reasons, the treatment in cost accounts of the following in arriving at the cost of materials purchased: (i) Cash discount received, (ii) Trade discount, (iii) Cost of containers (returnable and non-returnable), and (iv) GST paid on purchases where input tax credit is available.

**Answer:**
The cost of material purchased is the invoice price adjusted for trade discounts and directly attributable acquisition costs, in accordance with the principle that only costs incurred to bring the material to its present location and condition form part of its cost.

| Item | Treatment in Cost Accounts | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| (i) **Cash discount received** | **Excluded** from material cost; treated as a financial item (credited to Costing P&L / income). | It is a reward for prompt payment — a matter of financial management, not of material acquisition. |
| (ii) **Trade discount** | **Deducted** from the purchase price; material is recorded net of trade discount. | It is a reduction in the purchase price itself, so the true cost of the material is the net figure. |
| (iii) **Cost of containers** | Non-returnable containers → **added** to material cost. Returnable containers → charged at the difference between the price charged and the refund on return; the recoverable part is **not** included. | Non-returnable containers are an unavoidable part of acquiring the material; only the non-recoverable portion of returnable containers is a real cost. |
| (iv) **GST paid (input tax credit available)** | **Excluded** from material cost. | Where ITC can be set off against output tax, GST is not a cost to the business; only when credit is NOT available is it added to material cost. |

**Conclusion:** Trade discount and recoverable GST/container charges are removed to reach the true acquisition cost, whereas cash discount is a financial adjustment kept out of material cost.

*(Full-marks tip: The distinction between TRADE discount (deducted from cost) and CASH discount (a finance item excluded) is the classic testing point; also state that GST is added to cost only when input credit is unavailable.)*

### Q9. Ch: Employee (Labour) Cost — Halsey vs Rowan Premium Bonus Plans (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between the Halsey Premium Plan and the Rowan Premium Plan of wage payment. State the circumstance (in terms of the proportion of time saved to time allowed) under which a worker earns more under each plan.

**Answer:**
Both the Halsey and Rowan plans are incentive schemes that guarantee the time wage and, in addition, pay the worker a bonus for the time saved against the standard (allowed) time; they differ only in the manner of computing the bonus.

| Basis | Halsey Premium Plan | Rowan Premium Plan |
|---|---|---|
| Guaranteed wage | Time rate is guaranteed (day wage assured even if no time is saved). | Time rate is guaranteed. |
| Bonus formula | Bonus = 50% (usually) × Time saved × Rate per hour. | Bonus = (Time saved ÷ Time allowed) × Time taken × Rate per hour. |
| Total earnings | (Time taken × Rate) + 50% (Time saved × Rate). | (Time taken × Rate) + (Time saved ÷ Time allowed) × Time taken × Rate. |
| Sharing of benefit | Employer and worker share the value of time saved in a fixed ratio (50 : 50). | Bonus is a proportion of wages; the worker's share rises then falls as efficiency increases. |
| Effect of over-speeding | Encourages very high speed (bonus grows without limit with time saved). | Automatically checks over-speeding, since bonus rises up to 50% time saving and then declines, safeguarding quality. |

**Comparison of earnings:**
- When **time saved is less than 50%** of time allowed → the worker earns **more under Rowan**.
- When **time saved equals exactly 50%** of time allowed → earnings are **equal** under both plans.
- When **time saved is more than 50%** of time allowed → the worker earns **more under Halsey**.

**Conclusion:** Halsey rewards high speed without limit and is simpler, while Rowan gives a larger bonus at moderate efficiency and automatically restrains over-speeding; up to 50% time saving Rowan pays more, beyond it Halsey pays more.

*(Full-marks tip: State the exact bonus formulae AND the "less-than / equal-to / more-than 50%" rule — that rule is the single most-tested point and is worth the concluding marks.)*

### Q10. Ch: Employee (Labour) Cost — Halsey vs Rowan Earnings at Different Efficiency Levels (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** The standard time allowed for a job is **40 hours** and the wage rate is **₹50 per hour**. Under the Halsey Plan a bonus of 50% of the time saved is paid. Compute the total earnings and the effective hourly rate of a worker under (a) the Halsey Plan and (b) the Rowan Plan for **each** of the following three cases, and comment on the comparison in each case.

| Case | Actual time taken | Time saved |
|---|---|---|
| A | 32 hours | 8 hours |
| B | 20 hours | 20 hours |
| C | 15 hours | 25 hours |

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Formulae**
Halsey earnings = (Time taken × Rate) + 50% × (Time saved × Rate)
Rowan earnings = (Time taken × Rate) + (Time saved ÷ Time allowed) × Time taken × Rate
Effective hourly rate = Total earnings ÷ Time taken. Time allowed = 40 hours; Rate = ₹50.

**WN-2 — Case A (Time taken 32 hrs, saved 8 hrs; time saved = 20% of 40 hrs)**
Halsey = (32 × 50) + 0.5 × (8 × 50) = 1,600 + 200 = **₹1,800**; effective rate = 1,800 ÷ 32 = **₹56.25**
Rowan = (32 × 50) + (8 ÷ 40) × 32 × 50 = 1,600 + 0.20 × 1,600 = 1,600 + 320 = **₹1,920**; effective rate = 1,920 ÷ 32 = **₹60.00**

**WN-3 — Case B (Time taken 20 hrs, saved 20 hrs; time saved = 50% of 40 hrs)**
Halsey = (20 × 50) + 0.5 × (20 × 50) = 1,000 + 500 = **₹1,500**; effective rate = 1,500 ÷ 20 = **₹75.00**
Rowan = (20 × 50) + (20 ÷ 40) × 20 × 50 = 1,000 + 0.50 × 1,000 = 1,000 + 500 = **₹1,500**; effective rate = 1,500 ÷ 20 = **₹75.00**

**WN-4 — Case C (Time taken 15 hrs, saved 25 hrs; time saved = 62.5% of 40 hrs)**
Halsey = (15 × 50) + 0.5 × (25 × 50) = 750 + 625 = **₹1,375**; effective rate = 1,375 ÷ 15 = **₹91.67**
Rowan = (15 × 50) + (25 ÷ 40) × 15 × 50 = 750 + 0.625 × 750 = 750 + 468.75 = **₹1,218.75**; effective rate = 1,218.75 ÷ 15 = **₹81.25**

**WN-5 — Comparative Statement**

| Statement Showing Comparative Earnings | Case A (20% saved) | Case B (50% saved) | Case C (62.5% saved) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Halsey earnings (₹) | 1,800.00 | 1,500.00 | 1,375.00 |
| Rowan earnings (₹) | 1,920.00 | 1,500.00 | 1,218.75 |
| Higher plan | Rowan | Equal | Halsey |

**Answer / Comment:** The results confirm the general rule — when time saved is **below 50%** of time allowed (Case A) Rowan pays more (₹1,920 vs ₹1,800); when time saved is **exactly 50%** (Case B) both pay the same (₹1,500); and when time saved is **above 50%** (Case C) Halsey pays more (₹1,375 vs ₹1,218.75).

*(Full-marks tip: Present all three cases in one comparative table and expressly link each to the 50%-of-time-allowed benchmark. Marks are lost when students compute the numbers correctly but omit the concluding comparison, which is what the question is really testing.)*

### Q11. Ch: Employee (Labour) Cost — Piece Rate with a Guaranteed Day-Rate Floor (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** In a factory the workers are paid at a straight piece rate of **₹6 per unit**, subject to a **guaranteed daily wage of ₹500** for a standard 8-hour day (i.e., an hourly time rate of ₹62.50). During a particular day the output of three workers was:

| Worker | Units produced in the day |
|---|---|
| A | 70 units |
| B | 84 units |
| C | 95 units |

Compute the daily earnings and the effective rate per unit of each worker, showing the effect of the guaranteed-wage floor.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Rule of payment**
The worker is paid the HIGHER of (a) piece-rate earnings (₹6 × units) and (b) the guaranteed day wage of ₹500. The floor bites only when piece-rate earnings fall below ₹500, i.e., below 500 ÷ 6 = 83.33 ≈ 84 units.

**WN-2 — Piece-rate earnings and comparison**

| Worker | Piece-rate earnings (Units × ₹6) | Guaranteed floor | Wage payable (higher) | Effective rate per unit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 70 × 6 = ₹420 | ₹500 | **₹500** (floor applies) | 500 ÷ 70 = ₹7.14 |
| B | 84 × 6 = ₹504 | ₹500 | **₹504** (piece rate) | 504 ÷ 84 = ₹6.00 |
| C | 95 × 6 = ₹570 | ₹500 | **₹570** (piece rate) | 570 ÷ 95 = ₹6.00 |

**WN-3 — Effect of the floor**
Worker A, whose piece output (70 units) yields only ₹420, is lifted to the guaranteed ₹500; the extra ₹80 (₹500 − ₹420) is the cost of the guarantee, and A's effective labour cost per unit rises to ₹7.14. Workers B and C, producing at or above the break-even of ≈84 units, are governed by the piece rate and cost the firm exactly ₹6 per unit.

**Answer:** A earns ₹500 (floor; ₹7.14 per unit); B earns ₹504 (₹6 per unit); C earns ₹570 (₹6 per unit). The guaranteed floor protects the low-output worker but raises the per-unit labour cost for that worker.

*(Full-marks tip: Identify the break-even output (guaranteed wage ÷ piece rate) and show that the floor only binds below it. A common error is paying A the piece rate of ₹420 — the guarantee must be honoured. Showing the effective per-unit rate demonstrates the cost impact.)*

### Q12. Ch: Employee (Labour) Cost — Cost of Labour Turnover including Intangible Losses (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** "The cost of labour turnover comprises not only the visible preventive and replacement costs but also several intangible losses." Explain the meaning of labour turnover and enumerate the various costs (including the intangible losses) that make up the cost of labour turnover.

**Answer:**
Labour turnover means the rate of change in the composition of the labour force of an organisation during a period — that is, the movement of workers into and out of the organisation through separations, replacements and fresh recruitment. A high labour turnover disrupts production and imposes costs, which are broadly grouped into preventive costs and replacement costs, and within replacement costs there are both tangible and intangible elements.

**(A) Preventive costs** — costs incurred to keep workers contented and reduce the desire to leave: cost of personnel and welfare administration, medical services, safety and welfare measures, provident-fund/pension and other retirement benefits, and cost of a sound wage and incentive policy.

**(B) Replacement costs** — costs arising because workers actually leave and have to be replaced:
- *Tangible / measurable:* cost of recruitment, selection and placement; cost of training and induction of new workers; cost of tools and machines broken by, and defective work of, inexperienced new workers; cost of extra scrap and wastage.
- *Intangible / hidden losses:*
  - Loss of output (and hence loss of contribution/profit) during the interval between separation and the new worker attaining full efficiency;
  - Loss arising from the lower productivity and higher spoilage of raw material during the training period;
  - Loss of goodwill and delayed deliveries owing to interrupted production;
  - Increased accident and machine-breakdown costs caused by the inexperience of replacements.

### Q15. Ch: Employee (Labour) Cost — Taylor's Differential Piece-Rate System (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** Under Taylor's Differential Piece-Rate System the standard output is **200 units per day** and the normal piece rate is **₹2 per unit**. Low piece rate = 80% × ₹2 = ₹1.60 per unit (applied when output is below the standard of 200 units).
High piece rate = 120% × ₹2 = ₹2.40 per unit (applied when output is at or above 200 units).

### Q16. Ch: Employee (Labour) Cost — Treatment of Idle Time and Overtime Premium (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the treatment in cost accounts of (i) normal idle time, (ii) abnormal idle time, and (iii) overtime premium.

**Answer:**
*Idle time* is the time for which wages are paid to workers but no direct production is obtained; *overtime premium* is the extra amount paid over the normal rate for work done beyond normal hours. Their treatment depends on whether the cause is normal (inherent) or abnormal (avoidable), and on the reason for the overtime.

| Item | Treatment in Cost Accounts | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| (i) **Normal idle time** | The wages for normal idle time are treated as part of the cost of production — either by inflating the direct labour rate or by charging it as a factory overhead. | It is unavoidable (e.g., time between jobs, tea breaks, machine set-up) and inherent in the work, so it is a legitimate production cost. |
| (ii) **Abnormal idle time** | The wages for abnormal idle time are **excluded** from cost and charged to the **Costing Profit and Loss Account**. | It arises from avoidable causes (e.g., strike, machine breakdown, power failure, shortage of material); loading it on production would distort product cost. |
| (iii) **Overtime premium** | If overtime is at the customer's request → charged to that **job**. If due to a general pressure of work / seasonal demand → treated as **factory overhead**. If due to abnormal causes (e.g., making good a loss) → charged to **Costing P&L**. The normal-rate portion of overtime wages is always a direct labour cost. | The premium is treated according to the reason it was incurred, so that only genuinely productive costs attach to output. |

**Conclusion:** Normal idle time and demand-driven overtime premium are absorbed as production cost, whereas abnormal idle time and abnormal-cause overtime premium are written off to the Costing Profit and Loss Account.

*(Full-marks tip: For overtime premium, the three-way split by CAUSE (customer's request → job; general → overhead; abnormal → P&L) is the examiner's key point. Do not forget that the ordinary-rate element of overtime is normal direct wages.)*

### Q17. Ch: Employee (Labour) Cost — Integrated Incentive Comparison with a Guaranteed Weekly Floor (Marks: 10) [Application]
**Question:** The standard time allowed for a job is **48 hours** and the wage rate is **₹30 per hour**. The factory guarantees each worker a **weekly wage of ₹1,200** (equivalent to the time wage for a 40-hour normal week). Two workers completed the job in the following times during a week:

| Worker | Actual time taken |
|---|---|
| P | 36 hours |
| Q | 44 hours |

For **each** worker compute the total weekly earnings and the labour cost per hour (effective rate) under (i) the Halsey Plan (50% bonus), (ii) the Rowan Plan, and (iii) a **guaranteed time-wage basis** (subject to the weekly floor of ₹1,200). Then advise which plan the employer should prefer for the more efficient worker P, and state which plan protects the slower worker Q.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Basic data and formulae**
Time allowed = 48 hours; Rate = ₹30/hour; Guaranteed weekly floor = ₹1,200.
Time saved: Worker P = 48 − 36 = 12 hours; Worker Q = 48 − 44 = 4 hours.
Halsey earnings = (Time taken × Rate) + 50% × (Time saved × Rate).
Rowan earnings = (Time taken × Rate) + (Time saved ÷ Time allowed) × Time taken × Rate.
Guaranteed time wage = Time taken × Rate, but not less than ₹1,200 (the weekly floor).

**WN-2 — Worker P (Time taken 36 hrs, time saved 12 hrs = 25% of 48 hrs)**
- Halsey = (36 × 30) + 0.5 × (12 × 30) = 1,080 + 180 = **₹1,260**; labour cost per hour = 1,260 ÷ 36 = **₹35.00**
- Rowan = (36 × 30) + (12 ÷ 48) × 36 × 30 = 1,080 + 0.25 × 1,080 = 1,080 + 270 = **₹1,350**; per hour = 1,350 ÷ 36 = **₹37.50**
- Guaranteed time wage = 36 × 30 = ₹1,080; but the weekly floor of ₹1,200 applies, so P is paid **₹1,200**; per hour = 1,200 ÷ 36 = **₹33.33**

**WN-3 — Worker Q (Time taken 44 hrs, time saved 4 hrs = 8.33% of 48 hrs)**
- Halsey = (44 × 30) + 0.5 × (4 × 30) = 1,320 + 60 = **₹1,380**; per hour = 1,380 ÷ 44 = **₹31.36**
- Rowan = (44 × 30) + (4 ÷ 48) × 44 × 30 = 1,320 + 0.0833 × 1,320 = 1,320 + 110 = **₹1,430**; per hour = 1,430 ÷ 44 = **₹32.50**
- Guaranteed time wage = 44 × 30 = ₹1,320 (above the ₹1,200 floor, so the floor does not bind); paid **₹1,320**; per hour = 1,320 ÷ 44 = **₹30.00**

**WN-4 — Comparative Statement of Earnings and Labour Cost per Hour**

| Statement Showing Earnings under each Plan | Worker P (₹) | Cost/hr (₹) | Worker Q (₹) | Cost/hr (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Halsey Plan | 1,260 | 35.00 | 1,380 | 31.36 |
| Rowan Plan | 1,350 | 37.50 | 1,430 | 32.50 |
| Guaranteed time wage (floor ₹1,200) | 1,200 | 33.33 | 1,320 | 30.00 |

**WN-5 — Advice**
For both workers the time saved is **less than 50%** of time allowed (P = 25%, Q = 8.33%), so Rowan gives the higher wage and hence the higher labour cost per hour to the employer. From the **employer's cost angle for the efficient worker P**, the Halsey Plan is cheaper (₹1,260, ₹35/hr) than Rowan (₹1,350, ₹37.50/hr) while still rewarding effort, so **the employer should prefer the Halsey Plan for P**. The **guaranteed time-wage floor of ₹1,200 protects the slower worker** — it becomes relevant for P only on the pure time-wage basis (lifting ₹1,080 to ₹1,200); Q, taking 44 hours, already earns above the floor on every basis, so the floor guarantees a worker will never be paid below ₹1,200 however low the output.

**Answer:** Worker P — Halsey ₹1,260, Rowan ₹1,350, guaranteed ₹1,200. Worker Q — Halsey ₹1,380, Rowan ₹1,430, guaranteed ₹1,320. Since time saved is below 50% in both cases, Rowan pays more; the employer should adopt the **Halsey Plan for the efficient worker P** on cost grounds, and the **₹1,200 weekly floor safeguards low-output workers**.

*(Full-marks tip: This integrates three schemes plus a floor — the examiner rewards (a) correct application of the guarantee (paying P ₹1,200, not ₹1,080), (b) the observation that with <50% time saving Rowan always exceeds Halsey, and (c) a reasoned employer recommendation. Show the labour cost per hour, not just total earnings.)*

### Q19. Ch: Overheads – Absorption Costing — Treatment of Research & Development and Administration overhead (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the accounting treatment of (a) Research and Development costs and (b) Administration overheads in Cost Accounting.

**Answer:**

**(a) Treatment of Research and Development (R&D) costs:** Research cost is the cost of searching for new or improved products, applications of materials or methods; development cost is the cost of the process which begins with the implementation of the decision to produce the new or improved product. The treatment depends on the *nature and purpose* of the expenditure:

1. **Research of a general or basic nature** (not directed towards any particular product or process) is treated as a general overhead and either apportioned to production or, being of a fixed policy nature, transferred to the Costing Profit and Loss Account of the period in which it is incurred.
2. **Research relating to an existing product or method** is charged to the cost of that product or process as a production overhead, since it improves current operations.
3. **Research and development relating to a new product or process** is, if it is expected to yield benefits over future periods, treated as a **deferred cost** and charged (amortised) to the future production of that new product; if the project is abandoned or its results are not commercially usable, the accumulated cost is written off to the Costing Profit and Loss Account.
4. Development cost incurred in **improving processes/plant of a capital nature** may be capitalised and recovered through depreciation.

**(b) Treatment of Administration overheads:** Administration overhead is the cost of formulating policy, directing the organisation and controlling the operations of an undertaking, which is not directly related to production, selling or distribution. There are three recognised methods of treatment:

| Method | Description |
|---|---|
| (i) Apportion to Production and Selling & Distribution | Administration is regarded as ancillary to production and sales; its cost is apportioned between the two functions on a suitable basis and absorbed as part of works cost and cost of sales respectively. |
| (ii) Charge to Costing Profit and Loss Account | Administration is treated as a distinct policy cost that cannot be logically apportioned; the whole amount is written off to the Costing Profit and Loss Account of the period. |
| (iii) Treat as a separate addition to Cost of Production | Administration overhead is recovered as an independent element and added after works cost to arrive at the cost of production. This is the **most commonly adopted** method. |

**Conclusion:** R&D cost is charged according to whether it relates to existing operations (revenue/production overhead) or to new products of enduring benefit (deferred/capitalised), while administration overhead is most commonly recovered as a separate addition to cost of production.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner looks for the *classification-by-purpose* logic of R&D and for all three named methods of administration overhead. Deduction is common where students give only one treatment or confuse administration overhead with selling & distribution overhead.)*

| Particulars | Machining (A) | Assembly (B) | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Budgeted production overhead (₹) | 2,40,000 | 1,60,000 | 4,00,000 |
| Budgeted machine hours | 40,000 | 10,000 | 50,000 |

| Machine hours required | Dept A | Dept B |
|---|---|---|
| Job X | 5 | 15 |
| Job Y | 18 | 2 |

|---|---|---|

x = 20,600 + 0.20y … (i)

### Q24. Ch: Overheads – Absorption Costing — Allocation vs Apportionment; bases of apportionment (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between "Allocation" and "Apportionment" of overheads and state a suitable basis of apportionment for any three items of overhead.

**Answer:** **Allocation** of overhead is the process of *charging the whole amount* of an item of overhead to a particular cost centre or cost unit to which it is directly and wholly identifiable. **Apportionment** is the process of *distributing an item of overhead over several* cost centres on an equitable basis, because the item is common and cannot be identified wholly with any one cost centre.

| Basis of distinction | Allocation | Apportionment |
|---|---|---|
| Nature of cost | Directly identifiable with one cost centre | Common to two or more cost centres |
| Amount charged | Whole/entire amount | A proportion, on a suitable basis |
| Basis needed | No basis required (direct) | Requires an equitable basis |
| Example | Salary of a departmental foreman to his department | Factory rent shared among departments |

**Suitable bases of apportionment (any three):**

| Item of overhead | Basis of apportionment |
|---|---|
| Rent, rates, building depreciation, lighting | Floor area (or volume) occupied |
| Power, machine depreciation, machine insurance | Machine hours or capital value of machinery |
| Personnel/canteen/supervision, ESI, labour welfare | Number of employees or direct labour hours |

**Conclusion:** Allocation charges an entire identifiable overhead to one cost centre, whereas apportionment spreads a common overhead over several cost centres on an equitable basis appropriate to the nature of the cost.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner wants the sharp "whole amount vs proportionate/equitable basis" distinction plus correctly matched bases. Marks are lost for giving mismatched bases, e.g., apportioning power on floor area.)*

### Q26. Ch: Activity Based Costing — Cost of unused (idle) capacity under ABC (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** Apex Ltd. operates a set-up activity whose budgeted cost at **practical capacity** of 3,000 set-up hours is ₹6,00,000. During the year the activity was actually used only to the extent of 2,400 set-up hours. You are required to (i) compute the cost driver rate based on practical capacity, (ii) determine the cost of capacity used and the cost of unused capacity, and (iii) explain how the cost of unused capacity should be reported.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Cost driver rate at practical capacity**
Rate = Budgeted activity cost ÷ Practical capacity = ₹6,00,000 ÷ 3,000 set-up hours = **₹200 per set-up hour**

*Note:* Under ABC the cost driver rate is deliberately computed on **practical capacity** (not on budgeted/actual usage), so that products bear only the cost of resources they actually use and the cost of idle resources is isolated for management attention.

**WN-2: Cost of capacity used and unused**

| Particulars | Set-up hours | Rate (₹) | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cost of capacity **used** (charged to products) | 2,400 | 200 | 4,80,000 |
| Cost of **unused** capacity | 600 | 200 | 1,20,000 |
| **Total cost of resources supplied** | 3,000 | 200 | 6,00,000 |

**(iii) Reporting the cost of unused capacity:** The ₹1,20,000 relating to 600 idle set-up hours represents the cost of resources *supplied but not used*. It should **not** be loaded on to the products (doing so would inflate product cost through no fault of the products and could trigger a "death spiral" of rising prices and falling demand). Instead it is highlighted separately as **cost of unused capacity** and written off to the Costing Profit and Loss Account, or reported to the manager responsible for acquiring that capacity, so that management can act to either utilise or reduce the surplus capacity.

**Answer:** Rate = **₹200 per set-up hour**; cost of capacity used = **₹4,80,000** (charged to products) and cost of unused capacity = **₹1,20,000** (reported separately / written off to Costing P&L).

*(Full-marks tip: The key insight — and the mark-earning point — is that the driver rate must be based on *practical capacity* and that unused-capacity cost is isolated, not spread over products. Students who use actual hours (2,400) as the denominator lose the central mark.)*

### Q27. Ch: Activity Based Costing — Cost hierarchy / levels of activities (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the "cost hierarchy" used in Activity Based Costing, giving an example of each level.

**Answer:** In Activity Based Costing, activities and their costs are classified into a hierarchy of four levels based on the *level at which the cost is caused* and the driver that best measures it. This classification helps in selecting an appropriate cost driver and in identifying which costs vary with which decisions.

| Level | Description | Suitable cost driver | Example of cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| (i) Unit-level activities | Performed each time a unit is produced; cost varies with the number of units | Units, machine hours, labour hours | Power, direct machining, consumables per unit |
| (ii) Batch-level activities | Performed each time a batch is produced, irrespective of the number of units in the batch | Number of batches / set-ups / orders | Machine set-up cost, material handling, first-item inspection per batch |
| (iii) Product (product-sustaining) level activities | Performed to enable the production and sale of a particular product line, independent of units or batches | Number of products / product changes | Product design, maintaining product specifications, special testing |
| (iv) Facility (facility-sustaining) level activities | Undertaken to sustain the manufacturing facility as a whole; not caused by any product | Cannot be sensibly assigned; treated as period cost | Factory rent, general administration, plant security, lighting of the building |

**Conclusion:** Unit-level and batch-level costs are readily traceable through their drivers; product-level costs are assigned to product lines; facility-level costs, having no meaningful driver, are commonly treated as period costs and not allocated to units — this hierarchy is the foundation of accurate ABC costing.

*(Full-marks tip: Name all four levels with a driver and an example each. The examiner especially checks that facility-level cost is described as not sensibly assignable to units. Confusing batch-level (set-ups) with unit-level (power) costs a mark.)*

### Q29. Ch: Activity Based Costing — Requirements, benefits and limitations of ABC (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the circumstances in which Activity Based Costing is particularly useful and briefly mention two of its limitations.

**Answer:** Activity Based Costing (ABC) is a costing methodology that identifies the activities performed in an organisation, assigns the cost of resources to those activities through resource cost drivers, and then assigns activity costs to products/services through activity cost drivers, on the principle that *activities cause cost and products consume activities*.

**Circumstances in which ABC is particularly useful:**
1. Where **overheads form a high proportion** of total cost, so that a broad averaging base would materially distort product cost.
2. Where the firm produces a **wide range of products** differing in volume, complexity and batch size, causing volume-based rates to cross-subsidise between products.
3. Where **product diversity** creates diverse demands on support activities (set-ups, inspections, material handling), which unit-based rates cannot capture.
4. Where **stiff competition and pricing decisions** demand accurate product/customer cost information.

**Limitations (any two):**
1. **Costly and complex** to design and operate — identifying activities, cost pools and drivers and collecting driver data is expensive and time-consuming.
2. **Facility-level costs remain arbitrary** — some overheads (e.g., factory rent, general administration) have no meaningful cost driver and their assignment is as arbitrary as under traditional costing.

**Conclusion:** ABC yields the greatest benefit where overheads are large and products are diverse, but the improved accuracy must be weighed against its higher cost and complexity of operation.

*(Full-marks tip: Give the "activities consume resources; products consume activities" principle and at least two genuine limitations. A common deduction is listing only benefits and omitting limitations, or forgetting the high-overhead / product-diversity precondition.)*

### Q32. Ch: Cost Accounting Systems (Cost Ledgers) & Reconciliation — Reasons for difference and need for reconciliation (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain why the profit shown by the Cost Accounts differs from that shown by the Financial Accounts, and state the circumstances in which such reconciliation becomes unnecessary.

**Answer:** When cost and financial accounts are maintained as two independent (non-integrated) sets of books, the profit disclosed by each will generally differ. Reconciliation is required to (a) verify the arithmetical and conceptual accuracy of both sets, (b) explain and locate the reasons for the difference, and (c) ensure that the cost data used for control and decision-making is reliable. The reasons for the difference fall into three groups:

**1. Items shown only in Financial Accounts (not in Cost Accounts):**

| Purely financial expenses/losses | Purely financial incomes/gains |
|---|---|
| Interest on loans, loss on sale of assets, penalties, preliminary expenses written off, goodwill/discount on issue written off, donations, income-tax | Interest/dividend received, rent received, profit on sale of assets, transfer-fee received |

These are excluded from cost accounts (being non-operating/appropriation items) but affect financial profit.

**2. Items shown only in Cost Accounts (notional charges):** Notional rent of owned premises, notional interest on own capital and notional salary of the proprietor are charged in cost accounts to reflect true economic cost, but do not appear in the financial accounts.

**3. Differences in the *basis of valuation or absorption*:**
- **Over- or under-absorption of overheads** — cost accounts use pre-determined rates whereas financial accounts record actual overhead.
- **Different methods of stock valuation** — e.g., FIFO/weighted-average in cost accounts vs cost-or-NRV in financial accounts.
- **Different methods/rates of depreciation** — e.g., machine-hour method in cost accounts vs straight-line/WDV in financial accounts.

**Circumstances in which reconciliation is unnecessary:** Where the organisation maintains an **Integrated (Integral) Accounting System** — a single set of self-balancing books serving both cost and financial purposes — only one profit figure emerges, and therefore **no reconciliation is required**.

**Conclusion:** The difference arises from purely financial items, notional charges in cost accounts and differing bases of valuation/absorption; reconciliation is dispensed with only under an integrated accounting system.

*(Full-marks tip: Group the reasons into the three heads and give concrete examples under each — the examiner marks by head. Always add the integrated-system point in answer to "when is reconciliation not needed.")*

### Q36. Ch: Cost Sheet — Treatment of Items in a Cost Sheet (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** State, with reasons, how you would treat each of the following items while preparing a cost sheet: (i) Carriage inward on raw material; (ii) Cash discount received; (iii) Primary packing and secondary packing; (iv) Research and development cost incurred for an existing product; (v) Royalty paid on production and royalty paid on sales.

**Answer:**
The guiding principle is that only **normal costs incurred to bring a product to its present location and condition** are charged to the cost sheet, while purely **financial items** (having no relation to production or sale) are excluded and dealt with in financial accounts.

(i) **Carriage inward on raw material** is a cost incurred to bring materials to the factory; it is added to the cost of material purchased and hence forms part of **direct material / prime cost**.

(ii) **Cash discount received** is a financial item arising from prompt payment to suppliers; it is a matter of financial policy, not of production, and is therefore **excluded from the cost sheet** (credited in financial accounts). Similarly, cash discount allowed is excluded.

(iii) **Primary packing** (packing necessary to protect and hold the product, e.g. a tube for toothpaste) is essential to make the product saleable and is included in **factory / works cost (cost of production)**. **Secondary packing** (packing for transport and marketing, e.g. an outer carton for despatch) is charged to **selling and distribution overheads**.

(iv) **Research and development cost for an existing product** (i.e. incurred to improve a product already in production) is treated as part of the **cost of production** and, if the amount is heavy, is spread over a number of years. R&D of a general/basic nature is treated as administration overhead.

(v) **Royalty paid on production** (payable per unit produced) is a **direct expense** forming part of prime cost; **royalty paid on sales** (payable per unit sold) is charged to **selling and distribution overheads**.

**Conclusion:** Production-related costs (carriage inward, primary packing, production royalty, product R&D) enter the appropriate cost stage, selling-related costs (secondary packing, sales royalty) enter selling overhead, and pure financial items (cash discount) are excluded from the cost sheet.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner wants the reason ("why") for each classification, not just a one-word answer; the primary vs secondary packing and production vs sales royalty distinctions are the frequently-tested traps — always give both halves.)*

### Q38. Ch: Cost Sheet — Build-up of Total Cost and Stock Treatment (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the four principal stages in the build-up of total cost in a cost sheet, and state at which stage the stock of work-in-progress and the stock of finished goods are adjusted, and why.

**Answer:**
A cost sheet builds up total cost in four successive stages, each adding a further group of costs:

1. **Prime Cost** = Direct material consumed + Direct wages + Direct (chargeable) expenses. It represents all costs directly traceable to the product.
2. **Works / Factory Cost** = Prime cost + Factory (works) overheads, **adjusted for the opening and closing stock of work-in-progress** (add opening WIP, deduct closing WIP). WIP is adjusted here because partly-finished units have absorbed prime cost and factory overheads but not yet office/administration cost — hence WIP is valued at **works cost** and adjusted at the works-cost stage.
3. **Cost of Production** = Works cost + Office and administration overheads. Finished goods that emerge are valued at cost of production.
4. **Cost of Sales** = Cost of production **adjusted for opening and closing stock of finished goods** (add opening FG, deduct closing FG — giving Cost of Goods Sold) + Selling and distribution overheads. Finished goods are adjusted here because completed units have absorbed all production cost (up to cost of production) but not yet selling cost — hence finished stock is valued at **cost of production**.

**Conclusion:** WIP is adjusted at the works-cost stage (valued at works cost) and finished goods at the cost-of-production stage (valued at cost of production); Profit is finally added to cost of sales to arrive at sales value.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner specifically checks that you value WIP at works cost and finished goods at cost of production and that you place each adjustment at the correct stage — stating the "why" (which costs the stock has/has not absorbed) earns the reasoning marks.)*

### Q41. Ch: Unit & Batch Costing — Economic Batch Quantity and Set-up/Holding Trade-off across Two Products (Marks: 8) [Problem]

**Total cost at EBQ = ₹12,000** (set-up = holding, confirming EBQ).

**WN-2: EBQ of Product B**
EBQ = √(2 × 48,000 × 500 ÷ 3) = √(4,80,00,000 ÷ 3) = √1,60,00,000 = **4,000 units**.
Number of batches = 48,000 ÷ 4,000 = **12 batches**.
Set-up cost = 12 × 500 = ₹6,000; Holding cost = (4,000 ÷ 2) × 3 = 2,000 × 3 = ₹6,000.
**Total cost at EBQ = ₹12,000**.

**WN-3: Product A at the current batch size of 5,000 units**
Number of batches = 90,000 ÷ 5,000 = 18 batches.

### Q42. Ch: Unit & Batch Costing — Batch Costing and EBQ Concept (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** What is batch costing? State the industries where it is applied, explain the concept of Economic Batch Quantity and the trade-off it balances, and briefly distinguish batch costing from job costing.

**Answer:**
**Batch costing** is a form of specific-order costing in which a **batch** — a group of identical articles that maintains its identity through one or more stages of production — is treated as a single **cost unit**. The total cost of the batch is accumulated and the cost per article is obtained by dividing the batch cost by the number of good units in the batch:
Cost per unit = Total batch cost ÷ Number of units in the batch.

**Industries:** batch costing is used where articles are produced in definite lots of identical items — e.g. **pharmaceuticals (tablets/injections), biscuits and bakeries, ready-made garments, footwear, toys, spare parts and component manufacture**.

**Economic Batch Quantity (EBQ)** is the batch size that **minimises the total of set-up cost and carrying (holding) cost**. A larger batch means fewer set-ups (lower set-up cost) but more units held in stock (higher carrying cost); a smaller batch reverses this. EBQ balances this trade-off and is given by:
EBQ = √(2DS ÷ C), where D = annual demand, S = set-up cost per batch, C = carrying cost per unit per annum. At the EBQ, set-up cost equals carrying cost.

**Batch costing vs Job costing:**

| Basis | Job Costing | Batch Costing |
|---|---|---|
| Cost unit | A single job/order | A batch of identical units |
| Nature of output | Usually unique, to customer spec | Identical articles in a lot |
| Cost per unit | Whole job cost | Batch cost ÷ units in batch |
| Typical use | Printing, repairs, construction jobs | Pharma, bakery, garments, spares |

**Conclusion:** Batch costing accumulates cost for a lot of identical units and, through the EBQ, determines the lot size that minimises the combined set-up and holding cost.

*(Full-marks tip: give the EBQ formula and explicitly state the two opposing costs it balances (set-up vs holding) — merely defining batch costing without the trade-off loses the analytical marks; a labelled distinction table with the correct cost unit clinches full marks.)*

### Q45. Ch: Unit & Batch Costing — Unit (Output) Costing Features (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the meaning and essential features of unit (output/single) costing, giving suitable examples of industries and their cost units, and state how it differs from batch costing.

**Answer:**
**Unit costing** (also called **output or single costing**) is a method used where production is **continuous, the output consists of identical units of a single product**, and cost is ascertained **per unit of output** by dividing total cost by the number of units produced during the period:
Cost per unit = Total cost of the period ÷ Number of units produced.

**Essential features:** (i) production is of a single uniform product or a few grades of it; (ii) output is expressed in a natural physical unit; (iii) costs are collected under prime cost, works cost, cost of production and cost of sales and reduced to a per-unit figure; and (iv) a cost sheet (unit cost statement) is the principal document.

**Examples of industries and cost units:** cement (per tonne), bricks (per 1,000 bricks), steel (per tonne), collieries/mining (per tonne), breweries (per litre), paper (per ream/tonne).

**Difference from batch costing:**

| Basis | Unit Costing | Batch Costing |
|---|---|---|
| Output | Continuous, single identical product | Distinct lots/batches of identical items |
| Cost unit | A single natural unit (tonne, litre) | A batch of units |
| Production | Mass/continuous | In definite lots on specific orders |
| Example | Cement, steel, bricks | Pharma tablets, garments, spares |

**Conclusion:** Unit costing ascertains cost per unit of a single continuously-produced product, whereas batch costing ascertains cost per batch of identical articles produced in separately-identifiable lots.

*(Full-marks tip: pair each industry with its correct cost unit (tonne/litre/1,000 bricks) — vague answers without cost units lose marks; the continuous vs lot-wise distinction from batch costing is the specifically-tested contrast.)*

### Q48. Ch: Job & Contract Costing — Job Costing vs Contract Costing (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between job costing and contract costing.

**Answer:**
Both job costing and contract costing are methods of **specific-order costing**, in which costs are accumulated for a distinct, identifiable order; the difference lies mainly in the size, duration and location of the work.

| Basis | Job Costing | Contract Costing |
|---|---|---|
| Size and duration | Small jobs, usually completed within a short period | Large works, often spanning more than one accounting year |
| Place of work | Carried out within the factory premises | Carried out at the contract site |
| Cost unit | Each job or work order | Each contract |
| Nature of costs | A large share is indirect (overheads apportioned) | Most costs (materials, wages, plant) are direct to the contract |
| Profit recognition | Profit recognised on completion of the job | For incomplete contracts, a portion of notional profit is recognised prudently based on the stage of completion |
| Payment | Usually paid on completion | Paid in instalments against work certified, subject to retention money |
| Examples | Printing, repair jobs, machine tools, furniture to order | Roads, bridges, buildings, dams, ship-building |

**Conclusion:** Job costing suits small, short-duration in-house orders whose profit is taken on completion, whereas contract costing suits large, long-duration site-based works whose profit is recognised progressively based on the stage of completion.

*(Full-marks tip: present at least five points of distinction in a table; the examiner particularly looks for the "most costs are direct" and "progressive/prudent profit recognition with retention money" points, which are unique to contract costing.)*

### Q50. Ch: Job & Contract Costing — Key Contract-Costing Terms (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain briefly the following terms used in contract costing: (i) Work certified and work uncertified; (ii) Retention money; (iii) Escalation clause; (iv) Cost-plus contract.

**Answer:**
(i) **Work certified** is that part of the work done on a contract which has been **inspected and approved by the contractee's architect/surveyor** and certified for payment; it is valued at the contract (selling) price and is credited to the contract account as work-in-progress. **Work uncertified** is work that has been physically completed but **not yet certified** (usually work done just after the last certificate); it is valued at **cost** (not contract price) and also carried as work-in-progress.

(ii) **Retention money** is the portion of the value of work certified that the contractee is **entitled to withhold** (e.g. 10%–20%) for a specified period, releasing it only after satisfactory completion and the expiry of the defects/maintenance period. It protects the contractee against defective work: Retention money = Value of work certified − Cash received.

(iii) An **escalation clause** is a stipulation in the contract permitting the contractor to **claim an increase in the contract price** if the prices of specified materials, labour or utilities rise beyond an agreed level during execution. It protects the contractor against cost inflation on long-duration contracts. (Conversely, a **de-escalation clause** requires the price to be reduced if such costs fall.)

(iv) A **cost-plus contract** is one in which the **contract price is not fixed in advance** but is ascertained by adding to the **allowable cost** of the contract an agreed **percentage or fixed amount of profit**. It is used where costs cannot be estimated reliably in advance (e.g. urgent, novel or defence works); the contractor is assured of profit, while the contractee usually reserves the right to audit the cost records.

**Conclusion:** These terms govern how a contractor is paid and protected — certification and retention control payment, while escalation and cost-plus arrangements protect the parties against cost uncertainty on long-duration contracts.

*(Full-marks tip: state that work certified is valued at contract price but work uncertified at cost, and give the formula for retention money; for cost-plus contracts, mention the contractee's audit right — these precise points distinguish a full-mark answer from a vague one.)*

### Q51. Ch: Job & Contract Costing — Profit Recognition on Incomplete Contracts (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** What is notional profit on a contract, and what rules are followed for transferring profit on incomplete contracts to the Profit & Loss Account based on the stage of completion?

**Answer:**
**Notional profit** on an incomplete contract is the excess of the value of work certified (plus the cost of work uncertified) over the cost of work done to date:
Notional profit = (Work certified + Cost of work uncertified) − Cost of work to date.
It is a **provisional** profit; on grounds of **prudence**, only a part of it is transferred to the Profit & Loss Account, the balance being retained as a **reserve** to cover future contingencies. The portion recognised depends on the **stage of completion** (Work certified ÷ Contract price):

| Stage of completion (Work certified ÷ Contract price) | Profit to be transferred to Profit & Loss A/c |
|---|---|
| Less than 25% | Nil (no profit recognised) |
| 25% to less than 50% | ⅓ × Notional profit × (Cash received ÷ Work certified) |
| 50% to less than 90% | ⅔ × Notional profit × (Cash received ÷ Work certified) |
| 90% and above (near completion) | Estimated total profit × (Work certified ÷ Contract price) — or another recognised formula using estimated profit |

Where the contract results in an **estimated loss**, the **entire loss** is charged to the Profit & Loss Account immediately, irrespective of the stage of completion.

**Conclusion:** Profit on incomplete contracts is recognised prudently in proportion to the stage of completion and the cash received, ranging from nil in the early stages to the estimated-profit basis when the contract is near completion, while any anticipated loss is provided for in full at once.

*(Full-marks tip: reproduce the four-tier table with the correct fractions (⅓ and ⅔) and always include the "cash received ÷ work certified" factor; do not forget the rule that a foreseeable loss is written off in full immediately — this exception is a frequent exam point.)*

### Q52. Ch: Process & Operation Costing — Equivalent Units when Material is Introduced at a Later Stage (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** In many continuous processes an additional material is not introduced at the beginning but is added when the units reach a certain stage of completion. Explain the concept of **equivalent production** and state clearly how the degree of completion for such a "later-stage material" is to be determined for (i) units completed and transferred, (ii) closing work-in-progress that has crossed the introduction point, and (iii) closing work-in-progress that has not yet reached the introduction point.

**Answer:**
**Concept.** Equivalent production (or equivalent units) represents the production of a process in terms of *completed units*. Since at the period-end some units are only partly finished, the partly-completed units are expressed as an equivalent number of fully-completed units by multiplying the physical units by their percentage of completion, element by element (material, labour, overhead). This is essential because process costs are incurred on both finished and unfinished output, and cost per unit can be computed only when the two are placed on a common (completed-unit) footing.

**Material introduced at a later stage.** When a material is added not at the start but on the units attaining, say, 60% of the process, the degree of completion for that specific material element is **not** the same as the conversion-completion percentage; it is governed solely by whether the unit has *crossed the point of introduction*. The treatment is:

| Category of units | Has the introduction point been crossed? | Completion for the later-stage material |
|---|---|---|
| (i) Units completed & transferred | Yes (they are 100% complete) | **100%** — full material included |
| (ii) Closing WIP that has passed the introduction stage | Yes | **100%** — the material is already fully in the units |
| (iii) Closing WIP that has not yet reached the introduction stage | No | **0%** — no equivalent units for that material |

Thus a unit that is 70% complete as to conversion but on which the later-stage material enters at 60% is treated as **100% complete for that material** (because it has crossed 60%), even though it is only 70% complete for labour and overhead. Conversely, a unit that is only 40% complete as to conversion carries **0%** of that material.

**Conclusion:** For a later-stage material, completion is an "all-or-nothing" test based on the point of introduction — full equivalent units once the stage is crossed and nil before it — independent of the conversion-completion percentage.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the explicit "all-or-nothing at the point of introduction" rule and a worked illustration of a 70%-complete unit being 100% complete for the late material; the common deduction is mechanically applying the conversion % to the late material.)*

### Q58. Ch: Process & Operation Costing — Inter-Process Profit and Unrealised Profit in Stock (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Some organisations transfer output from one process to the next **at a price above cost** (cost plus a percentage of profit). Explain (i) why inter-process profit is charged, and (ii) the treatment of the **unrealised profit** included in the closing stock of a process. State how the "true" stock value is arrived at.

**Answer:**
**(i) Reason for charging inter-process profit.** When output of one process is transferred to the next at cost *plus* a profit margin, each process is treated as a **profit centre** rather than a mere cost centre. The purpose is to (a) reveal whether each process is *individually* profitable and (b) enable a comparison of the process cost/transfer price with the **market price** at which the semi-finished output could be bought or sold outside, thereby exposing inefficient processes and supporting make-or-buy decisions. The profit added by a process on transfer to the next is called **inter-process profit**.

**(ii) Treatment of unrealised profit in stock.** The difficulty is that so long as the goods remain **unsold** (lying as closing stock of a process or of finished goods), the inter-process profit loaded on to them has **not been realised** through a sale to an outside party. From the point of view of the business as a whole this profit is **fictitious/unrealised**, and if left in stock it would overstate both profit and the value of inventory in the Balance Sheet. Therefore a **provision for unrealised profit** is created and deducted from the closing stock so that stock is carried **at cost to the business**.

Unrealised profit in closing stock is computed as:
**Unrealised profit = (Profit included in the transfer price ÷ Transfer price) × Closing stock value**, i.e. the profit element is stripped out on a proportionate basis. The stock is shown in the Balance Sheet at **transfer value less provision for unrealised profit = true cost**.

**Conclusion:** Inter-process profit measures process-wise performance, but the profit loaded on unsold stock must be eliminated through a provision so that inventory is stated at genuine cost and reported profit is not inflated.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the exact proportion formula (profit/transfer-price applied to stock value) and the reasoning that only *realised* profit — profit on goods actually sold outside — may be recognised; the common deduction is computing the provision on cost instead of on transfer value.)*

### Q59. Ch: Process & Operation Costing — FIFO versus Weighted Average Method of Equivalent Production (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between the **FIFO method** and the **Weighted Average method** of computing equivalent production and valuing process output. State one situation in which each is preferable.

**Answer:**
Both methods convert partly-finished units into equivalent completed units to value process output; they differ in how they treat the **opening work-in-progress and its cost**.

| Basis | FIFO Method | Weighted Average Method |
|---|---|---|
| Treatment of opening WIP | Kept **separate**; only the work needed to *complete* it this period is included in equivalent units | **Merged** with current production; opening units treated as if started and finished this period |
| Cost used in the per-unit rate | **Current-period cost only** | **Opening cost + current-period cost** combined |
| Equivalent units | Include only the *degree of completion added this period* (opening WIP gets the balancing %) | Include the full completed units plus closing WIP % |
| Cost of goods transferred | In **two layers** — completed opening WIP (old + added cost) and units started-and-completed | At a **single average** cost per equivalent unit |
| Effect of price fluctuation | Reflects **current** cost trends; better cost control | Smooths out fluctuations; opening and current costs averaged |
| Complexity | More detailed / more computation | Simpler to operate |

**Preferable situation.** FIFO is preferable when input prices are fluctuating and management wants the *current* period's cost performance measured accurately (and for control/variance purposes). The Weighted Average method is preferable when prices are stable and simplicity is valued, or where physical identification of opening stock is impossible in a continuous flow.

**Conclusion:** FIFO isolates current-period efficiency by keeping opening WIP distinct and using current cost only, whereas the average method blends opening and current cost into one rate for simplicity.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the two contrasts that matter most — separate vs merged opening WIP, and current-only vs blended cost — laid out as a table; a common deduction is describing only the arithmetic without stating *when* each method is preferable.)*

| Product | Output (kg) | Selling price (₹/kg) |
|---|---|---|
| A | 6,000 | 80 |
| B | 4,000 | 45 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Sales value of each product.**
A = 6,000 × ₹80 = ₹4,80,000; B = 4,000 × ₹45 = ₹1,80,000; **Total sales = ₹6,60,000**.
Total physical output = 6,000 + 4,000 = 10,000 kg.

**WN-2: Apportionment of joint cost ₹3,30,000.**

| Product | Physical Units basis | Joint cost (₹) | Sales Value basis | Joint cost (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 6,000 / 10,000 | 1,98,000 | 4,80,000 / 6,60,000 | 2,40,000 |
| B | 4,000 / 10,000 | 1,32,000 | 1,80,000 / 6,60,000 | 90,000 |
| **Total** | | **3,30,000** | | **3,30,000** |

**WN-3: Product-wise Profit Statement.**

| Particulars | Physical Units method | Sales Value method |
|---|---|---|
| **Product A** — Sales (₹) | 4,80,000 | 4,80,000 |
| Less: Joint cost (₹) | 1,98,000 | 2,40,000 |

### Q62. Ch: Joint Products & By-Products — Methods of Accounting for By-Products (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the principal methods of accounting for **by-products** in cost accounts. Bring out clearly the difference between the "non-cost / sales value" approach and the "cost" approach, and state where the **reverse-cost (net realisable value) method** fits in.

**Answer:**
A by-product is a product of relatively small value that emerges incidentally in the course of manufacturing the main product. The methods of accounting for by-products fall into two broad groups.

**(A) Non-Cost or Sales-Value methods.** Here no attempt is made to apportion any part of the joint cost to the by-product; the by-product realisation is treated as an incidental recovery. The main variants are:
1. **Other income method** — the net sales value of the by-product is credited to the **Costing Profit & Loss Account** as miscellaneous/other income, leaving the main-product cost untouched. Suitable when the by-product value is negligible.
2. **Crediting sales value less selling/distribution cost** — the sale proceeds *net of* the selling and distribution expense of the by-product are so credited.
3. **Crediting sales value to the process/main product cost** — the by-product realisation is **deducted from the process cost** of the main product, thereby reducing the main product's cost per unit. Suitable when the value is more than negligible.

**(B) Cost methods.** Here a share of the joint cost is assigned to the by-product. The main variants are the **replacement/opportunity cost method** (used where the by-product is consumed internally, valued at the price that would otherwise be paid to buy it) and the **standard cost method** (by-product valued at a pre-set standard).

**(C) Reverse-Cost / Net Realisable Value method.** This is a refinement used where the by-product requires **further processing** and/or incurs selling costs before it can be sold. Working **backwards** from the by-product's ultimate selling price, one deducts the estimated **profit margin, selling and distribution expense, and further-processing cost** to arrive at the by-product's share of joint cost / the value to be credited to the main process. It thus bridges the two groups by giving the by-product a cost figure derived from its net realisation.

**Conclusion:** The non-cost approach simply credits by-product realisation (as other income or as a reduction of main-product cost), whereas the cost approach assigns a cost to the by-product; the reverse-cost method computes that credit by deducting profit and post-split costs from the by-product's selling price.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards naming the specific variants and stating *when* each is appropriate (negligible vs significant value; further processing needed); the common deduction is listing only one or two methods without the sales-value-vs-cost classification.)*

### Q67. Ch: Joint Products & By-Products — Joint Products, Co-Products and By-Products Distinguished (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between **joint products**, **co-products** and **by-products**. Give one example of each and state why the distinction matters for cost apportionment.

**Answer:**
All three arise where a single input or process yields more than one output, but they differ in relative importance and in how they originate.

| Basis | Joint Products | Co-Products | By-Products |
|---|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Two or more products of **comparable / significant value** produced simultaneously from the **same process and same raw material**, none of which can be regarded as the "main" product | Two or more products of comparable value that are produced **together but not necessarily from the same raw material or process** (may use common facilities) | A product of **relatively small value** that emerges **incidentally** while producing the main product |
| Relative value | High and roughly comparable | Comparable | Minor / secondary |
| Intention of manufacture | Deliberately produced together | Deliberately produced | Arises incidentally, not the object of manufacture |
| Example | Petrol, diesel and kerosene from crude oil | Wheat and gram grown together; car and its spare-parts line | Molasses in sugar manufacture; sawdust in a saw-mill |
| Cost treatment | Joint cost **apportioned** among them (sales value, physical units, NRV, etc.) | Costs identified/allocated to each | Usually **credited** to the main process or to Costing P&L; small share of cost, if any |

**Why the distinction matters.** The classification decides *how much of the joint cost is assigned*. Joint products **share** the joint cost through an apportionment basis, whereas a by-product is generally **not** charged with joint cost — instead its realisation is credited against the main product's cost or taken as other income. Mis-classifying a valuable output as a by-product would understate its cost and distort the main product's profitability.

**Conclusion:** The essential test is **relative value and intention**: comparable-value deliberate outputs are joint/co-products sharing the joint cost, while an incidental minor-value output is a by-product whose realisation merely reduces main-product cost.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the "relative value + intention of manufacture" test and correct examples (crude-oil fractions for joint; molasses for by-product); the common deduction is treating joint products and by-products as differing only by name without linking to the apportionment consequence.)*

### Q74. Ch: Standard Costing — Types of standards used in setting a standard (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the different **types of standards** that may be set while operating a standard costing system, and state which type is generally considered the most useful for control purposes.

**Answer:** A standard is a pre-determined, carefully developed yardstick of what cost *should* be under stated conditions. The tightness or looseness with which it is set gives rise to the following recognised types of standards:

- **Ideal Standard:** This is set on the assumption of the most favourable conditions possible — no machine breakdowns, no idle time, no wastage and peak efficiency. Because such perfection is unattainable in practice, ideal standards are almost always adverse, they demoralise workers and are of little practical use for cost control.
- **Basic Standard:** This is a long-period standard that is left unaltered over a long time and serves as a fixed base against which current results and price-level changes are compared, much like a base-year index number. It becomes out of date for day-to-day control because it ignores current conditions.
- **Current Standard:** This is a standard established for use over a short period and relates to current conditions. It reflects the performance expected during the current period and is therefore realistic, but it needs frequent revision.
- **Expected (Attainable) Standard:** This is a standard that is anticipated to be attained during a future specified budget period. It is based on normal, efficient operating conditions after making reasonable allowance for unavoidable losses, idle time and wastage. Being realistic yet challenging, it is regarded as the most suitable for planning, motivation and cost control.
- **Normal Standard:** This is an average standard that is expected to be attained over a future period long enough to cover one full trade cycle, thereby smoothing out cyclical fluctuations.

**Distinction — Ideal vs Expected (Attainable) Standard**

| Basis | Ideal Standard | Expected (Attainable) Standard |
|---|---|---|
| Conditions assumed | Perfect, best possible | Normal, efficient with allowances |
| Attainability | Practically unattainable | Realistically attainable |
| Effect on staff | Demotivating (always adverse) | Motivating and fair |
| Use for control | Poor | Best suited |

**Conclusion:** The **Expected (Attainable) Standard**, being realistic yet demanding, is the most useful type for cost control and performance appraisal.

*(Full-marks tip: name all the types and give one crisp identifying phrase for each; the examiner specifically wants the reasoning that the attainable standard — not the ideal — is best for control. Vague one-liners without the "why" lose half the marks.)*

- A = 5,280 × 60/110 = **2,880 kg**; B = 5,280 × 50/110 = **2,400 kg**

**WN-3: Revised standard quantity (actual total input 5,500 kg re-cast in standard proportion 6:5)**
- A = 5,500 × 60/110 = **3,000 kg**; B = 5,500 × 50/110 = **2,500 kg**

**WN-4: Variances**

| Material | Usage = (Std for output − Actual)×SP | Mix = (Rev. Std − Actual)×SP |
|---|---|---|
| A | (2,880 − 3,200) × 10 = 3,200 (A) | (3,000 − 3,200) × 10 = 2,000 (A) |
| B | (2,400 − 2,300) × 8 = 800 (F) | (2,500 − 2,300) × 8 = 1,600 (F) |
| **Total** | **2,400 (A)** | **400 (A)** |

**Material Yield Variance** = (Actual Output − Standard Output from actual input) × Std cost per kg output
- Standard output from 5,500 kg input = 5,500 × 100/110 = 5,000 kg
- = (4,800 − 5,000) × 10 = **₹2,000 (A)**

**Reconciliation:** Mix ₹400 (A) + Yield ₹2,000 (A) = **₹2,400 (A)** = Material Usage Variance ✓

**Answer:** Usage Variance **₹2,400 (A)** = Mix Variance **₹400 (A)** + Yield Variance **₹2,000 (A)**.

*(Full-marks tip: the mix variance re-casts the ACTUAL total input in the standard proportion (WN-3); the yield variance measures the loss/gain in output at the standard cost per unit of output. The examiner checks that Mix + Yield sums exactly to the Usage variance — round mix quantities carefully so the reconciliation holds.)*

### Q78. Ch: Marginal Costing — CVP with step-fixed costs (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** Zenith Ltd. sells its product at **₹50 per unit** with a variable cost of **₹30 per unit**. Its fixed costs behave in *steps* depending on the level of activity:

| Output range (units) | Fixed Cost (₹) |
|---|---|
| 0 – 10,000 | 1,50,000 |
| 10,001 – 20,000 | 2,50,000 |
| 20,001 – 30,000 | 3,60,000 |

You are required to: (i) determine the break-even point(s); (ii) compute the profit at 10,000, 20,000 and 30,000 units; and (iii) comment on the effect of the step-fixed costs.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Contribution per unit**
- Contribution = 50 − 30 = **₹20 per unit**

**WN-2: Break-even point(s)** — With step-fixed costs, a break-even must be tested for validity within its own range.
- **First tier:** BEP = 1,50,000 ÷ 20 = **7,500 units** — lies within 0–10,000, hence **valid**.
- **Second tier:** BEP = 2,50,000 ÷ 20 = **12,500 units** — lies within 10,001–20,000, hence **valid**.
- **Third tier:** required = 3,60,000 ÷ 20 = 18,000 units, which is *below* the tier floor of 20,001 — the firm is already past break-even before entering this tier, so there is **no separate BEP** here.

**WN-3: Profit at selected levels**

| Output (units) | Contribution (₹) | Fixed Cost (₹) | Profit (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10,000 | 10,000 × 20 = 2,00,000 | 1,50,000 | **50,000** |
| 20,000 | 20,000 × 20 = 4,00,000 | 2,50,000 | **1,50,000** |
| 30,000 | 30,000 × 20 = 6,00,000 | 3,60,000 | **2,40,000** |

**WN-4: The "dip" caused by the step**
Just after 20,000 units the fixed cost jumps from ₹2,50,000 to ₹3,60,000 (+₹1,10,000). At 20,001 units profit falls sharply to roughly (20,001 × 20) − 3,60,000 = ₹40,020, well below the ₹1,50,000 earned at 20,000 units. Output must climb to (3,60,000 + 1,50,000) ÷ 20 = **25,500 units** merely to regain the ₹1,50,000 profit level.

**Comment:** Because fixed costs rise in steps, there is **more than one break-even point (7,500 and 12,500 units)** and profit does **not increase smoothly** — crossing a step causes a temporary fall in profit, so the extra capacity is worth taking only if output can be pushed well beyond the step (here past 25,500 units).

**Answer:** Break-even at **7,500 units** and **12,500 units**; Profit = **₹50,000 / ₹1,50,000 / ₹2,40,000** at 10,000 / 20,000 / 30,000 units respectively; a step in fixed cost creates multiple BEPs and a profit dip beyond 20,000 units.

*(Full-marks tip: the whole idea is that a computed BEP is valid only if it falls within the fixed-cost band used — the examiner rewards testing each tier and rejecting the invalid third-tier figure. State clearly that step-fixed costs produce multiple break-even points; a single BEP answer scores poorly.)*

### Q79. Ch: Marginal Costing — Most profitable product mix under two limiting factors (Marks: 10) [Problem]
**Question:** Apex Ltd. manufactures two products, **X and Y**. The relevant per-unit data and the total availability of the two scarce resources are:

| Particulars | Product X | Product Y | Total available |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contribution per unit (₹) | 40 | 30 | — |
| Material required (kg) | 4 | 2 | 800 kg |
| Labour required (hours) | 2 | 4 | 1,000 hrs |

Both material and labour are in short supply (two limiting factors). Determine the **product mix that maximises total contribution** and compute that contribution.

**Solution:**

Because **two** resources are simultaneously scarce, contribution per single limiting factor cannot give the answer; the optimal mix is found by the **linear-programming (graphical / corner-point) method**.

**WN-1: Formulate**
Let output be X units and Y units. Maximise Contribution **Z = 40X + 30Y**, subject to:
- Material: 4X + 2Y ≤ 800
- Labour: 2X + 4Y ≤ 1,000
- X, Y ≥ 0

**WN-2: Feasible corner points**
- Material line intercepts: X = 200 (Y=0), Y = 400 (X=0)
- Labour line intercepts: X = 500 (Y=0), Y = 250 (X=0)
- Binding maxima on axes: on X-axis, material limits X to 200; on Y-axis, labour limits Y to 250.

**Intersection of the two constraints** (solve simultaneously):
- 4X + 2Y = 800 ⟹ 2X + Y = 400 ⟹ Y = 400 − 2X
- Substitute into 2X + 4Y = 1,000: 2X + 4(400 − 2X) = 1,000 ⟹ 2X + 1,600 − 8X = 1,000 ⟹ −6X = −600 ⟹ **X = 100, Y = 200**
- Verify: Material 4(100)+2(200)=800 ✓; Labour 2(100)+4(200)=1,000 ✓ (both fully used)

**WN-3: Contribution at each corner point**

| Corner point (X, Y) | Contribution = 40X + 30Y (₹) |
|---|---|
| (0, 0) | 0 |
| (200, 0) | 8,000 |
| (0, 250) | 7,500 |
| **(100, 200)** | **40×100 + 30×200 = 4,000 + 6,000 = 10,000** |

The maximum contribution occurs at the intersection point where **both** resources are exhausted.

**Statement Showing Optimal Product Mix**

| Product | Units | Contribution/unit (₹) | Total Contribution (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|
| X | 100 | 40 | 4,000 |
| Y | 200 | 30 | 6,000 |
| **Total** | | | **10,000** |

**Answer:** Optimal mix = **X 100 units and Y 200 units**, giving the maximum total contribution of **₹10,000**, with both material (800 kg) and labour (1,000 hrs) fully utilised.

*(Full-marks tip: state explicitly that with two simultaneous limiting factors the "contribution per key factor" ranking fails and LP corner-point evaluation is required. Marks are awarded for testing every corner point — the optimum is frequently the intersection where both constraints bind, not an axis point.)*

### Q80. Ch: Marginal Costing — Decision to close down a division (Marks: 8) [Application]
**Question:** Meridian Ltd. has three divisions A, B and C. The management is considering **closing down Division C** because it shows a loss. The following statement is available (fixed costs include division-specific/avoidable costs and general costs apportioned on sales):

| Particulars | Division A (₹) | Division B (₹) | Division C (₹) | Total (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sales | 12,00,000 | 8,00,000 | 5,00,000 | 25,00,000 |
| Variable Cost | 7,00,000 | 5,00,000 | 4,00,000 | 16,00,000 |
| Fixed Cost (specific) | 1,50,000 | 1,20,000 | 60,000 | 3,30,000 |
| Fixed Cost (general, apportioned) | 1,30,000 | 90,000 | 50,000 | 2,70,000 |

Advise whether Division C should be closed down, supporting your advice with figures.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Divisional contribution and profitability**

| Particulars | A (₹) | B (₹) | C (₹) | Total (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sales | 12,00,000 | 8,00,000 | 5,00,000 | 25,00,000 |
| Less: Variable Cost | 7,00,000 | 5,00,000 | 4,00,000 | 16,00,000 |
| **Contribution** | **5,00,000** | **3,00,000** | **1,00,000** | **9,00,000** |
| Less: Specific Fixed Cost | 1,50,000 | 1,20,000 | 60,000 | 3,30,000 |
| **Divisional Segment Margin** | **3,50,000** | **1,80,000** | **40,000** | **5,70,000** |
| Less: General Fixed Cost | 1,30,000 | 90,000 | 50,000 | 2,70,000 |
| Net Profit / (Loss) | 2,20,000 | 90,000 | **(10,000)** | 3,00,000 |

Division C shows an apparent net loss of ₹10,000 only because ₹50,000 of *general* fixed cost is apportioned to it — and this general cost is **unavoidable**; it will not be saved if C is closed but merely re-apportioned to A and B.

**WN-2: Effect of closing Division C**

| Particulars | Continue C (₹) | Close C (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| Contribution lost | — | (1,00,000) |
| Specific (avoidable) fixed cost saved | — | 60,000 |
| **Net effect on company profit** | — | **(40,000)** |

Closing C would forgo a contribution of ₹1,00,000 while saving only its avoidable/specific fixed cost of ₹60,000, giving a **net fall in company profit of ₹40,000**. The company's total profit would drop from ₹3,00,000 to **₹2,60,000**.

**Conclusion / Advice:** Division C earns a **positive segment margin of ₹40,000** (contribution ₹1,00,000 less avoidable fixed ₹60,000), i.e. it recovers all its own costs and still contributes ₹40,000 towards general fixed overheads. **Division C should NOT be closed down.**

**Answer:** Do **not** close Division C — closure would reduce company profit by **₹40,000** (from ₹3,00,000 to ₹2,60,000).

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner rewards separating fixed cost into avoidable (specific) and unavoidable (apportioned general). The decision turns on the segment margin — contribution less avoidable fixed cost — not on the apportioned net loss. Concluding "close because it shows a loss" is the classic trap that loses most of the marks.)*

### Q82. Ch: Marginal Costing — Marginal vs Absorption costing (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between **Marginal Costing and Absorption Costing**. State any two limitations of marginal costing.

**Answer:** **Marginal costing** is a technique in which only variable (marginal) costs are charged to cost units, while fixed costs are treated as period costs and written off in full against the contribution of the period. **Absorption costing** is the technique in which both variable and fixed manufacturing costs are absorbed into the cost of the product.

**Distinction between Marginal Costing and Absorption Costing**

| Basis | Marginal Costing | Absorption Costing |
|---|---|---|
| Treatment of fixed cost | Period cost, charged in full to the period | Product cost, absorbed into units |
| Valuation of stock | At variable (marginal) cost only | At total cost (variable + fixed) |
| Effect on profit | Profit varies with sales only | Profit is affected by both production and sales (fixed cost carried in stock) |
| Cost per unit | Not affected by changes in output | Varies with the level of output |
| Emphasis | Contribution and cost-volume-profit analysis | Recovery of total cost |
| Suitability | Short-term decision-making | External reporting / statutory profit |

**Two limitations of marginal costing:**
1. The **classification of costs into fixed and variable is difficult** in practice; many costs are semi-variable and the split is only approximate.
2. It **understates stock values** by excluding fixed manufacturing overhead, which is not acceptable for financial reporting (AS 2 / Ind AS 2 require fixed production overhead to be included in inventory).

**Conclusion:** Marginal costing aids managerial decision-making through contribution analysis, whereas absorption costing, by charging fixed cost to products, is required for external financial reporting.

*(Full-marks tip: present the distinction in a table with at least four bases and finish with genuine limitations; linking the stock-valuation limitation to AS 2 lifts the answer to ICAI standard.)*

### Q87. Ch: Budgets & Budgetary Control — Zero-Based Budgeting (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the concept of **Zero-Based Budgeting (ZBB)**. State the stages involved in it and any two advantages over traditional (incremental) budgeting.

**Answer:** Zero-Based Budgeting is a method of budgeting in which **all activities are re-evaluated each time a budget is prepared, and every line of expenditure has to be justified afresh starting from a "zero base"** — that is, as if the activity were being undertaken for the first time — rather than merely taking the previous year's figures and adjusting them for the expected change in volume or price. The technique was developed and popularised by **Peter A. Pyhrr**, and it shifts the burden of proof onto the manager, who must demonstrate why any expenditure should be incurred at all.

The principal **stages** in Zero-Based Budgeting are as follows. First, the organisation identifies its **decision units** — the lowest cost/responsibility centres for which a budget is prepared. Second, for each decision unit a **decision package** is prepared, describing the activity, its purpose, the cost, the benefit, the consequences of not performing it and the alternative ways of performing it. Third, the decision packages are **evaluated and ranked** in order of priority on a cost-benefit basis. Finally, resources are **allocated** to the packages according to their ranking and the funds available.

**Distinction — ZBB vs. Traditional (Incremental) Budgeting:**

| Basis | Zero-Based Budgeting | Traditional Budgeting |
|---|---|---|
| Starting point | Zero base; every item justified afresh | Previous year's figures as base |
| Focus | Cost-benefit of each activity | Increase/decrease over last year |
| Emphasis | Efficiency and priorities | Continuity of existing activities |

Two **advantages** are: (i) it eliminates inefficient and obsolete activities because no expenditure is carried forward unquestioned, thereby promoting a cost-conscious culture; and (ii) it allocates scarce resources to the most beneficial activities through systematic ranking, improving the value obtained for money spent.

**Conclusion:** ZBB is a rational, priority-driven approach that questions the very existence of each activity, making it particularly useful for controlling discretionary and support-function costs.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner looks for the phrase "starts from zero base / every item justified afresh", the four stages (decision units → packages → ranking → allocation), and a clear contrast with incremental budgeting. Common deduction: merely defining the term without listing the stages.)*

### Q89. Ch: Budgets & Budgetary Control — Fixed Budget vs. Flexible Budget (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between a **Fixed Budget** and a **Flexible Budget**, and explain why a flexible budget is a superior instrument of cost control.

**Answer:** A **Fixed Budget** (also called a static budget) is a budget which is designed to remain unchanged **irrespective of the level of activity actually attained**. It is prepared for a single, assumed level of output and is not adjusted even if the actual volume of production or sales differs from the budgeted volume. A **Flexible Budget**, on the other hand, is a budget which is designed to **change in accordance with the level of activity actually attained**; it recognises the difference in behaviour of fixed and variable costs and therefore presents budgeted figures for a range of activity levels.

The essential distinctions are tabulated below:

| Basis | Fixed Budget | Flexible Budget |
|---|---|---|
| Activity level | Prepared for one level only | Prepared for a range of levels |
| Cost classification | Costs not split by behaviour | Costs split into fixed, variable, semi-variable |
| Effect of volume change | Not adjusted for actual output | Recasts budget to actual output |
| Cost control | Comparison is unrealistic if volume differs | Meaningful "like-with-like" comparison |
| Suitability | Where activity is stable and predictable | Where activity fluctuates |

A flexible budget is a **superior instrument of cost control** because it enables a valid comparison: actual costs at the actual level of activity are compared with **budgeted costs recast to that same level of activity**. This isolates genuine cost variances (efficiency and spending) from variances that arise merely because output differed from plan, which a fixed budget cannot do.

**Conclusion:** Whereas a fixed budget compares unlike quantities, a flexible budget "flexes" the yardstick to the actual level of activity, making variance analysis fair and control effective.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner wants the behaviour-of-cost point and the "compare actual with budget recast to actual activity" argument. Presenting the distinction as a table earns easy marks.)*

### Q91. Ch: Service Costing — Nature and Selection of the Cost Unit (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** What is **Service (Operating) Costing**? State its distinctive features and give the **composite cost unit** appropriate to (i) a hospital, (ii) a goods-transport undertaking, (iii) a hotel, and (iv) an electricity generating undertaking.

**Answer:** **Service costing**, also called **operating costing**, is that method of costing which is applied to **undertakings that provide services rather than produce tangible goods**. It is used to ascertain the cost of the service rendered and, from it, the price to be charged. It is employed by internal service departments (such as a canteen or a power house serving a factory) as well as by external service organisations (such as transport companies, hospitals and hotels).

The **distinctive features** of service costing are as follows. Costs are ordinarily classified into **fixed (standing) costs, variable (running) costs and semi-variable (maintenance) costs** to help control and to compute the cost per unit at different levels of activity. Because the output is intangible and cannot be stored, there is **no closing stock of finished service**. The cost unit is very often a **composite (compound) cost unit**, which combines two measurable factors so as to reflect the service more meaningfully than a single factor would. The cost per unit is obtained as: **Total cost of operating the service ÷ Number of service (cost) units**.

**Appropriate composite cost units:**

| Service undertaking | Composite cost unit |
|---|---|
| Hospital | Per patient-day |
| Goods-transport undertaking | Per tonne-kilometre |
| Hotel | Per room-day (or guest-day) |
| Electricity generating undertaking | Per kilowatt-hour (kWh) |

**Conclusion:** Service costing measures the cost of an intangible service through a suitably chosen (often composite) cost unit, enabling both cost control and rational pricing.

### Q97. Ch: Service Costing — Toll-Road (BOT) Costing and the Composite Cost Unit (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** In the context of a **toll road operated on a Build-Operate-Transfer (BOT) basis**, explain how the **toll rate per vehicle** is arrived at under service costing, and why a **composite/equivalent cost unit** (Passenger Car Unit) is used.

**Answer:** A toll road built and operated under a **Build-Operate-Transfer (BOT)** arrangement is a classic application of **service (operating) costing**, because the operator renders the intangible service of "road usage" and recovers its cost, along with a return, through a **toll charged per vehicle**. The service being priced is the passage of a vehicle over the road, so the cost unit is the **vehicle-passage**, adjusted for the differing road space and wear caused by different vehicle types.

The **toll rate** is arrived at as follows. The operator first accumulates the **total cost** to be recovered over the concession period — comprising the **capital cost** of construction (recovered through depreciation/amortisation over the concession period), **interest** on the funds invested, and the **operating and maintenance costs** of running the toll plaza and maintaining the road. To this total cost the operator adds the **desired profit margin (return on investment)** permitted under the concession agreement. This sum is then divided by the **estimated number of cost units** (vehicle-passages) expected over the period to give the toll per unit:

> **Toll per unit = (Total cost to be recovered + Desired return) ÷ Estimated number of cost units.**

A **composite/equivalent cost unit — the Passenger Car Unit (PCU)** — is used because different classes of vehicles (a car, a bus, a heavy truck) occupy different amounts of road space and cause different degrees of wear and congestion. Each vehicle class is therefore assigned a **weight (equivalence factor)** relative to a passenger car (e.g., car = 1, bus/truck = 3, and so on), and all traffic is converted into **equivalent Passenger Car Units** before the per-unit toll is computed and then re-loaded onto each vehicle class in that proportion.

**Conclusion:** Under BOT toll costing, the toll rate recovers construction, interest and O&M costs plus an agreed return, spread over the estimated traffic expressed in equivalent Passenger Car Units so that each class of vehicle bears a fair share.

*(Full-marks tip: Mention the three cost blocks — capital/depreciation, interest, and O&M — the added return, and the PCU weighting for different vehicle classes. Common deduction: omitting that capital cost is recovered over the concession period.)*

**200 students** are enrolled in the batch. The institute wishes to earn a **profit of 20% on the fee**. Compute (a) the cost per student, and (b) the **fee per student** to be charged for the term.

**Statement Showing Total Cost — BrightMinds (per term)**

### Q100. Ch: Service Costing — Multiplex Cinema Cost per Ticket (Marks: 6) [Problem/Application]
**Question:** StarPlex Multiplex has **3 screens with 200 seats each**. It runs **4 shows per day for 30 days** in a month, with an average seat occupancy of **60%**. The monthly costs are:

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Film/content screening cost | 18,00,000 |
| Staff salaries | 6,00,000 |
| Electricity and air-conditioning | 4,00,000 |
| Rent of premises | 5,00,000 |
| Depreciation of projection and sound equipment | 3,00,000 |
| Housekeeping and consumables | 1,50,000 |
| Administration overheads | 2,50,000 |

Compute (a) the number of tickets sold in the month, (b) the cost per ticket, and (c) the **ticket price** if the multiplex desires a **profit of 25% on cost**.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Number of tickets sold**
- Total seat-shows available = (3 screens × 200 seats) × 4 shows × 30 days = 600 seats × 120 = 72,000 seat-shows
- Tickets sold at 60% occupancy = 72,000 × 60% = **43,200 tickets**

**WN-2: Total operating cost**

**Statement Showing Total Cost — StarPlex Multiplex (per month)**

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Film/content screening cost | 18,00,000 |
| Staff salaries | 6,00,000 |
| Electricity and air-conditioning | 4,00,000 |
| Rent of premises | 5,00,000 |
| Depreciation of equipment | 3,00,000 |
| Housekeeping and consumables | 1,50,000 |
| Administration overheads | 2,50,000 |
| **Total cost** | **40,00,000** |

**WN-3: Cost per ticket and ticket price**

| Particulars | Computation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| (b) Cost per ticket | ₹40,00,000 ÷ 43,200 | **₹92.59** |
| (c) Ticket price (25% profit on cost) | ₹92.59 × 1.25 | **₹115.74** |

**OR (check on price):** Profit per ticket = 25% × ₹92.59 = ₹23.15; Price = ₹92.59 + ₹23.15 = **₹115.74** ✓.

**Answer:** Tickets sold = **43,200**; cost per ticket = **₹92.59**; **ticket price = ₹115.74** (say ₹116) to earn 25% profit on cost.

*(Full-marks tip: Note the profit basis differs from the hotel/coaching problems — here it is "25% ON COST", so multiply cost by 1.25. Marks are lost for using 3 × 200 = 600 seats without multiplying by shows and days, or for ignoring the 60% occupancy.)*
