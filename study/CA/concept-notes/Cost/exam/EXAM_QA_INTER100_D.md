### Q1. Ch: Material Cost — Inventory Turnover Ratio & Slow-moving Stock (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** From the following details relating to two raw materials A and B for the year ended 31st March, compute the **Inventory Turnover Ratio** for each material, express the **average number of days of consumption** each stock represents, and comment which material is **fast-moving** and which is **slow-moving**:

| Particulars | Material A (₹) | Material B (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| Opening Stock | 40,000 | 90,000 |
| Purchases during the year | 4,60,000 | 1,50,000 |
| Closing Stock | 60,000 | 1,20,000 |

Assume 360 days in the year.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Cost of materials consumed**
Consumption = Opening Stock + Purchases − Closing Stock
- Material A = 40,000 + 4,60,000 − 60,000 = ₹4,40,000
- Material B = 90,000 + 1,50,000 − 1,20,000 = ₹1,20,000

**WN-2: Average stock held**
Average Stock = (Opening Stock + Closing Stock) ÷ 2
- Material A = (40,000 + 60,000) ÷ 2 = ₹50,000
- Material B = (90,000 + 1,20,000) ÷ 2 = ₹1,05,000

**WN-3: Inventory Turnover Ratio (ITR)**
ITR = Cost of materials consumed ÷ Average stock held
- Material A = 4,40,000 ÷ 50,000 = **8.8 times**
- Material B = 1,20,000 ÷ 1,05,000 = **1.143 times**

**Statement Showing Inventory Turnover Ratio and Days of Consumption**

| Particulars | Material A | Material B |
|---|---|---|
| Materials consumed (₹) | 4,40,000 | 1,20,000 |
| Average stock (₹) | 50,000 | 1,05,000 |
| Inventory Turnover Ratio | 8.8 times | 1.143 times |
| Days of stock held = 360 ÷ ITR | 40.9 days | 315 days |

**Comment:** A higher Inventory Turnover Ratio indicates that the material is used frequently and does not remain idle in stores; a lower ratio signals slow movement and a risk of the stock becoming non-moving/obsolete with attendant carrying cost. Material **A (8.8 times, ~41 days)** is **fast-moving**, whereas Material **B (1.14 times, ~315 days)** is **slow-moving** and calls for investigation into over-stocking, deterioration or obsolescence.

**Answer:** ITR — Material A = 8.8 times (≈41 days); Material B = 1.143 times (≈315 days). Material B is slow-moving.

*(Full-marks tip: ICAI rewards using **consumption** (not purchases) as the numerator and **average** stock as the denominator; a common deduction is computing days as 365/ITR when the question fixes 360, or dividing by closing stock instead of average stock.)*

### Q2. Ch: Material Cost — Bin Card vs Stores Ledger (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between a **Bin Card** and a **Stores Ledger**. State any two reasons why the balances shown by the two records may not agree.

**Answer:** A **Bin Card** is a quantitative record of receipts, issues and balance of each item of stores, maintained by the **storekeeper** and kept **inside the store (attached to the bin/rack)**; entries are made **at the time of each movement** of material. A **Stores Ledger** is a record maintained by the **Costing/Accounts department** in the **cost office**, recording each item **both in quantity and in value**, and entries are usually posted **periodically** from the supporting documents (GRN, material requisition notes).

| Basis | Bin Card | Stores Ledger |
|---|---|---|
| Nature of record | Quantity only | Quantity **and** value |
| Maintained by | Storekeeper | Cost/Accounts department |
| Location | Inside the stores | In the cost office |
| Timing of entry | At the moment of each transaction | Posted periodically from documents |
| Primary purpose | Physical control of stock | Costing/valuation of stock |

**Reasons for disagreement between the two balances:** (i) **Arithmetical or posting errors**, such as an item posted in one record and omitted or wrongly carried forward in the other; (ii) **timing differences** — a receipt or issue recorded in the bin card but not yet posted in the stores ledger (or vice versa); other causes include entries posted to the wrong code/sheet, or non-recording of shortages, breakages and returns.

**Conclusion:** The bin card is the storekeeper's quantitative control record and the stores ledger is the cost office's value record; periodic reconciliation of the two ensures accuracy of both physical control and stock valuation.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner looks for at least four crisp points of distinction laid out in a table plus two valid, distinct reasons; merely writing "both record stock" without the quantity-vs-value and storekeeper-vs-cost-office contrast loses marks.)*

### Q3. Ch: Material Cost — Bin Card / Stores Ledger Reconciliation (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** On physical verification, the balance of Material 'X' as per the **Bin Card** was **840 units** while the **Stores Ledger** showed **910 units**. On scrutiny the following discrepancies were noticed:

| S.No. | Discrepancy | Units |
|---|---|---|
| (i) | A goods received note for 60 units was posted in the Stores Ledger but not in the Bin Card | 60 |
| (ii) | A material requisition (issue) of 40 units was entered in the Bin Card but omitted in the Stores Ledger | 40 |
| (iii) | A return of 25 units to stores was posted twice in the Stores Ledger | 25 |
| (iv) | An issue of 15 units recorded in the Bin Card was posted as 51 units in the Stores Ledger | 36 (excess) |

Prepare a statement reconciling the Bin Card balance with the Stores Ledger balance.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Effect of each item**
- (i) 60 units received not posted in Bin Card → Bin Card understated by 60.
- (ii) 40-unit issue not posted in Stores Ledger → Stores Ledger overstated by 40.
- (iii) Return of 25 posted twice in Stores Ledger → Stores Ledger overstated by 25.
- (iv) 15-unit issue posted as 51 in Stores Ledger (over-issue of 36) → Stores Ledger understated balance by 36 (i.e. it deducted 36 units too many).

**Statement Reconciling Bin Card Balance with Stores Ledger Balance**

| Particulars | Units |
|---|---|
| Balance as per **Bin Card** | 840 |
| Add: GRN of 60 units not posted in Bin Card (item i) | 60 |
| **Adjusted quantity that should appear** | 900 |
| Balance as per **Stores Ledger** | 910 |
| Add: Issue of 40 units omitted in Stores Ledger — should reduce it (item ii) → deduct | (40) |
| Add: Return of 25 units posted twice — reverse one entry (item iii) → deduct | (25) |
| Add: 15-unit issue over-posted as 51; excess 36 units wrongly deducted — add back (item iv) | 36 |
| **Corrected Stores Ledger balance** | 881 |

**OR (Reconciliation to a common corrected figure):**

| Particulars | Bin Card (units) | Stores Ledger (units) |
|---|---|---|
| Recorded balance | 840 | 910 |
| (i) Receipt of 60 not in Bin Card | +60 | — |
| (ii) Issue of 40 not in Ledger | — | −40 |
| (iii) Double-posted return of 25 | — | −25 |
| (iv) Over-posted issue (51 vs 15) = 36 | — | +36 |
| **Corrected balance** | **900** | **881** |

The residual difference of **19 units (900 − 881)** represents an **unexplained shortage** (pilferage/uncovered discrepancy) to be written off after authorisation.

**Answer:** Corrected Bin Card = 900 units; Corrected Stores Ledger = 881 units; unexplained shortage = **19 units** to be investigated and adjusted.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards getting the **direction** of each adjustment right — an omitted issue *reduces* the ledger, a double-posted return *reduces* it, an over-posted issue must be *added back*. Reversing a sign is the most common deduction.)*

### Q4. Ch: Material Cost — Pricing of Returns under FIFO (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** The following transactions relate to Material 'M' for the month of June. The firm follows the **FIFO** method. Note the treatment of **materials returned to stores from the shop floor** and **returns to supplier**. Prepare the Stores Ledger Account.

| Date | Transaction | Units | Rate/Unit (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|
| June 1 | Opening balance | 200 | 20 |
| June 4 | Purchased | 300 | 22 |
| June 8 | Issued to Job 11 | 350 | — |
| June 12 | Returned by Job 11 to stores (out of 8 June issue) | 30 | — |
| June 16 | Purchased | 250 | 24 |
| June 20 | Returned to supplier (out of 16 June purchase) | 20 | 24 |
| June 25 | Issued to Job 12 | 300 | — |

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Treatment principles**
- 8 June issue (350) under FIFO: 200 @ ₹20 + 150 @ ₹22.
- 12 June return from shop of 30 units is **priced at the rate at which it was last issued**, i.e. **₹22** (the latest issue price of the 8-June issue). These 30 units re-enter stock at ₹22.
- 20 June return to supplier of 20 units is taken out **at its purchase price ₹24** (not a shop issue).

**Stores Ledger Account — Material 'M' (FIFO)**

| Date | Receipts (Units × Rate = ₹) | Issues (Units × Rate = ₹) | Balance (Units, ₹) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jun 1 | Bal b/d 200 × 20 = 4,000 | — | 200 = 4,000 |
| Jun 4 | 300 × 22 = 6,600 | — | 200 @20; 300 @22 = 10,600 |
| Jun 8 | — | 200 × 20 = 4,000; 150 × 22 = 3,300 (=7,300) | 150 @22 = 3,300 |
| Jun 12 | Return 30 × 22 = 660 | — | 150 @22; 30 @22 = 180 @22 = 3,960 |
| Jun 16 | 250 × 24 = 6,000 | — | 180 @22; 250 @24 = 9,960 |
| Jun 20 | — | Return to supplier 20 × 24 = 480 | 180 @22; 230 @24 = 9,480 |
| Jun 25 | — | 180 × 22 = 3,960; 120 × 24 = 2,880 (=6,840) | 110 @24 = 2,640 |

**WN-2: Closing stock**
Closing balance = 110 units @ ₹24 = **₹2,640**.

**Answer:** Value of 25 June issue = ₹6,840; Closing Stock = **110 units valued at ₹2,640** (FIFO). Shop-floor returns priced at last issue rate (₹22); return to supplier removed at purchase rate (₹24).

*(Full-marks tip: ICAI treats a **return from the shop** as fresh receipt priced at the **rate at which it was issued**, kept as a separate FIFO layer; a **return to supplier** is a reduction of the relevant purchase lot at its purchase price. Pricing the shop return at the oldest FIFO rate is a frequent error.)*

### Q5. Ch: Material Cost — Obsolescence, Deterioration & Slow-moving Stores (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the terms **obsolescence** and **deterioration** of materials, and state how the cost of **obsolete stores** should be treated. Suggest two measures to control obsolescence.

**Answer:** **Obsolescence** is the **loss in usefulness of a material owing to a change** — a change in product design, technology, method of manufacture, or discontinuance of a product line — as a result of which the material can no longer be used for its intended purpose although it may still be physically fit. **Deterioration**, in contrast, is a **physical loss in the quality or fitness of a material** arising from the passage of time or from adverse storage conditions such as evaporation, rusting, chemical action or spoilage; here the item has physically degraded.

| Basis | Obsolescence | Deterioration |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | Change in design/technology/demand | Physical/chemical degradation over time |
| Physical condition | Item may be physically sound | Item has physically worsened |
| Recoverability | Sold as-is / scrap / returned | Usually scrap or salvage value only |

**Treatment of obsolete stores:** The material should be identified and, if it cannot be used, its **realisable (scrap/disposal) value** is set off and the **irrecoverable loss written off to the Costing Profit & Loss Account** as it is an abnormal loss not attributable to any specific job. It should not be loaded onto good production.

**Measures to control obsolescence:** (i) Fixing and reviewing **maximum and reorder levels** and periodic review of slow/non-moving items through an **ABC / inventory-ageing analysis**; (ii) **standardisation and variety reduction** of materials and prompt disposal of items flagged as non-moving so that capital is not locked up.

**Conclusion:** Obsolescence stems from external change while deterioration is physical decay; the unrecoverable cost of obsolete stores is an abnormal loss written off to the Costing P&L, and disciplined stock-level control and standardisation minimise its incidence.

*(Full-marks tip: State the crisp cause-based distinction and the write-off to Costing P&L (net of realisable value); merely defining the two terms without the accounting treatment caps the marks.)*

### Q6. Ch: Material Cost — Non-moving / Ageing Analysis (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** A company classifies its stores by age. From the ageing schedule below, identify the value of **non-moving stock** (no issue for more than 12 months), compute the **percentage of non-moving stock to total stock value**, and state the accounting/managerial action for each ageing bucket.

| Age of stock | No. of items | Value (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| 0–3 months | 120 | 6,00,000 |
| 3–6 months | 60 | 2,40,000 |
| 6–12 months | 30 | 90,000 |
| 12–24 months | 15 | 45,000 |
| Above 24 months | 8 | 25,000 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Total stock value**
= 6,00,000 + 2,40,000 + 90,000 + 45,000 + 25,000 = **₹10,00,000**

**WN-2: Non-moving stock (no issue > 12 months)**
= 45,000 (12–24 m) + 25,000 (above 24 m) = **₹70,000**

**WN-3: Percentage of non-moving stock**
= (70,000 ÷ 10,00,000) × 100 = **7%**

**Statement of Inventory Ageing and Action**

| Ageing bucket | Value (₹) | % of total | Classification | Suggested action |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0–3 months | 6,00,000 | 60% | Fast-moving | Normal reorder control |
| 3–6 months | 2,40,000 | 24% | Normal-moving | Routine monitoring |
| 6–12 months | 90,000 | 9% | Slow-moving | Review reorder levels; restrict fresh purchase |
| 12–24 months | 45,000 | 4.5% | Non-moving | Investigate; attempt alternate use/transfer |
| Above 24 months | 25,000 | 2.5% | Non-moving/obsolete | Provide/write down to realisable value; dispose |

**Answer:** Non-moving stock = ₹70,000, being **7%** of total stock value; the ₹25,000 held over 24 months is likely obsolete and should be written down to net realisable value, with the loss charged to the Costing P&L.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner wants the non-moving cut-off (>12 months) applied correctly and a bucket-wise action column; treating 6–12 month stock as non-moving, or forgetting the write-down of the >24-month bucket, loses marks.)*

### Q7. Ch: Material Cost — Input-Output Ratio & Material Yield (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** In a process, **1,200 kg** of raw material costing **₹48 per kg** was introduced. Normal loss is **10%** of input. Actual output obtained was **1,020 kg**. You are required to compute the **Input-Output Ratio**, the **material yield variance in physical and money terms**, and comment on efficiency.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Expected (standard) output**
Normal loss = 10% of 1,200 = 120 kg → Expected output = 1,200 − 120 = **1,080 kg**.

**WN-2: Input-Output Ratio (actual)**
= (Input ÷ Output) = 1,200 ÷ 1,020 = **1.176 : 1** (i.e. 117.6% of output).
Standard input-output ratio = 1,200 ÷ 1,080 = **1.111 : 1**.

**WN-3: Yield / abnormal loss in quantity**
Actual output 1,020 kg vs expected 1,080 kg → shortfall = **60 kg (abnormal loss)**.

**WN-4: Cost per kg of good output (standard)**
Material cost = 1,200 × ₹48 = ₹57,600. Standard cost per kg of good output = 57,600 ÷ 1,080 = **₹53.33 per kg**.

**WN-5: Material yield variance (money)**
= (Actual yield − Standard yield) × Standard cost per unit of output
= (1,020 − 1,080) × ₹53.33 = − ₹3,200 (Adverse)

**Statement Showing Yield Performance**

| Particulars | Amount |
|---|---|
| Input introduced | 1,200 kg |
| Standard (expected) output | 1,080 kg |
| Actual output | 1,020 kg |
| Actual Input-Output Ratio | 1.176 : 1 |
| Standard Input-Output Ratio | 1.111 : 1 |
| Abnormal loss (physical) | 60 kg |
| Material yield variance (₹) | ₹3,200 (A) |

**Answer:** Input-Output Ratio = **1.176 : 1** against a standard of 1.111 : 1; there is an abnormal loss of **60 kg** and an adverse material yield variance of **₹3,200 (A)**, indicating below-standard yield efficiency.

*(Full-marks tip: Base yield variance on the **standard cost per unit of good output** and label F/A correctly; students often use cost per kg of input (₹48) instead of ₹53.33 per kg of output, which understates the variance.)*

### Q8. Ch: Material Cost — Treatment of Normal & Abnormal Loss of Materials (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State how the following are treated in cost accounts: (a) **normal loss of material in stores** (e.g. evaporation, breaking of bulk); (b) **abnormal loss of material** (e.g. fire, theft); (c) **cost of special packing** required by a customer; (d) **materials scrapped due to obsolescence**.

**Answer:** The costing treatment of each item is governed by whether the item is a **normal, unavoidable cost of production** (to be absorbed by good output) or an **abnormal loss** (to be excluded from cost and charged to the Costing Profit & Loss Account):

| Item | Cost accounting treatment |
|---|---|
| (a) Normal stores loss (evaporation, breaking bulk) | Unavoidable; **absorbed by good units** by inflating the issue price of the remaining material (spread over quantity actually received/issued). |
| (b) Abnormal loss (fire, theft) | Not a cost of production; **charged to the Costing P&L Account** at cost, net of any insurance/salvage recovery. |
| (c) Special packing at customer's request | A **direct expense** traceable to that order; **charged directly to the specific job/order** (part of selling/distribution or direct cost as the case may be). |
| (d) Materials scrapped due to obsolescence | Realisable value credited; the **irrecoverable balance written off to the Costing P&L** as an abnormal loss. |

**Conclusion:** Normal, unavoidable losses are absorbed by good production, whereas abnormal losses and obsolescence write-offs are excluded from product cost and taken to the Costing P&L; customer-specific packing is a direct charge to that order.

*(Full-marks tip: The mark scheme keys on the normal-vs-abnormal distinction and naming the **Costing P&L** for abnormal items; writing "add to overhead" for abnormal loss is a standard deduction.)*

### Q9. Ch: Material Cost — Consumption Ratios & Reorder Control (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** A manufacturer uses component 'Z'. The following data is available. Compute (i) **Reorder Level**, (ii) **Minimum Level**, (iii) **Maximum Level**, (iv) **Average Stock Level**, and (v) the **Inventory Turnover Ratio** given annual consumption is **valued at ₹57,60,000** and average stock is to be taken from (iv) valued at **₹120 per unit**.

| Particulars | Value |
|---|---|
| Maximum consumption | 900 units/week |
| Minimum consumption | 500 units/week |
| Normal consumption | 700 units/week |
| Reorder quantity | 8,000 units |
| Reorder period (lead time) | 4 to 6 weeks |

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Reorder Level (ROL)**
= Maximum consumption × Maximum reorder period = 900 × 6 = **5,400 units**

**WN-2: Minimum Level**
= ROL − (Normal consumption × Normal reorder period)
Normal reorder period = (4 + 6)/2 = 5 weeks
= 5,400 − (700 × 5) = 5,400 − 3,500 = **1,900 units**

**WN-3: Maximum Level**
= ROL + ROQ − (Minimum consumption × Minimum reorder period)
= 5,400 + 8,000 − (500 × 4) = 13,400 − 2,000 = **11,400 units**

**WN-4: Average Stock Level**
= Minimum level + ½ × ROQ = 1,900 + ½ × 8,000 = 1,900 + 4,000 = **5,900 units**
(OR = (Maximum + Minimum) ÷ 2 = (11,400 + 1,900)/2 = 6,650 units)

**WN-5: Inventory Turnover Ratio**
Average stock value (using ½ ROQ method) = 5,900 × ₹120 = ₹7,08,000
ITR = 57,60,000 ÷ 7,08,000 = **8.14 times**

**Statement of Stock Levels**

| Level | Units |
|---|---|
| Reorder Level | 5,400 |
| Minimum Level | 1,900 |
| Maximum Level | 11,400 |
| Average Stock Level (Min + ½ ROQ) | 5,900 |
| Inventory Turnover Ratio | 8.14 times |

**Answer:** ROL = 5,400 units; Minimum = 1,900 units; Maximum = 11,400 units; Average = 5,900 units; ITR ≈ **8.14 times**.

*(Full-marks tip: ICAI accepts either average-stock formula but marks demand consistency; the classic error is using minimum consumption/period in ROL — always use **maximum × maximum** for ROL and **minimum × minimum** for the maximum-level deduction.)*

### Q10. Ch: Employee (Labour) Cost — Overtime: Own Convenience vs Customer Request (Marks: 8) [Application]
**Question:** During a month a worker put in **200 normal hours** at ₹50 per hour plus **40 hours of overtime**. Overtime is paid at **double** the normal rate (i.e. normal ₹50 + overtime premium ₹50 per OT hour). Of the 40 OT hours: **24 hours** were worked to complete a **rush order specifically requested by a customer** who agreed to bear the extra cost; **10 hours** arose from **general pressure of work / the firm's own scheduling**; and **6 hours** were due to the worker's **own inefficiency** (making good defective work). Show the treatment of the total wages, splitting the **overtime premium** appropriately.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Basic wages (all hours at normal rate)**
Total hours = 200 + 40 = 240 hours × ₹50 = **₹12,000** (this is the normal-rate element and is direct wages of the job).

**WN-2: Overtime premium (extra ₹50 per OT hour)**
Total OT premium = 40 × ₹50 = **₹2,000**, to be split by cause:

| Cause of overtime | OT hours | Premium @ ₹50 (₹) | Treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Customer's specific request (rush order) | 24 | 1,200 | Charged **directly to that job/order** as direct cost |
| General pressure of work (firm's convenience) | 10 | 500 | Treated as **factory overhead** (apportioned over all jobs) |
| Worker's own inefficiency/defective work | 6 | 300 | **Abnormal — charged to Costing P&L** |
| **Total** | **40** | **2,000** | |

**Statement Showing Treatment of Wages**

| Element | Amount (₹) | Charged to |
|---|---|---|
| Normal wages 240 hrs × ₹50 | 12,000 | Direct wages of jobs (240 hrs at normal rate) |
| OT premium — customer request | 1,200 | Direct to the specific job |
| OT premium — general pressure | 500 | Factory overhead |
| OT premium — worker inefficiency | 300 | Costing P&L (abnormal) |
| **Total wages payable** | **14,000** | |

**Answer:** Total wages = **₹14,000**. Of the ₹2,000 overtime premium: **₹1,200 charged directly to the customer's job**, **₹500 to factory overhead**, and **₹300 written off to the Costing P&L** as abnormal.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner wants the OT **premium** (not the whole OT wage) split by cause: customer-request → direct to job; firm's general convenience → overhead; inefficiency/abnormal → Costing P&L. Charging the entire ₹2,000 to one head is the usual mistake.)*

### Q11. Ch: Employee (Labour) Cost — Overtime Premium: Theory & Treatment (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** What is **overtime premium**? Explain the circumstances that decide whether overtime premium is charged to (a) the specific job, (b) factory overhead, or (c) the Costing Profit & Loss Account.

**Answer:** **Overtime** is the work done **beyond the normal working hours** fixed by statute or agreement; under the Factories Act the worker is entitled to wages at **twice the ordinary rate** for such hours. The **overtime premium** is the **extra amount paid over and above the normal wage rate** for the overtime hours (i.e. only the excess, not the whole overtime payment).

The treatment of the **premium portion** depends on the **reason** the overtime was worked:

| Circumstance / reason for overtime | Treatment of overtime premium |
|---|---|
| (a) Worked at the **specific request of a customer** to complete his order early | Charged **directly to that job/order** as a direct cost |
| (b) Worked due to **general pressure of work / seasonal load** for the factory as a whole | Treated as **factory (production) overhead** and apportioned over all jobs |
| (c) Arising from **abnormal causes** — worker's inefficiency, breakdown, fire, flood, negligence | Charged to the **Costing Profit & Loss Account** as an abnormal item |

**Conclusion:** The **normal-rate** element of overtime hours is always direct wages; only the **premium** requires cause-based classification — direct to the job when at customer's request, overhead when for general convenience, and Costing P&L when the cause is abnormal.

*(Full-marks tip: Define premium as the **excess over normal rate** only, and give all three cause-based treatments in a table; equating "overtime" with "overtime premium" is a common error that loses marks.)*

### Q12. Ch: Employee (Labour) Cost — Learning Curve Effect on Labour Time (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** A firm's workforce experiences an **80% learning curve**. The **first batch** of a new product took **1,000 direct labour hours**. Labour is paid **₹60 per hour** and variable overhead is **₹25 per labour hour**. You are required to compute: (i) the cumulative average time and total time for **2, 4 and 8 batches**; (ii) the **labour hours required for the 8th batch alone** (i.e. incremental time for the 5th to 8th batches); and (iii) the **labour + variable overhead cost** of producing the first 8 batches.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Learning curve principle**
Under an 80% curve, every time cumulative output **doubles**, the **cumulative average time per batch falls to 80%** of the previous average.

**WN-2: Cumulative average time per batch**
| Cumulative batches | Cumulative avg time per batch (hours) |
|---|---|
| 1 | 1,000 |
| 2 | 1,000 × 0.80 = 800 |
| 4 | 800 × 0.80 = 640 |
| 8 | 640 × 0.80 = 512 |

**WN-3: Total (cumulative) time**
| Cumulative batches | Total time (hrs) = avg × batches |
|---|---|
| 2 | 800 × 2 = 1,600 |
| 4 | 640 × 4 = 2,560 |
| 8 | 512 × 8 = 4,096 |

**WN-4: Time for 5th–8th batches (the "next four" after 4)**
= Total time for 8 − Total time for 4 = 4,096 − 2,560 = **1,536 hours** (for batches 5 to 8 together).

**WN-5: Cost of first 8 batches**
Total labour hours for 8 batches = 4,096
- Labour cost = 4,096 × ₹60 = ₹2,45,760
- Variable overhead = 4,096 × ₹25 = ₹1,02,400
- **Total = ₹3,48,160**

**Statement Showing Labour Time and Cost (80% Learning Curve)**

| Particulars | Amount |
|---|---|
| Cumulative avg time — 2 batches | 800 hrs |
| Cumulative avg time — 4 batches | 640 hrs |
| Cumulative avg time — 8 batches | 512 hrs |
| Total time — 8 batches | 4,096 hrs |
| Time for batches 5–8 | 1,536 hrs |
| Labour cost (4,096 × ₹60) | ₹2,45,760 |
| Variable overhead (4,096 × ₹25) | ₹1,02,400 |
| **Total labour + variable overhead** | **₹3,48,160** |

**Answer:** Total time for 8 batches = **4,096 hours**; batches 5–8 take **1,536 hours**; total labour + variable overhead cost of first 8 batches = **₹3,48,160**.

*(Full-marks tip: The curve applies to **cumulative average** time and only at **doublings** of output; multiply average by the number of batches to get total, then difference totals for incremental blocks. Applying 80% to the incremental (marginal) time instead of the cumulative average is the classic error.)*

### Q14. Ch: Employee (Labour) Cost — Casual vs Contract Labour Cost (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between **casual labour** and **labour engaged through a contractor (contract labour)**. Explain how the cost of each is dealt with in cost accounts, and state one control concern for each.

**Answer:** **Casual labour** (often called "**badli**" or daily-rated workers) is labour engaged **directly by the employer on a day-to-day / temporary basis** to meet a sudden or short-term increase in work; such workers are paid **directly** by the firm, usually in cash on a daily basis. **Contract labour** is labour supplied **through an intermediary contractor** who engages, controls and pays the workers; the firm pays the **contractor a lump sum or a rate** and does not employ the workers directly.

| Basis | Casual labour | Contract labour |
|---|---|---|
| Engaged by | The firm directly | An outside contractor |
| Paid by | The firm (daily/cash) | The contractor (firm pays the contractor) |
| Employer–employee relation | With the firm | With the contractor |
| Statutory compliance | Firm's responsibility | Primarily contractor's (firm is principal employer) |

**Cost treatment:** Where casual or contract labour can be **identified with a specific job/department**, its cost is treated as **direct labour** of that job; where it is engaged for general or indirect activities (maintenance, material handling) it is treated as **indirect labour / overhead** and apportioned. Payment to the contractor is booked on the basis of the contractor's bill and allocated to the benefiting cost centre.

**Control concerns:** For **casual labour** — the risk of **"dummy/ghost" workers** and inflated muster rolls, controlled by proper time-keeping and independent payment; for **contract labour** — the risk of **over-billing / poor output quality** and the firm's exposure as **principal employer** for the contractor's statutory dues, controlled by contract terms and verification of work done.

**Conclusion:** Casual labour is directly employed and paid by the firm, contract labour is routed through a contractor; both are charged as direct or indirect cost according to traceability, with ghost-worker control for casual labour and billing/statutory control for contract labour.

*(Full-marks tip: Bring out the **direct engagement vs through-contractor** relationship and the direct/indirect cost split; naming a distinct control risk for each earns the last mark.)*

### Q19. Ch: Overheads – Absorption Costing — Pre-determined vs Actual Overhead Rate (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between a **pre-determined (budgeted) overhead absorption rate** and an **actual (historical) overhead absorption rate**, and state why industry generally prefers the pre-determined rate.

**Answer:**
An **overhead absorption rate** is the rate at which overheads are charged to cost units, computed as budgeted/actual overheads divided by the budgeted/actual quantum of the absorption base (labour hours, machine hours, etc.).

A **pre-determined overhead rate** is computed **in advance of the accounting period** using budgeted overheads and the budgeted base — Pre-determined rate = Budgeted overheads ÷ Budgeted base. An **actual (historical) rate** is computed **after the period is over** from figures actually incurred — Actual rate = Actual overheads ÷ Actual base.

| Basis | Pre-determined rate | Actual rate |
|---|---|---|
| Timing of computation | Before the period begins | Only after the period ends |
| Data used | Estimated/budgeted figures | Actual/historical figures |
| Availability for pricing & quotations | Immediately available for prompt costing/quotations | Delayed until books are closed |
| Effect of seasonal/monthly fluctuation | Smoothed out — a stable, uniform rate | Distorted by monthly volume & cost swings |
| Over/under absorption | Arises (difference must be adjusted) | Does not arise (rate always absorbs full cost) |

Industry prefers the **pre-determined rate** because prompt cost ascertainment, price quotations and tender submissions cannot wait until the period closes; it also eliminates the distortion that low-output or high-repair months would otherwise cause in the actual rate. The price paid is that a difference between overhead absorbed (units × pre-determined rate) and overhead actually incurred arises, giving **over- or under-absorption** that is later adjusted by a supplementary rate, or written off to Costing P&L, or carried forward.

**Conclusion:** The pre-determined rate is favoured for its timeliness and stability, accepting over/under-absorption as a manageable trade-off.

*(Full-marks tip: State both formulae and give at least one point on over/under-absorption arising only under the pre-determined rate — examiners look for the reason behind industry preference, not just the definition.)*

### Q20. Ch: Overheads – Absorption Costing — Treatment of Idle Facilities & Fringe Benefits (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain how the following are treated while accounting for overheads: (i) cost of **idle facilities / idle capacity**, and (ii) **fringe benefits** paid to employees.

**Answer:**
**(i) Cost of idle facilities / idle capacity.** Idle facility is that part of the available plant, equipment or space that remains unutilised, and idle capacity is the difference between practical (or normal) capacity and the capacity actually utilised. The costs of idle facilities are essentially the fixed costs (depreciation, rent, insurance, minimum maintenance) of the unused portion. Their treatment depends on the cause:
- If idle capacity arises from **normal, unavoidable** reasons (e.g. routine repairs, setting-up time, statutory holidays), the cost is treated as a **normal manufacturing overhead** and absorbed into production through an inflated overhead rate.
- If it arises from **abnormal** reasons (e.g. lack of demand, strikes, power failure, mismanagement), the cost is **abnormal**, is **excluded from cost of production** and is **charged to the Costing Profit & Loss Account** so that product cost is not distorted.
- If it is due to **seasonal** factors, the cost is charged to production through a **supplementary/inflated overhead rate**.

**(ii) Fringe benefits.** Fringe benefits are the monetary and non-monetary benefits given to employees over and above their normal wages/salary — e.g. employer's contribution to PF and ESI, gratuity, subsidised canteen, medical and housing benefits, leave travel concession. Their treatment is:
- If the amount is **small/insignificant**, it is treated as **production overhead** and absorbed by an appropriate rate (usually a percentage of wages).
- If the amount is **substantial**, it is collected separately and **recovered as a direct charge** by means of a supplementary rate per labour hour so that each job/product bears its due share.
- Fringe benefits relating to **selling/administration staff** are treated as **administration or selling & distribution overhead**, not as factory overhead.

**Conclusion:** Normal idle-facility cost and modest fringe benefits are absorbed as overhead, whereas abnormal idle cost is written off to Costing P&L and large fringe benefits are recovered through a separate supplementary rate.

*(Full-marks tip: The mark split rewards the normal-vs-abnormal treatment of idle capacity (abnormal → Costing P&L) and stating that fringe benefits go to overhead of the relevant function. Do not simply call fringe benefits "wages".)*

### Q23. Ch: Overheads – Absorption Costing — Blanket vs Departmental Overhead Rate (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** What is a **blanket (single) overhead rate**? State the circumstances in which its use is appropriate and why a **departmental overhead rate** is generally preferred.

**Answer:**
A **blanket overhead rate** (also called a single or plant-wide rate) is **one overhead absorption rate computed for the entire factory** as a whole, without dividing the works into departments or cost centres:

Blanket rate = Overhead cost for the entire factory ÷ Total quantum of the base for the entire factory.

A **departmental overhead rate** is a **separate rate computed for each production department/cost centre**, using that department's own overheads and its own base.

Use of a **blanket rate is appropriate** only where: (i) the factory produces a **single/uniform product**; or (ii) all products pass through **all departments and spend roughly the same proportion of time** in each; or (iii) the concern is **small** and departmentalisation is not cost-effective.

| Basis | Blanket rate | Departmental rate |
|---|---|---|
| Number of rates | One for the whole factory | One per department |
| Accuracy where work-mix differs | Low — distorts job cost | High — each job bears its true share |
| Suitability | Single product / uniform routing | Multiple products, varied routing |

A **departmental rate is preferred** because different products spend different amounts of time in different departments having different overhead intensities; a single rate over-charges jobs that skip costly departments and under-charges jobs that use them heavily, distorting cost and pricing.

**Conclusion:** A blanket rate is acceptable only for a single-product or uniformly-routed factory; otherwise departmental rates give more accurate product costs.

*(Full-marks tip: Give the formula and at least two specific circumstances where blanket rate is valid; the examiner rewards the "different products, different departments" logic that justifies departmental rates.)*

**Question:** Explain the **hierarchy of activities and cost drivers** used in Activity-Based Costing (ABC). Give one example of a cost driver at each level.

**Answer:**
Activity-Based Costing traces overheads to products through the **activities** that consume resources, using **cost drivers** — the factors that cause the cost of an activity to change. To assign costs correctly, ABC classifies activities into a four-level **cost hierarchy** (Cooper & Kaplan):

| Level | Nature of activity | Behaviour of cost | Example cost driver |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Unit-level** | Performed each time a unit is produced | Varies with number of units | Machine hours; direct labour hours; units produced |
| **Batch-level** | Performed each time a batch is made, regardless of units in the batch | Varies with number of batches | No. of set-ups; no. of purchase orders; no. of inspections |
| **Product- (product-sustaining) level** | Performed to enable a product line to exist | Varies with number of products, not units/batches | No. of product designs; engineering change notices; no. of BOMs maintained |
| **Facility- (facility-sustaining) level** | Performed to sustain the plant as a whole | Fixed; cannot be traced to any product | Factory rent, depreciation of building, plant security (absorbed on an arbitrary basis) |

The significance of the hierarchy is that **only unit-level cost varies with volume**; batch- and product-level costs vary with the diversity and complexity of operations, not with volume — which is precisely what traditional volume-based absorption ignores. Facility-level costs cannot be meaningfully driven to products and are apportioned on some reasonable basis or treated as period cost.

**Conclusion:** Recognising the four-level hierarchy lets ABC charge batch- and product-sustaining overheads to the products that actually cause them, giving more accurate cost than a single volume-based rate.

*(Full-marks tip: Name all four levels with a distinct example driver for each and state the key insight that only unit-level cost is volume-driven. Confusing batch-level and product-level examples is the usual mark loss.)*

### Q27. Ch: Activity-Based Costing — Meaning, Benefits & Limitations (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** State the **benefits** and **limitations** of Activity-Based Costing (ABC) and indicate the situations in which ABC is **most useful**.

**Answer:**
**Activity-Based Costing** is a costing system that identifies the **activities** performed in an organisation, accumulates overheads into **activity cost pools**, and assigns them to cost objects using **cost drivers** that reflect actual consumption of each activity.

**Benefits of ABC:**
1. **More accurate product cost** — overheads are traced to products in proportion to the activities they actually consume, removing the volume-based cross-subsidy of traditional absorption.
2. **Better pricing, product-mix and make-or-buy decisions** flow from the more reliable costs.
3. **Cost control through activity/cost-driver analysis** — highlighting non-value-adding activities enables cost reduction (basis of Activity-Based Management).
4. **Fair overhead absorption** where products differ widely in volume and complexity.
5. **Useful in service industry** where overheads dominate and there is no obvious volume base.

**Limitations of ABC:**
1. **Expensive to implement and maintain** — identifying activities, drivers and collecting data is costly.
2. **Selection of cost drivers is subjective**; a wrong driver distorts cost.
3. **Facility-level costs** still cannot be driven to products and must be apportioned arbitrarily.
4. **Not cost-effective** for a firm where overheads are a small part of total cost or the product range is uniform.

**Situations where ABC is most useful:** where overheads form a **large proportion of total cost**; where the firm makes a **wide, diverse range** of products differing in volume and complexity; where products place **very different demands** on support resources; and in **service organisations** (banks, hospitals, transport) lacking a natural volume base.

**Conclusion:** ABC delivers accuracy and cost-control insight where overheads are high and products diverse, but its data-collection cost makes it unsuitable for simple, single-product, low-overhead operations.

*(Full-marks tip: Balance benefits with limitations and finish with the "high overhead + diverse products" condition — examiners deduct if only advantages are given without the cost/subjectivity limitations.)*

### Q29. Ch: Cost Accounting Systems & Reconciliation — Integrated vs Non-Integrated Accounting (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between **Integrated Accounting System** and **Non-Integrated (Cost Ledger) Accounting System**. State any two advantages of an integrated system.

**Answer:**
Under a **Non-Integrated (Cost Ledger) Accounting System**, the cost accounts are maintained **separately and independently** of the financial accounts. As cost books have no personal, real or nominal accounts of the financial system, a **Cost Ledger Control Account (also called General Ledger Adjustment Account)** is opened to complete the double entry and make the cost ledger self-balancing. Two separate sets of books therefore exist, and their profits must be **reconciled**.

Under an **Integrated (or Integral) Accounting System**, cost and financial accounts are **merged into one single set of books**, so that both cost information and financial information are drawn from the same records; no Cost Ledger Control Account is needed, and **no reconciliation is required** because there is only one profit figure.

| Basis | Non-Integrated | Integrated |
|---|---|---|
| Sets of books | Two separate sets | One common set |
| Cost Ledger / General Ledger Adjustment A/c | Required (self-balancing) | Not required |
| Reconciliation of profit | Necessary | Not necessary — single profit |
| Duplication of effort | Present | Eliminated |
| Cost of maintenance | Higher | Lower |

**Advantages of an integrated system:** (i) **No reconciliation** is needed as there is a single profit figure, saving time and effort; (ii) it **avoids duplication** of recording, so the system is **economical** and produces information more promptly, with better coordination between the cost and financial functions.

**Conclusion:** A non-integrated system keeps two self-balancing ledgers requiring periodic reconciliation, whereas an integrated system unifies both into one set, eliminating reconciliation and duplication.

*(Full-marks tip: Name the Cost Ledger Control / General Ledger Adjustment Account as the device that makes the non-integrated cost ledger self-balancing, and state clearly that reconciliation is needed only under the non-integrated system.)*

### Q30. Ch: Cost Accounting Systems & Reconciliation — Causes of Difference in Profit (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** Reconciliation of cost and financial profit is required only under a non-integrated system. Enumerate the **causes** that give rise to a difference between the profit shown by the cost accounts and that shown by the financial accounts, classifying them appropriately. Pay particular attention to **notional items** and **stock-valuation differences**.

**Answer:**
The two sets of books differ because certain items are recorded in only one set, or the same item is valued differently. The causes are grouped as follows:

**(A) Items shown only in Financial Accounts (purely financial, not in cost books):**
- **Purely financial charges** — loss on sale of investments/fixed assets, interest on loans/debentures paid, discount on debentures/shares written off, fines and penalties, goodwill and preliminary expenses written off, donations. These reduce financial profit but not cost profit.
- **Purely financial incomes** — interest received on bank deposits and investments, dividend received, rent received, profit on sale of fixed assets/investments, transfer fees. These raise financial profit but not cost profit.
- **Appropriations of profit** — income tax, dividend paid, transfer to reserves/sinking fund — appearing only in financial books.

**(B) Notional (imputed) items charged only in Cost Accounts:**
Cost accounts often include **notional charges** to reflect the true economic cost even though no cash is paid — **notional rent** on owned premises, **notional interest on own capital**, and **notional salary of the proprietor**. Because these reduce cost profit but never appear in financial books, they cause a difference and must be added back when moving from cost profit to financial profit.

**(C) Items treated differently in the two sets (different amounts):**
- **Stock valuation differences** — financial accounts value stock as per **AS 2** (cost or NRV, whichever is lower), whereas cost accounts may value stock at cost using FIFO/LIFO/weighted average or at a standard/pre-determined price. A higher closing-stock value in one set raises that set's profit; a higher opening-stock value lowers it.
- **Overhead absorption differences** — under/over-absorption of overheads in cost books (on pre-determined rates) versus actual overheads in financial books.
- **Different bases of depreciation** — e.g. straight-line in one, WDV or machine-hour in the other.

**Conclusion:** Differences arise from purely financial items (in financial books only), notional charges (in cost books only) and items valued differently — chiefly stock valuation and overhead absorption; reconciliation adjusts for each to tie the two profits.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner specifically rewards the three-fold classification and correctly placing notional charges as "cost books only" and stock/overhead as "valued differently". Losing marks is common when candidates forget notional items or list stock valuation under the wrong head.)*

### Q34. Ch: Cost Accounting Systems & Reconciliation — When Reconciliation Is/Isn't Needed (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** "Reconciliation of cost and financial accounts is not necessary in all cases." Comment, and state the purposes served by reconciliation where it is required.

**Answer:**
The statement is **correct**. Reconciliation of cost and financial accounts is **necessary only where the two are maintained as separate sets of books, i.e. under a Non-Integrated (Cost Ledger) Accounting System**. There, cost profit and financial profit are computed independently and usually differ, so a reconciliation is required to explain and tie up the difference.

Where an **Integrated (Integral) Accounting System** is followed, cost and financial records are kept in **one common set of books** producing a **single profit figure**; consequently **no reconciliation is necessary** — the very cause of the difference is absent.

**Purposes served by reconciliation (where required):**
1. To **verify the arithmetical and conceptual accuracy** of both sets of books by explaining every item of difference.
2. To **identify the reasons** for the variation — purely financial items, notional charges, and differences in stock valuation, overhead absorption and depreciation.
3. To ensure the **reliability of the cost data** used for decision-making, pricing and control.
4. To bring to light **under- or over-absorption of overheads** and errors, enabling corrective action.
5. To promote **coordination and cross-check** between the costing and financial departments.

**Conclusion:** Reconciliation is dispensed with under an integrated system but, where two sets of books exist, it is essential to validate the accounts and explain the difference between the two profit figures.

*(Full-marks tip: The key mark lies in linking "not necessary" to the integrated system (single profit) and "necessary" to the non-integrated system, then listing at least three purposes. Merely defining reconciliation without the integrated/non-integrated distinction loses marks.)*

### Q36. Ch: Cost Sheet — Treatment of Research & Development Cost and Selling/Distribution Overheads (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the correct treatment of (a) Research and Development costs and (b) Selling and Distribution overheads while preparing a Cost Sheet, and state which items are excluded from the Cost Sheet altogether.

**Answer:**
The Cost Sheet records only those costs that are incurred for and are traceable to the production and sale of goods; costs of a purely financial or appropriation nature are excluded.

**(a) Research and Development (R&D) cost** — As per **CAS-18 (Cost Accounting Standard on Research and Development Costs)**, R&D cost must be classified by its purpose. **Research or development cost incurred for improving the existing process or the existing product, or to find new uses for an existing product,** is treated as a **production/works-related cost and is charged to the Cost of Production** of the period in which it is incurred. **Development cost incurred for a brand-new product or process that has not yet reached commercial production** is not attributable to current output; it is either **deferred/capitalised** to be absorbed over the future output of that product, or, if the project is abandoned or the outcome is uncertain, it is **charged to the Costing Profit & Loss Account** and kept out of the Cost Sheet. R&D undertaken **at the specific request of a customer** is charged directly to that job/contract.

**(b) Selling and Distribution overheads** — These are the indirect costs incurred to **create demand and to secure and execute orders** (selling) and to **make the goods available to customers** (distribution). They are added **after the Cost of Production**, i.e. to arrive at the **Cost of Sales**, and are recovered only in respect of the **units actually sold** (never on closing stock of finished goods, which is valued at Cost of Production). Examples: salesmen's salaries and commission, advertisement, market research, carriage outward, warehouse and delivery-van running costs, bad debts (normal).

**Items excluded from the Cost Sheet altogether** — Purely financial and appropriation items: interest on capital/loans and dividends, income-tax, donations, loss/profit on sale of fixed assets or investments, goodwill/preliminary expenses written off, transfers to general reserve, and abnormal losses (which are transferred to the Costing P&L A/c, not spread over cost).

| Item | Treatment in Cost Sheet |
|---|---|
| R&D on existing product/process | Included in Cost of Production |
| R&D on a new (not-yet-commercial) product | Deferred over future output, or charged to Costing P&L if abandoned |
| Selling & Distribution OH | Added to Cost of Production → Cost of Sales, on units sold only |
| Interest, income-tax, donations, loss on sale of asset | Excluded (financial/appropriation items) |

**Conclusion:** R&D is capitalised or expensed according to whether it benefits current or future output; selling and distribution overheads are charged only against goods sold; and financial/appropriation items never enter the Cost Sheet.

*(Full-marks tip: Cite CAS-18 by name and draw the current-benefit vs future-benefit distinction for R&D — that is the differentiator the examiner looks for. Do not merely list examples of selling overheads; state the rule that they attach to units sold, not to closing stock.)*

### Q39. Ch: Cost Sheet — Items Excluded from Cost Accounts / Cost Sheet vs Financial P&L (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State any four items that appear in the Financial Profit & Loss Account but are excluded from the Cost Sheet / cost accounts, and explain briefly why each is excluded.

**Answer:**
Cost accounts capture only costs incurred in, and traceable to, the manufacture and sale of products; items that are **financial, appropriations of profit, or abnormal** in nature are kept out because including them would distort the true cost of the product.

| Item excluded | Reason for exclusion |
|---|---|
| Interest on loans/capital and dividends paid | A financial charge on the arrangement of funds, not a cost of manufacture; treated as a matter of finance, not of costing. |
| Income-tax and provision for taxation | An appropriation of profit levied by statute, not a cost of producing or selling goods. |
| Loss (or profit) on sale of fixed assets/investments | A capital/financial transaction unconnected with normal operations. |
| Donations, charity and transfers to reserves | Voluntary or appropriation items; they distribute profit rather than build product cost. |
| Abnormal losses (e.g. loss by fire, abnormal wastage) | Non-recurring and outside normal operating efficiency; charged to Costing P&L so as not to inflate normal product cost. |
| Goodwill / preliminary expenses / discount on issue written off | Purely financial write-offs of a fictitious/intangible nature. |

Because these are excluded from cost accounts but present in financial accounts, the profit shown by the two sets of books differs, and a **Cost–Financial Reconciliation Statement** is prepared to bridge the gap.

**Conclusion:** Any financial, appropriation or abnormal item is excluded from the Cost Sheet to keep the reported product cost a true reflection of normal manufacturing and selling activity.

### Q41. Ch: Unit & Batch Costing — Economic Batch Quantity: Concept and Trade-off (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** What is meant by **Economic Batch Quantity (EBQ)** in batch costing? State the formula, explain the two opposing costs it balances, and how a change in set-up cost affects the EBQ.

**Answer:**
**Batch costing** is a form of specific-order costing used where articles are manufactured in identifiable lots or **batches**, each batch being treated as a cost unit (e.g. pharmaceuticals, components, bakery, toys). Since each batch requires a **set-up** of machines before production, the size of the batch influences cost.

**Economic Batch Quantity** is that batch size at which the **total of set-up cost and carrying (holding) cost is minimum.** It is the batch-production analogue of the Economic Order Quantity.

Formula:
**EBQ = √(2 × D × S ÷ C)**, where D = annual demand/output of the component, S = set-up cost per batch, and C = carrying cost per unit per annum (C may be expressed as an absolute amount, or as the cost per unit × carrying-cost rate %).

**The two opposing costs:**

| Cost | Behaviour with larger batches |
|---|---|
| **Set-up (batch) cost** — cost of preparing machines for each batch | *Falls* — a bigger batch means fewer batches per year, hence fewer set-ups. |
| **Carrying / holding cost** — storage, interest, insurance, obsolescence on the average inventory | *Rises* — a bigger batch means more units held in stock on average. |

At the EBQ these two costs are equal, and total cost is minimised.

**Effect of a change in set-up cost:** Since S is in the numerator under the square root, EBQ varies with the **square root** of the set-up cost. If set-up cost **increases four-fold**, EBQ **doubles** (√4 = 2); if set-up cost is **halved**, EBQ falls to about **0.707** of its former level. Thus reducing set-up cost (e.g. through quick-changeover techniques) lowers the economic batch size and enables smaller, more frequent runs.

**Conclusion:** EBQ = √(2DS ÷ C) minimises the combined set-up and carrying cost, and because S sits under the square root, the batch size responds only to the square root of any change in set-up cost.

*(Full-marks tip: The distinguishing content is the square-root sensitivity — state that a 4× change in set-up cost only doubles EBQ. Simply quoting the formula without explaining the two opposing costs and their behaviour caps you at half marks.)*

### Q43. Ch: Unit & Batch Costing — Economic Batch Quantity with Planned Shortages (Back-orders) (Marks: 10) [Problem]

= √(2 × 15,000 × 120 ÷ 2) × √((2 + 8) ÷ 8)

= √(18,00,000 × 1.25) = √(22,50,000) = **1,500 units**.

S_max = Q* × C_s ÷ (C_h + C_s) = 1,500 × 8 ÷ 10 = **1,200 units**.

**WN-3: Maximum shortage level (b)**
b = Q* × C_h ÷ (C_h + C_s) = 1,500 × 2 ÷ 10 = **300 units**.
(Check: S_max + b = 1,200 + 300 = 1,500 = Q*. ✓)

**WN-4: Number of set-ups** = D ÷ Q* = 15,000 ÷ 1,500 = **10 set-ups**.

**WN-5: Total relevant cost**

| Cost element | Computation | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|---:|
| Set-up cost | 10 × ₹120 | 1,200 |
| Carrying cost | C_h × S_max² ÷ (2Q) = 2 × 1,200² ÷ (2 × 1,500) = 2 × 14,40,000 ÷ 3,000 | 960 |
| Shortage cost | C_s × b² ÷ (2Q) = 8 × 300² ÷ (2 × 1,500) = 8 × 90,000 ÷ 3,000 | 240 |
| **Total relevant cost** | | **2,400** |

**WN-6: Comparison with the no-shortage model**
Basic EBQ (no shortage) = √(2DS ÷ C_h) = √18,00,000 = 1,341.64 units.
Total cost (no shortage) = √(2DS × C_h) = √(2 × 15,000 × 120 × 2) = √72,00,000 = ₹2,683.28.
Saving by allowing planned shortages = ₹2,683.28 − ₹2,400.00 = **₹283.28 per annum**.

**Answer:** Optimum batch = 1,500 units; maximum inventory = 1,200 units; maximum shortage = 300 units; 10 set-ups per year; minimum total cost = ₹2,400 p.a., a saving of ₹283.28 over the no-shortage policy.

Reconcile the three cost elements (set-up + carrying + shortage) back to the closed-form total — that reconciliation is where the higher marks sit.)*

### Q47. Ch: Job & Contract Costing — Rules for Profit on Incomplete Contracts at Different Stages (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** State the generally accepted rules for determining the amount of profit to be transferred to the Profit & Loss Account on **incomplete contracts** at various stages of completion.

**Answer:**
Because a contract may span several accounting years, the profit on the work done so far must be recognised **prudently** — a part of the notional profit is credited to the Profit & Loss Account and the balance is held back as a **reserve** to cover future contingencies and possible losses. The proportion depends on the **stage of completion**, measured as Work Certified ÷ Contract Price. The rate of cash received (Cash received ÷ Work certified) is applied to keep recognition tied to money realised.

| Stage of completion (Work certified ÷ Contract price) | Profit to be transferred to P&L A/c |
|---|---|
| Less than **¼ (25%)** | **Nil** — completion too small to estimate profit reliably; whole notional profit kept in reserve. |
| **¼ or more but less than ½ (25%–50%)** | **⅓ × Notional Profit × (Cash received ÷ Work certified)** |
| **½ or more but less than ¾/90% (50%–90%)** | **⅔ × Notional Profit × (Cash received ÷ Work certified)** |
| **Near completion (about 90% and above)** | Profit is computed on an **estimated-total-profit** basis, e.g. Estimated Total Profit × (Work certified ÷ Contract price) × (Cash received ÷ Work certified), or × (Cost of work to date ÷ Estimated total cost). |

Two overriding principles apply in every case:
(i) **If a loss is anticipated on the contract as a whole**, the entire foreseeable loss is transferred to the Profit & Loss Account immediately, irrespective of the stage of completion (conservatism).
(ii) The portion of profit not transferred is retained as a **reserve/provision** (deducted from Work-in-progress in the Balance Sheet) to absorb future risks.

**Conclusion:** No profit is taken below 25% completion; ⅓ and ⅔ of the notional profit (scaled by the cash ratio) are taken in the 25–50% and 50–90% bands respectively; and near completion the estimated-total-profit method is used — but any expected overall loss is always provided for in full at once.

*(Full-marks tip: Present the four bands in a table and, crucially, state the two overriding principles (immediate provision for expected total loss, and the reserve for unrecognised profit). Omitting the "anticipated loss provided in full" rule loses a mark even if the bands are perfect.)*

### Q51. Ch: Job & Contract Costing — Job Costing vs Contract Costing; Cost-plus Contracts (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between **Job Costing** and **Contract Costing**, and explain briefly what is meant by a **cost-plus contract**.

**Answer:**
Both job costing and contract costing are forms of **specific-order costing**, in which costs are accumulated for identifiable, distinct units of work carried out to a customer's requirements. A **contract** is essentially a **big job**, and the differences between them are ones of size, location and duration rather than of principle.

| Basis | Job Costing | Contract Costing |
|---|---|---|
| Size of work | Comparatively small jobs | Large undertakings |
| Location | Carried out inside the factory premises | Executed at the customer's site |
| Duration | Usually completed within a short period / one accounting year | Often spread over more than one accounting year |
| Overheads | A significant share of cost is indirect (factory overheads absorbed) | Most costs are direct to the contract; overheads are a small proportion |
| Profit recognition | Profit recognised on completion of the job | Profit on incomplete contracts recognised in stages, keeping a reserve (prudence) |
| Payment | Usually paid in full on completion | Progress payments made against work certified, subject to retention money |

**Cost-plus contract:** A cost-plus contract is one in which the **contract price is not fixed in advance** but is determined by adding to the **allowable/actual cost of the work an agreed amount or percentage as profit** (i.e. Contract Price = Allowable Cost + Agreed Margin). It is used where the cost cannot be estimated reliably in advance — for example, work of a novel nature, urgent or emergency work, long-duration projects with volatile prices, and government/defence contracts. Its advantage to the contractor is protection against loss from cost increases; its drawback to the customer is a weaker incentive for the contractor to control costs, which is why such contracts define the **allowable costs** carefully and often carry an **escalation clause** and a right of audit.

**Conclusion:** Job and contract costing share the specific-order principle but differ mainly in scale, site, duration and profit recognition; a cost-plus contract fixes the price as allowable cost plus an agreed margin, suiting situations where costs cannot be estimated reliably beforehand.

*(Full-marks tip: Present the distinctions as a table with at least four crisp bases, then define the cost-plus contract with its formula (cost + agreed margin) and one situation where it is used. Adding the customer-side drawback (weak cost control) and the audit/escalation safeguard rounds out the answer for full marks.)*

### Q55. Ch: Process & Operation Costing — Normal vs Abnormal Loss: Concept & Treatment (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between "normal loss" and "abnormal loss" in process costing and state the accounting treatment of each.

**Answer:** In process costing, **loss of units** is inseparable from the manufacturing operation and is classified according to whether it is inherent and unavoidable or unexpected and controllable.

**Normal loss** is the loss which is **unavoidable** and inherent in the process under efficient operating conditions (evaporation, shrinkage, chemical change, unavoidable spoilage). It is anticipated in advance and expressed as a percentage of input. It is a legitimate cost of production: the number of good units is reduced, so the **cost of normal loss is absorbed by the good output**, thereby increasing the cost per good unit. Any scrap realisable on normal-loss units is **credited to the Process Account**.

**Abnormal loss** is the loss which is **avoidable** and arises from unexpected or abnormal conditions (careless handling, sub-standard material, machine breakdown, accidents) in excess of the normal loss. It is **not** treated as a part of the cost of good output; it is valued at the **same cost per unit as good output** and transferred to a separate **Abnormal Loss Account**, whose net amount (after crediting any scrap realised) is written off to the **Costing Profit & Loss Account**.

| Basis | Normal Loss | Abnormal Loss |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Unavoidable / inherent | Avoidable / due to abnormal causes |
| Anticipation | Estimated in advance | Not anticipated |
| Cost borne by | Good units (absorbed) | Costing P&L A/c |
| Valuation | At scrap value only | At full cost per good unit |
| Effect on unit cost | Increases cost per good unit | No effect on cost per good unit |

**Conclusion:** Normal loss is a **product cost** absorbed by good units, whereas abnormal loss is a **period loss** charged to the Costing Profit & Loss Account.

*(Full-marks tip: State the valuation basis for each (normal = scrap value; abnormal = full unit cost) and note that normal-loss scrap is credited to the Process A/c while abnormal-loss scrap is credited to the Abnormal Loss A/c — that distinction is the most-awarded point.)*

### Q59. Ch: Process & Operation Costing — Operation Costing / Process vs Operation Costing (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** What is "operation costing"? How does it differ from "process costing"?

**Answer:** **Operation costing** is a refinement of process costing in which cost is ascertained for each **distinct operation** (an individually identifiable stage of activity, e.g. cutting, drilling, polishing) rather than for a whole process. It is used where a product passes through a number of clearly separable operations and management wants the **cost per operation per unit** for tighter control, comparison of alternative methods, and pricing of conversion work. The cost of each operation is obtained by dividing the total operation cost by the number of units passing through that operation (adjusted for rejections), and the unit cost of the product is the sum of the costs of all operations it undergoes.

| Basis | Process Costing | Operation Costing |
|---|---|---|
| Unit of costing | A whole process | A single operation within a process |
| Degree of analysis | Broader (process-wise) | Finer (operation-wise) |
| Suitability | Continuous, homogeneous output (chemicals, oil) | Repetitive operations, mass production of parts |
| Purpose | Cost per process | Cost per operation for close control & method comparison |

**Conclusion:** Operation costing applies the **same principles** as process costing but at a **finer level of detail** — the operation, rather than the process, is the cost unit.

*(Full-marks tip: Emphasise that operation costing gives **cost per operation** enabling method/efficiency comparison; a distinguishing table with a valid example of each earns the marks.)*

### Q60. Ch: Process & Operation Costing — FIFO vs Average Method: Selection & Effect (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** A cost accountant must choose between the FIFO and the weighted-average method of computing equivalent production. Explain how the two methods differ and advise when each is appropriate, especially when input-cost prices are fluctuating.

**Answer:** Both methods value work-in-progress by converting partly-finished units into **equivalent completed units**, but they treat opening WIP differently.

Under the **weighted-average method**, the cost of opening WIP is **merged** with current-period cost and the total is divided by total equivalent units (which include the whole of the units completed, without deducting the opening-WIP work already done in the previous period). It therefore produces a **single averaged cost** that blends last period's and this period's prices.

Under the **FIFO method**, the opening WIP is treated as a **separate batch** finished first; only the work **done in the current period** (the balance needed to complete opening WIP, plus units started-and-completed, plus closing WIP) is counted, and **only current-period cost** is used for the rate. Consequently FIFO keeps each period's cost distinct.

| Basis | Weighted-Average | FIFO |
|---|---|---|
| Opening WIP cost | Merged with current cost | Kept separate; completed first |
| Cost rate reflects | Blend of old + new prices | Current-period prices only |
| Computation | Simpler | More detailed (3 layers) |
| Best when prices are | Stable | Fluctuating (better cost control) |

**Application/advice:** When input prices are **fluctuating**, FIFO is preferable because current cost is not diluted by last period's cost — this gives a truer picture of current efficiency and better cost control and is consistent with a FIFO physical flow. When prices are **stable** or opening WIP is small, the two methods give almost identical results and the **average method** is chosen for its simplicity.

**Conclusion:** Choose **FIFO when prices fluctuate and control is paramount**; choose the **average method when prices are stable and simplicity is valued.**

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner wants the reasoning that FIFO isolates current-period cost — state this explicitly and link it to fluctuating prices and performance measurement; a bare list of differences without the "when to use" advice loses the application marks.)*

### Q64. Ch: Joint Products & By-Products — Methods of Apportioning Joint Costs (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the principal methods used for apportioning joint costs among joint products.

**Answer:** Joint costs are the costs incurred **up to the split-off point**, at which two or more joint products become separately identifiable; because they are common, they must be apportioned on a rational basis. The principal methods are:

1. **Physical units (quantity) method** — joint cost is apportioned in the ratio of the **physical quantity** (weight, volume, units) of each product at split-off. Simple, but ignores differing selling values, so it may over-cost low-value products.
2. **Average unit cost method** — total joint cost is divided by total units produced to get an average cost per unit, applied uniformly. Suitable only when products are broadly similar.
3. **Survey / weighted-average (points) method** — each product is assigned **weights (points)** reflecting relative size, difficulty, time or importance, and joint cost is apportioned on weighted units. It refines the physical method.
4. **Market (sales) value at split-off method** — joint cost is apportioned in the ratio of the **sales value** of each product at the split-off point; it recognises each product's capacity to bear cost.
5. **Net Realisable Value (reverse-cost) method** — used where products need further processing; joint cost is apportioned in the ratio of **NRV = final sales − further processing cost − selling expenses**.
6. **Constant gross-margin percentage method** — joint cost is apportioned so that **every product shows the same gross-margin percentage** after accounting for further processing costs.

**Conclusion:** Where reliable market values exist, the **market-value/NRV** approach is preferred as it apportions cost according to each product's ability to bear it; physical-units methods are used only where values are unavailable or products are homogeneous.

*(Full-marks tip: List at least four methods with a one-line rationale each and end with a reasoned preference for the value-based methods; naming the methods without explaining the apportionment basis loses half the marks.)*

### Q66. Ch: Joint Products & By-Products — Methods of Accounting for By-Products (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the methods available for treating by-product income/cost in the cost accounts.

**Answer:** A **by-product** is a product of relatively small value that arises incidentally in the course of manufacturing the main product. Its treatment in cost accounts falls into two broad categories:

**(A) Non-cost / sales-value methods** — the by-product is not assigned any share of the joint cost; instead its realisation is used to relieve the cost of the main product:
- **Other income method:** by-product sales are treated as **miscellaneous income** and credited to the Costing P&L A/c (suitable when the by-product value is negligible).
- **Total sales less selling cost:** net sales value of the by-product is **deducted from the total cost** of the main product.
- **Sales value reduced by further processing & selling cost (NRV):** where the by-product needs further processing, its **net realisable value** is credited to the main-product cost.

**(B) Cost methods** — the by-product is charged with a share of cost:
- **Opportunity / replacement-cost method:** where the by-product is **used internally**, it is valued at the price of the material it replaces and credited to the process.
- **Standard cost method:** the by-product is valued at a **predetermined standard cost**, giving stability irrespective of fluctuating market prices.
- **Joint-cost apportionment method:** the by-product is treated almost like a joint product and given a share of joint cost (used when its value is significant).

**Conclusion:** The method chosen depends on the **relative value** of the by-product — negligible-value by-products are credited as other income, while significant or internally-used by-products are valued at replacement/standard cost or given a share of joint cost.

*(Full-marks tip: Group the methods into "non-cost" and "cost" categories and link the choice to the by-product's relative value — that structure and the internal-use (replacement cost) point are the most-awarded elements.)*

### Q67. Ch: Joint Products & By-Products — Constant Gross-Margin Percentage Method (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** A joint process costing ₹6,00,000 yields two joint products which are further processed before sale. Apportion the joint cost by the **constant gross-margin percentage method** so that each product earns the same gross-margin percentage on sales.

| Product | Final sales value (₹) | Further processing cost (₹) |
|---|---:|---:|
| M | 8,00,000 | 1,00,000 |
| N | 4,00,000 | 50,000 |

| Particulars | Product M (₹) | Product N (₹) |

### Q74. Ch: Standard Costing — Theory: Types of Standards & Reconciliation Uses (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** "The type of standard set influences the size and interpretation of variances." Explain the four types of standards used in standard costing and state which is most appropriate for variance analysis and why.

**Answer:** A standard is a predetermined measurable quantity set in defined conditions against which actual performance is compared. Depending on the level of efficiency and the base period assumed, standards are classified into four types.

| Type of Standard | Meaning | Effect on variances |
|---|---|---|
| **Ideal Standard** | Set assuming perfect, ideal conditions — no idle time, breakdown, wastage or spoilage. | Almost always produces large adverse variances; demotivating; rarely attainable, so of limited practical use. |
| **Basic Standard** | A long-term standard left unaltered over years, used only to study trends. | Not useful for current control because it ignores changed conditions; variances mix price-level and efficiency effects. |
| **Current Standard** | Set for a short period, reflecting current conditions, prices and efficiency. | Reflects present circumstances but does not encourage improvement. |
| **Expected / Attainable Standard** | Set on the basis of conditions expected to prevail, allowing for a normal level of unavoidable loss, wastage and machine downtime. | Realistic and motivating; variances genuinely reveal controllable inefficiency. |

The **Expected (Normal/Attainable) standard** is regarded as the most appropriate for meaningful variance analysis because it is neither so tight as to be perpetually adverse (like the ideal) nor so loose as to conceal inefficiency (like the basic). Variances measured against it isolate genuinely controllable deviations, making them a fair basis for management action and performance appraisal.

**Conclusion:** The attainable/expected standard is the preferred basis, as it makes variances realistic, motivating and useful for control.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner expects all four types named with a one-line distinction each, plus a reasoned choice. Merely listing the types without stating why the attainable standard is best loses the application mark.)*

- Total contribution = 40,000 × 15 = ₹6,00,000; Profit = 6,00,000 − 4,00,000 = ₹2,00,000.

**WN-2 — Proposal (a): SP up 8% → new SP = 50 × 1.08 = ₹54:**
- Contribution/unit = 54 − 35 = ₹19; P/V ratio = 19 ÷ 54 = **35.19%**.
- BEP (₹) = 4,00,000 ÷ 0.3519 = ₹11,36,970 (≈).
- Profit = 40,000 × 19 − 4,00,000 = 7,60,000 − 4,00,000 = **₹3,60,000**.

**WN-3 — Proposal (b): VC down ₹3 → new VC = ₹32:**
- Contribution/unit = 50 − 32 = ₹18; P/V ratio = 18 ÷ 50 = **36%**.
- BEP (₹) = 4,00,000 ÷ 0.36 = ₹11,11,111 (≈).
- Profit = 40,000 × 18 − 4,00,000 = 7,20,000 − 4,00,000 = **₹3,20,000**.

**Statement Comparing the Proposals:**

| Particulars | Present | (a) SP +8% | (b) VC −₹3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contribution/unit (₹) | 15 | 19 | 18 |
| P/V ratio | 30% | 35.19% | 36% |
| Break-even sales (₹) | 13,33,333 | 11,36,970 | 11,11,111 |
| Profit (₹) | 2,00,000 | 3,60,000 | 3,20,000 |

**Conclusion:** Proposal (b) gives the **higher P/V ratio (36% vs 35.19%)** and the lowest break-even, but Proposal (a) yields **higher profit (₹3,60,000)** because the price rise adds contribution on the full selling price base. If the sole objective is to improve the P/V ratio, **(b) is best**; if profit maximisation is the goal (and volume holds), **(a) is preferable**.

*(Full-marks tip: The P/V ratio rises fastest when selling price increases OR variable cost falls; a fixed-cost change never alters the P/V ratio (only the BEP and profit). State clearly that fixed-cost proposals cannot improve the P/V ratio — a frequent conceptual error.)*

### Q77. Ch: Marginal Costing — Cash Break-Even Point (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** A firm has annual fixed costs of ₹9,60,000, of which ₹2,40,000 is depreciation and other non-cash charges. Selling price is ₹80 per unit and variable cost is ₹50 per unit. Compute (i) the conventional break-even point in units and value, and (ii) the cash break-even point in units. Also determine the margin between the two and explain its significance.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Contribution:**
- Contribution/unit = 80 − 50 = ₹30; P/V ratio = 30 ÷ 80 = 37.5%.

**WN-2 — Conventional break-even:**
- BEP (units) = Total Fixed Cost ÷ Contribution/unit = 9,60,000 ÷ 30 = **32,000 units**.
- BEP (value) = 32,000 × 80 = **₹25,60,000** (or 9,60,000 ÷ 0.375 = ₹25,60,000). ✔

**WN-3 — Cash break-even:**
- Cash fixed cost = Total fixed cost − Non-cash charges = 9,60,000 − 2,40,000 = ₹7,20,000.
- Cash BEP (units) = Cash Fixed Cost ÷ Contribution/unit = 7,20,000 ÷ 30 = **24,000 units**.

**Statement of Break-even Comparison:**

| Basis | Fixed cost (₹) | BEP (units) | BEP (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conventional | 9,60,000 | 32,000 | 25,60,000 |
| Cash | 7,20,000 | 24,000 | 19,20,000 |
| **Difference** | 2,40,000 | **8,000** | 6,40,000 |

**Significance:** The cash break-even point (24,000 units) is lower than the conventional break-even (32,000 units) because non-cash charges such as depreciation do not require an actual cash outflow. Between 24,000 and 32,000 units the firm makes an accounting loss yet still generates enough contribution to cover all its cash commitments — it remains solvent and can survive a downturn. This makes the cash BEP a useful indicator of short-run survival and liquidity.

**Answer:** Conventional BEP = 32,000 units (₹25,60,000); Cash BEP = 24,000 units; margin = 8,000 units.

*(Full-marks tip: Cash BEP uses only cash fixed costs (deduct depreciation and other non-cash items); the contribution per unit is unchanged. Deducting depreciation from variable cost, or forgetting to deduct it at all, is the usual mistake.)*

### Q78. Ch: Marginal Costing — Export Order vs Local Market (Marks: 8) [Application]
**Question:** A manufacturer has a capacity of 1,20,000 units per annum but currently operates at 90,000 units for the domestic market. Cost and price data per unit are given. An overseas buyer offers to purchase 25,000 units at ₹70 per unit (FOB). Accepting the export order will require additional packing and freight of ₹6 per unit and a one-time export licence & inspection cost of ₹80,000. The domestic price would be unaffected. Advise whether the export order should be accepted, and compute the change in total profit.

| Particulars (per unit) | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Selling price (domestic) | 100 |
| Direct material | 40 |
| Direct labour | 20 |
| Variable overhead | 10 |
| Fixed overhead (₹18,00,000 ÷ 90,000) | 20 |
| Total cost | 90 |
| Profit | 10 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Spare capacity check:**
- Available capacity = 1,20,000 − 90,000 = 30,000 units ≥ 25,000 order units. The order can be met from idle capacity, so existing fixed overhead of ₹18,00,000 is unaffected (a sunk/committed cost for this decision).

**WN-2 — Relevant (marginal) cost per export unit:**
- Direct material 40 + Direct labour 20 + Variable OH 10 = ₹70 base variable cost.
- Add extra packing & freight ₹6 → total variable/incremental cost = ₹76 per unit.

**WN-3 — Contribution from the export order:**

| Particulars | Per unit (₹) | 25,000 units (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| Export price (FOB) | 70 | 17,50,000 |
| Less: Variable cost (40+20+10) | 70 | 17,50,000 |
| Less: Extra packing & freight | 6 | 1,50,000 |
| **Contribution** | **(6)** | **(1,50,000)** |
| Less: One-time licence & inspection | | 80,000 |
| **Net effect on profit** | | **(2,30,000)** |

**Statement Showing Incremental Profitability of Export Order:**

| Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Sales value (25,000 × 70) | 17,50,000 |
| Less: Variable production cost (25,000 × 70) | 17,50,000 |
| Less: Additional packing & freight (25,000 × 6) | 1,50,000 |
| Less: One-time export licence & inspection | 80,000 |
| **Incremental Profit / (Loss)** | **(2,30,000)** |

**Conclusion:** The export price of ₹70 does not even cover the variable production cost of ₹70, and after the extra ₹6 packing/freight each unit yields a **negative contribution of ₹6**. The order produces an incremental loss of ₹2,30,000 and **should be rejected**. (Fixed overhead of ₹20/unit is irrelevant as it is already absorbed by domestic output.)

Here the trap is that ₹70 looks "above cost of ₹90 less fixed 20"; students who wrongly include the ₹20 fixed OH conclude a loss for the wrong reason. ### Q79. Ch: Marginal Costing — Drop a Product Line Decision (Marks: 10) [Application]
**Question:** A company manufactures three products A, B and C. The following results relate to the last year. The management is considering dropping product C because it shows a net loss. Advise whether product C should be discontinued, assuming the fixed costs of ₹3,60,000 are apportioned to products on the basis of sales value and ₹90,000 of the total fixed cost is directly attributable to C and would be saved if C is dropped. Also determine the profit if C is dropped.

| Particulars | A | B | C | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sales (₹) | 6,00,000 | 4,00,000 | 2,00,000 | 12,00,000 |
| Variable cost (₹) | 3,60,000 | 2,60,000 | 1,70,000 | 7,90,000 |
| Fixed cost (apportioned, ₹) | 1,80,000 | 1,20,000 | 60,000 | 3,60,000 |
| Profit / (Loss) (₹) | 60,000 | 20,000 | (30,000) | 50,000 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Contribution of each product:**

| Particulars | A | B | C | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sales (₹) | 6,00,000 | 4,00,000 | 2,00,000 | 12,00,000 |
| Less: Variable cost (₹) | 3,60,000 | 2,60,000 | 1,70,000 | 7,90,000 |
| **Contribution (₹)** | **2,40,000** | **1,40,000** | **30,000** | **4,10,000** |
| P/V ratio | 40% | 35% | 15% | 34.17% |

Product C still earns a **positive contribution of ₹30,000**; the "loss" arises only from apportioned fixed cost.

**WN-2 — Fixed cost behaviour if C is dropped:**
- Total fixed cost = ₹3,60,000.
- Directly attributable to C (avoidable) = ₹90,000 (of which ₹60,000 was apportioned to C and ₹30,000 was borne by the general pool but is specific to C's operations).
- Unavoidable/common fixed cost that remains = 3,60,000 − 90,000 = ₹2,70,000.

**WN-3 — Statement Showing Profit if Product C is Dropped:**

| Particulars | Continue C (₹) | Drop C (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| Contribution: A | 2,40,000 | 2,40,000 |
| Contribution: B | 1,40,000 | 1,40,000 |
| Contribution: C | 30,000 | — |
| **Total Contribution** | **4,10,000** | **3,80,000** |
| Less: Fixed cost | 3,60,000 | 2,70,000 |
| **Profit** | **50,000** | **1,10,000** |

**WN-4 — Reconciliation of the decision (incremental):**
- Contribution lost by dropping C = ₹30,000.
- Fixed cost saved (avoidable) = ₹90,000.
- **Net gain from dropping C = 90,000 − 30,000 = ₹60,000** (profit rises from ₹50,000 to ₹1,10,000). ✔

**Conclusion:** Although C shows a book loss of ₹30,000, it contributes ₹30,000 towards fixed costs. However, because ₹90,000 of fixed cost is **directly avoidable** on dropping C — which exceeds the ₹30,000 contribution lost — **product C should be discontinued**, raising overall profit by ₹60,000 to ₹1,10,000.

**Answer:** Drop product C — profit increases from ₹50,000 to **₹1,10,000** (net gain ₹60,000).

*(Full-marks tip: The decision turns on avoidable fixed cost versus contribution lost, NOT on the apportioned book loss. Had the avoidable fixed cost been only ₹60,000 (the apportioned share), the contribution lost (₹30,000) would be less than the saving and C would still be dropped — but if avoidable cost were below ₹30,000, C must be retained. Show both the comparative statement and the incremental check.)*

### Q80. Ch: Marginal Costing — Composite (Multi-Product) Break-Even & Margin of Safety (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** A company sells two products in a fixed sales-value mix of 3:2 (X:Y). Data per unit: Product X — SP ₹60, VC ₹36; Product Y — SP ₹40, VC ₹30. Total fixed costs are ₹5,88,000. Compute (i) the composite (overall) P/V ratio, (ii) the composite break-even sales value, (iii) the break-even value of each product, and (iv) the margin of safety if total actual sales are ₹21,00,000.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Product P/V ratios:**
- X: Contribution = 60 − 36 = ₹24; P/V = 24 ÷ 60 = 40%.
- Y: Contribution = 40 − 30 = ₹10; P/V = 10 ÷ 40 = 25%.

**WN-2 — Composite P/V ratio (weighted by sales-value mix 3:2):**
- Composite P/V = (40% × 3/5) + (25% × 2/5) = 24% + 10% = **34%**.

**WN-3 — Composite break-even sales value:**
- BEP (₹) = Fixed cost ÷ Composite P/V = 5,88,000 ÷ 0.34 = **₹17,29,412 (≈)**.

**WN-4 — Break-even split by product (3:2 of value):**
- X = 17,29,412 × 3/5 = ₹10,37,647; Y = 17,29,412 × 2/5 = ₹6,91,765.

**WN-5 — Margin of Safety:**
- MOS (₹) = Actual sales − BEP = 21,00,000 − 17,29,412 = **₹3,70,588**.
- MOS ratio = 3,70,588 ÷ 21,00,000 = **17.65%**.
- Profit at actual sales = MOS × P/V = 3,70,588 × 0.34 = ₹1,26,000 (check: 21,00,000 × 34% − 5,88,000 = 7,14,000 − 5,88,000 = ₹1,26,000). ✔

**Statement of Results:**

| Particulars | Value |
|---|---|
| Composite P/V ratio | 34% |
| Composite Break-even sales | ₹17,29,412 |
| — of which Product X | ₹10,37,647 |
| — of which Product Y | ₹6,91,765 |
| Margin of Safety (₹) | ₹3,70,588 |
| Margin of Safety (%) | 17.65% |

**Answer:** Composite P/V = 34%; Composite BEP = ₹17,29,412 (X ₹10,37,647; Y ₹6,91,765); Margin of Safety = ₹3,70,588 (17.65%).

*(Full-marks tip: With a sales-VALUE mix, weight the individual P/V ratios by value proportions to get the composite P/V — do not average unit contributions. The cross-check that profit = MOS × P/V must reconcile, or marks are deducted for an internally inconsistent answer.)*

### Q81. Ch: Marginal Costing — Required Sales for Target Profit After Tax (Marks: 6) [Problem]

Compute (i) the sales required to earn the target after-tax profit, (ii) the margin of safety at that level, and (iii) the sales required merely to earn the present pre-tax profit if fixed costs rise by ₹1,50,000.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Convert after-tax profit to pre-tax profit:**
- Pre-tax profit = After-tax profit ÷ (1 − tax rate) = 4,20,000 ÷ (1 − 0.30) = 4,20,000 ÷ 0.70 = ₹6,00,000.

- Required Sales = (Fixed Cost + Desired pre-tax profit) ÷ P/V ratio
- = (12,00,000 + 6,00,000) ÷ 0.40 = 18,00,000 ÷ 0.40 = **₹45,00,000**.

**WN-3 — Break-even and margin of safety at that level:**
- BEP (₹) = 12,00,000 ÷ 0.40 = ₹30,00,000.
- MOS = 45,00,000 − 30,00,000 = **₹15,00,000** (33.33% of sales).

### Q83. Ch: Marginal Costing — Theory: Marginal vs Absorption Costing & Uses (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between Marginal Costing and Absorption Costing, and state why marginal costing is preferred for short-term managerial decision-making.

**Answer:** **Marginal costing** is a technique in which only variable (marginal) costs are charged to products, while fixed costs are treated as period costs and written off in full against the contribution of the period. **Absorption costing** (total costing) charges both variable and fixed manufacturing costs to products, so fixed cost is absorbed into unit cost and carried in inventory.

| Basis | Marginal Costing | Absorption Costing |
|---|---|---|
| Treatment of fixed cost | Period cost; charged in full to the period | Product cost; absorbed into unit cost via an OH rate |
| Inventory valuation | At variable cost only | At full cost (variable + fixed) |
| Effect on profit when stock changes | Profit varies only with sales | Profit affected by production too (fixed cost deferred in stock) |
| Cost per unit | Constant per unit | Varies with output level (fixed cost spread) |
| Key concept | Contribution (Sales − Variable cost) | Gross/Net profit after full absorption |
| Suitability | Short-term decisions, cost control | External reporting, statutory profit (per AS 2 for inventory) |

**Why marginal costing aids short-term decisions:** Because fixed costs in the short run are largely committed and unaffected by the decision, only variable costs and contribution are relevant. Marginal costing directly reveals the contribution each product, order or segment earns, making decisions on pricing, special/export orders, make-or-buy, product-mix under a limiting factor, and shut-down or continue much clearer. It also avoids the distortion of profit caused by fixed-cost absorption when stock levels change.

**Conclusion:** For internal short-term decisions marginal costing is preferred because it isolates relevant contribution, though absorption costing remains necessary for external inventory valuation under **AS 2 (Valuation of Inventories)**.

*(Full-marks tip: A tabular distinction plus the "contribution/relevance" rationale scores full marks. Remember that for statutory financial statements absorption costing is mandatory under AS 2, so state marginal costing is a decision tool, not a reporting method.)*

### Q84. Ch: Marginal Costing — Shut-Down vs Continue Decision (Marks: 8) [Application]
**Question:** Due to a temporary slump, a company is considering suspending operations of one of its plants for six months. Present monthly data are given. If the plant is shut down, ₹40,000 per month of the fixed cost can be avoided; additionally a one-time shut-down cost of ₹1,50,000 (maintenance, security and re-start) will be incurred for the whole six-month period. Determine whether the plant should continue operating or be shut down for the six months, and compute the shut-down point in units.

| Particulars (per month) | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Sales (5,000 units × ₹50) | 2,50,000 |
| Variable cost (5,000 units × ₹34) | 1,70,000 |
| Fixed cost | 1,20,000 |
| Loss | (40,000) |

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Contribution while operating:**
- Contribution/unit = 50 − 34 = ₹16; Total monthly contribution = 5,000 × 16 = ₹80,000.
- Monthly loss if operating = Contribution − Fixed cost = 80,000 − 1,20,000 = **(₹40,000)**.
- Loss for 6 months if operating = 40,000 × 6 = **₹2,40,000**.

**WN-2 — Cost if shut down for 6 months:**
- Unavoidable (continuing) fixed cost = 1,20,000 − 40,000 = ₹80,000 per month.
- For 6 months = 80,000 × 6 = ₹4,80,000.
- Add: one-time shut-down cost = ₹1,50,000.
- **Total cost/loss if shut down = 4,80,000 + 1,50,000 = ₹6,30,000.**

**Statement Comparing Continue vs Shut-Down (6 months):**

| Particulars | Continue (₹) | Shut Down (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| Contribution earned (80,000 × 6) | 4,80,000 | Nil |
| Less: Fixed cost (1,20,000 × 6) | 7,20,000 | — |
| Less: Unavoidable fixed cost (80,000 × 6) | — | 4,80,000 |
| Less: One-time shut-down cost | — | 1,50,000 |
| **Net Loss** | **(2,40,000)** | **(6,30,000)** |

**Conclusion:** Operating gives a loss of ₹2,40,000 over six months, whereas shutting down gives a larger loss of ₹6,30,000. Since the loss on continuing (₹2,40,000) is **less** than the loss on shutting down (₹6,30,000), the company should **continue operating** — the contribution of ₹4,80,000 more than covers the avoidable fixed cost and the shut-down costs it would save.

**WN-3 — Shut-down point (units):**
- Shut-down point = Avoidable Fixed Cost ÷ Contribution per unit = (Total fixed − unavoidable fixed) ÷ contribution/unit.
- Avoidable fixed cost per month = ₹40,000.
- Shut-down point = 40,000 ÷ 16 = **2,500 units per month**.

Since actual output (5,000 units) is above the shut-down point of 2,500 units, continuing is justified. ✔

**Answer:** **Continue operating** — loss of ₹2,40,000 vs ₹6,30,000 on shut-down; shut-down point = 2,500 units per month.

*(Full-marks tip: Compare avoidable fixed cost saved on shutdown against the contribution forgone, and add any one-time shut-down/re-start costs to the shut-down side. The shut-down point uses AVOIDABLE fixed cost ÷ contribution per unit — using total fixed cost gives the ordinary BEP and is the standard error.)*

### Q86. Ch: Budgets & Budgetary Control — Sales-Forecast Based Quarterly Master/Production Budget (Marks: 8) [Problem]

Opening stock on 1st April 2027 is 2,000 units and the desired closing stock at 31st March 2028 is 2,500 units. Prepare the quarter-wise Production Budget (in units).

|---|---|---|
| Q1 | 25% | 10,000 |

| Q3 | 25% | 10,000 |

### Q90. Ch: Budgets & Budgetary Control — Zero-Based Budgeting (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the concept of Zero-Based Budgeting (ZBB). State its main stages and bring out how it differs from traditional (incremental) budgeting.

**Answer:** Zero-Based Budgeting is a method of budgeting under which every activity is re-evaluated afresh each time a budget is prepared, and each cost element must be justified from a **"zero base"** as if the activity were being undertaken for the first time. Unlike the traditional approach, no expenditure is carried forward merely because it was incurred in the past; the manager must justify why the money should be spent at all and at what level.

The main stages of ZBB are: (i) **determination of the objectives** of the budgeting exercise; (ii) **identifying decision units** — the lowest activity/cost centres for which budgets are prepared; (iii) preparing **decision packages** for each unit, describing the activity, its cost, benefit, and alternative ways/levels of performing it; (iv) **evaluating and ranking** the decision packages in order of priority on a cost-benefit basis; and (v) **allocating available resources** to the packages in the order of their ranking.

**Distinction between Zero-Based and Traditional Budgeting:**

| Basis | Zero-Based Budgeting | Traditional (Incremental) Budgeting |
|---|---|---|
| Starting point | Every item starts from zero and must be justified | Previous year's figures taken as base and adjusted |
| Emphasis | On cost-benefit / "why spend at all" | On "how much more/less" than last year |
| Approach | Decision-package and priority-ranking based | Function/expense based, incremental |
| Cost justification | Fresh justification for every rupee | Only the increment is examined |
| Efficiency focus | Highlights and eliminates inefficient/obsolete activities | Past inefficiencies may get perpetuated |

**Conclusion:** ZBB promotes efficient allocation of resources by forcing a fresh cost-benefit justification of every activity, though it is more time-consuming than incremental budgeting.

*(Full-marks tip: The "decision package" and "priority ranking" stages are the heart of ZBB — mention them explicitly. A comparison table with traditional budgeting is what converts a 2-mark answer into a 4-mark answer.)*

### Q91. Ch: Budgets & Budgetary Control — Principal Budget Factor & Budget Manual (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** (a) What is the "Principal Budget Factor" (Key Factor)? Why must it be identified first? Give two examples. (b) What is a "Budget Manual"?

**Answer:** **(a) Principal Budget Factor (Key Factor / Limiting Factor):** It is the factor which, at a given point of time, **limits the activities of an organisation** and therefore governs the extent to which the various functional budgets can be prepared. It must be identified **at the very outset** of the budgeting process because it determines the priority of functional budgets — the budget of the limiting factor must be prepared first, and all other budgets are then built around it, so as to ensure that the budgets are coordinated, realistic and capable of being achieved. If the key factor is ignored, the budgets prepared will be internally inconsistent and unattainable. Common examples include: **sales demand** (when the market cannot absorb more than a certain quantity), **availability of raw material**, **plant/machine capacity**, **availability of skilled labour**, and **availability of funds**. In most organisations, sales demand is the principal budget factor, hence the sales budget is usually prepared first.

**(b) Budget Manual:** A budget manual is a **document (or set of documents) which sets out the responsibilities of the persons engaged in, and the procedures, forms and records relating to, the preparation and use of budgets.** It standardises the budgeting process by specifying who prepares which budget, the timetable to be followed, the formats to be used, the classification of accounts, and the routing of budget reports. It thus acts as a written guide that ensures uniformity and coordination across all departments in the budgeting exercise.

**Conclusion:** The principal budget factor dictates the sequence of budget preparation, while the budget manual provides the standardised procedural framework within which all budgets are drawn up.

*(Full-marks tip: State clearly that the budget of the key factor is prepared FIRST and everything is coordinated around it — that is the reasoning the examiner looks for. Distinguish the two parts cleanly; do not merge the manual definition into the key-factor answer.)*

### Q92. Ch: Budgets & Budgetary Control — Classification of Budgets (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Budgets may be classified on the basis of (i) time period, (ii) function and (iii) flexibility (capacity). Explain the different types of budgets under each of these three bases.

**Answer:** A budget is a quantitative and/or financial statement, prepared prior to a defined period, of the policy to be pursued during that period for attaining a given objective. Budgets are classified as follows:

**(i) On the basis of TIME PERIOD:**
- **Long-term budget** — prepared for a period generally exceeding one year (e.g., capital expenditure/R&D budgets); concerned with the organisation's long-range planning.
- **Short-term budget** — prepared for a period up to one year (usually broken into quarters/months); used for operational control, e.g., cash budget, material budget.
- **Current budget** — a very short-period budget prepared for current conditions/operations, adjusted for prevailing circumstances.

**(ii) On the basis of FUNCTION:**
- **Functional (subsidiary) budgets** — prepared for each function of the business, e.g., sales budget, production budget, materials budget, labour budget, plant-utilisation budget, cash budget, capital-expenditure budget.
- **Master budget** — the summary budget incorporating all functional budgets in a consolidated form; it typically comprises the budgeted Profit & Loss Account and the budgeted Balance Sheet, and represents the overall plan of the organisation.

**(iii) On the basis of FLEXIBILITY (capacity):**

| Type | Nature |
|---|---|
| **Fixed budget** | Prepared for a single (budgeted) level of activity and NOT adjusted to the actual level attained; useful only where activity can be accurately forecast. |
| **Flexible budget** | Designed to change in accordance with the actual level of activity attained, by recognising the behaviour of fixed, variable and semi-variable costs; gives meaningful cost control when activity fluctuates. |

**Conclusion:** Classifying budgets by time, function and flexibility enables an organisation to plan over different horizons, coordinate every function through the master budget, and control costs meaningfully at whatever activity level is actually achieved.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner expects all three bases with at least the main sub-types under each. The fixed-vs-flexible distinction and the point that the master budget consolidates all functional budgets are the high-value elements.)*

### Q96. Ch: Service Costing — Meaning & Composite Cost Units (Marks: 4) [Theory]

| Service organisation | Cost unit |
|---|---|
| Goods transport | Tonne-kilometre |
| Passenger transport | Passenger-kilometre / Seat-km |
| Hospital | Patient-day / Bed-day |
| Hotel | Room-day |
| Electricity / power | Kilowatt-hour (kWh) |
| Canteen | Per meal / per employee |
| Courier | Consignment-km |

**Conclusion:** Service costing ascertains the cost of rendering a service, and where a single factor is inadequate, a composite (two-factor) cost unit such as tonne-km or patient-day is adopted to represent the service meaningfully.

| Repairs and maintenance | 500 |
| Other running expenses | 1,000 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Absolute tonne-km (Σ load × distance of each leg)**
= (10 × 100) + (6 × 50) + (0 × 150)
= 1,000 + 300 + 0 = **1,300 tonne-km.**

**WN-2: Commercial tonne-km (average load × total distance)**
Average load = (10 + 6 + 0) ÷ 3 legs = 16 ÷ 3 = 5.333 tonnes.
Total distance = 100 + 50 + 150 = 300 km.
Commercial tonne-km = 5.333 × 300 = **1,600 tonne-km.**

**WN-3: Total operating cost (verification)**

| Element | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Driver's wages | 800 |
| Fuel | 3,000 |
| Depreciation | 1,200 |
| Repairs and maintenance | 500 |
| Other running expenses | 1,000 |
| **Total daily cost** | **6,500** |

**WN-4: Cost per absolute tonne-km**
= Total cost ÷ Absolute tonne-km = ₹6,500 ÷ 1,300 = **₹5.00 per absolute tonne-km.**

**Answer:** Absolute tonne-km = **1,300**; Commercial tonne-km = **1,600**; Cost per absolute tonne-km = **₹5.00**.

*(Full-marks tip: Absolute tonne-km weights each leg's actual load by that leg's distance (the empty return contributes zero); commercial tonne-km uses the simple average load over the whole distance. Costing per unit is normally done on ABSOLUTE tonne-km because it reflects the real work done — state this.)*

### Q99. Ch: Service Costing — Hotel Costing: Room-Day & Tariff (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** Grand Vista Hotel has 40 single rooms and 20 double rooms. The occupancy for the year is 75% and the hotel operates 365 days. A double room is treated as equivalent to 1.5 single-room-days for cost apportionment. Total annual operating cost is ₹1,53,30,000. Compute (i) the cost per equivalent room-day, and (ii) the room tariff for single and double rooms if the hotel wants a profit of 20% on cost.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Occupied room-days**
- Single rooms = 40 × 365 × 75% = 10,950 room-days.
- Double rooms = 20 × 365 × 75% = 5,475 room-days.

**WN-2: Equivalent (single) room-days**
Double room = 1.5 equivalents; hence double room-days in equivalent terms = 5,475 × 1.5 = 8,212.5.
Total equivalent room-days = 10,950 + 8,212.5 = **19,162.5 equivalent room-days.**

**WN-3: Cost per equivalent room-day**
= Total cost ÷ Equivalent room-days = ₹1,53,30,000 ÷ 19,162.5 = **₹800 per equivalent room-day.**

**WN-4: Room tariff (profit 20% on cost)**

| Room type | Cost per room-day | Tariff = Cost × 1.20 |
|---|---|---|
| Single (1.0 unit) | ₹800 | **₹960** |
| Double (1.5 units → ₹800 × 1.5 = ₹1,200) | ₹1,200 | **₹1,440** |

**Answer:** Cost per equivalent room-day = **₹800**; tariff — Single room **₹960 per day**, Double room **₹1,440 per day**.

*(Full-marks tip: Convert double rooms into equivalent single-room-days using the given weight BEFORE dividing total cost — this is the operating-costing analogue of equivalent units. The double-room tariff carries the 1.5 weight AND the 20% mark-up; missing the weight on the tariff is the usual error.)*

### Q100. Ch: Service Costing — Classification of Costs in Service Costing (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** In service costing, the total cost of operating a service is generally analysed into three groups of costs. Explain these three classifications with reference to a transport (motor) undertaking.

**Answer:** In service costing, and particularly in transport (motor) costing, the costs of operating the service are collected and classified into **three groups** according to their behaviour, so as to enable meaningful control and rate-fixation:

**(i) Standing (Fixed) charges:** These are costs incurred irrespective of the distance run or the extent to which the vehicle is used; they remain constant over a period. Examples in a transport undertaking include road tax and licence fees, insurance premium, garage rent, salary of drivers/cleaners on a fixed basis, and general supervision and establishment charges.

**(ii) Running (Variable) charges:** These are costs which vary more or less directly with the distance covered or the running of the vehicle. Examples include cost of petrol/diesel, lubricating oil, cost of tyres and tubes, and depreciation charged on a mileage/usage basis.

**(iii) Maintenance (Semi-variable) charges:** These are costs of an intermediate nature which are partly fixed and partly variable. Examples include repairs and maintenance, cost of spare parts, servicing, tyre re-treading, and painting/overhauling.

| Classification | Behaviour | Examples (transport) |
|---|---|---|
| Standing charges | Fixed — independent of running | Road tax, insurance, garage rent, fixed wages |
| Running charges | Variable — vary with distance | Fuel, oil, tyres, mileage-based depreciation |
| Maintenance charges | Semi-variable — partly fixed, partly variable | Repairs, spares, servicing, overhaul |

**Conclusion:** Grouping operating costs into standing, running and maintenance charges enables a transport undertaking to control each category appropriately and to fix a fair rate per tonne-km or passenger-km covering all three.

*(Full-marks tip: Name the three groups precisely — standing/fixed, running/variable, maintenance/semi-variable — and give two or three transport examples under each. Note that depreciation may be either a standing charge (if time-based) or a running charge (if mileage-based), which the examiner rewards as a nuance.)*
