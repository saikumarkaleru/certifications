### Q1. Ch: Material Cost — FSN Analysis (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain **FSN Analysis** as a technique of inventory control. State the basis of classification, how the three categories are treated by the stores management, and one limitation of the technique.

**Answer:**
FSN analysis is a technique of selective inventory control under which items in the stores are classified on the basis of their **rate of consumption / movement (frequency of issue)** rather than on the basis of their value (as in ABC analysis) or their criticality (as in VED analysis). The letters stand for **Fast-moving, Slow-moving and Non-moving** items.

The classification proceeds by measuring, for each item, the period of stay in the stores or the number of issues over a defined period (say the last one or two years), and then grouping the items accordingly:

| Category | Basis of classification | Management treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **F — Fast-moving** | Items consumed/issued very frequently; short average stay in stores | Kept under continuous review; ensure adequate stock as st-out cost is high; reorder frequently in smaller lots |
| **S — Slow-moving** | Items issued at a low frequency; longer average stay | Order in smaller quantities; review to release blocked working capital |
| **N — Non-moving** | Items that have had **no issue over the defined period** (dead stock) | Investigate the cause; dispose off / scrap to recover salvage value and free storage space and capital |

The technique is particularly useful in identifying **obsolete and dead stock** locked up in the stores so that it may be disposed of, and in deciding the frequency and quantity of ordering for the remaining items.

**Limitation:** FSN analysis is based only on the movement/consumption pattern and **ignores the value and criticality** of the item; a non-moving item may still be a critical insurance spare that must be retained. Hence it is usually applied **in combination** with ABC and VED analysis.

**Conclusion:** FSN analysis controls inventory by classifying items into Fast, Slow and Non-moving on the basis of consumption speed, its chief merit being the identification of dead stock.

*(Full-marks tip: examiner rewards the clear statement that the basis is **rate of movement, not value** — the common deduction is confusing FSN with ABC (value) or VED (criticality). Naming "dead stock identification" as the key use earns the concluding mark.)*

### Q2. Ch: Material Cost — HML Analysis vs ABC Analysis (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between **HML Analysis** and **ABC Analysis** of inventory control on the basis of (i) meaning, (ii) basis of classification and (iii) purpose.

**Answer:**
Both HML and ABC are techniques of **selective inventory control**, i.e. they concentrate managerial attention on a few important items rather than treating all items alike; but they differ as follows:

| Basis | HML Analysis | ABC Analysis |
|---|---|---|
| **Meaning** | Items are classified as **High, Medium and Low** priced | Items are classified as **A, B and C** categories |
| **Basis of classification** | Classification is done on the basis of the **unit price (cost per unit)** of the material, irrespective of the annual consumption value | Classification is done on the basis of the **annual consumption value** (unit price × annual usage), not on unit price alone |
| **Purpose / use** | Mainly used to **control purchases, to decide the authority levels for placing orders and the frequency of stock verification**, and to keep a check on high-priced items to avoid pilferage | Used to decide the **degree of control, frequency of review, safety stock and ordering policy** — tight control over few high-value 'A' items and loose control over many low-value 'C' items |

The essential distinction is that HML looks only at the **per-unit price** of an item, whereas ABC looks at the **total annual value** consumed; an item may be 'High' priced under HML yet fall in category 'C' under ABC if very few units are consumed in a year.

**Conclusion:** HML classifies by unit price to control ordering authority and security, while ABC classifies by annual usage value to allocate the intensity of inventory control.

*(Full-marks tip: the discriminating line — "unit price vs annual consumption value" — must appear explicitly. Students who write "both classify by value" lose the core mark because they miss that HML uses **unit price only**.)*

### Q3. Ch: Material Cost — Treatment of Insurance, Carriage, Discounts & Taxes (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** A manufacturer purchased raw material from a supplier. The following particulars are furnished for the consignment. Compute the **total cost of material** and the **cost per kg** to be entered in the stores ledger.

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Listed price of 10,000 kg of material | 8,00,000 |
| Trade discount allowed by supplier | 10% on list price |
| GST paid on net price (input credit available) | 18% |
| Cash discount for prompt payment | 2% |
| Railway freight & carriage inward | 24,000 |
| Transit insurance (on the consignment) | 6,000 |
| Cost of returnable drums (returned & refunded in full) | 5,000 |
| Detention/demurrage charges due to buyer's delay | 3,000 |
| Normal loss in transit (weight): 2% of quantity purchased | — |

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Net purchase price of material**
List price ₹8,00,000 − Trade discount @10% (₹80,000) = **₹7,20,000**.
(Trade discount is always **deducted** to arrive at the purchase price.)

**WN-2: Items to be EXCLUDED with reasons**
- **GST @18% (₹1,29,600)** — input credit is available, hence it is **recoverable** and NOT part of material cost.
- **Cash discount (2%)** — it is a **financial item** (reward for prompt payment), so it is **not deducted** from material cost.
- **Returnable drums ₹5,000** — refunded in full, hence **excluded**.
- **Detention/demurrage ₹3,000** — an **abnormal charge** due to the buyer's own delay; charged to **Costing P&L A/c**, not to material cost.

**WN-3: Items to be INCLUDED**
Railway freight & carriage inward ₹24,000 + Transit insurance ₹6,000 = **₹30,000** (both are costs incurred to bring the material to the present location and condition).

**WN-4: Effective quantity received after normal loss**
Normal loss = 2% × 10,000 kg = 200 kg. **Good quantity = 9,800 kg.**
(Cost of normal loss is **absorbed** by good units; it is not separately charged.)

**Statement Showing Total Cost of Material and Cost per kg**

| Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Net purchase price (WN-1) | 7,20,000 |
| Add: Railway freight & carriage inward | 24,000 |
| Add: Transit insurance | 6,000 |
| **Total cost of material** | **7,50,000** |
| Good quantity received (kg) | 9,800 |
| **Cost per kg = 7,50,000 ÷ 9,800** | **₹76.53** |

**Answer:** Total cost of material = **₹7,50,000**; cost per kg (absorbing normal transit loss over 9,800 good kg) = **₹76.53 per kg**.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner awards marks for the **treatment with reason** of each item — recoverable GST out, cash discount out, abnormal demurrage to Costing P&L, and normal loss spread over good units. The commonest deduction is dividing cost by 10,000 kg instead of 9,800 kg.)*

### Q4. Ch: Material Cost — Waste, Scrap, Spoilage & Defectives (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** "Waste, scrap, spoilage and defectives, though loosely used as synonyms, are four distinct concepts in material control." Distinguish among them and state the accounting treatment of the **normal** portion of each.

**Answer:**
Losses of material in a manufacturing process arise in four distinct forms which differ in their physical nature, their realisable value and their treatment:

| Term | Meaning | Realisable value | Treatment of NORMAL loss |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Waste** | Portion of raw material lost in processing having **no measurable/recoverable value** (e.g. evaporation, gas, dust, smoke); may be visible or invisible | Nil (may even carry a **disposal cost**) | Cost of normal waste is **absorbed by good units** (increases the cost per good unit) |
| **Scrap** | Residue/incidental material arising from the process that has a **low but definite realisable value** and is recoverable **without further processing** (e.g. metal turnings, off-cuts) | Small, definite | Sale value of normal scrap is **credited to the job/process/overhead** (reduces material or factory cost) |
| **Spoilage** | Goods so **badly damaged in manufacture** that they cannot be rectified economically and are taken out of process and sold as **seconds/salvage** | Salvage value | Normal spoilage cost (net of salvage) is **charged to good production** (as overhead or to the process) |
| **Defectives** | Units of production that **fall short of standard but can be RECTIFIED** by additional material/labour and made saleable as good/first-quality | Rectifiable — no direct salvage | Normal **rectification cost** is treated as **factory overhead / charged to the job or process** |

The key distinctions are: **waste has no value; scrap has value and needs no further work; spoilage cannot be rectified; defectives can be rectified.** In every case the **abnormal** portion (excess over the normal allowance) is transferred to the **Costing Profit & Loss Account** and is not allowed to distort product cost.

**Conclusion:** The four terms differ chiefly in recoverability — waste (no value), scrap (recoverable value), spoilage (irreparable, sold as seconds) and defectives (rectifiable) — and normal losses are absorbed by good production while abnormal losses go to Costing P&L.

*(Full-marks tip: full marks require the **rectifiable vs non-rectifiable** contrast (defectives vs spoilage) and the **value vs no-value** contrast (scrap vs waste). Adding the abnormal-to-Costing-P&L line clinches the answer.)*

### Q5. Ch: Material Cost — Scrap & Defectives Valuation (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** In a manufacturing process for Product 'Z', 20,000 units were introduced during the month. The following losses were noticed. Compute the **cost per good unit** and pass the treatment for the normal and abnormal portions.

| Particulars | Data |
|---|---|
| Units introduced | 20,000 units |
| Total process cost (material, labour, overhead) | ₹9,60,000 |
| Normal scrap (allowed) | 5% of input |
| Actual scrap generated | 1,400 units |
| Realisable value of scrap | ₹12 per unit |
| Good units transferred to finished stock | 18,600 units |

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Normal and abnormal scrap (in units)**
Normal scrap allowed = 5% × 20,000 = **1,000 units**.
Actual scrap = 1,400 units → **Abnormal loss = 1,400 − 1,000 = 400 units**.
Good units = 20,000 − 1,400 = **18,600 units** (agrees with data).

**WN-2: Cost per good unit (normal-loss cost absorbed by good units)**
Sale value of **normal** scrap = 1,000 units × ₹12 = ₹12,000 (credited to process).
Net process cost to be spread = ₹9,60,000 − ₹12,000 = ₹9,48,000.
Effective (expected good) units after normal loss = 20,000 − 1,000 = 19,000 units.
**Cost per good unit = ₹9,48,000 ÷ 19,000 = ₹49.8947 ≈ ₹49.89.**

**WN-3: Valuation of abnormal loss**
Abnormal loss units = 400; valued at cost per good unit = 400 × ₹49.89 = **₹19,957**.
Its own scrap realisation = 400 × ₹12 = ₹4,800 → **Net abnormal loss to Costing P&L = ₹19,957 − ₹4,800 = ₹15,157.**

**Statement Showing Cost Allocation**

| Particulars | Units | ₹ |
|---|---|---|
| Good units transferred (18,600 × ₹49.89) | 18,600 | 9,27,943 |
| Abnormal loss transferred (400 × ₹49.89) | 400 | 19,957 |
| Normal scrap credited (1,000 × ₹12) | 1,000 | 12,000 |
| **Total (reconciles with process cost)** | **20,000** | **9,59,900\*** |

\*Rounding difference of ₹100 due to per-unit rounding; exact figures reconcile to ₹9,60,000.

**Answer:** Cost per good unit = **₹49.89**; value of good output = **₹9,27,943**; **net abnormal loss ₹15,157** charged to Costing Profit & Loss Account.

*(Full-marks tip: examiner rewards spreading only **normal** loss over good units (÷19,000, not ÷18,600) and charging the abnormal loss at the **good-unit cost less its own scrap value**. Common deduction: crediting the whole scrap (1,400 units) to the process instead of only the normal 1,000 units.)*

### Q6. Ch: Material Cost — EOQ with Discount & Carriage (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** A company uses a special component. From the following data, (a) compute the **Economic Order Quantity**, and (b) advise whether the company should accept a supplier's offer of **3% quantity discount on the purchase price for a minimum order of 2,000 units**.

| Particulars | Data |
|---|---|
| Annual requirement | 12,000 units |
| Purchase price per unit | ₹200 |
| Ordering cost per order (incl. carriage per order ₹100) | ₹300 per order |
| Carrying cost | 15% of purchase price per unit per annum |

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Economic Order Quantity (basic price)**
Carrying cost per unit p.a. = 15% × ₹200 = ₹30.
EOQ = √(2AO ÷ C) = √(2 × 12,000 × 300 ÷ 30) = √2,40,000 = **692.8 ≈ 693 units**.

**WN-2: Total annual cost at EOQ (693 units) — no discount**

| Element | Computation | ₹ |
|---|---|---|
| Purchase cost | 12,000 × ₹200 | 24,00,000 |
| Ordering cost | (12,000 ÷ 693) × ₹300 | 5,195 |
| Carrying cost | (693 ÷ 2) × ₹30 | 10,395 |
| **Total cost** | | **24,15,590** |

**WN-3: Total annual cost at order size 2,000 units — with 3% discount**
Discounted price = ₹200 × 97% = ₹194; carrying cost per unit = 15% × ₹194 = ₹29.10.

| Element | Computation | ₹ |
|---|---|---|
| Purchase cost | 12,000 × ₹194 | 23,28,000 |
| Ordering cost | (12,000 ÷ 2,000) × ₹300 | 1,800 |
| Carrying cost | (2,000 ÷ 2) × ₹29.10 | 29,100 |
| **Total cost** | | **23,58,900** |

**WN-4: Comparison**
Saving on accepting the offer = ₹24,15,590 − ₹23,58,900 = **₹56,690** (chiefly the ₹72,000 price saving, part offset by higher carrying cost).

**Answer:** EOQ = **693 units**. The **discount offer should be ACCEPTED**, as ordering 2,000 units reduces total annual cost by **₹56,690** despite higher inventory carrying cost.

*(Full-marks tip: the deciding step is comparing **total cost including purchase price** at EOQ vs at the discount quantity — not merely ordering + carrying cost. Common deduction: forgetting to reduce carrying cost to 15% of the **discounted** price ₹194.)*

### Q7. Ch: Material Cost — Stock Levels (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** From the following information relating to a component, calculate (i) Re-order Level, (ii) Minimum Level, (iii) Maximum Level and (iv) Average Stock Level.

| Particulars | Data |
|---|---|
| Normal usage | 300 units per week |
| Minimum usage | 200 units per week |
| Maximum usage | 400 units per week |
| Re-order quantity (EOQ) | 2,400 units |
| Lead time (re-order period) | 4 to 6 weeks |

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Re-order Level (ROL)**
ROL = Maximum usage × Maximum lead time = 400 × 6 = **2,400 units**.

**WN-2: Minimum Level**
Minimum Level = ROL − (Normal usage × Normal lead time) = 2,400 − (300 × 5) = 2,400 − 1,500 = **900 units**.
(Normal lead time = (4 + 6)/2 = 5 weeks.)

**WN-3: Maximum Level**
Maximum Level = ROL + ROQ − (Minimum usage × Minimum lead time) = 2,400 + 2,400 − (200 × 4) = 4,800 − 800 = **4,000 units**.

**WN-4: Average Stock Level**
Average Stock = Minimum Level + ½ ROQ = 900 + ½(2,400) = 900 + 1,200 = **2,100 units**.
**OR** Average = (Maximum + Minimum) ÷ 2 = (4,000 + 900) ÷ 2 = **2,450 units** (cross-check by alternative formula).

**Statement Showing Stock Levels**

| Level | Units |
|---|---|
| Re-order Level | 2,400 |
| Minimum Level | 900 |
| Maximum Level | 4,000 |
| Average Stock Level | 2,100 (or 2,450) |

**Answer:** ROL = **2,400 units**, Minimum = **900 units**, Maximum = **4,000 units**, Average = **2,100 units** (formula 1) / **2,450 units** (formula 2).

*(Full-marks tip: examiner accepts either average-stock formula but rewards showing both. Common deduction: using maximum lead time in the Minimum-Level formula instead of normal, or minimum lead time in place of maximum for ROL.)*

### Q8. Ch: Material Cost — Inventory Turnover & Slow-moving Stock (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** From the following figures relating to two components used by a factory, compute the **Inventory Turnover Ratio** and the **average number of days for which each item is held in stock**, and comment on which item is slow-moving.

| Particulars | Component A (₹) | Component B (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| Opening stock | 20,000 | 90,000 |
| Purchases during the year | 1,80,000 | 60,000 |
| Closing stock | 30,000 | 1,10,000 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Material consumed during the year**
Consumption = Opening stock + Purchases − Closing stock.
Component A = 20,000 + 1,80,000 − 30,000 = **₹1,70,000**.
Component B = 90,000 + 60,000 − 1,10,000 = **₹40,000**.

**WN-2: Average stock**
Average stock = (Opening + Closing) ÷ 2.
Component A = (20,000 + 30,000) ÷ 2 = **₹25,000**.
Component B = (90,000 + 1,10,000) ÷ 2 = **₹1,00,000**.

**WN-3: Inventory Turnover Ratio (times) and holding period (days)**

| Particulars | Component A | Component B |
|---|---|---|
| Material consumed (₹) | 1,70,000 | 40,000 |
| Average stock (₹) | 25,000 | 1,00,000 |
| **ITR = Consumed ÷ Avg stock** | **6.8 times** | **0.4 times** |
| **Holding days = 365 ÷ ITR** | **53.7 days** | **912.5 days** |

**WN-4: Comment**
A high turnover ratio indicates fast movement; a low ratio indicates slow movement and blocked capital. Component A turns over **6.8 times** a year (≈54 days) whereas Component B turns over only **0.4 times** (≈2.5 years).

**Answer:** ITR — Component A = **6.8 times**, Component B = **0.4 times**. **Component B is the slow-moving item**; the stock is held on average for about 912 days, indicating over-stocking and locked-up working capital that management should investigate for possible obsolescence.

*(Full-marks tip: examiner rewards using **material consumed** (not purchases) in the numerator and correctly identifying B as slow-moving with the days figure. Common deduction: taking purchases instead of consumption, or dividing average stock by consumption (inverting the ratio).)*

### Q9. Ch: Material Cost — Pricing of Issues (Weighted Average) (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** The following transactions took place in respect of a material during March. Prepare the **Stores Ledger Account** under the **Weighted Average Price** method and state the value of closing stock.

| Date | Transaction | Quantity (units) | Rate (₹ per unit) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mar 1 | Opening balance | 500 | 20.00 |
| Mar 5 | Purchase | 700 | 22.00 |
| Mar 10 | Issue | 600 | — |
| Mar 15 | Purchase | 800 | 23.00 |
| Mar 20 | Issue | 900 | — |
| Mar 25 | Purchase (returned to supplier from Mar 15 lot) | (100) | 23.00 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Weighted average after Mar 5 purchase**
Balance = 500 × 20 + 700 × 22 = 10,000 + 15,400 = ₹25,400 for 1,200 units → **₹21.1667 per unit**.

**WN-2: After Mar 10 issue (600 @ 21.1667)**
Issue value = 600 × 21.1667 = ₹12,700. Balance = 600 units × ₹21.1667 = ₹12,700.

**WN-3: After Mar 15 purchase (800 @ 23)**
Balance = 12,700 + (800 × 23) = 12,700 + 18,400 = ₹31,100 for 1,400 units → **₹22.2143 per unit**.

**WN-4: After Mar 20 issue (900 @ 22.2143)** = ₹19,993. Balance = 500 units × ₹22.2143 = ₹11,107.

**WN-5: Mar 25 return to supplier (100 units of Mar 15 lot @ ₹23)**
Return to supplier is valued at the **purchase price of that lot (₹23)**, reducing stock by 100 × 23 = ₹2,300.
Balance = 500 − 100 = **400 units**; value = 11,107 − 2,300 = ₹8,807 → new rate ₹22.0175.

**Stores Ledger Account (Weighted Average)**

| Date | Particulars | Receipts (Qty / ₹) | Issues (Qty / ₹) | Balance (Qty / Rate / ₹) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mar 1 | Balance | — | — | 500 / 20.00 / 10,000 |
| Mar 5 | Purchase | 700 / 15,400 | — | 1,200 / 21.1667 / 25,400 |
| Mar 10 | Issue | — | 600 / 12,700 | 600 / 21.1667 / 12,700 |
| Mar 15 | Purchase | 800 / 18,400 | — | 1,400 / 22.2143 / 31,100 |
| Mar 20 | Issue | — | 900 / 19,993 | 500 / 22.2143 / 11,107 |
| Mar 25 | Return to supplier | — | 100 / 2,300 | 400 / 22.0175 / 8,807 |

**Answer:** Closing stock = **400 units valued at ₹8,807** (≈₹22.02 per unit) under the weighted average price method.

*(Full-marks tip: examiner rewards recomputing the weighted average **only after each receipt** and valuing a **return to supplier at the original purchase price of that lot (₹23)**, not at the running average. Common deduction: recalculating the average after issues, or valuing the supplier return at the weighted-average rate.)*

### Q10. Ch: Employee (Labour) Cost — Taylor's Differential Piece Rate (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** A factory operates **Taylor's Differential Piece Rate System**. The standard time allowed is **20 units per hour** in an 8-hour day. The normal (ordinary) piece rate is **₹5 per unit**. Taylor's scheme applies **80% of the normal rate below standard** and **120% of the normal rate at or above standard**. Compute the **daily earnings and labour cost per unit** for three workers whose daily output is: Ram 130 units, Shyam 160 units, Mohan 176 units.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Standard output and differential rates**
Standard output per day = 20 units/hr × 8 hr = **160 units**.
Lower piece rate (below standard) = 80% × ₹5 = **₹4.00 per unit**.
Higher piece rate (at/above standard) = 120% × ₹5 = **₹6.00 per unit**.

**WN-2: Classify each worker**
Ram 130 units (< 160) → below standard → ₹4 rate.
Shyam 160 units (= 160) → at standard → ₹6 rate.
Mohan 176 units (> 160) → above standard → ₹6 rate.

**Statement Showing Daily Earnings and Labour Cost per Unit**

| Worker | Output (units) | Rate applied (₹) | Daily earnings (₹) | Labour cost/unit (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ram | 130 | 4.00 | 520 | 4.00 |
| Shyam | 160 | 6.00 | 960 | 6.00 |
| Mohan | 176 | 6.00 | 1,056 | 6.00 |

**WN-3: Note on the incentive effect**
Taylor's system deliberately gives **no guaranteed minimum**; the efficient worker (at/above standard) is rewarded at the higher rate applied to **all** units, while the inefficient worker is penalised at the lower rate on all units — hence the sharp jump in Shyam's cost per unit (₹6) over Ram's (₹4).

**Answer:** Daily earnings — Ram **₹520**, Shyam **₹960**, Mohan **₹1,056**; labour cost per unit — Ram **₹4.00**, Shyam & Mohan **₹6.00** each.

*(Full-marks tip: the essence of Taylor is that the differential rate applies to the **whole output**, not marginally, and there is **no guaranteed day wage**. Common deduction: applying the low rate to output up to standard and the high rate only to the excess — that is a Merrick/Gantt-type split, not Taylor.)*

### Q11. Ch: Employee (Labour) Cost — Merrick Differential Piece Rate (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** Under the **Merrick Multiple / Differential Piece Rate System**, the standard production is **240 units per day** and the ordinary piece rate is **₹2.50 per unit**. Merrick's slabs are: **up to 83% efficiency — ordinary rate; above 83% and up to 100% — 110% of ordinary rate; above 100% — 120% of ordinary rate**. Compute the day's earnings of four workers whose outputs are: P 180 units, Q 210 units, R 240 units, S 264 units.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Efficiency of each worker (Actual ÷ Standard 240)**
P = 180/240 = **75%**; Q = 210/240 = **87.5%**; R = 240/240 = **100%**; S = 264/240 = **110%**.

**WN-2: Applicable Merrick rate**
Ordinary rate = ₹2.50; 110% = ₹2.75; 120% = ₹3.00.
- P (75% ≤ 83%) → ₹2.50.
- Q (87.5%, between 83% and 100%) → ₹2.75.
- R (100%, up to 100%) → ₹2.75.
- S (110% > 100%) → ₹3.00.

**Statement Showing Day's Earnings (Merrick System)**

| Worker | Output | Efficiency | Rate applied (₹) | Earnings (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| P | 180 | 75% | 2.50 | 450.00 |
| Q | 210 | 87.5% | 2.75 | 577.50 |
| R | 240 | 100% | 2.75 | 660.00 |
| S | 264 | 110% | 3.00 | 792.00 |

**WN-3: Note — Merrick vs Taylor**
Unlike Taylor's **two** rates, Merrick uses **three** rates and does **not penalise** the below-standard worker as harshly (P still gets the ordinary rate, not a reduced 80% rate). The high rate applies to the **entire output** of the qualifying worker.

**Answer:** Day's earnings — P **₹450.00**, Q **₹577.50**, R **₹660.00**, S **₹792.00**.

*(Full-marks tip: full marks require the **efficiency %** computed first and the correct three-slab boundary (note 100% falls in the 110% slab, above 100% gets 120%). Common deduction: giving R the 120% rate — at exactly 100% the rate is 110%, not 120%.)*

### Q12. Ch: Employee (Labour) Cost — Emerson Efficiency Bonus (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** Under the **Emerson's Efficiency Bonus System**, a worker is guaranteed a **time (day) wage of ₹80 per day of 8 hours**. Standard output is **8 units per hour**. Bonus is paid as follows: **below 66⅔% efficiency — only time wage (no bonus); from 66⅔% to 100% — bonus rising to 20% of time wage at 100% (take the bonus as 20% at exactly 100%); above 100% — 20% bonus plus 1% of time wage for every 1% efficiency above 100%.** Compute the total earnings and effective rate per hour of three workers whose outputs in an 8-hour day are: A 40 units, B 64 units, C 80 units.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Standard output and efficiency**
Standard output per day = 8 units/hr × 8 hr = **64 units**.
A = 40/64 = **62.5%**; B = 64/64 = **100%**; C = 80/64 = **125%**.

**WN-2: Bonus applicable**
- **A (62.5% < 66⅔%)** → no bonus; only time wage ₹80.
- **B (100%)** → bonus = 20% of time wage = 20% × ₹80 = ₹16.
- **C (125%, i.e. 25% above 100%)** → bonus = 20% + 25% = **45% of time wage** = 45% × ₹80 = ₹36.

**Statement Showing Total Earnings (Emerson System)**

| Worker | Output | Efficiency | Time wage (₹) | Bonus % | Bonus (₹) | Total earnings (₹) | Rate/hour (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

### Q15. Ch: Employee (Labour) Cost — Idle Time & Overtime Treatment (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between **normal idle time** and **abnormal idle time**, and explain the accounting treatment of (i) idle time and (ii) **overtime premium** under three different circumstances.

**Answer:**
**Idle time** is the time for which the employer pays wages but obtains **no direct production**, i.e. the difference between the time paid for and the time actually booked to jobs.

| Basis | Normal idle time | Abnormal idle time |
|---|---|---|
| **Nature** | Unavoidable, inherent in the job (e.g. tea/lunch break, setting-up time, movement between departments, tool changes) | Avoidable, arising from abnormal causes (e.g. strikes, machine breakdown, power failure, shortage of material) |
| **Treatment** | Cost is **absorbed as production overhead** (or the wage rate is inflated to recover it) so that it forms part of product cost | Cost is treated as a **loss and charged to the Costing Profit & Loss Account**; it must not distort product cost |

**Overtime premium** is the **extra amount** (over the normal rate) paid for work done beyond normal hours. Its treatment depends on the reason:

| Circumstance | Treatment of overtime premium |
|---|---|
| (a) Overtime worked at the **customer's request** to expedite delivery | Charged directly to that **job/order** (part of its cost) |
| (b) Overtime due to **general pressure of work / seasonal rush** | Treated as **production overhead** and spread over all jobs |
| (c) Overtime due to **abnormal causes** (management's fault, e.g. delay, breakdown) | Charged to the **Costing Profit & Loss Account** as an abnormal loss |

The **normal wages** for overtime hours are always charged to the job at the ordinary rate; only the **premium** portion needs special treatment as above.

**Conclusion:** Normal idle time and normal overtime are absorbed as overhead (or to the job), whereas the abnormal portions of both are written off to the Costing Profit & Loss Account so that product cost reflects only efficient operation.

*(Full-marks tip: examiner rewards the three-circumstance table for overtime premium and the clean normal/abnormal split for idle time. Common deduction: writing that all overtime goes to Costing P&L — only the **abnormal-cause** premium does.)*

### Q16. Ch: Employee (Labour) Cost — Halsey vs Rowan Comparison (Marks: 8) [Problem]

### Q17. Ch: Employee (Labour) Cost — Labour Cost Control & Group Bonus (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** State the objectives of a **Group Bonus Scheme** of wage payment, list any three situations in which it is preferred over an individual incentive scheme, and give two limitations.

**Answer:**
A **Group (Collective) Bonus Scheme** is one under which a bonus is paid to a **group/team of workers collectively** on the basis of the group's combined output or performance, the bonus then being distributed among members (usually in the ratio of their basic wages or time-rate earnings). The individual worker's reward depends on the **team's** result, not on his own output alone.

**Objectives of a group bonus scheme:**
1. To create a **spirit of co-operation and team-work** among workers, since the bonus depends on collective effort.
2. To secure **higher overall output and better plant utilisation** where output cannot be attributed to any one worker.
3. To **reduce supervision, idle time and waste**, as the group exercises mutual control over its members.
4. To simplify wage computation where measuring individual output is impracticable.

**Situations where a group scheme is preferred:**

| Situation | Reason |
|---|---|
| Work is performed by a **team on interdependent operations** (e.g. an assembly line, a furnace crew) | Output cannot be traced to one worker, so individual incentive is impossible |
| **Automated/continuous process** industries where the machine, not the worker, sets the pace | Individual effort has little separate effect; team upkeep matters |
| Where **quality and co-ordination** matter more than individual speed | Group responsibility discourages one member rushing at the cost of others |

**Limitations:**
1. An **efficient worker may be discouraged** because he shares his bonus with less efficient members (the "free-rider" problem).
2. The **individual incentive is diluted**, so the direct link between one's own effort and reward is weakened, and idle members may still draw the bonus.

**Conclusion:** A group bonus scheme rewards collective performance to promote team-work and is best suited to interdependent or process operations, its chief drawback being the demotivation of efficient workers who must share the reward.

*(Full-marks tip: examiner rewards linking each preferred situation to the reason "output not traceable to an individual" and naming the free-rider/dilution limitation. Common deduction: merely listing schemes (Priestman, Rucker, Scanlon) without stating objectives and situations as asked.)*

### Q19. Ch: Overheads – Absorption Costing — Accounting Treatment of Overtime Premium, Idle-Capacity Cost and Interest on Capital (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the treatment in cost accounts of (i) overtime premium, (ii) idle-capacity cost, and (iii) interest on capital.

**Answer:**
**(i) Overtime premium.** Overtime wages consist of the normal rate (charged as direct wages) plus the *overtime premium* (the excess over the normal rate). The treatment of the premium depends on the cause:
- If overtime is worked **at the specific request of a customer** to complete the job earlier, the premium is charged **directly to that job**.
- If it arises from a **general pressure of work / seasonal rush**, the premium is treated as **factory overhead** and recovered over all jobs, so that no single job bears the whole burden.
- If it is due to **abnormal conditions** such as a breakdown, flood or fire, the premium is **excluded from cost** and charged to the **Costing Profit & Loss Account**.
- If it results from the **management's own fault/defective planning**, it is charged to the **Costing Profit & Loss Account** as it does not represent normal production cost.

**(ii) Idle-capacity cost.** Idle capacity is the difference between practical (or normal) capacity and the capacity actually utilised; its cost is the unabsorbed **fixed** overhead relating to the unused capacity. Treatment depends on the reason:
- **Normal/unavoidable** idle capacity (e.g. routine repairs, preventive maintenance) — the related cost is treated as a part of **production overhead** and absorbed through an inflated overhead rate.
- **Abnormal** idle capacity (e.g. lack of orders, strikes, power failure, non-availability of material) — the cost is **not** charged to production; it is transferred to the **Costing Profit & Loss Account**.
- Idle capacity **caused by seasonal factors** — the cost may be charged to the cost of production by a supplementary rate during the working season.

**(iii) Interest on capital.** There is a controversy on whether interest on capital (own funds employed) should form part of cost. The **conventional/accepted practice in cost accounts is to exclude interest on capital**, because it is a matter of financial arrangement (an appropriation of profit) and not a cost of production, and its inclusion involves an element of estimation that impairs cost comparison. However, for **managerial decisions** — such as make-or-buy, choice between processes with differing capital intensity, or valuation of comparable jobs — **notional interest** may be imputed so that the true economic cost of employing capital is not overlooked.

**Conclusion:** Each item is charged to the job, to overhead, or to the Costing P&L Account according to whether its cause is job-specific, normal, or abnormal; interest on capital is normally excluded from cost but may be imputed notionally for decision-making.

*(Full-marks tip: state the **cause-based rule** for each item — examiners award marks for the "abnormal → Costing P&L" line, which candidates frequently omit.)*

### Q23. Ch: Overheads – Absorption Costing — Blanket Rate vs Departmental Rate (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between a "blanket overhead rate" and "departmental overhead rates", and state when the use of a blanket rate is appropriate.

**Answer:**
A **blanket (single) overhead rate** is one single overhead absorption rate computed for the factory as a whole, whereas **departmental overhead rates** are separate rates computed for each production department (or cost centre).

| Basis | Blanket rate | Departmental rates |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | One rate for the entire factory | A separate rate for each department |
| Computation | Total factory overhead ÷ Total factory base (e.g. total labour/machine hours) | Departmental overhead ÷ Departmental base |
| Accuracy | May distort product cost when jobs pass unequally through departments | Reflects the actual overhead of each department a job passes through |
| Suitability | Small units, or where all products pass through **all** departments in the **same proportion** | Where products consume departmental facilities **unequally**, or departments differ widely in overhead |

**When a blanket rate is appropriate:** a blanket rate is justified only when (a) there is **one major product** or all products spend broadly the same time in each department, or (b) the factory is **small** with a single cost centre, so that the additional cost of maintaining departmental rates is not warranted. In all other cases departmental rates give a more accurate and fairer absorption of overheads.

**Conclusion:** A blanket rate is simple but can misprice jobs; departmental rates are more accurate and should be preferred wherever products consume department facilities in differing proportions.

*(Full-marks tip: give the one decisive condition — a blanket rate is fair **only when every product passes through every department in the same proportion**; state it explicitly.)*

### Q24. Ch: Overheads – Absorption Costing — Principles and Bases for Apportioning Joint/Common Overheads (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** What is meant by "apportionment of overhead"? State the principles governing the selection of a basis, and give the most appropriate basis of apportionment for any four common (joint) overhead items.

**Answer:**
**Apportionment of overhead** is the process of **distributing a common (joint) item of overhead — one that cannot be identified wholly with a single cost centre — over two or more cost centres on an equitable basis**, in proportion to the benefit received by, or the burden imposed on, each centre. (Where an overhead can be wholly identified with one cost centre it is *allocated*, not apportioned.)

**Principles governing the choice of basis:**
1. **Service or benefit received** — cost apportioned in proportion to the service enjoyed by each department (e.g. maintenance cost on machine hours).
2. **Ability to pay** — a heavier share to departments/products with greater capacity to bear it (used sparingly).
3. **Survey / analysis** — where no direct relationship exists, a periodic survey estimates the fair share.
4. **Efficiency / incentive** — bases set to encourage efficiency; departments exceeding a norm bear more.

**Appropriate bases for common items:**

| Overhead item | Most appropriate basis |
|---|---|
| Rent, rates, heating, lighting | Floor area / cubic content |
| Depreciation & insurance of plant | Value of plant & machinery |
| Power | Horse power × machine hours (or metered units) |
| Canteen, welfare, ESI, supervision | Number of employees |
| Stores service cost | Number/value of material requisitions |

**Conclusion:** The basis chosen must bear a **logical, equitable relationship** to the overhead so that each cost centre absorbs a fair share of the joint overhead.

*(Full-marks tip: distinguish allocation from apportionment in the first sentence, then give a clean item→basis table; the "benefit-received" principle is the one examiners most want stated.)*

### Q26. Ch: Activity-Based Costing — Cost Hierarchy and Cost Drivers (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the "cost hierarchy" used in Activity-Based Costing, giving an example of each level and a suitable cost driver.

**Answer:**
In ABC, activities (and their costs) are classified into a **cost hierarchy** according to the level at which the cost is caused. This classification determines the appropriate cost driver and prevents batch- or product-level costs from being wrongly averaged over units.

| Level | Meaning | Example of cost | Suitable cost driver |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Unit-level** | Performed each time a unit is produced; vary with volume | Power, machine operation, direct-material handling | Machine hours / units produced |
| **Batch-level** | Performed each time a batch is processed, irrespective of units in the batch | Machine set-ups, purchase-order/production-order processing, first-item inspection | No. of set-ups / no. of orders / no. of batches |
| **Product- (or product-sustaining) level** | Performed to enable a product to be produced and sold, regardless of units or batches | Product design, maintaining bill of materials, product-specification changes, special tooling | No. of products / no. of design changes |
| **Facility- (or facility-sustaining) level** | Sustain the plant as a whole; cannot be traced to any product | Factory rent, general administration, plant security, lighting | Not driver-traceable; apportioned to products on an arbitrary base (e.g. floor area) or kept as a period cost |

**Key point on the two extremes:** only **unit-level** costs behave like traditional "variable-with-volume" overheads. **Batch- and product-level** costs are the ones traditional costing mishandles — they do not vary with units, so averaging them over volume over-costs high-volume and under-costs low-volume products. **Facility-level** costs have no genuine driver and are best treated as period costs or apportioned on an agreed base rather than forced into a driver rate.

**Conclusion:** The cost hierarchy tells the cost accountant *at what level* each activity's cost is incurred, and hence which cost driver to use, giving ABC its accuracy over a single volume-based rate.

*(Full-marks tip: name all four levels with a driver each, and add the observation that facility-level cost has no true driver — that distinction earns the last mark.)*

### Q29. Ch: Activity-Based Costing — Requirements, Benefits and Limitations of ABC (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the circumstances in which Activity-Based Costing is most useful and its main limitations.

**Answer:**
**Meaning:** ABC is a costing system that first accumulates overhead into **activity cost pools** and then charges it to products/services using **cost drivers** that measure the demand each product makes on those activities.

**When ABC is most useful (circumstances):**
1. Where **overheads are a large proportion** of total cost, so distortion in overhead absorption materially affects product cost.
2. Where the firm produces a **wide, diverse product range** differing in volume, size, complexity and batch size, causing traditional volume-based rates to cross-subsidise.
3. Where **support/indirect activities** (set-ups, inspection, material handling, order processing) are significant and are driven by transactions rather than volume.
4. Where **competition is intense** and accurate product/customer cost is vital for pricing, product-mix and make-or-buy decisions.

**Limitations:**
1. It is **costly and time-consuming** to design and operate (identifying activities, drivers and collecting data).
2. Selection of **cost drivers is judgemental**; a wrong driver reintroduces distortion.
3. **Facility-level costs** still cannot be traced to products by any meaningful driver.
4. It may give **too much detail**, and not all activity cost is truly variable in the short run, so care is needed in using ABC data for decisions.

**Conclusion:** ABC gives more accurate product/customer costs where overheads are high and products diverse, but its benefits must justify its higher operating cost.

*(Full-marks tip: give the two decisive triggers — **high overhead proportion** and **product diversity** — plus at least two genuine limitations; a balanced answer scores full.)*

### Q33. Ch: Cost Accounting Systems & Reconciliation — Why Cost and Financial Profits Differ; Integrated System Features (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** (a) Explain the reasons that give rise to a difference between the profit shown by the cost accounts and that shown by the financial accounts. (b) State how these reasons are eliminated under an integrated accounting system.

**Answer:**
**(a) Reasons for the difference (in a non-integrated system):**

**1. Items shown only in financial accounts (not in cost accounts):**
- *Purely financial charges/losses* — loss on sale of fixed assets/investments, interest on loans, fines and penalties, discount on issue of shares/debentures, goodwill/preliminary expenses written off.
- *Purely financial incomes* — interest and dividend received, rent received, profit on sale of assets, transfer-fee income.
- *Appropriations of profit* — income tax, dividends paid, transfers to reserves, donations.

**2. Items shown only in cost accounts (not in financial accounts):**
- Notional charges such as **notional rent** of own premises, **notional interest** on capital, and notional salary of the proprietor — included in cost to reflect true economic cost but not recorded as financial expenditure.

**3. Differences in the basis of valuation / charge:**
- **Stock valuation** — cost accounts may value stock at cost while financial accounts follow AS 2 (lower of cost and NRV), giving different closing/opening stock figures.
- **Depreciation** — different method or rate (e.g. machine-hour rate in cost vs straight-line/WDV in financial books).
- **Overhead absorption** — overheads are absorbed at **pre-determined rates** in cost accounts, producing **under- or over-absorption** compared with the actual overhead recorded financially.

**(b) Position under an integrated accounting system:**
Under an integrated system, **cost and financial accounts are merged into one set of books** using common data and a single figure of profit. Consequently:
- There is only **one profit figure**, so **no reconciliation is required** — the very cause of the difference is removed.
- Purely financial items and cost-only notional items are dealt with in the **same ledger**, and stock, depreciation and overhead figures are recorded **once on a common basis**, avoiding valuation differences.
(Where notional charges are still used for internal costing, they are shown as inter-account transfers within the same books rather than as separate profits.)

**Conclusion:** Differences arise mainly from purely financial items, cost-only notional items, and differing bases of valuation/absorption; an integrated system eliminates them by keeping a single, common set of accounts producing one profit.

*(Full-marks tip: organise the reasons into the **three standard groups** (financial-only, cost-only, valuation differences) — the grouped structure is exactly what the ICAI marking scheme looks for.)*

### Q37. Ch: Cost Sheet — Treatment of Items in a Cost Sheet (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** State, with reasons, how the following items are treated while preparing a Cost Sheet: (i) Carriage inward on raw material, (ii) Cash discount received, (iii) Sale value of scrap of material, (iv) Royalty on production, (v) Primary packing vs. Secondary packing.

**Answer:** The treatment of an item in a cost sheet depends on whether it is a cost of production/sale or a financial/appropriation item, and on the element and function to which it relates.

(i) **Carriage inward on raw material** — It is a cost incurred to bring material to the factory and therefore forms part of the **cost of material purchased**. It is added to the purchase cost of raw material (i.e., included in material consumed under Prime Cost). It is **not** an overhead.

(ii) **Cash discount received** — Cash discount is a **financial item** arising from prompt payment (a financing decision), not a manufacturing cost. It is therefore **excluded from the cost sheet** and dealt with in financial accounts.

(iii) **Sale value of scrap of material** — Normal scrap realised is **deducted from factory/works overhead** (or from material cost/factory cost) so that the cost of good output is not inflated. Abnormal scrap value is excluded from cost and taken to Costing P&L.

(iv) **Royalty on production** — Royalty paid on the basis of units produced is a **direct expense (chargeable/direct expenses)** and is included in **Prime Cost**. (If royalty is based on units sold, it is treated as a selling overhead.)

(v) **Primary vs. Secondary packing** — **Primary packing** (essential to protect/contain the product, e.g., a tube for toothpaste) is a **production/works cost**. **Secondary packing** (for bulk handling and despatch, e.g., an outer carton) is a **selling & distribution overhead**.

**Conclusion:** Manufacturing-related items enter Prime Cost or the relevant overhead, financing items (cash discount) are excluded, and packing is split by its function between works cost and selling overhead.

*(Full-marks tip: Always give the **reason and the element/function head** for each item, not just the treatment; examiners award marks per correct justification, and confusing cash discount with trade discount loses an easy mark.)*

### Q40. Ch: Unit & Batch Costing — Economic Batch Quantity (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** A component is required at the rate of **90,000 units per annum**. Each set-up costs **₹500** and the cost of holding one unit in stock for a year is **10% of the unit cost of ₹25**. Compute (a) the Economic Batch Quantity, (b) the number of batches (set-ups) per year, and (c) verify that set-up cost equals carrying cost at the EBQ.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Carrying cost per unit p.a.:** 10% of ₹25 = **₹2.50**.

**(b) Number of batches** = 90,000 ÷ 6,000 = **15 batches per year**.

**(c) Cross-check at EBQ:**
- Total set-up cost = 15 batches × ₹500 = **₹7,500**.
- Total carrying cost = (EBQ ÷ 2) × C = (6,000 ÷ 2) × ₹2.50 = 3,000 × 2.50 = **₹7,500**.

Since set-up cost = carrying cost = ₹7,500, the total of the two costs is minimised — confirming 6,000 units is the EBQ.

**Answer:** EBQ = **6,000 units**; **15 batches** p.a.; minimum combined set-up + carrying cost = ₹7,500 + ₹7,500 = **₹15,000**.

### Q42. Ch: Unit & Batch Costing — Batch Costing vs. Job Costing (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain "Batch Costing" and state its suitability. Distinguish between Batch Costing and Job Costing.

**Answer:** **Batch costing** is that form of specific-order costing in which the cost object is a **batch** — a group of identical products manufactured together as one identifiable lot. All costs (material, labour, set-up and overhead) are accumulated for the whole batch and the cost per unit is obtained by dividing the total batch cost by the number of units in the batch. It is a variant of job costing where **the job is a batch**.

**Suitability:** It is used where articles are produced in **definite lots of identical units** — e.g., pharmaceuticals (tablets/capsules), biscuits and bakery, ready-made garments, component/spare-part manufacture, and toy making.

**Distinction between Batch Costing and Job Costing:**

| Basis | Batch Costing | Job Costing |
|---|---|---|
| Cost unit | A batch of identical units | A single job/work order |
| Nature of output | Homogeneous units within a batch | Each job is distinct/unique |
| Cost per unit | Batch cost ÷ units in batch | Job cost = cost of the job itself |
| Set-up cost | Significant; leads to EBQ analysis | Generally not a repetitive concern |
| Example | Tablets, garments, components | Ship-building, printing a specific order |

**Conclusion:** Batch costing applies the specific-order principle to a lot of identical products, making it ideal for repetitive small-lot manufacture where set-up economics (EBQ) matter.

*(Full-marks tip: Anchor batch costing as a **sub-type of job costing** and give at least two real industry examples; the comparison table earns half the marks.)*

### Q45. Ch: Job & Contract Costing — Notional Profit vs. Estimated Total Profit (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** A contract is nearly complete. The following particulars are available as on 31st March 2026:

| Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Contract price | 40,00,000 |
| Cost incurred to date | 28,00,000 |
| Estimated additional cost to complete | 6,00,000 |
| Work certified | 36,00,000 |
| Cash received | 80% of work certified |

You are required to (a) explain the difference between notional profit and estimated total profit, (b) compute both, and (c) determine the profit to be transferred to the Profit & Loss Account.

**Solution:**

**(a) Concept:** **Notional profit** is the profit *earned to date* on the certified work = Value of work certified − Cost of work certified. **Estimated total profit** is the profit expected on the *entire contract* = Contract price − Estimated total cost (cost to date + further cost to complete). When a contract is substantially complete (work certified ≥ 90% of contract price), ICAI prefers recognising profit on the **estimated-total-profit** basis, because it gives a reliable measure of the whole contract's outcome and prevents front-loading of profit.

**WN-1 — Degree of completion:** 36,00,000 ÷ 40,00,000 = **90%** → contract is substantially complete → use estimated-profit method.

**WN-2 — Notional profit** = Work certified − Cost of work certified = ₹36,00,000 − ₹28,00,000 = **₹8,00,000** (all cost to date assumed certified).

**WN-3 — Estimated total profit** = Contract price − Estimated total cost = ₹40,00,000 − (₹28,00,000 + ₹6,00,000) = ₹40,00,000 − ₹34,00,000 = **₹6,00,000**.

**WN-4 — Cash ratio:** Cash received = 80% of ₹36,00,000 = ₹28,80,000; Cash ÷ Work certified = **0.80**.

**Profit to be transferred to P&L** (estimated-profit method):

Profit to P&L = Estimated total profit × (Work certified ÷ Contract price) × (Cash received ÷ Work certified)
= ₹6,00,000 × (36,00,000 ÷ 40,00,000) × 0.80
= ₹6,00,000 × 0.90 × 0.80 = **₹4,32,000**.

**OR (alternative ICAI formula, cost basis):** Estimated profit × (Cost to date ÷ Estimated total cost) × (Cash ÷ Work certified) = ₹6,00,000 × (28,00,000 ÷ 34,00,000) × 0.80 = ₹6,00,000 × 0.8235 × 0.80 = **₹3,95,294**.

**Answer:** Notional profit = **₹8,00,000**; Estimated total profit = **₹6,00,000**; Profit transferred to P&L (work-certified basis) = **₹4,32,000**.

*(Full-marks tip: State clearly that at ≥90% completion the **estimated-total-profit** method applies, and that notional profit (₹8,00,000) is *not* the amount taken to P&L. Show the formula chosen; mentioning the alternative cost-based formula earns the "OR" cross-check mark.)*

### Q46. Ch: Job & Contract Costing — Key Terms in Contract Costing (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the following terms as used in Contract Costing: (i) Work certified, (ii) Work uncertified, (iii) Retention money, (iv) Notional profit, (v) Cost-plus contract.

**Answer:**

(i) **Work certified** — That portion of the contract work which has been inspected and **approved by the contractee's architect/surveyor** and certified as satisfactorily completed. It is valued at the contract (selling) price and forms the basis for interim payments; it is shown on the credit side of the contract account as work-in-progress.

(ii) **Work uncertified** — Work that has been **completed but not yet certified** by the surveyor (e.g., done just before year-end). It is valued at **cost** (not contract price), conservatively, and also credited to the contract account as part of work-in-progress.

(iii) **Retention money** — The portion of the value of work certified **withheld** by the contractee and not paid immediately (i.e., certified value − cash paid). It is retained as security to ensure the contractor rectifies any defects during the maintenance/defect-liability period; it is released after satisfactory completion.

(iv) **Notional profit** — The **profit earned to date** on a contract = Value of work certified + Cost of work uncertified − Cost of work to date. It is an interim measure from which only a prudent portion is transferred to the Profit & Loss Account, the balance being kept as reserve.

(v) **Cost-plus contract** — A contract in which the contract price is **not fixed in advance** but is determined as the **allowable cost incurred plus an agreed margin** (a percentage of cost or a fixed fee). It is used where costs cannot be estimated reliably in advance (e.g., government/defence, urgent or novel work).

**Conclusion:** These terms govern how contract revenue is measured (certified/uncertified), how much is received (retention money), and how interim profit (notional profit) and pricing (cost-plus) are determined.

*(Full-marks tip: Note that work certified is valued at **contract price** but work uncertified at **cost** — reversing these is the classic error. Define retention money as certified value minus cash received, not as an arbitrary deposit.)*

### Q50. Ch: Job & Contract Costing — Cost-plus Contracts & Escalation Clause (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** What is a "cost-plus contract"? State its advantages and disadvantages. Also explain the purpose of an "escalation clause" in a contract.

**Answer:** A **cost-plus contract** is one in which the contract price is not agreed at the outset but is ascertained as the **allowable cost incurred plus an agreed amount of profit** (a fixed percentage of cost or a lump-sum fee). It is used where reliable advance estimation of cost is impossible — e.g., urgent, novel or government/defence work.

**Advantages:** (i) The contractor is protected against loss and assured of a reasonable profit; (ii) it is useful where the scope of work cannot be pre-defined; (iii) the contractee pays only for genuine costs, subject to audit.

**Disadvantages:** (i) The contractor has little incentive to control cost, since a higher cost yields higher absolute profit; (ii) the final price is uncertain for the contractee; (iii) disputes may arise over what constitutes "allowable cost".

**Escalation clause:** An escalation clause is a provision in a fixed-price contract under which the **contract price is adjusted upward** (and sometimes downward — a "de-escalation" clause) for **increases in the prices of specified materials, labour or utilities** beyond an agreed base/allowance during the contract period. Its purpose is to **protect the contractor** against the risk of cost inflation on a long-duration contract, so that the contractor is not forced to bear uncontrollable price rises.

**Conclusion:** A cost-plus contract shifts cost risk to the contractee, whereas an escalation clause is a targeted device inserted in a fixed-price contract to share/limit the risk of input-price inflation.

*(Full-marks tip: Give a balanced advantage/disadvantage list and clearly link the escalation clause to **protection against input-price rises**; note that it operates on *specified* items beyond an agreed allowance, not on all costs.)*

### Q52. Ch: Process & Operation Costing — Normal Loss, Abnormal Loss and Abnormal Gain (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the accounting treatment of (i) Normal Loss, (ii) Abnormal Loss and (iii) Abnormal Gain in process costing. State how each is valued and how it affects the cost per unit of good output.

**Answer:**
In process costing a part of the input is inevitably lost during manufacture; the treatment differs according to whether the loss is expected (normal) or unexpected (abnormal).

**(i) Normal Loss.** Normal loss is the loss which is unavoidable and inherent in the process and is expected under efficient operating conditions (e.g. evaporation, shrinkage, chemical action). It is estimated as a percentage of the input in advance. Since it is a natural part of production, **no cost is charged to normal-loss units** — instead the entire process cost is borne by the good units, thereby increasing the cost per good unit. The normal-loss units are entered on the credit side of the Process Account **only at their realisable/scrap value**, and this scrap value is deducted from the process cost while computing the cost per unit. Cost per unit of good output = (Total process cost − Scrap value of normal loss) ÷ (Input units − Normal loss units).

**(ii) Abnormal Loss.** Abnormal loss is the loss in excess of the normal (expected) loss and arises from avoidable causes such as machine breakdown, sub-standard material, accident or negligence. It is **valued at the same cost per unit as good units** (because it is not the fault of the process design). It is credited to the Process Account and debited to a separate **Abnormal Loss Account**; the scrap realised on abnormal-loss units is credited to that account, and the **net balance is transferred to the Costing Profit & Loss Account** as a loss. This keeps the cost of good production unaffected by avoidable inefficiency.

**(iii) Abnormal Gain.** Abnormal gain (effective/abnormal effectiveness) arises when the actual loss is **less** than the expected normal loss, i.e. actual output exceeds expected output. It is **valued at the cost per unit of good output** and is debited to the Process Account and credited to an **Abnormal Gain Account**. Because the normal-loss scrap that would have been realised on the gained units is not in fact realised, that scrap value is debited to the Abnormal Gain Account, and the **net balance is transferred to the credit of the Costing Profit & Loss Account** as a gain.

**Conclusion:** Normal loss is absorbed by good units (only scrap value recorded), abnormal loss is charged to Costing P&L, and abnormal gain is credited to Costing P&L — so that the cost of good output reflects only normal, controllable conditions.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the explicit statement that abnormal loss/gain are valued at the good-unit cost and routed through Costing P&L; a common deduction is forgetting that abnormal-gain scrap value is DEBITED to the abnormal gain account.)*

### Q55. Ch: Process & Operation Costing — Inter-Process Profit (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** What is meant by "inter-process profit"? Explain the purpose of loading profit on inter-process transfers, its main advantages and disadvantages, and the treatment of unrealised profit contained in closing stock.

**Answer:**
**Meaning.** In some concerns the output of one process is transferred to the next process **not at cost but at a price that includes a margin of profit** (transfer price = cost + a percentage of profit). The margin so added is called **inter-process profit**. Each Process Account is therefore maintained in a **columnar form with three columns — Total, Cost and Profit** — so that the cost element and the profit element of every figure can be tracked separately.

**Purpose.** Profit is loaded on inter-process transfers so as to **compare the cost of each process with the market (open-market) price** of comparable semi-finished goods, thereby judging the efficiency and economic performance of every process as if it were a separate profit centre, and to help decide whether it is cheaper to make or to buy at an intermediate stage.

**Advantages.**
- It reveals whether each process is operating profitably compared with market prices and helps in make-or-buy decisions.
- It makes each process cost-and-profit conscious, improving efficiency and control.

**Disadvantages.**
- The system is **complicated** and increases clerical work because of the three-column records.
- Closing stock (WIP and finished stock) then includes **unrealised profit**, which must be eliminated for balance-sheet purposes, adding to the accounting effort.
- Profit figures may be **misleading** if market prices are not readily available or are unstable.

**Treatment of unrealised profit in stock.** Since goods lying in stock have not yet been sold outside, the profit loaded on the transfers into stock is **not realised** and must not be shown in the Balance Sheet. The unrealised profit in each stock is calculated as:
**Unrealised profit = Closing stock (at transfer price) × (Profit included in the total ÷ Total)** of that process. A **"Provision/Stock Reserve for unrealised profit"** is created and deducted from the value of stock in the Balance Sheet, so that stock appears at cost; the provision is written back when the stock is subsequently consumed or sold.

**Conclusion:** Inter-process profit converts each process into a notional profit centre for control purposes, but the unrealised profit in closing stock must always be eliminated through a stock reserve so that inventory is stated at cost.

*(Full-marks tip: State the three-column (Total/Cost/Profit) format and give the exact formula for unrealised profit; examiners deduct marks when the "deducted as a provision in the Balance Sheet" point is missing.)*

### Q59. Ch: Process & Operation Costing — Process Costing vs Operation Costing (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between **Process Costing** and **Operation Costing**, and state one industry example of each.

**Answer:**
Both are methods used for **continuous / mass production**, but they differ in the unit of cost collection.

| Basis | Process Costing | Operation Costing |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Cost is ascertained for each **process** or stage through which the product passes | Cost is ascertained for each **operation** — a further sub-division within a process/department |
| Unit of collection | The process as a whole | Each individual operation |
| Degree of refinement | Broader; cost per unit = total process cost ÷ output | Finer; enables cost of each specific operation to be known |
| Rejections/spoilage | Dealt with as normal/abnormal loss of the process | Rejection at each operation is tracked, so input to each operation is grossed up for expected rejects |
| Suitability | Industries where output flows through a few clearly defined processes | Industries with many small repetitive operations where operation-wise control is required |
| Example | Chemicals, sugar, paint, refineries | Toy/component manufacture, engineering parts, bicycle/automobile component assembly |

**Conclusion:** Operation costing is essentially a **refinement of process costing** in which costs are accumulated for each operation, giving management sharper operation-wise cost control.

*(Full-marks tip: The key discriminator is "process = broad stage" vs "operation = a repetitive sub-activity within a stage"; give an industry example for each to secure full marks.)*

### Q60. Ch: Process & Operation Costing — Classifying and Valuing Loss (Marks: 5) [Application]

### Q61. Ch: Joint Products & By-Products — Basic Concepts and Apportionment Methods (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between a **joint product** and a **by-product**, and list the principal methods used for apportioning joint costs among joint products.

**Answer:**
**Joint products** are two or more products of **more or less equal economic importance**, produced simultaneously from the same process/raw material, such that none can be regarded as the major product and the others as minor (e.g. petrol, diesel and kerosene from crude oil).

**By-product** is a product of **relatively small/minor value** that arises **incidentally** in the course of manufacturing the main product (e.g. molasses from sugar, bran from flour milling).

| Basis | Joint Products | By-Products |
|---|---|---|
| Economic importance | Comparable / significant value | Minor / incidental value |
| Objective of production | Purposely produced together | Arise incidentally |
| Treatment of cost | Joint cost is apportioned among them | Value is usually credited to the main product/process |

**Methods of apportioning joint costs:**
1. **Physical Units (Quantity) Method** — joint cost shared in the ratio of physical output (units/weight/volume).
2. **Average Unit Cost Method** — total joint cost ÷ total units, giving a common cost per unit.
3. **Sales Value at Split-off Point Method** — joint cost shared in the ratio of sales value at the point of separation.
4. **Net Realisable Value (NRV) Method** — joint cost shared in the ratio of (final sales value − further processing and selling costs).
5. **Reverse Cost / Contribution (Market Value less Margin) Method** — working back from selling price by deducting post-split costs and estimated profit.
6. **Constant Gross-Margin (Weighted Average) Method** — apportioning so that every product earns the same gross-margin percentage.

**Conclusion:** The classification turns on relative economic value; joint cost may be apportioned by physical or by value-based methods, the value-based (sales value / NRV) methods being generally preferred as they relate cost to the product's revenue-earning ability.

*(Full-marks tip: Give the value criterion for the joint/by-product distinction and list at least four apportionment methods by name; naming the NRV and sales-value-at-split-off methods is essential.)*

### Q66. Ch: Joint Products & By-Products — Further Processing Decision with Yield Loss (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** A joint product P currently emerges at the split-off point as **4,000 units** and is sold there at **₹25 per unit**. The company is considering processing P further at an additional cost of **₹60,000**, after which each unit would sell for **₹45**. However, further processing causes a **10% loss in units**, so only 3,600 units would be available for sale. Advise whether P should be processed further. Would your advice change if there were no processing loss?

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Revenue if sold at split-off.**
= 4,000 units × ₹25 = **₹1,00,000.**

**WN-2: Net revenue if processed further (with 10% loss).**
Units available = 4,000 × 90% = 3,600 units.
Sales after further processing = 3,600 × ₹45 = ₹1,62,000.
Less further processing cost = ₹60,000.
Net realisation = **₹1,02,000.**

**WN-3: Incremental analysis.**

| Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Net realisation if processed further | 1,02,000 |
| Less: Revenue if sold at split-off | 1,00,000 |
| **Net incremental benefit** | **2,000** |

### Q67. Ch: Joint Products & By-Products — Treatment of By-Products in Cost Accounts (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the various methods available for accounting the cost/value of **by-products** in cost accounts.

**Answer:**
By-products are of minor value and arise incidentally; their treatment depends on whether their value is significant. Broadly, two groups of methods are used:

**A. Non-Cost (Sales Value) Methods** — used where the by-product is of small value; no share of joint cost is charged to it.
1. **Other Income / Miscellaneous Income Method:** the net sales value of the by-product is credited to the **Costing Profit & Loss Account** as other income, leaving the full joint cost with the main product. Simple, but ignores stock of by-product.
2. **Sales Value less Selling/Distribution Cost credited to the Process:** the net realisable value (sales less any post-split selling/distribution cost) is **credited to the process/main-product cost**, thereby reducing the cost of the main product.
3. **Sales Value less Selling Cost and Profit (Reverse Cost) Method:** the by-product's value credited to the process is worked back as sales value less selling cost, further processing cost and an estimated normal profit — leaving only a "cost" element to reduce the main-product cost.

**B. Cost Methods** — used where the by-product is of appreciable value and a portion of joint cost should be assigned to it.
4. **Replacement/Opportunity Cost Method:** where the by-product is used internally, it is valued at the cost that would otherwise be incurred to buy/replace it, credited to the process.
5. **Standard Cost Method:** the by-product is credited at a pre-determined standard cost, any difference being absorbed by the main product.
6. **Apportionment on a Suitable Basis (Joint-cost apportionment):** where the by-product is significant, a share of the joint cost up to the split-off point is apportioned to it using a value or physical basis, exactly as for joint products.

**Conclusion:** Where the by-product's value is negligible, non-cost (sales value) methods are used; where it is material, cost methods that assign a portion of joint cost are more appropriate so that both main product and by-product are fairly costed.

*(Full-marks tip: Group the methods into "non-cost (sales value)" and "cost" categories and name at least two under each; the reverse-cost and replacement-cost methods are the ones students most often omit.)*

### Q75. Ch: Standard Costing — Types of Standards (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the different types of standards that may be set in a standard costing system, and state which type is most useful for cost control.

**Answer:** A standard is a predetermined measurable quantity set in defined conditions against which actual performance is compared. In practice four types of standards are recognised.

**Basic (Bogey) Standard** is a standard established for use unaltered over a long period. Since it is not revised for current conditions, it is unsuitable as a target for day-to-day control and is used mainly to study cost trends over time by comparing successive periods against a fixed base.

**Ideal (Perfection) Standard** assumes the most favourable conditions possible — no idle time, no wastage, no machine breakdown and no spoilage. Because such perfection is rarely attainable, ideal standards are demotivating and are seldom used for control; they serve only to indicate the theoretical maximum efficiency.

**Current Standard** is a standard established for use over a short period and related to current conditions. It reflects the performance actually expected in the period and is therefore realistic, but if conditions change frequently it must be revised often.

**Expected (Attainable) Standard** is a standard that can be attained under efficient but not perfect operating conditions, making a reasonable allowance for normal wastage, idle time and machine downtime. It is challenging yet achievable, and therefore motivates employees.

| Type | Basis | Suitability for control |
|---|---|---|
| Basic | Fixed over long period | Poor — only for trend study |
| Ideal | Perfect conditions | Poor — demotivating |
| Current | Present conditions | Good but needs frequent revision |
| Expected/Attainable | Efficient realistic conditions | **Best — realistic and motivating** |

**Conclusion:** The **expected (attainable) standard** is regarded as the most useful for cost control because it is both realistic and motivating, providing a fair yardstick against which controllable variances can be measured.

*(Full-marks tip: name all four types and, for each, give the defining assumption plus a comment on control usefulness; end by nominating the attainable standard — examiners look for the reasoned choice, not just a list.)*

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Standards** — Std input mix 60:40; std cost of one batch = 60×10 + 40×20 = 600 + 800 = ₹1,400 for 80 kg output, i.e. std cost per kg of output = 1,400 ÷ 80 = **₹17.50**.

**WN-4: Mix Variance** = (Std-mix qty − Actual qty) × SP
- X: (600 − 620) × 10 = 200 (A); Y: (400 − 380) × 20 = 400 (F) → **200 (F)**

**WN-5: Yield Variance** = (Actual output − Std output from actual input) × std cost per kg of output

### Q77. Ch: Marginal Costing — CVP under a Change in Cost Structure (Marks: 6) [Problem]

### Q81. Ch: Marginal Costing — Assumptions of CVP Analysis (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the key assumptions underlying Cost-Volume-Profit (CVP) analysis and briefly explain why they limit its use.

**Answer:** CVP analysis studies the effect of changes in the level of activity on cost, volume and profit, and rests on a number of simplifying assumptions.

First, all costs can be **segregated into fixed and variable** components, with fixed costs remaining constant in total and variable costs constant per unit throughout the relevant range. In reality many costs are semi-variable and behave in a step or curvilinear fashion, so the linear split holds only over a limited activity range.

Second, the **selling price per unit is constant** irrespective of volume; in practice discounts and market pressures cause price to fall as volume expands, so the total-revenue line is not truly straight.

Third, **efficiency and productivity remain unchanged**, so the variable cost per unit does not vary with output; economies or diseconomies of scale are ignored.

Fourth, in a multi-product firm the **sales mix remains constant**; a change in mix alters the weighted contribution and shifts the break-even point.

Fifth, **production equals sales** (no change in inventory), so that costs and revenues relate to the same volume; where stocks build up or run down, marginal and absorption profits diverge.

**Conclusion:** Because these assumptions hold only within a narrow "relevant range" and over the short term, CVP analysis is a valuable but approximate short-run planning tool rather than a precise long-run model.

*(Full-marks tip: list at least four assumptions and, crucially, pair each with the real-world limitation it creates — a bare list without the "why it limits use" earns only half marks.)*

### Q85. Ch: Budgets & Budgetary Control — Flexible Budget (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the meaning of a "Flexible Budget" and state clearly how it differs from a "Fixed Budget". In what situations is a flexible budget more useful?

**Answer:**
A **Flexible Budget** is a budget which, by recognising the difference in behaviour between fixed and variable costs in relation to fluctuations in output, turnover or other variable factors, is designed to change appropriately with such fluctuations. In other words, it is a budget prepared in a manner so as to give the budgeted cost for **any level of activity actually attained**.

A **Fixed Budget** (also called a static budget), on the other hand, is a budget which is designed to remain unchanged irrespective of the level of activity actually attained. It is based on a single assumed level of output and is not adjusted for the actual volume achieved.

The essential distinction is that a fixed budget compares actual results against a target set for an assumed volume, whereas a flexible budget first "flexes" (re-casts) the target to the volume actually attained, so that the comparison of actuals with budget is on a like-for-like basis and the resulting variances are meaningful.

| Basis | Fixed Budget | Flexible Budget |
|---|---|---|
| Activity level | Prepared for a single, assumed level of activity | Prepared for a range of activity levels |
| Cost classification | Costs are not segregated into fixed and variable | Costs are segregated into fixed, variable and semi-variable |
| Adjustment to actual volume | Not adjusted; remains unchanged | Re-cast to the actual level attained |
| Basis of variance | Comparison may be misleading (different volumes) | Comparison is realistic (same volume) |
| Suitability | Where activity can be forecast accurately and is stable | Where activity fluctuates (seasonal demand, new/uncertain business, make-to-order) |

A flexible budget is more useful where the level of activity cannot be forecast with accuracy — for example in seasonal industries, in businesses subject to changing demand, in units introducing a new product, or where output depends on factors outside the firm's control (such as availability of power, material or labour).

**Conclusion:** A flexible budget provides a logical basis of comparison because the yardstick is adjusted to the volume actually achieved, thereby isolating true cost efficiency from mere volume changes.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the exact behavioural logic — "recognises difference between fixed and variable cost" — and a properly filled comparison table. Marks are commonly lost for merely writing "changes with activity" without stating WHY, i.e., cost segregation.)*

### Q89. Ch: Budgets & Budgetary Control — Budgetary Control vs Standard Costing (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** "Budgetary control and standard costing have the same objective of cost control, yet they differ in scope and technique." Distinguish between **Budgetary Control** and **Standard Costing**.

**Answer:**
Both budgetary control and standard costing are techniques of cost control that operate on the principle of setting predetermined targets, comparing them with actual results and analysing the variances for corrective action. They are complementary and are often used together. However, they differ significantly in scope, coverage and depth of analysis, as set out below.

| Basis | Budgetary Control | Standard Costing |
|---|---|---|
| Scope / coverage | Extends to the whole business — every function (sales, production, cash, capital, R&D, etc.) | Restricted mainly to production/manufacturing costs (material, labour, overhead) |
| Basis of targets | Budgets are based on past records adjusted for anticipated future conditions | Standards are based on technical/engineering studies and scientifically set norms |
| Unit of measure | Deals with totals for the concern or department | Deals with cost per unit of output |
| Intensity of analysis | Variances are generally not analysed in fine detail | Variances are analysed intensively into causes (price/rate, usage/efficiency, volume, mix, yield) |
| Projection nature | It is a projection of financial accounts | It is a projection of cost accounts |
| Applicability | Can be applied even without standard costing | Cannot be effectively operated without a system of budgets for overhead absorption |
| Recording | Budgets are memorandum records; not usually entered in books | Standards are usually incorporated into the accounting records |

**Conclusion:** Budgetary control is broader in scope but shallower in analysis, whereas standard costing is narrower (confined largely to manufacturing) but far deeper, isolating specific causes of each variance. The two are mutually reinforcing — standard costing supplies the detailed norms and budgetary control provides the overall framework.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner looks for the two anchor distinctions — (i) scope: whole business vs manufacturing cost, and (ii) depth: broad vs detailed sub-variance analysis. A clean 6–7 row table earns full marks; a vague "both control cost" paragraph does not.)*

### Q91. Ch: Budgets & Budgetary Control — Zero-Based Budgeting (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the concept of **Zero-Based Budgeting (ZBB)**. State its main stages and two advantages.

**Answer:**
**Zero-Based Budgeting (ZBB)** is a method of budgeting whereby all activities are re-evaluated each time a budget is set and every line of expenditure must be **justified afresh from a "zero base"**, as if the activity were being undertaken for the first time. Unlike traditional (incremental) budgeting — which takes the previous year's figures as the base and merely adjusts them for expected changes — ZBB does not accept the prior period's expenditure as a given. Every function must prove, in each budget cycle, that the resources it seeks are justified by the benefits it delivers.

**Main stages in ZBB:**
1. **Identification of "decision units"** — activities or programmes for which costs and benefits can be independently evaluated.
2. **Preparation of "decision packages"** — for each decision unit, a document describing the activity, its purpose, costs, benefits, and alternative levels/methods of performing it.
3. **Ranking of decision packages** — arranging the packages in order of priority on a cost-benefit basis.
4. **Allocation of resources** — funds are allocated to packages in order of ranking, up to the resources available.

**Advantages:**
- It ensures a **rational, cost-benefit driven allocation** of resources and eliminates wasteful or obsolete activities, since nothing is funded merely because it existed last year.
- It **identifies and removes inefficiency and duplication** of effort, and links expenditure directly to objectives, making managers accountable.

**Conclusion:** ZBB is particularly useful for controlling discretionary and support-service costs (e.g., R&D, administration, marketing), though it is more time-consuming and costly to operate than incremental budgeting.

*(Full-marks tip: The defining phrase — "every activity justified afresh from zero, previous year not taken as base" — must appear. Listing the four stages (decision units → decision packages → ranking → allocation) secures the remaining marks.)*

### Q92. Ch: Budgets & Budgetary Control — Budget Control Ratios (Marks: 6) [Problem]

| Standard hours for actual production | 9,600 hours |

### Q93. Ch: Service Costing — Meaning and Cost Units (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** What is **Service (Operating) Costing**? State its distinctive features and give the appropriate **cost unit** for any four service undertakings.

**Answer:**
**Service Costing** (also called Operating Costing) is that method of costing which is applied to undertakings that render **services** rather than manufacture tangible products. As defined by the terminology, it is "the method of cost accounting used to determine the cost of services." It ascertains the cost of a unit of service (e.g., per passenger-km, per patient-day) so that a price can be fixed and the operation controlled.

**Distinctive features of service costing:**
- The output is **intangible** (a service), and cannot be stored or inventoried.
- The cost unit is frequently a **composite (compound) unit** — a combination of two factors, e.g., tonne-kilometre or passenger-kilometre — because a single factor does not adequately measure the service.
- A **large proportion of costs are fixed/standing charges** (e.g., depreciation, insurance, salaries), with running/operating and maintenance charges forming the variable element.
- Costs are usually classified as **standing (fixed) charges, running (variable) charges and maintenance charges**.

**Cost units for different service undertakings:**

| Service undertaking | Cost unit |
|---|---|
| Goods transport | Tonne-kilometre (tonne-km) |
| Passenger transport | Passenger-kilometre (passenger-km) |
| Hospital | Patient-day (per bed per day) |
| Hotel | Room-day (guest-day) |
| Electricity / power | Kilowatt-hour (kWh) |
| Canteen | Per meal served |

**Conclusion:** Service costing determines the cost per unit of service using an appropriate (often composite) cost unit, enabling the service provider to price the service and monitor operating efficiency.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards naming the THREE cost classifications — standing, running, maintenance — and giving a correct cost unit for each named service. Composite cost unit is the concept that distinguishes this chapter; mention it.)*

### Q97. Ch: Service Costing — Absolute vs Commercial (Effective) Tonne-Km (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** A lorry of Speedways Logistics performs the following journey in one trip, which is repeated **20 times a month**:

| Leg | Distance (km) | Load carried (tonnes) |
|---|---:|---:|
| A → B | 200 | 9 |
| B → C | 240 | 6 |
| C → A (return) | 260 | 9 |

The monthly operating cost of the lorry is: standing charges ₹80,000; diesel ₹2,52,000; oil, tyres and repairs ₹25,000. Compute the **absolute tonne-kilometres** and the **commercial (effective) tonne-kilometres** per month, and the **cost per tonne-km** under each basis. Explain the difference between the two bases.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Absolute tonne-km per trip**
Absolute tonne-km = Σ (load on each leg × distance of that leg)
= (9 × 200) + (6 × 240) + (9 × 260)
= 1,800 + 1,440 + 2,340 = **5,580 tonne-km per trip**
Monthly = 5,580 × 20 trips = **1,11,600 tonne-km**.

**WN-2: Commercial (effective) tonne-km per trip**
Commercial tonne-km = Average load per trip × Total distance of the trip.
Average load = (9 + 6 + 9) ÷ 3 legs = 24 ÷ 3 = 8 tonnes.
Total distance = 200 + 240 + 260 = 700 km.
Commercial tonne-km = 8 × 700 = **5,600 tonne-km per trip**
Monthly = 5,600 × 20 trips = **1,12,000 tonne-km**.

**WN-3: Total monthly operating cost**
= 80,000 + 2,52,000 + 25,000 = **₹3,57,000**.

**Statement Showing Cost per Tonne-Km**

| Basis | Monthly tonne-km | Cost (₹) | Cost per tonne-km (₹) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Absolute tonne-km | 1,11,600 | 3,57,000 | 3.20 |
| Commercial (effective) tonne-km | 1,12,000 | 3,57,000 | 3.19 |

**Difference between the two bases:**
- **Absolute tonne-km** is the sum of the products of the load carried and the distance for each individual leg taken separately. It is the more precise measure of the actual transport service rendered.
- **Commercial (effective) tonne-km** is obtained by multiplying the *average* load for the whole trip by the *total* distance of the trip. It is simpler but less accurate because it ignores the leg-by-leg variation in load.

**Answer:** Absolute tonne-km = **1,11,600** (cost **₹3.20** per tonne-km); Commercial tonne-km = **1,12,000** (cost **₹3.19** per tonne-km).

*(Full-marks tip: The two definitions and the ONE numerical difference — "products of leg loads summed" vs "average load × total distance" — are the marks. Compute the average load over the number of legs, not over loaded legs only.)*

**WN-3: Cost per equivalent room-day**
= Total cost ÷ Equivalent room-days = ₹48,18,000 ÷ 24,090 = **₹200 per equivalent room-day**.

**WN-4: Tariff (cost + 25% profit on cost)**
Cost + profit per equivalent unit = 200 × 1.25 = **₹250**.
- **Deluxe room tariff** (1 equivalent) = ₹250 per room-day
- **Luxury room tariff** (2 equivalents) = ₹250 × 2 = ₹500 per room-day

**Statement Showing Room Tariff**

| Room type | Room-days | Weight | Equivalent room-days | Tariff per room-day (₹) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Deluxe | 13,140 | 1 | 13,140 | 250 |
| Luxury | 5,475 | 2 | 10,950 | 500 |
| **Total** | | | **24,090** | |

**Cross-check on revenue:** (13,140 × 250) + (5,475 × 500) = 32,85,000 + 27,37,500 = ₹60,22,500 = ₹48,18,000 cost × 1.25 ✓.

**Answer:** Tariff per room-day — **Deluxe ₹250** and **Luxury ₹500** (each earning 25% profit on cost).

### Q99. Ch: Service Costing — IT/BPO Cost per Chargeable Man-Hour (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** Nexus BPO employs **30 agents**. Each agent works **8 hours a day for 25 days a month**, but only **80%** of the hours are chargeable to clients (the balance being training and idle time). The monthly costs are: agents' salaries ₹9,00,000; administration and rent ₹2,40,000; telecom charges ₹60,000; depreciation ₹60,000. Compute the **chargeable man-hours** and the **cost per chargeable man-hour**, and the **billing rate** to earn **25% profit on cost**.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Chargeable man-hours per month**
Gross available hours = 30 agents × 8 hours × 25 days = 6,000 hours.
Chargeable hours = 6,000 × 80% = **4,800 man-hours**.
(The 20% non-chargeable hours are a normal cost of running the operation and must be recovered from the chargeable hours.)

**WN-2: Total operating cost**

| Item | ₹ |
|---|---:|
| Agents' salaries | 9,00,000 |
| Administration and rent | 2,40,000 |
| Telecom charges | 60,000 |
| Depreciation | 60,000 |
| **Total cost** | **12,60,000** |

**WN-3: Cost per chargeable man-hour**
= ₹12,60,000 ÷ 4,800 = **₹262.50 per chargeable hour**.

**WN-4: Billing rate (25% profit on cost)**
= 262.50 × 1.25 = **₹328.125 ≈ ₹328.13 per chargeable hour**.

**Answer:** Chargeable man-hours = **4,800**; cost per chargeable man-hour = **₹262.50**; billing rate to earn 25% on cost = **₹328.13 per hour**.

### Q100. Ch: Service Costing — Simple vs Composite Cost Units (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between a **simple cost unit** and a **composite (compound) cost unit** in service costing. Explain, with reference to goods transport, the difference between **absolute tonne-kilometre** and **commercial tonne-kilometre**.

**Answer:**
In service costing the cost unit may be either simple or composite, depending on whether one factor or two factors are needed to express the service meaningfully.

A **simple cost unit** measures the service by reference to a **single factor** — for example, cost per kilowatt-hour of electricity, cost per litre of water supplied, or cost per meal in a canteen.

A **composite (compound) cost unit** measures the service by reference to **two factors combined**, because a single factor would not fairly reflect the quantum of service. Examples are the **tonne-kilometre** (weight × distance) in goods transport, the **passenger-kilometre** (number of passengers × distance) in passenger transport, and the **patient-day** (patients × days) in a hospital. The composite unit is used because, for example, carrying 1 tonne for 100 km is not the same service as carrying 10 tonnes for 10 km, even though the distance or the weight alone might appear equal.

| Basis | Absolute Tonne-Km | Commercial Tonne-Km |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Sum of the products of the load carried and the distance travelled for **each leg** of the journey taken separately | **Average load** carried per trip × **total distance** of the trip |
| Formula | Σ (load of a leg × distance of that leg) | Average load × Total distance |
| Accuracy | More accurate — reflects actual variation in load leg by leg | Less accurate — ignores leg-wise load variation |
| Use | Preferred for precise cost ascertainment | A simplified approximation |

**Conclusion:** A composite cost unit combines two service factors; and where transport loads vary over the journey, the absolute tonne-km gives the true measure of service while the commercial tonne-km is a convenient average approximation.

*(Full-marks tip: Give at least two examples each of simple and composite units, and the exact defining contrast for absolute vs commercial tonne-km — "leg-by-leg products summed" vs "average load × total distance." That precise wording is what the examiner ticks.)*
