### Q1. Ch: Material Cost — Perpetual Inventory System vs Periodic Inventory & Continuous Stock Verification (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between the "Perpetual Inventory System" and the "Periodic Inventory System" of recording stores. Also explain how "Continuous Stock Verification" reinforces the perpetual system, and state two advantages a firm derives from operating it.

**Answer:**
The **Perpetual Inventory System** is a system of records — maintained through **Bin Cards** and the **Stores Ledger** — that reflects the physical movement of stocks and their current balance **after every single receipt and issue**, so that the balance of any item of stores can be ascertained at any point of time **without physical counting**. It is a *records-based* running balance system.

The **Periodic Inventory System**, by contrast, ascertains the value of closing stock **only at the end of an accounting period** by conducting a complete physical count on one date, and consumption is worked out as a residual: **Opening Stock + Purchases − Closing Stock = Consumption**. It does not throw up losses, wastage or pilferage separately because everything not physically present is buried inside "consumption".

| Basis | Perpetual Inventory System | Periodic Inventory System |
|---|---|---|
| Balance ascertained | Continuously, after each transaction | Only at period-end |
| Basis | Book records (Bin Card + Stores Ledger) | Physical count |
| Consumption | Directly recorded (issues) | Residual figure |
| Stock-out / loss | Revealed item-wise | Absorbed silently into consumption |
| Cost & effort | Higher (continuous records) | Lower, but disruptive at period-end |

**Continuous Stock Verification** is the programme of physically checking a **few items every day throughout the year** (on a rotating, surprise basis) and reconciling the physical quantity with the Bin Card / Stores Ledger balance. It is the *independent physical check* that validates the *book balances* of the perpetual system — the two are complementary: perpetual inventory maintains the records, continuous verification proves them.

Two advantages: (i) it **avoids the dislocation of production** caused by closing the factory for annual stock-taking, and (ii) **discrepancies, obsolescence, slow-moving items and pilferage are detected early**, item by item, allowing timely corrective action and reliable interim accounts.

**Conclusion:** The perpetual system gives a continuous, verifiable stock position, and continuous stock verification is the physical audit that keeps those book balances trustworthy.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the explicit link "Bin Card + Stores Ledger = perpetual records" and "continuous verification = the physical check that proves them." Students lose marks by confusing *continuous stock verification* with *perpetual inventory* — state clearly that one is physical checking and the other is record-keeping.)*

### Q2. Ch: Material Cost — Inventory Turnover Ratio & Slow-Moving Items (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** From the following data of three raw materials for the year ended 31st March, 2026, compute (i) the Inventory Turnover Ratio (in times), and (ii) the average number of days each item is held in stock (take 365 days). Comment on which material is slow-moving.

| Material | Opening Stock (₹) | Closing Stock (₹) | Purchases during year (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|
| P | 10,000 | 6,000 | 40,000 |
| Q | 5,000 | 15,000 | 20,000 |
| R | 8,000 | 4,000 | 28,000 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Materials Consumed** (Opening + Purchases − Closing):
- P = 10,000 + 40,000 − 6,000 = **₹44,000**
- Q = 5,000 + 20,000 − 15,000 = **₹10,000**
- R = 8,000 + 28,000 − 4,000 = **₹32,000**

**WN-2 — Average Stock** [(Opening + Closing) ÷ 2]:
- P = (10,000 + 6,000)/2 = **₹8,000**
- Q = (5,000 + 15,000)/2 = **₹10,000**
- R = (8,000 + 4,000)/2 = **₹6,000**

**Statement Showing Inventory Turnover Ratio and Holding Period**

| Material | Consumption (₹) | Avg. Stock (₹) | ITR = Cons. ÷ Avg. Stock (times) | Holding Days = 365 ÷ ITR |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| P | 44,000 | 8,000 | 5.50 | 66 days |
| Q | 10,000 | 10,000 | 1.00 | 365 days |
| R | 32,000 | 6,000 | 5.33 | 68 days |

**Answer:** Inventory Turnover Ratios are P = 5.50 times, Q = 1.00 time, R = 5.33 times; holding periods are 66, 365 and 68 days respectively. **Material Q is the slow-moving item** — it turns over only once a year (365 days in stock), signalling over-stocking / possible obsolescence, and calls for investigation before further procurement.

*(Full-marks tip: Examiners want the turnover ratio computed on *consumption*, not on purchases or closing stock. The comment identifying the slow-mover (lowest ITR = Q) carries a mark on its own — never omit the interpretation.)*

### Q3. Ch: Material Cost — Evaluation of a JIT Purchasing Proposal (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** A company currently buys a component in bulk. Its purchase manager proposes switching to a **Just-in-Time (JIT)** purchasing arrangement with a nearby supplier who will deliver daily. Evaluate the proposal and advise, from the following data:

| Particulars | Present (Bulk) | Proposed (JIT) |
|---|---|---|
| Annual requirement (units) | 60,000 | 60,000 |
| Purchase price per unit | ₹100 | ₹103 |
| Average inventory held (units) | 6,000 | 500 |
| Inventory carrying cost (% of average inventory value p.a.) | 20% | 20% |
| Warehouse rent & handling (fixed, p.a.) | ₹1,50,000 | Warehouse released — Nil |
| Stock-out / insurance premium (p.a.) | Nil | 40,000 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Extra Purchase Price under JIT:** 60,000 units × ₹(103 − 100) = 60,000 × ₹3 = **₹1,80,000 (extra cost)**

**WN-2 — Carrying Cost of Average Inventory (20% of avg. inventory value):**
- Present: 6,000 × ₹100 × 20% = **₹1,20,000**
- JIT: 500 × ₹103 × 20% = **₹10,300**
- Saving = 1,20,000 − 10,300 = **₹1,09,700**

**WN-3 — Warehouse rent & handling saved:** ₹1,50,000 (released) − Nil = **₹1,50,000 saving**

**Statement Showing Net Benefit / (Cost) of Adopting JIT**

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| **Incremental costs of JIT** | |
| Extra purchase price (WN-1) | 1,80,000 |
| Stock-out / insurance premium | 40,000 |
| **Total incremental cost (A)** | **2,20,000** |
| **Savings under JIT** | |
| Reduction in carrying cost (WN-2) | 1,09,700 |
| Warehouse rent & handling saved (WN-3) | 1,50,000 |
| **Total savings (B)** | **2,59,700** |
| **Net benefit of JIT (B − A)** | **39,700** |

**OR (cross-check — total relevant cost of each option):**
- Present = Purchase 60,00,000 + Carrying 1,20,000 + Warehouse 1,50,000 = **₹62,70,000**
- JIT = Purchase 61,80,000 + Carrying 10,300 + Insurance 40,000 = **₹62,30,300**
- Present − JIT = **₹39,700 saving** — reconciles.

**Answer:** Adopting JIT yields a **net saving of ₹39,700 per annum**; the proposal should be **accepted**, subject to the supplier's reliability of daily delivery (since JIT eliminates the safety cushion).

*(Full-marks tip: Show the extra purchase price as a cost and both the carrying-cost reduction and released warehouse rent as savings; the decisive figure is the *net*. Mention the qualitative rider — supplier reliability — that JIT demands, as ICAI awards a mark for it.)*

### Q4. Ch: Material Cost — VED Analysis vs ABC Analysis (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain "VED Analysis" of inventory control. How does its basis of classification differ from "ABC Analysis"? For which type of stores is VED analysis specially suited?

**Answer:**
**VED Analysis** classifies items of inventory according to their **criticality to production / functioning**, i.e. the consequence of a stock-out, into three categories:
- **V — Vital:** items whose absence brings production or the plant to a **complete standstill** (e.g. a critical spare of a bottleneck machine). A high level of safety stock is justified regardless of value.
- **E — Essential:** items whose stock-out causes a **temporary loss / dislocation** of production that can be tolerated for a short while; a moderate stock is kept.
- **D — Desirable:** items whose absence causes **only minor inconvenience**; minimum or nil stock is held.

**Difference in basis from ABC Analysis:**

| Basis | ABC Analysis | VED Analysis |
|---|---|---|
| Classification criterion | **Value of consumption** (money) | **Criticality / consequence of stock-out** |
| Categories | A (high value), B (medium), C (low) | V (vital), E (essential), D (desirable) |
| Focus | Financial / usage-value control | Continuity of production |
| Suited for | General stores & raw materials | **Spare parts** and maintenance stores |

VED analysis is **specially suited to spare parts**, because a spare may be very **low in value** (so ABC would place it in class C and under-control it) yet **vital** to production — its criticality, not its rupee value, must govern the stock policy. In practice the two are often combined into a **VED-ABC matrix**.

**Conclusion:** ABC controls stock by *value*, VED by *criticality*; VED is indispensable for spare parts where a cheap item can still halt production.

*(Full-marks tip: The single most-rewarded point is that a *low-value but vital spare* would be mis-classified by ABC — this justifies VED for spares. Naming all three categories with their stock-out consequence secures the theory marks.)*

### Q5. Ch: Material Cost — Optimum Safety Stock / Re-order Level using Stock-out Cost (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** A company places **12 orders** of a material during the year. The carrying cost of one unit of the material is **₹40 per annum** and the stock-out cost is **₹150 per unit**. The probability distribution of demand during the lead time is given below. Determine the **optimum re-order level (ROL)** that minimises the total of carrying cost of safety stock and stock-out cost.

| Demand during lead time (units) | 400 | 450 | 500 | 550 | 600 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Probability | 0.10 | 0.20 | 0.40 | 0.20 | 0.10 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Average (expected) demand during lead time:**
(400×0.10)+(450×0.20)+(500×0.40)+(550×0.20)+(600×0.10)
= 40 + 90 + 200 + 110 + 60 = **500 units**.
Safety Stock at any ROL = (ROL − 500 units). We test ROL = 500, 550 and 600.

**WN-2 — Carrying cost of Safety Stock** = Safety stock × ₹40:
- ROL 600 → SS 100 → 100 × 40 = ₹4,000
- ROL 550 → SS 50 → 50 × 40 = ₹2,000
- ROL 500 → SS 0 → ₹0

**WN-3 — Expected annual stock-out cost** (shortage occurs when demand > ROL); stock-out cost = expected shortage units per order × 12 orders × ₹150:

*ROL = 500:* shortage if demand 550 (50 units × 0.20) or 600 (100 × 0.10) → (10 + 10) = 20 units/order → 20 × 12 × 150 = **₹36,000**
*ROL = 550:* shortage only if demand 600 (50 units × 0.10) → 5 units/order → 5 × 12 × 150 = **₹9,000**
*ROL = 600:* no demand exceeds 600 → shortage Nil → **₹0**

**Statement Showing Total Cost at each Re-order Level**

| Re-order Level (units) | Carrying cost of SS (₹) | Expected stock-out cost (₹) | Total Cost (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 500 | 0 | 36,000 | 36,000 |
| 550 | 2,000 | 9,000 | 11,000 |
| **600** | **4,000** | **0** | **4,000** |

**Answer:** The **optimum re-order level is 600 units** (safety stock 100 units), at which the combined carrying-plus-stock-out cost is minimum at **₹4,000 per annum**.

*(Full-marks tip: The tested variable is the ROL, and stock-out cost must be multiplied by the number of orders (12), not counted once. Students who forget the "×12 orders" understate stock-out cost and pick the wrong ROL — always annualise the expected shortage.)*

### Q6. Ch: Material Cost — EOQ with Quantity (Price-break) Discount (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** A firm uses **24,000 units** of a material per year. The ordering cost is **₹300 per order** and the annual carrying cost is **20% of the unit purchase price**. The supplier offers the following price schedule. Determine the most economical purchase quantity.

| Order quantity (units) | Price per unit (₹) |
|---|---|
| Less than 3,000 | 20.00 |
| 3,000 – 5,999 | 19.50 |
| 6,000 and above | 19.00 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Basic EOQ at the highest price (₹20):**
EOQ = √(2 × A × O ÷ C) = √(2 × 24,000 × 300 ÷ (20% × 20)) = √(1,44,00,000 ÷ 4) = √36,00,000 ≈ **1,897 units** (price ₹20 applies, as EOQ < 3,000).

Because carrying cost rises with each lower price the EOQ at ₹19.50 and ₹19 also falls below their respective discount thresholds, so the discount quantities can only be obtained by ordering **at the price-break points (3,000 and 6,000 units)**. We therefore compare total annual cost at Q = 1,897, 3,000 and 6,000.

**WN-2 — Total Annual Cost = Purchase + Ordering + Carrying**
(Ordering = A/Q × 300; Carrying = Q/2 × 20% × price)

| Particulars | Q = 1,897 @ ₹20 | Q = 3,000 @ ₹19.50 | Q = 6,000 @ ₹19 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Purchase cost (24,000 × price) | 4,80,000 | 4,68,000 | 4,56,000 |
| Ordering cost (24,000/Q × 300) | 3,795 | 2,400 | 1,200 |
| Carrying cost (Q/2 × 20% × price) | 3,794 | 5,850 | 11,400 |
| **Total Annual Cost (₹)** | **4,87,589** | **4,76,250** | **4,68,600** |

**Answer:** The **most economical order quantity is 6,000 units** at ₹19 per unit, giving the lowest total annual cost of **₹4,68,600**. The purchase-price saving from the 6,000-unit discount (₹24,000 vs the base price) outweighs the higher carrying cost.

*(Full-marks tip: With quantity discounts, EOQ alone is not the answer — you must add **purchase cost** into the comparison and evaluate every price-break quantity. Omitting purchase cost (₹4.56–4.80 lakh) is the classic error that flips the decision.)*

### Q7. Ch: Material Cost — Treatment of Waste, Scrap, Spoilage and Defectives in Cost Accounts (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between **Waste, Scrap, Spoilage and Defectives**, and state the treatment of each (normal and abnormal) in cost accounts.

**Answer:**
These are four distinct forms of material loss in production, differing in physical form and in recoverability:

| Term | Meaning | Realisable value |
|---|---|---|
| **Waste** | Portion of raw material **lost in processing with no measurable recovery** (evaporation, smoke, gas, dust, shrinkage) | Nil (may even cost money to dispose of) |
| **Scrap** | **Residue material of small measurable value** incidentally arising from manufacture (turnings, borings, off-cuts) | Small realisable value |
| **Spoilage** | Goods **so damaged in manufacture that they cannot be rectified** and are sold as seconds / re-melted | Some salvage / disposal value |
| **Defectives** | Production that is **sub-standard but can be rectified** and made saleable by incurring additional cost | Rectifiable — reworked |

**Treatment in cost accounts:**
- **Normal Waste:** the cost of normal waste is **absorbed by the good units**, raising their per-unit cost (loss quantity is ignored in the cost per good unit denominator).
- **Abnormal Waste:** valued at full cost and **transferred to the Costing Profit & Loss Account**, so it does not distort product cost.
- **Normal Scrap:** its realisable value is credited either to the specific **job/process** that produced it (if identifiable) or to **factory overhead** (if general). If the amount is trivial it may be treated as **other income**.
- **Abnormal Scrap:** the cost is transferred to the **Costing Profit & Loss Account** (net of any sale value).
- **Normal Spoilage:** cost (net of salvage) is **borne by the good production** or charged to the department/overhead.
- **Abnormal Spoilage:** written off to the **Costing Profit & Loss Account**.
- **Defectives (rectification):** if normal, the **rework cost is charged to the job / department / overhead**; if abnormal, the rework cost is charged to the **Costing Profit & Loss Account**.

**Conclusion:** In every case the **normal** loss is absorbed by good production (or credited to job/overhead for scrap), while the **abnormal** portion is isolated and charged to the Costing P&L so that product cost reflects only expected losses.

*(Full-marks tip: The two-way split — normal → product/overhead, abnormal → Costing P&L — is the marking backbone. Distinguishing **defectives (rectifiable)** from **spoilage (not rectifiable)** is the discriminating point examiners look for.)*

### Q8. Ch: Material Cost — Stores Ledger under Weighted Average (Perpetual) with Return to Stores (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** Prepare the Stores Ledger Account of Material X under the **Weighted Average price method** (perpetual) from the following transactions in March 2026:

| Date | Transaction |
|---|---|
| Mar 1 | Opening balance 500 units @ ₹20 |
| Mar 3 | Received 300 units @ ₹22 |
| Mar 7 | Issued 400 units to Job A |
| Mar 12 | Received 400 units @ ₹24 |
| Mar 18 | Issued 500 units to Job B |
| Mar 25 | 50 units returned to stores from Job B |

**Solution:**

**WN — Weighted average rate is recomputed after each *receipt*; issues are made at the rate ruling on the date of issue. Materials returned to stores from a job are taken back at the rate at which they were last issued (₹22.375 of Mar 18).**

**Stores Ledger Account — Material X (Weighted Average, Perpetual)**

| Date | Receipts (Qty / Rate ₹ / Amt ₹) | Issues (Qty / Rate ₹ / Amt ₹) | Balance (Qty / Rate ₹ / Amt ₹) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mar 1 | — | — | 500 / 20.000 / 10,000.00 |
| Mar 3 | 300 / 22.00 / 6,600 | — | 800 / 20.750 / 16,600.00 |
| Mar 7 | — | 400 / 20.750 / 8,300.00 | 400 / 20.750 / 8,300.00 |
| Mar 12 | 400 / 24.00 / 9,600 | — | 800 / 22.375 / 17,900.00 |
| Mar 18 | — | 500 / 22.375 / 11,187.50 | 300 / 22.375 / 6,712.50 |
| Mar 25 | 50 / 22.375 / 1,118.75 (return) | — | 350 / 22.375 / 7,831.25 |

**Working of rates:**
- Mar 3: (10,000 + 6,600) ÷ (500 + 300) = 16,600 ÷ 800 = **₹20.750**
- Mar 12: (8,300 + 9,600) ÷ (400 + 400) = 17,900 ÷ 800 = **₹22.375**

**Answer:** Closing stock as on 31st March 2026 = **350 units, value ₹7,831.25** (rate ₹22.375 per unit).

*(Full-marks tip: Recompute the weighted-average rate only on **receipts**, never on issues. The tested twist is the **return from job** — take it back at the **issue rate (₹22.375)**, not at a fresh average and not at the original ₹20; treating a return as a fresh receipt at ₹22.375 keeps the balance rate unchanged, which is correct here.)*

### Q9. Ch: Material Cost — Stock Control Levels including Danger Level & Average Stock (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** From the following data of a material, compute the **Re-order Level, Minimum Level, Maximum Level, Average Stock Level and Danger Level.**

| Particulars | Data |
|---|---|
| Re-order Quantity (units) | 4,000 |
| Normal usage (units/day) | 300 |
| Minimum usage (units/day) | 200 |
| Maximum usage (units/day) | 400 |
| Normal re-order (lead) period | 12.5 days (range 10–15 days) |
| Emergency (danger) lead time | 3 days |

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Re-order Level** = Maximum usage × Maximum re-order period = 400 × 15 = **6,000 units**.

**WN-2 — Minimum Level** = ROL − (Normal usage × Normal re-order period) = 6,000 − (300 × 12.5) = 6,000 − 3,750 = **2,250 units**.

**WN-3 — Maximum Level** = ROL + Re-order Quantity − (Minimum usage × Minimum re-order period) = 6,000 + 4,000 − (200 × 10) = 10,000 − 2,000 = **8,000 units**.

**WN-4 — Average Stock Level** = Minimum Level + ½ × Re-order Quantity = 2,250 + ½ × 4,000 = 2,250 + 2,000 = **4,250 units**.
*(Cross-check by the other formula: (Maximum + Minimum) ÷ 2 = (8,000 + 2,250) ÷ 2 = 5,125 units — either formula is acceptable if the one used is stated.)*

**WN-5 — Danger Level** = Normal (average) usage × Emergency (maximum) re-order period for urgent purchases = 300 × 3 = **900 units**.

**Statement of Stock Control Levels**

| Level | Formula | Units |
|---|---|---|
| Re-order Level | Max usage × Max period | 6,000 |
| Minimum Level | ROL − (Normal usage × Normal period) | 2,250 |
| Maximum Level | ROL + ROQ − (Min usage × Min period) | 8,000 |
| Average Stock Level | Min level + ½ ROQ | 4,250 |
| Danger Level | Normal usage × Emergency lead time | 900 |

**Answer:** ROL 6,000; Minimum 2,250; Maximum 8,000; Average Stock 4,250 units; **Danger Level 900 units** (below which only emergency purchases are made).

*(Full-marks tip: The fresh element is the **Danger Level** — use the *emergency* lead time (3 days) not the normal one, and average usage. State the average-stock formula you adopt, since two acceptable formulae exist and marks are given for consistency, not for a single "right" number.)*

### Q10. Ch: Employee (Labour) Cost — Labour Turnover Rates: Separation, Replacement, New Recruitment & Flux (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** The following data of a factory relate to the year ended 31st March 2026. Compute the Labour Turnover Rate under the **Separation method, Replacement method, New Recruitment method and Flux method.**

| Particulars | Number of workers |
|---|---|
| On roll at the beginning of the year | 4,800 |
| On roll at the end of the year | 5,200 |
| Workers who left (resignation + discharge) during the year | 200 |
| Workers engaged as replacements of those who left | 150 |
| Workers recruited afresh for a newly-opened line (expansion) | 450 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Average number of workers** = (4,800 + 5,200) ÷ 2 = **5,000 workers.**

**WN-2 — Accessions** = Replacements + New recruits for expansion = 150 + 450 = **600.**
(Check: Opening 4,800 + Accessions 600 − Separations 200 = 5,200 = Closing ✔)

**Statement Showing Labour Turnover Rates**

| Method | Formula | Computation | Rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Separation Rate | (Separations ÷ Avg. workers) × 100 | (200 ÷ 5,000) × 100 | **4.00%** |
| Replacement Rate | (Replacements ÷ Avg. workers) × 100 | (150 ÷ 5,000) × 100 | **3.00%** |
| New Recruitment Rate | (New recruits ÷ Avg. workers) × 100 | (450 ÷ 5,000) × 100 | **9.00%** |
| Flux Rate | (Separations + Accessions) ÷ Avg. × 100 | (200 + 600) ÷ 5,000 × 100 | **16.00%** |

**Answer:** Separation 4%, Replacement 3%, New Recruitment 9%, **Flux 16%**.

*(Full-marks tip: The discriminating point is separating **replacements (150)** from **fresh recruits for expansion (450)** — only replacements enter the Replacement rate, but *both* enter Accessions for the Flux rate. Show the reconciliation Opening + Accessions − Separations = Closing to prove your accession figure.)*

### Q11. Ch: Employee (Labour) Cost — Group / Gang Bonus (Priestman's Production Bonus) (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** A gang of 5 workers is engaged on a job. The **standard output** for the gang is **500 units per week**; the **actual output** in a particular week was **600 units**. Under the **Priestman's Production Bonus** scheme a bonus, calculated as the percentage by which actual output exceeds standard output, is paid on each worker's time wages. Each worker worked 40 hours in the week. Compute the total earnings of each worker and of the gang.

| Worker | Hourly rate (₹) |
|---|---|
| A | 50 |
| B | 45 |
| C | 40 |
| D | 40 |
| E | 35 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Bonus percentage (Priestman's):**
= (Actual output − Standard output) ÷ Standard output × 100
= (600 − 500) ÷ 500 × 100 = **20%** of time wages.

**WN-2 — Time wages of each worker** = Hourly rate × 40 hours; **Bonus** = 20% of time wages.

**Statement Showing Earnings of the Gang**

| Worker | Rate (₹) | Time wages = Rate × 40 (₹) | Bonus @ 20% (₹) | Total earnings (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 50 | 2,000 | 400 | 2,400 |
| B | 45 | 1,800 | 360 | 2,160 |
| C | 40 | 1,600 | 320 | 1,920 |
| D | 40 | 1,600 | 320 | 1,920 |
| E | 35 | 1,400 | 280 | 1,680 |
| **Total** | — | **8,400** | **1,680** | **10,080** |

**Answer:** The gang's total earnings for the week are **₹10,080** (time wages ₹8,400 + bonus ₹1,680); individual earnings A ₹2,400, B ₹2,160, C ₹1,920, D ₹1,920, E ₹1,680.

### Q12. Ch: Employee (Labour) Cost — Overtime Premium: Treatment & Recovery Choices (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Explain how the **overtime premium** should be treated in cost accounts depending on the reason for the overtime. Illustrate with a worker paid ₹80 per hour who worked 4 hours of overtime, overtime being paid at double the normal rate.

**Answer:**
**Overtime** is the extra time worked beyond normal working hours; the wages for such hours comprise the **normal rate** plus the **overtime premium** (the extra amount over the normal rate). The **normal-rate portion** of overtime wages is always charged to the job/cost as ordinary labour; the **treatment of the premium** depends on *why* the overtime arose:

| Cause of overtime | Treatment of the overtime **premium** |
|---|---|
| At a **customer's specific request** to expedite delivery | Charged **directly to that job / work order** |
| **General pressure of work** / to meet overall targets | Treated as **factory (production) overhead** and absorbed by all jobs |
| **Abnormal conditions** — management's fault, e.g. faulty planning, machine breakdown, defective material | Charged to the **Costing Profit & Loss Account** (not to product cost) |
| **Seasonal / rush-season** work | Treated as **overhead** for the season |
| Due to **abnormal reasons beyond control** (power cut, flood) | Charged to **Costing Profit & Loss Account** |

**Illustration:** Normal rate ₹80/hour; overtime rate = 2 × ₹80 = ₹160/hour.
- Overtime wages for 4 hours = 4 × ₹160 = **₹640**, of which
- Normal-rate portion = 4 × ₹80 = **₹320** (always to job), and
- Overtime **premium** = 4 × (₹160 − ₹80) = 4 × ₹80 = **₹320** (₹80 per hour extra).

If the 4 hours were worked on a customer's urgent order, the whole ₹640 is charged to that job. If due to general work pressure, ₹320 (normal) goes to the job and the ₹320 premium goes to factory overhead. If due to management's poor scheduling, the ₹320 premium is written off to the Costing Profit & Loss Account.

**Conclusion:** Only the **premium** needs special treatment, and it is routed by cause — to the job (customer request), to overhead (general pressure), or to Costing P&L (abnormal / management's fault).

*(Full-marks tip: Separate the **normal-rate wages** (always a product cost) from the **premium** (₹320 here). The marks lie in matching each *cause* to its *destination* — customer-driven → the job; management-driven abnormal → Costing P&L.)*

### Q13. Ch: Employee (Labour) Cost — Preventive vs Replacement Costs of Labour Turnover (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between the **Preventive Costs** and **Replacement Costs** of labour turnover. State two causes of labour turnover that are **avoidable**.

**Answer:**
The **cost of labour turnover** is analysed into two groups:

**Preventive Costs** are the costs incurred to **keep workers satisfied and discourage them from leaving** — i.e., to *prevent* turnover. They include the cost of personnel administration for maintaining good relations, medical and welfare services, subsidised canteens and housing, pension / gratuity and other retirement benefits, and safety measures. Money spent here is expected to **reduce future turnover**.

**Replacement Costs** are the costs that arise **because workers have already left and must be replaced** — i.e., the *consequences* of turnover. They include the cost of recruitment, selection and training of new workers, loss of output during the interval before replacement and during the learner's training period, higher spoilage and defective work by inexperienced hands, extra machine breakdowns and tool damage, and the higher scrap and accident rates of new workers.

| Basis | Preventive Costs | Replacement Costs |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | To **avoid** workers leaving | Arise **after** workers have left |
| Nature | Incurred willingly, in advance | Forced, remedial |
| Examples | Welfare, medical, pension, good HR | Recruitment, training, lost output, spoilage |
| Effect of higher spend | **Lowers** future turnover | Reflects the **damage** already done |

**Two avoidable causes of labour turnover:** (i) **low wages and poor working conditions / lack of incentives**, and (ii) **unfair treatment or bad relations with supervisors** (dissatisfaction with management). These are within the employer's control, unlike unavoidable causes such as retirement, death, marriage or illness.

**Conclusion:** Preventive costs are a proactive investment to retain workers, whereas replacement costs are the reactive burden of turnover already occurred; well-directed preventive spending usually reduces total turnover cost.

*(Full-marks tip: The clean split — preventive = *before/avoid*, replacement = *after/remedy* — plus at least two examples in each bucket earns the theory marks. Distinguishing **avoidable** from **unavoidable** causes is the extra-mark point.)*

### Q14. Ch: Employee (Labour) Cost — Idle Time: Normal vs Abnormal & Treatment (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** What is meant by **Idle Time**? Distinguish between **normal** and **abnormal** idle time and state the treatment of each in cost accounts.

**Answer:**
**Idle Time** is that portion of the time for which the workers are **paid but no direct production is obtained** — the difference between the time for which a worker is paid and the time he actually spends on productive work. It arises from causes such as waiting for materials, tools, instructions or power, machine set-up, breakdowns, or the movement of a worker between jobs.

Idle time is classified into two types:

| Basis | Normal Idle Time | Abnormal Idle Time |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | **Unavoidable**, inherent in the work situation | **Avoidable**, arising from abnormal causes |
| Examples | Time between jobs, tea/personal breaks, tool-setting, normal machine maintenance | Idleness due to strikes, lock-outs, power failure, machine breakdown, shortage of materials |
| Controllability | Cannot be eliminated | Can and should be eliminated |

**Treatment in cost accounts:**
- **Normal idle time** cost is a **legitimate part of the cost of production**. It is treated either by **inflating the effective labour hour rate** (dividing wages by *productive* hours) so that the idle-time wages are absorbed by good production, or by charging it to **factory overhead**.
- **Abnormal idle time** cost is **not charged to production**; it is transferred to the **Costing Profit & Loss Account**, so that product cost is not distorted by avoidable inefficiency and management's attention is drawn to the loss.

**Conclusion:** Normal idle time is absorbed into product cost (through the labour hour rate or overhead), while abnormal idle time is written off to the Costing Profit & Loss Account as a period loss.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner wants the exact routing — normal → product cost / overhead (or a loaded labour rate), abnormal → Costing P&L. Give a correct example of each; strike/breakdown = abnormal, tool-setting/between-jobs = normal.)*

### Q23. Ch: Overheads – Absorption Costing — Causes and Disposal of Under/Over-Absorption (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the principal causes of under- or over-absorption of overheads and explain the methods available for its accounting treatment.

**Answer:**
Under- or over-absorption of overhead arises whenever the overhead *absorbed* into production (using a predetermined rate) differs from the overhead *actually incurred*. When absorbed exceeds actual it is over-absorption; when actual exceeds absorbed it is under-absorption.

**Causes.** (i) Errors in estimating overhead expenditure or the base (machine/labour hours) while fixing the predetermined rate; (ii) actual output, hours or activity level differing from the budgeted level on which the rate was set; (iii) unforeseen changes in production methods or capacity utilisation, e.g. idle capacity or unexpected seasonal fluctuation; (iv) major changes in the price of indirect materials, power or labour after the rate was fixed; and (v) abnormal factors such as a strike, machine breakdown or a fire.

**Methods of treatment.** There are three recognised methods:

| Method | When appropriate | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **(1) Write-off to Costing P&L A/c** | Where the amount is small, or is due to abnormal reasons (idle capacity, defective planning) | Charged entirely to the period; inventory not disturbed |
| **(2) Use of a Supplementary Rate** | Where the amount is large and due to normal causes | Spread over Cost of Sales, Finished Goods and WIP, restating them to actual cost |
| **(3) Carry-forward to next period** | Where the business is seasonal and the imbalance is expected to reverse | Held in a suspense/overhead reserve account and carried to the next period |

**Conclusion:** The correct method depends on the size of the balance and whether the cause is normal or abnormal — small/abnormal amounts are written off, while large/normal amounts are apportioned by a supplementary rate.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner looks for the normal-vs-abnormal distinction driving the choice of method. Naming all three disposal methods with a one-line "when to use" earns full marks; merely defining under-absorption does not.)*

### Q24. Ch: Overheads – Absorption Costing — Allocation vs Apportionment vs Absorption (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between allocation, apportionment and absorption of overheads.

**Answer:**
These are three successive stages in dealing with overheads. **Allocation** is the charging of a whole item of overhead directly to a single cost centre to which it wholly relates (e.g. the salary of a departmental foreman charged entirely to that department). **Apportionment** is the *distribution* of an overhead that is common to several cost centres over those centres on an equitable basis (e.g. factory rent apportioned over departments in the ratio of floor area). **Absorption** is the *final* charging of the overhead of a cost centre to the individual cost units (products/jobs) passing through it, using an overhead rate such as a machine-hour or labour-hour rate.

| Basis | Allocation | Apportionment | Absorption |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nature of cost | Directly identifiable with one cost centre | Common to two or more cost centres | Overhead of a cost centre recovered by cost units |
| Basis used | No basis needed — charged in full | An equitable base (area, value, hours) | An overhead rate (per hour / per unit / % of cost) |
| Charged to | A single cost centre | Several cost centres | Cost units (products/jobs) |
| Stage | First | Second | Final |

**Conclusion:** Allocation and apportionment build up the total overhead of each cost centre, whereas absorption transfers that accumulated overhead onto the products — allocation and apportionment relate cost to *centres*, absorption relates cost to *units*.

### Q27. Ch: Activity-Based Costing — Concept and Cost Hierarchy (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the concept of Activity-Based Costing (ABC) and describe the four levels of the cost hierarchy on which it is based, giving an example of a suitable cost driver for each.

**Answer:**
**Concept.** Activity-Based Costing is a costing system that first traces overheads to the *activities* that cause them (grouping costs into "activity cost pools"), and then charges those activity costs to products, jobs or customers in proportion to their *consumption* of each activity, measured by an appropriate **cost driver**. Its central premise is that "activities consume resources, and products consume activities." Unlike traditional absorption — which recovers all overhead on a single volume base such as labour or machine hours — ABC uses multiple cause-and-effect drivers, so it gives more accurate costs where products differ in volume and complexity.

**Cost hierarchy.** ABC classifies activities and their costs into four levels:

| Level | Meaning | Example cost driver |
|---|---|---|
| **Unit-level** | Performed each time a unit is produced; vary with volume | Machine hours; direct labour hours |
| **Batch-level** | Performed each time a batch is handled, regardless of batch size | No. of set-ups; no. of purchase orders; no. of material movements |
| **Product- (line-) sustaining** | Performed to sustain a product line irrespective of units or batches | No. of product designs; no. of engineering change notes |
| **Facility- (or business-) sustaining** | Support the plant/business as a whole; cannot be traced to a product | Not driver-based — often apportioned or charged to period (e.g. factory rent, plant management salary) |

**Conclusion:** ABC improves cost accuracy by recognising that many overheads are driven by batch- and product-level activities, not by production volume; facility-level costs, having no true driver, are usually treated as period costs or apportioned separately.

*(Full-marks tip: the mark-earning content is the four-level hierarchy WITH a correct driver for each — unit (hours), batch (set-ups/orders), product (design changes), facility (rent, no driver). State the "activities consume resources, products consume activities" premise up front.)*

### Q29. Ch: Activity-Based Costing — Customer Profitability Analysis: Concept and Uses (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** What is meant by Customer Profitability Analysis under an ABC framework? State its uses to management.

**Answer:**
**Meaning.** Customer Profitability Analysis (CPA) is the application of Activity-Based Costing to *customers* rather than to products. It measures the total revenue earned from an individual customer (or customer group) less all the costs attributable to serving that customer — including not only the cost of goods sold but also customer-driven costs such as order processing, deliveries, sales visits, after-sales service, rush orders, returns and the discounts/credit terms granted. The aim is to determine the *net profit each customer actually contributes*, since two customers with the same sales value can be very differently profitable.

**Uses to management.** (i) It identifies high-cost, low-margin ("loss-making") customers who erode profit despite large sales; (ii) it guides pricing, discount and minimum-order-size policy for each customer; (iii) it helps re-shape customer behaviour — e.g. encouraging larger, less frequent orders or charging for rush deliveries; (iv) it directs selling effort and relationship investment toward the most profitable accounts; and (v) it informs decisions on whether to retain, re-negotiate with, or drop a customer.

**Conclusion:** By tracing service costs to their cost drivers, CPA reveals the "whale curve" reality that a minority of customers often generate the bulk of profit while some large-revenue customers may be unprofitable — enabling better customer and pricing strategy.

*(Full-marks tip: stress that CPA charges *customer-driven* costs (deliveries, visits, rush orders, discounts), not just COGS, and list at least three concrete management uses. The line "equal sales need not mean equal profit" captures the examiner's key idea.)*

### Q32. Ch: Cost Accounting Systems & Reconciliation — Causes of Difference between Cost & Financial Profit (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** When cost and financial accounts are maintained separately (non-integrated system), their profits seldom agree. Explain the principal reasons that cause this difference and necessitate reconciliation.

**Answer:**
Under a non-integrated system, cost accounts and financial accounts are kept independently, and their profit figures differ because certain items are recorded in one set of books but not the other, or are valued differently. The reasons fall into three groups:

**(1) Items shown only in financial accounts** (of a purely financial or appropriation nature, excluded from cost accounts) — e.g. interest and dividend received, profit/loss on sale of fixed assets, interest on loans/debentures paid, income tax, donations, preliminary expenses and goodwill written off, and dividends paid. Their presence in the financial books alone makes the two profits differ.

**(2) Items shown only in cost accounts** (notional charges) — e.g. notional rent on owned premises, notional interest on capital, and notional salary of the proprietor. These reduce cost profit but never appear in the financial books.

**(3) Differences in the basis of valuation or charge:**

| Item | Possible difference |
|---|---|
| Overheads | Absorbed on estimate in cost books vs actual in financial books (under/over-absorption) |
| Depreciation | Different method/rate in the two sets of books |
| Stock valuation | Cost books may use standard/marginal cost; financial books use cost-or-NRV (AS-2) |

**Conclusion:** These purely-financial items, purely-cost (notional) items, and differing valuation bases together explain the gap; a Reconciliation Statement is prepared to tie the two profit figures together and confirm the accuracy of both sets of books.

*(Full-marks tip: organise the answer under the three heads — financial-only items, cost-only (notional) items, and valuation differences. Listing examples under each, especially notional charges, is what distinguishes a full-mark answer from a vague one.)*

### Q34. Ch: Cost Accounting Systems & Reconciliation — Integrated vs Non-Integrated Accounting (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between an integrated (integral) accounting system and a non-integrated accounting system, and state the advantages of the integrated system.

**Answer:**
**Integrated accounting** is a single, self-contained set of books that records both cost and financial transactions together, so that one ledger serves the needs of both cost and financial accounting; no separate cost and financial profit arises. **Non-integrated accounting** keeps the cost ledger separate from the financial ledger; the cost ledger is made self-balancing by a **Cost Ledger Control Account (General Ledger Adjustment Account)** which stands in place of the financial items, and the two independently-computed profits must later be reconciled.

| Basis | Integrated System | Non-Integrated System |
|---|---|---|
| Sets of books | One combined set | Two separate sets (cost + financial) |
| Cost Ledger Control A/c | Not required; real accounts appear directly | Required, to make the cost ledger self-balancing |
| Profit figures | Only one profit; no reconciliation | Two profits; reconciliation needed |
| Duplication of effort | Avoided | Same data recorded twice |

**Advantages of the integrated system:** (i) it avoids the **duplication** of recording cost and financial data separately, saving time and clerical cost; (ii) **no reconciliation** of two profit figures is needed, since a single profit emerges; (iii) it produces **one coherent set of figures**, reducing the chance of error and disagreement; (iv) cost and financial information is available **promptly and together**, aiding quicker management decisions; and (v) it fosters "cost consciousness" throughout the accounting function.

**Conclusion:** The integrated system merges cost and financial recording into one economical, reconciliation-free framework, whereas the non-integrated system keeps them apart and relies on a control account and a periodic reconciliation.

*(Full-marks tip: the two decisive distinctions are (i) one set of books vs two, and (ii) no reconciliation vs reconciliation required (with a Cost Ledger Control A/c). List at least three advantages — "avoids duplication" and "no reconciliation" are the ones the examiner expects first.)*

### Q36. Ch: Cost Sheet — Treatment of waste, scrap, spoilage and defectives (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between **waste, scrap, spoilage and defectives** and state the correct cost-accounting treatment of each in the cost sheet.

**Answer:** These four terms represent different forms of loss/imperfection arising in the manufacturing process, and each is treated distinctly in cost accounting.

**Waste** is that portion of the raw material which is lost in processing and has **no recoverable/realisable value** (e.g. evaporation, smoke, gases, dust). Normal waste is absorbed by good units by inflating the cost per good unit; abnormal waste is charged to the Costing Profit & Loss Account.

**Scrap** is the **residue** of material arising from manufacture which has a **low but definite realisable value** (e.g. metal turnings, off-cuts). Its realisable value is credited either to the specific job/process (if identifiable), to the material cost, or to factory overhead (if not identifiable); abnormal scrap value is transferred to the Costing P&L Account.

**Spoilage** is production that is **so damaged in processing that it cannot be rectified** and is disposed of without further processing. Normal spoilage cost (net of salvage) is borne by good production; abnormal spoilage (net of salvage) is written off to the Costing P&L Account.

**Defectives** are units that fall short of standard but **can be rectified and made saleable** by incurring additional (rectification) cost. Normal rectification cost is charged to the good output or the department; abnormal rectification cost is transferred to the Costing P&L Account.

| Basis | Waste | Scrap | Spoilage | Defectives |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Realisable value | Nil | Small | Some salvage | Rectifiable output |
| Rectifiable? | No | No | No | Yes |
| Normal loss treatment | Absorbed by good units | Credited to cost | Borne by good output | Charged to output/dept |
| Abnormal loss treatment | Costing P&L | Costing P&L | Costing P&L | Costing P&L |

**Conclusion:** The unifying rule is that **normal** losses form part of the cost of good production while **abnormal** losses are excluded from cost and charged to the Costing Profit & Loss Account, net of any salvage.

*(Full-marks tip: examiners look for the "normal vs abnormal" treatment stated for **each** term; simply defining the four words without the treatment loses half the marks.)*

### Q39. Ch: Cost Sheet — Items excluded from cost accounts (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State, with reasons, which items are **excluded** while preparing a cost sheet / cost accounts, giving at least six examples.

**Answer:** A cost sheet records only those expenses that are **incurred in and are attributable to the manufacture and sale of the product**. Items that are **purely financial** in nature, are **appropriations of profit**, or are of a **capital** character are **excluded** because they do not represent the cost of producing or selling goods.

The following items are excluded:

1. **Purely financial charges** — interest on debentures/loans, loss on sale of investments or fixed assets, discount on issue of shares/debentures, penalties and fines. These arise from financing/investment decisions, not from production.
2. **Purely financial incomes** — interest/dividend received, rent received, profit on sale of assets, transfer fees. These are not earned from the manufacturing operation.
3. **Appropriations of profit** — income tax, dividend paid, transfer to general reserve or sinking fund, donations, charity. These are distributions of profit, not costs.
4. **Capital expenditure and capital losses** — cost of assets purchased, goodwill and preliminary expenses written off. These are of a capital nature.
5. **Abnormal items** — abnormal wastage, abnormal idle time, cost of abnormal loss. These are excluded from cost and charged to the Costing P&L Account.
6. **Notional/imputed entries** — although notional rent or notional interest may be *added* in cost accounts, they are excluded from financial accounts, and pure book-entry write-offs are excluded from cost.

**Conclusion:** Only items of a **revenue nature that are directly related to production and sale** enter the cost sheet; financial, capital and profit-appropriation items are excluded — and this very exclusion is the cause of the difference reconciled in the Cost–Financial reconciliation statement.

*(Full-marks tip: give the *reason* (financial / appropriation / capital / abnormal) alongside each example — a bare list of items without the classifying reason is marked down.)*

### Q41. Ch: Unit & Batch Costing — Economic Batch Quantity: set-up vs holding trade-off (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** A company manufactures a component with an **annual demand of 60,000 units**. You are required to:
(i) compute the **Economic Batch Quantity (EBQ)**;
(ii) find the number of production runs and the total set-up + holding cost at the EBQ; and
(iii) show the **annual saving** achieved by switching from the present batch of 6,000 units to the EBQ.

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Economic Batch Quantity.**
EBQ = √(2 × D × S ÷ C), where D = annual demand = 60,000 units, S = set-up cost per run = ₹150, C = holding cost per unit p.a. = ₹2.
EBQ = √(2 × 60,000 × 150 ÷ 2) = √(90,00,000... ) = √90,00,000 = **3,000 units**.

**WN-2 — Costs at EBQ (3,000 units).**
- Number of runs = 60,000 ÷ 3,000 = **20 runs**; Set-up cost = 20 × ₹150 = ₹3,000.

**WN-3 — Costs at present batch (6,000 units).**
- Number of runs = 60,000 ÷ 6,000 = 10 runs; Set-up cost = 10 × ₹150 = ₹1,500.

- Total cost = ₹1,500 + ₹6,000 = **₹7,500**.

**Statement of Comparison**

| Basis | Present batch (6,000) | Optimum batch / EBQ (3,000) |
|---|---:|---:|
| No. of production runs | 10 | 20 |
| Set-up cost (₹) | 1,500 | 3,000 |
| Holding cost (₹) | 6,000 | 3,000 |
| **Total cost (₹)** | **7,500** | **6,000** |

**Answer:** EBQ = **3,000 units** (20 runs); minimum total cost = **₹6,000 p.a.**; **annual saving = ₹7,500 − ₹6,000 = ₹1,500** by moving to the EBQ.

### Q42. Ch: Unit & Batch Costing — Unit costing vs Batch costing (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between **Unit Costing** and **Batch Costing**, and state one industry where each is applied.

**Answer:** Both are forms of **specific-order / output costing**, but they differ in the unit of cost ascertainment.

**Unit (output/single) costing** is the method used where production is **continuous, of a single product or a few grades of an identical product**, and cost is ascertained **per unit of output** (per tonne, per litre, per 1,000 bricks). A cost sheet is prepared for a period and total cost is divided by output to arrive at cost per unit.

**Batch costing** is a form of **job costing** used where **articles are produced in definite lots or batches**, each batch being treated as a **cost unit**. The cost of the batch is accumulated and divided by the number of good units in the batch to obtain cost per unit; it introduces the concept of the **Economic Batch Quantity** to balance set-up and holding costs.

| Basis | Unit Costing | Batch Costing |
|---|---|---|
| Nature of production | Continuous, identical output | Production in distinct batches/lots |
| Cost unit | A single natural unit of output | A batch (then per unit within batch) |
| Set-up cost relevance | Generally not separately significant | Central — drives EBQ |
| Cost accumulation | Per period via cost sheet | Per batch |
| Example industry | Cement, sugar, steel, breweries | Pharmaceuticals, biscuits, bakery, garments, spare parts |

**Conclusion:** Unit costing suits continuous single-product manufacture, whereas batch costing suits repetitive production carried out in identifiable lots where set-up economics matter.

*(Full-marks tip: the discriminator worth marks is that a **batch** is itself the cost unit and that set-up cost drives the EBQ; naming a correct example industry for each is expected.)*

### Q46. Ch: Job & Contract Costing — Cost-plus vs fixed-price contract; escalation clause (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between a **cost-plus contract** and a **fixed-price contract**, and explain the purpose of an **escalation clause**.

**Answer:** A **cost-plus contract** is one in which the **contract price is not fixed at the outset**; instead the contractor is reimbursed the **allowable/actual cost of the work plus an agreed percentage (or fixed amount) of profit** on that cost. It is used where costs cannot be estimated with reasonable accuracy in advance — for example defence supplies, new/experimental work, or long-duration projects.

A **fixed-price contract** is one in which the **contract price is agreed and fixed at the time of entering into the contract**, irrespective of the actual cost later incurred; the contractor bears the risk of cost overruns and enjoys the benefit of cost savings.

| Basis | Cost-plus contract | Fixed-price contract |
|---|---|---|
| Price | Actual cost + agreed profit | Fixed in advance |
| Cost risk | Borne by the contractee | Borne by the contractor |
| Incentive to control cost | Weak (profit follows cost) | Strong |
| Suitability | Costs unpredictable / novel work | Costs reasonably estimable |
| Need for cost audit | High (costs are reimbursed) | Low |

**Escalation clause:** This is a clause inserted (usually in a **fixed-price** contract) that **protects the contractor against a rise in prices of materials, labour rates or utilities** beyond the levels assumed when the price was fixed. It provides that the contract price will be **suitably increased** to the extent of the agreed rise in such input prices, generally computed on the **standard/agreed quantities** (so the contractor still bears the cost of its own inefficiency in usage). A **de-escalation clause** provides the converse benefit to the contractee if prices fall.

**Conclusion:** A cost-plus contract shifts cost risk to the contractee, a fixed-price contract shifts it to the contractor, and an escalation clause is the device that shares **price-level** risk on a fixed-price contract.

*(Full-marks tip: state clearly *who bears the cost risk* in each type, and note that escalation is computed on **standard quantities at revised rates** — the point examiners specifically look for.)*

### Q50. Ch: Job & Contract Costing — Key terms in contract costing (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain briefly the following terms as used in contract costing: (i) **Work certified**, (ii) **Work uncertified**, (iii) **Retention money**, and (iv) **Notional profit**.

**Answer:**

**(i) Work certified** is the value of the work done on a contract **up to the reporting date that has been inspected and approved (certified) by the contractee's architect/engineer** at the contract (selling) price. It is credited to the Contract Account and forms the basis on which the contractee makes running payments.

**(ii) Work uncertified** is the value (at **cost**) of the work that has been **carried out but not yet certified** by the architect — either because it is not sufficiently advanced or the certification was after the reporting date. It is carried down as part of work-in-progress at cost, so as not to anticipate profit on it.

**(iii) Retention money** is the portion of the certified value that the contractee is **entitled to withhold** (i.e. not pay immediately), being the difference between the value of work certified and the cash actually paid. It safeguards the contractee against defective work and is released after satisfactory completion / the defects-liability period. *Retention money = Work certified − Cash received.*

**(iv) Notional profit** is the **profit shown by the Contract Account before deciding how much profit to transfer to the Profit & Loss Account**. It is computed as: (Value of work certified + Cost of work uncertified) − Cost of work to date. Only a prudent portion of it is credited to the P&L Account, the rest being held as reserve for future contingencies.

**Conclusion:** Together these terms embody the **prudence principle** in contract costing — profit is recognised only on certified work, uncertified work is held at cost, retention money guards quality, and only a conservative share of notional profit is taken to the P&L Account.

*(Full-marks tip: state that work certified is at **contract price** while work uncertified is at **cost**, and give the two one-line formulae for retention money and notional profit — these precise points carry the marks.)*

### Q53. Ch: Process & Operation Costing — Accounting Treatment of Losses & Gains (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** In process costing, distinguish between **normal loss, abnormal loss and abnormal gain**, and state the accounting treatment of each, including the effect of scrap value on the amount transferred to the Costing Profit & Loss Account.

**Answer:**
In every manufacturing process some loss of input is inevitable owing to evaporation, chemical change, spoilage or handling. Cost accounting classifies such loss according to whether it is expected or unexpected.

**Normal loss** is the loss that is **inherent in the process and unavoidable** under efficient operating conditions; it is estimated in advance as a percentage of input. As it is a **cost of producing good output, its cost is borne by (absorbed into) the good units** and is *not* separately charged to the Costing P&L Account. The Process Account is credited with the normal loss units at their **scrap/realisable value only** (via the Normal Loss Account), so that the cost of good output = (total process cost − scrap value of normal loss) ÷ (input units − normal loss units).

**Abnormal loss** is the loss **in excess of the normal (expected) loss**; it arises from avoidable causes such as machine breakdown, sub-standard material or carelessness. It is **valued at the same cost per unit as good output** and is credited out of the Process Account to the **Abnormal Loss Account**. Any scrap realised on abnormal-loss units is credited to the Abnormal Loss Account, and the **net loss is transferred to the Costing P&L Account** as an abnormal item.

**Abnormal gain** arises when **actual output exceeds the expected (normal) output**, i.e. actual loss is *less* than normal loss. It is also **valued at the normal cost per unit** and is **debited to the Process Account and credited to the Abnormal Gain Account**. Because fewer units were lost, the **scrap value that would have been earned on the reduction in loss is debited to the Abnormal Gain Account (transferred to Normal Loss A/c)**, and only the **net gain is credited to the Costing P&L Account.**

| Basis | Normal Loss | Abnormal Loss | Abnormal Gain |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nature | Expected, unavoidable | Excess, avoidable | Better-than-expected output |
| Valuation | Scrap value only | Normal cost per good unit | Normal cost per good unit |
| Cost per good unit | Increases (loss absorbed) | Unaffected | Unaffected |
| Costing P&L | Not charged | Net loss debited | Net gain credited |
| Scrap adjustment | Credited to Process A/c | Credited to Abnormal Loss A/c | Scrap foregone debited to Abnormal Gain A/c |

**Conclusion:** Normal loss is absorbed by good output; abnormal loss and abnormal gain are isolated at normal cost and taken **net of their scrap adjustment** to the Costing P&L Account, so that reported cost of good production is not distorted by avoidable inefficiency or unusual efficiency.

*(Full-marks tip: State explicitly that both abnormal loss and gain are valued at the **normal cost per unit** and that the scrap adjustment is what converts the gross figure into the net figure taken to P&L. Merely defining the three terms without the scrap treatment loses a mark.)*

**Question:** Why do some organisations charge **inter-process profits** when output is transferred from one process to the next? State the **advantages and disadvantages** of the system and explain why a **provision for unrealised profit** must be created at the year-end.

**Answer:**
**Meaning.** When the output of one process is transferred to the next process (or to the finished-goods warehouse) **not at cost but at a price that includes a margin of profit** (e.g. current market price, or cost plus a percentage), the difference between transfer price and cost is called **inter-process profit**. Each process is thus treated as a quasi-independent profit centre.

**Purpose / reasons for charging inter-process profit:**
1. To ascertain **whether each process is operating profitably** compared with buying the same intermediate product from the open market — a make-or-buy indicator for every stage.
2. To make each process **cost-conscious and comparable** with outside prices, thereby exposing inefficient processes.
3. To keep process results **independent of the profit or loss of subsequent processes**, aiding managerial control and performance appraisal.

**Advantages:** shows the profitability of each process separately; facilitates make-or-buy decisions at each stage; motivates process managers.

**Disadvantages:** the system is **complicated**, requiring accounts in Total/Cost/Profit columns; closing stock in later processes contains an element of **profit that has not yet been realised** by an external sale; it **inflates the value of inventory** and hence book profit unless corrected; it may cause friction over transfer pricing.

**Provision for unrealised profit.** Since closing stock lying in a later process is valued at transfer price, it includes profit charged by earlier processes that the firm has **not yet earned from an outside customer**. Under the prudence concept (and to state inventory at true cost), a **stock reserve equal to the profit element in the closing stock is created** and deducted from stock in the Balance Sheet; the same amount is charged in the Costing P&L. When the stock is sold in the next period the reserve is written back.

**Conclusion:** Inter-process profits improve managerial evaluation of each stage, but the **unrealised profit locked in closing inventory must be eliminated** through a stock reserve so that reported profit and stock value reflect only realised, arm's-length results.

*(Full-marks tip: Tie the provision to the **prudence/realisation concept** — profit is recognised only on sale to an outside party. Listing advantages/disadvantages without explaining *why* the reserve is needed loses half the marks.)*

### Q59. Ch: Process & Operation Costing — Operation Costing vs Process Costing (Marks: 5) [Application]

**Answer:**
**Meaning of Operation Costing.** Operation Costing is a **refinement of process costing** in which cost is ascertained **for each distinct operation** (a small, homogeneous, repetitive activity) rather than for the process or department as a whole. Where a process is capable of being sub-divided into several operations — such as cutting, drilling and finishing — the cost of each operation is separately accumulated and a **cost per operation-unit** is computed. **How it differs from Process Costing.**

| Basis | Process Costing | Operation Costing |
|---|---|---|
| Unit of cost collection | A whole process / department | A single operation within a process |
| Degree of detail | Broader — cost per process | Finer — cost per operation |
| Purpose | Cost of process output | Cost & efficiency of each operation |
| Control | Process-level control | Pin-points the exact operation causing high cost/loss |
| Suitability | Continuous flow (chemicals, refining) | Repetitive engineering/assembly operations |

**Application to the given situation.** The department's total cost would be split so that the cost of **cutting, drilling and finishing** is each divided by the number of good units passing through that operation, giving a **cost per unit per operation**. ### Q60. Ch: Process & Operation Costing — Equivalent Production & FIFO vs Weighted Average (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the concept of **Equivalent Production (equivalent units)** and state why it is needed. Distinguish between the **FIFO** and **Weighted Average** methods of computing the cost of equivalent units.

**Answer:**
**Concept.** At the end of an accounting period a process usually contains **partly-finished units (work-in-progress)** as well as fully completed units. Since incomplete units have not absorbed the full cost, they cannot be treated as complete units. **Equivalent production converts partly-finished units into an equivalent number of fully-completed units** according to their **degree of completion** for each cost element (materials, labour, overheads). For example, 400 units that are 40% complete as to conversion = 160 equivalent completed units. This is essential to **apportion the total process cost fairly between the finished output and the closing WIP**, and thus to value each correctly.

**FIFO vs Weighted Average.** Both are methods of relating cost to equivalent units when there is **opening WIP**.

| Basis | Weighted Average | FIFO |
|---|---|---|
| Treatment of opening WIP | Opening-WIP cost is **merged** with current cost | Opening-WIP cost is kept **separate** and completed first |
| Equivalent units counted | Full completion of all units (incl. opening) | **Only the work done in the current period** |
| Cost/EU based on | (Opening + current cost) ÷ total EU | **Current cost only** ÷ current-period EU |
| Cost of goods transferred | One blended average rate | Opening batch at (b/f + cost to finish) + new units at current rate |
| Distinguishes efficiency of periods? | No — periods are blended | **Yes** — current cost/EU reflects only this period |

**Conclusion:** Equivalent production allows total cost to be split correctly between complete and incomplete units. The **weighted average** method is simpler because it blends opening and current costs, whereas **FIFO** is more accurate for cost control as its cost per equivalent unit reflects **only the current period's** performance.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner wants the key discriminator — FIFO counts **only current-period work** and uses **only current cost**, whereas weighted average blends both. State the degree-of-completion idea with a small numerical illustration to secure full marks.)*

### Q61. Ch: Joint Products & By-Products — Constant Gross-Margin NRV Method (Marks: 8) [Problem]

| Product | Final sales value (₹) | Further processing cost (₹) |
|---|---:|---:|
| A | 3,00,000 | 40,000 |
| B | 5,00,000 | 60,000 |
| C | 2,00,000 | 30,000 |
| **Total** | **10,00,000** | **1,30,000** |

**Solution:**
Under this method every product is assumed to earn the **same gross-margin percentage** on its final sales value. The joint cost allocated to a product is the balancing figure after deducting its own further-processing cost from its total cost of goods sold.

**WN-1 — Overall gross-margin percentage**
Total cost = Joint cost + Total further processing = 4,00,000 + 1,30,000 = ₹5,30,000.
Overall gross profit = Total sales − Total cost = 10,00,000 − 5,30,000 = ₹4,70,000.
$$\text{Gross-margin \%}=\frac{4,70,000}{10,00,000}\times100=47\%$$

**WN-2 — Apportionment of joint cost**
For each product: Gross profit = Sales × 47%; Cost of goods sold (COGS) = Sales − Gross profit; **Joint cost = COGS − Further processing cost.**

|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|

| C | 2,00,000 | 94,000 | 1,06,000 | 30,000 | 76,000 |
| **Total** | **10,00,000** | **4,70,000** | **5,30,000** | **1,30,000** | **4,00,000** |

Total joint cost allocated = ₹4,00,000, agreeing with the joint cost incurred ✓.

**WN-3 — Product-wise Profit Statement (verification of constant margin)**

| Particulars | A (₹) | B (₹) | C (₹) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Sales | 3,00,000 | 5,00,000 | 2,00,000 |
| Joint cost | 1,19,000 | 2,05,000 | 76,000 |

### Q62. Ch: Joint Products & By-Products — Methods of Apportioning Joint Cost (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the principal **methods of apportioning joint costs** among joint products, stating the suitability and limitation of each.

**Answer:**
Joint costs are incurred **up to the split-off point**, before the individual products become separately identifiable. As they cannot be traced to a single product, they must be **apportioned** on a rational basis. The principal methods are:

**1. Physical Units (Average Unit Cost) Method.** Joint cost is apportioned in the ratio of the **physical quantity** (units, litres, kg) of each product at the split-off point. *Suitable* when products are homogeneous and measured in the same unit; *limitation* — it ignores differences in selling price, so a low-value product may absorb as much cost as a high-value one.

**2. Market Value at Split-off Point Method.** Joint cost is apportioned in the ratio of the **sales value of each product at the split-off point**. *Suitable* where such market values are available; it follows the "**ability-to-bear**" principle. *Limitation* — split-off market prices are often not available where products need further processing.

**3. Net Realisable Value (Reverse Cost) Method.** Where products are processed further, the **NRV = final sales value − further processing cost − selling expenses** is computed for each, and joint cost is apportioned in the ratio of NRVs. *Suitable* and widely used when further processing is required. *Limitation* — assumes further cost and margins are proportionate.

**4. Constant Gross-Margin Percentage NRV Method.** Joint cost is apportioned so that **every product earns the same gross-margin %** on sales. *Suitable* where products are viewed as a single production effort; *limitation* — it unrealistically assumes all products are equally profitable.

**5. Survey / Technical Estimate (Weighted Average) Method.** Weights (points) are assigned to each product on the basis of a technical survey reflecting size, difficulty, market, etc., and joint cost is apportioned on weighted units. *Suitable* where products differ widely; *limitation* — weights are subjective.

| Method | Basis of apportionment | Best suited when |
|---|---|---|
| Physical units | Quantity at split-off | Homogeneous products, same unit |
| Market value at split-off | Sale value at split-off | Split-off prices available |
| Net realisable value | Final sales − further & selling cost | Products need further processing |
| Constant gross margin | Equal GM% on sales | Products treated as one effort |
| Survey / technical | Assigned weights/points | Products differ widely |

**Conclusion:** No single method is correct for all situations; the choice depends on the **availability of split-off market prices and the extent of further processing**, the NRV method being the most commonly used where products are processed further.

*(Full-marks tip: Name at least four methods, give the **basis + one suitability/limitation each**, and put them in a table. Merely listing method names without their basis costs marks.)*

### Q64. Ch: Joint Products & By-Products — Sell or Process Further (Marks: 8) [Application]
**Question:** Anahata Ltd. produces three joint products A, B and C from a common process, the **joint cost being ₹1,80,000**. Each product can either be **sold at the split-off point** or **processed further** and sold at a higher price. Advise, for each product, whether it should be sold at split-off or processed further, and compute the change in total profit if the company adopts your advice.

| Product | Units | Selling price at split-off (₹/unit) | Further processing cost (₹) | Selling price after further processing (₹/unit) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 4,000 | 25 | 40,000 | 40 |
| B | 3,000 | 30 | 30,000 | 45 |
| C | 2,000 | 50 | 50,000 | 70 |

**Solution:**
The **joint cost of ₹1,80,000 is a sunk (irrelevant) cost** for this decision — it has already been incurred and does not change whether a product is sold at split-off or processed further. The decision rests solely on **incremental (differential) analysis**: process further **only if incremental revenue exceeds incremental (further-processing) cost.**

**WN-1 — Incremental revenue from further processing**
= (Selling price after − Selling price at split-off) × Units

| Product | Increase in price (₹) | Units | Incremental revenue (₹) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 40 − 25 = 15 | 4,000 | 60,000 |
| B | 45 − 30 = 15 | 3,000 | 45,000 |
| C | 70 − 50 = 20 | 2,000 | 40,000 |

**WN-2 — Incremental analysis and decision**

| Product | Incremental revenue (₹) | Further cost (₹) | Net benefit / (loss) (₹) | Decision |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| A | 60,000 | 40,000 | +20,000 | **Process further** |
| B | 45,000 | 30,000 | +15,000 | **Process further** |
| C | 40,000 | 50,000 | (10,000) | **Sell at split-off** |

**WN-3 — Increase in total profit by adopting the advice**
Process A and B further (extra profit 20,000 + 15,000 = ₹35,000); sell C at split-off (avoid the ₹10,000 loss that further processing would cause).
**Net increase in profit = ₹35,000** compared with selling everything at split-off, and **₹45,000** better than processing everything further (which would include C's ₹10,000 loss).

**Answer:** **Process A and B further; sell C at the split-off point.** This maximises profit — an incremental gain of **₹35,000** over selling all at split-off. The joint cost of ₹1,80,000 is irrelevant to the decision.

*(Full-marks tip: State explicitly that **joint cost is a sunk/irrelevant cost**, and base the decision only on **incremental revenue vs incremental cost**. ### Q65. Ch: Joint Products & By-Products — By-Product Accounting Methods (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** What is a **by-product**? Explain the different **methods of accounting for the value of by-products** in cost accounts.

**Answer:**
**Meaning.** A **by-product** is a product of **relatively small value** that emerges **incidentally** in the course of manufacturing the main product(s) from a common process — for example, molasses in sugar manufacture, or bran in flour milling. Because its value is minor, elaborate cost apportionment is usually avoided; instead its realisable value is credited to the main product's cost. The methods fall into two broad groups.

**(A) Non-Cost or Sales-Value Methods** (no share of joint cost is charged to the by-product):
1. **Other Income / Miscellaneous Income method** — the net sales value of the by-product is treated as **other income** and credited to the Costing P&L; the main product bears the entire joint cost. Suitable when by-product value is negligible.
2. **Crediting Sales Value less Selling/Distribution cost** — net realisable value of the by-product is **credited to the process/main product cost**, thereby reducing the cost of the main product.
3. **Crediting Net Realisable Value (Reverse Cost method)** — from the by-product's sales value, the **further processing cost, selling expenses and a normal profit margin** are deducted, and the balance is credited to the main product cost.

**(B) Cost Methods** (a share of joint cost IS allocated to the by-product):
4. **Opportunity / Replacement Cost method** — where the by-product is used internally, it is valued at the price that would otherwise be paid to buy it from outside.
5. **Standard Cost method** — the by-product is valued at a pre-determined standard cost, differences being absorbed elsewhere.
6. **Apportionment on suitable basis** — where the by-product is of significant value, joint cost is apportioned between main product and by-product as if both were joint products (e.g. on sales value).

**Conclusion:** The **choice depends on the relative value of the by-product** — where it is small, a non-cost method (crediting its NRV to the main product) is used; where it is significant, a cost method that allocates a share of joint cost is more appropriate.

*(Full-marks tip: Draw the **non-cost vs cost method** distinction clearly and give at least two methods under each. Naming molasses/bran as a concrete example of a by-product earns the definition mark.)*

NRV = By-product sales − Further processing − Selling expenses
= (2,000 × ₹20) − 8,000 − 4,000 = 40,000 − 12,000 = **₹28,000**.

**WN-2 — Net cost of the main product M**
Under the NRV method, the by-product's NRV is **credited to (deducted from) the process cost**:
Net cost of M = 3,00,000 − 28,000 = **₹2,72,000**.
Cost per unit of M = 2,72,000 ÷ 10,000 = **₹27.20 per unit.**

**Statement Showing Profit on Main Product M**

| Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---:|
| Sales (10,000 × ₹40) | 4,00,000 |
| Less: Net cost of M (3,00,000 − 28,000 by-product NRV) | 2,72,000 |
| **Profit on Main Product M** | **1,28,000** |

**OR (Miscellaneous Income method — cross-check):**
If instead the by-product NRV were treated as **other income**, cost of M = ₹3,00,000 → cost/unit ₹30; profit on M = 4,00,000 − 3,00,000 = ₹1,00,000, plus other income ₹28,000 = **₹1,28,000 total** — the overall profit is the same, only its presentation differs.

**Answer:** By-product NRV credited = ₹28,000; **cost per unit of M = ₹27.20**; profit on main product M = **₹1,28,000.**

*(Full-marks tip: By-product NRV must be **net of further processing AND selling expenses**, and is **credited to the main product's cost** (reducing it), not shown as a separate cost. Mentioning the alternative "other-income" method and showing that total profit is unchanged earns the cross-check mark.)*

### Q67. Ch: Joint Products & By-Products — Physical Units vs Market Value Method (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** A joint process yields three products A, B and C from a **joint cost of ₹1,20,000**. Apportion the joint cost under **(a) the Physical Units method** and **(b) the Market Value at split-off method**, and comment on which product is favoured by each.

| Product | Output (units) | Selling price at split-off (₹/unit) |
|---|---:|---:|
| A | 6,000 | 20 |
| B | 4,000 | 30 |
| C | 2,000 | 40 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1 — Physical units and market values**
- Total physical units = 6,000 + 4,000 + 2,000 = 12,000.
- Market value at split-off: A = 6,000 × 20 = ₹1,20,000; B = 4,000 × 30 = ₹1,20,000; C = 2,000 × 40 = ₹80,000. Total = ₹3,20,000.

**(a) Physical Units Method** — joint cost ₹1,20,000 in ratio of units (6,000 : 4,000 : 2,000 = 3 : 2 : 1)

| Product | Units | Joint cost (₹) |
|---|---:|---:|
| A | 6,000 | 1,20,000 × 6,000/12,000 = 60,000 |
| B | 4,000 | 1,20,000 × 4,000/12,000 = 40,000 |
| C | 2,000 | 1,20,000 × 2,000/12,000 = 20,000 |
| **Total** | **12,000** | **1,20,000** |

**(b) Market Value at Split-off Method** — joint cost in ratio of sales value (1,20,000 : 1,20,000 : 80,000 = 3 : 3 : 2)

| Product | Market value (₹) | Joint cost (₹) |
|---|---:|---:|
| A | 1,20,000 | 1,20,000 × 120/320 = 45,000 |
| B | 1,20,000 | 1,20,000 × 120/320 = 45,000 |
| C | 80,000 | 1,20,000 × 80/320 = 30,000 |
| **Total** | **3,20,000** | **1,20,000** |

**Comparison**

| Product | Physical units basis (₹) | Market value basis (₹) | Difference (₹) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 60,000 | 45,000 | −15,000 |
| B | 40,000 | 45,000 | +5,000 |
| C | 20,000 | 30,000 | +10,000 |

### Q73. Ch: Standard Costing — Planning vs Operational Variances (Concept) (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between a **Planning variance** and an **Operational variance**. When is the revision of standards appropriate, and why is this distinction useful for performance appraisal?

**Answer:**
Traditional variance analysis compares actual results with a single, ex-ante (before-the-event) standard fixed at the start of the period. When the standard itself turns out to be unrealistic because of factors that emerge only after it was set, this comparison mixes up two very different causes of deviation. To separate them, the total variance is split into a planning variance and an operational variance by introducing a **revised (ex-post) standard** — the standard that would have been set had the true conditions been known.

A **Planning variance** (also called a revision variance) is the difference between the **original standard** and the **revised standard**. It measures the part of the total variance caused by an incorrect original plan — for example, an unforeseen change in the market price of a material, a wage-award, or a change in technology. It is regarded as **uncontrollable** by the operating manager because it reflects a forecasting/standard-setting failure, not day-to-day performance.

An **Operational variance** is the difference between the **revised (realistic) standard** and the **actual result**. It measures how efficiently the manager performed against a fair, up-to-date benchmark, and is therefore regarded as **controllable** and the proper basis for appraising operating performance.

| Basis | Planning Variance | Operational Variance |
|---|---|---|
| Benchmark compared | Original std. vs Revised std. | Revised std. vs Actual |
| Cause | Faulty/obsolete original standard | Operating efficiency or inefficiency |
| Controllability | Uncontrollable | Controllable |
| Responsibility | Standard-setter / planning team | Line/operating manager |
| Use | Improve future standard-setting | Reward/investigate current performance |

**Revision of standards** is appropriate only when the change is **permanent and material** and arises from causes outside the operating manager's control (e.g., a structural market shift), not for temporary or self-inflicted fluctuations — otherwise standards lose their motivational and control value.

**Conclusion:** The planning/operational split ensures managers are held responsible only for what they can control, giving a fairer appraisal and a feedback loop that improves the quality of future standards.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner rewards the explicit "original → revised → actual" three-point framework and a clear controllable/uncontrollable mapping to responsibility. State the condition (permanent + material + external) for legitimate standard revision.)*

### Q81. Ch: Marginal Costing — Margin of Safety & Angle of Incidence (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain **(a)** the concept of **Margin of Safety** and the ways in which it can be improved, and **(b)** the **Angle of Incidence**. How do the two together indicate the soundness of a business?

**Answer:**
**(a) Margin of Safety (MOS):** The margin of safety is the excess of actual (or budgeted) sales over the break-even sales. It represents the amount of sales that may fall before the business begins to incur a loss, and is therefore a measure of the cushion or risk-buffer available.
- In value: MOS = Total Sales − Break-even Sales
- In units: MOS = Total Sales units − BEP units
- As a ratio: MOS ratio = (Profit ÷ P/V ratio) ÷ Sales, or simply Profit ÷ Contribution.

A **high margin of safety** signifies a sound position, since even a large fall in sales still leaves a profit; a low margin means the firm is dangerously close to its break-even point. The margin of safety can be **improved** by any action that widens the gap between sales and BEP, namely: (i) increasing the selling price (if the market permits); (ii) reducing variable cost per unit to raise contribution; (iii) reducing fixed cost, which lowers the BEP; (iv) increasing the sales volume; and (v) changing the sales mix toward products with a higher P/V ratio.

**(b) Angle of Incidence:** In a break-even chart, the angle of incidence is the angle formed at the break-even point between the **sales line** and the **total cost line**. It is governed by the P/V ratio — a large angle means contribution is being earned at a fast rate once the BEP is crossed, so profits accumulate quickly; a narrow angle indicates a low P/V ratio and slow profit build-up, often signalling high variable costs.

| Indicator | What it measures | "Sound" signal |
|---|---|---|
| Margin of Safety | Distance of sales above BEP (risk buffer) | Large MOS |
| Angle of Incidence | Rate of profit earning beyond BEP (P/V driven) | Wide angle |

**Conclusion:** Read together, a **large margin of safety with a wide angle of incidence** reflects the most favourable, low-risk, high-profitability position; a firm should aim to widen both.

*(Full-marks tip: give at least four concrete ways to improve MOS and correctly locate the angle of incidence as the sales-vs-total-cost angle at the BEP. Linking the angle to the P/V ratio earns the top band.)*

|---|---|---|---|---|

(i) Advise whether Product C should be discontinued (fixed cost is unavoidable). (ii) Would your answer change if dropping C releases capacity for a new Product D expected to earn a contribution of ₹70,000?

| | A | B | C | Total |

### Q85. Ch: Budgets & Budgetary Control — Zero-Base Budgeting (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** A newly appointed Cost Controller argues that the company's practice of preparing next year's budget by taking the current year's figures and adding a flat 8% is wasteful. He recommends adopting Zero-Base Budgeting (ZBB). Explain the concept of ZBB, outline the stages involved in its implementation, and state any two limitations.

**Answer:**
Zero-Base Budgeting is a method of budgeting in which **each cost element is required to be justified afresh, as though the activity were being undertaken for the first time**, without any reference to the previous period's level of expenditure. Unlike traditional (incremental) budgeting where the previous year's figure is taken as the base and only the increment is examined, under ZBB the base is taken as **"zero"** and every rupee of expenditure must be justified on a cost-benefit basis before it is sanctioned. The onus of proving that an activity should exist and receive funds rests on the manager proposing it.

The stages in implementing ZBB are as follows:
1. **Identification of "Decision Units"** — the smallest segment or activity of the organisation for which a separate budget is meaningfully prepared and for which a manager is responsible.
2. **Preparation of "Decision Packages"** — for each decision unit a set of documents is prepared identifying the activity, its purpose, the costs and benefits, the consequence of not performing it, and alternative ways of performing it (including different levels of effort).
3. **Ranking of decision packages** — all packages are ranked in descending order of importance/benefit to the organisation, so that management can see which activities most deserve funds.
4. **Allocation of resources** — available funds are allocated to the ranked packages from the top downward until the funds are exhausted; packages below the cut-off are not funded.

**Limitations:** (i) It is **time-consuming and costly**, as preparing and ranking decision packages for every unit requires substantial paperwork and managerial effort; (ii) **Ranking is subjective** and difficult where benefits (e.g., of R&D or staff welfare) cannot be quantified, which may lead to inter-departmental friction.

**Conclusion:** ZBB forces a fresh, cost-benefit justification of every activity and eliminates the perpetuation of past inefficiencies, but its benefits must be weighed against the considerable time and cost of operating it.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the four sequential stages named in the correct order — "decision unit → decision package → ranking → allocation." A common deduction is defining ZBB merely as "starting from zero" without contrasting it with the incremental base.)*

### Q86. Ch: Budgets & Budgetary Control — Performance Budgeting (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish, in the context of budgeting, between the traditional line-item approach and Performance Budgeting. What are the essential steps in installing a performance budgeting system?

**Answer:**
Performance Budgeting is a technique of presenting budgets in terms of **functions, programmes, activities and projects, and relating the physical outputs (performance) to the financial inputs (cost)**. It shifts the emphasis of the budget from *what is to be spent* (objects of expenditure such as salaries, rent, stores) to *what is to be achieved* (the results and services delivered). It is therefore an **output-oriented / objective-oriented** budget, whereas the traditional line-item budget is **input-oriented**, classifying expenditure merely by its nature.

| Basis | Traditional (Line-item) Budget | Performance Budget |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | Objects of expenditure (inputs) | Functions/programmes and their results (outputs) |
| Question answered | How much is to be spent, and on what items? | What is to be achieved, and at what cost per unit of output? |
| Basis of control | Comparison of spending against sanction | Comparison of achievement against physical & financial targets |
| Accountability | For not over-spending | For delivering the planned performance |

The essential steps in installing performance budgeting are: (i) **establishing the objectives** of each function/programme; (ii) **identifying the activities/programmes** required to achieve them; (iii) **fixing physical targets and the corresponding financial outlay** (cost) for each activity; and (iv) **evaluating performance** by comparing actual outputs and costs against the targets.

**Conclusion:** Performance budgeting links money to measurable results, making the budget a tool of accountability for achievement rather than merely a ceiling on spending.

*(Full-marks tip: State clearly the "input-oriented vs output-oriented" distinction — that single contrast carries most of the marks. Presenting the difference as a table earns the presentation marks.)*

### Q88. Ch: Budgets & Budgetary Control — Principal Budget Factor (Marks: 5) [Theory + Application]
**Question:** "The first task in budgetary control is to identify the principal budget factor." Explain what is meant by the principal budget factor, why it must be identified before any functional budget is prepared, and identify the principal budget factor in each of the following independent situations:
(i) A firm can sell all it produces, but its output is limited by a shortage of a specialised raw material.
(ii) A firm has ample material and labour, but demand for its product has fallen sharply in a recession.

**Answer:**
The **principal budget factor** (also called the **key factor, limiting factor or governing factor**) is that factor **the extent of whose influence must first be assessed in order to ensure that the functional budgets are capable of fulfilment**. In simple terms, it is the resource or constraint that **restricts the level of activity of the organisation** at a given point of time — for example, sales demand, a shortage of material, limited plant capacity, shortage of skilled labour, or shortage of cash.

It must be identified **first** because it sets the ceiling on the whole budget. The budget for the factor that is in short supply is prepared first, and **all other functional budgets are then built around it** so that they are mutually consistent and achievable. If, for instance, sales demand is the limiting factor, the sales budget is prepared first and the production, material and labour budgets are geared to it; preparing a production budget that ignores the limiting factor would produce a plan that cannot be executed.

**Application to the situations:**

| Situation | Principal budget factor | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| (i) Output limited by scarce raw material | **Availability of raw material** | Sales are unlimited; the constraint is the material, so the material budget is prepared first and production is geared to it. |
| (ii) Demand has fallen in a recession | **Sales demand** | Material and labour are abundant; the constraint is the market, so the sales budget governs all other budgets. |

**Conclusion:** The principal budget factor is the constraint that limits activity; identifying it first ensures that every functional budget is realistic and internally consistent.

*(Full-marks tip: Mention that the limiting factor is not permanent — it can shift (e.g., from material to demand) as circumstances change. State clearly which budget is prepared "first" in each case; that application is where the marks lie.)*

### Q92. Ch: Budgets & Budgetary Control — ZBB vs Traditional Budgeting (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between Zero-Base Budgeting and Traditional (Incremental) Budgeting.

**Answer:**
The two approaches differ fundamentally in the base they adopt and in the burden of justification they place on the manager, as tabulated below:

| Basis of distinction | Traditional (Incremental) Budgeting | Zero-Base Budgeting |
|---|---|---|
| Starting base | Previous year's actual/budget figure is the base; only the increment is examined | Base is **zero**; the entire expenditure must be justified afresh |
| Orientation | Accounting/cost-object oriented | Decision/objective oriented (cost-benefit) |
| Justification | Only the *increase* over last year needs justification | *Every* rupee, old and new, must be justified |
| Treatment of past inefficiencies | Carried forward, as last year's base is accepted | Eliminated, as no activity is taken for granted |
| Emphasis | On "how much more" than last year | On "why the activity should exist at all" |
| Cost and time | Relatively quick and inexpensive | Time-consuming and costly to operate |

**Conclusion:** Traditional budgeting perpetuates the past by accepting it as the base, whereas Zero-Base Budgeting questions the very existence of each activity and allocates funds strictly on a cost-benefit ranking — making it more rigorous but more expensive to operate.

### Q93. Ch: Service Costing — Features & Cost Units (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the essential features of service (operating) costing and the concept of a "composite cost unit". Give the appropriate cost unit for any four service undertakings.

**Answer:**
Service costing (also called operating costing) is the method of ascertaining the **cost of providing a service** rather than of manufacturing a tangible product. It is applied by undertakings that render services — such as transport, hospitals, hotels, canteens, power houses, toll roads and IT/software firms — whether to outside customers or to other departments internally.

Its essential **features** are: (i) the output is an **intangible service**, so there is normally no closing stock of finished output; (ii) the proportion of **fixed (standing) costs is generally high** relative to variable (running) costs; (iii) costs are commonly classified into **standing (fixed), running (variable) and maintenance** charges; and (iv) the cost is expressed per **unit of service** delivered.

A **composite cost unit** is a cost unit made up of **two factors combined**, used where a single factor would not fairly reflect the service rendered — for example, in transport a unit combining *distance* and *load* (a passenger-kilometre or tonne-kilometre) reflects the service far better than either distance or load alone.

| Service undertaking | Appropriate cost unit |
|---|---|
| Passenger transport | Passenger-kilometre |
| Goods transport | Tonne-kilometre |
| Hospital | Patient-day |
| Canteen | Per meal |
| Hotel | Room-day (or bed-night) |
| IT / software services | Man-day (or man-hour) |

**Conclusion:** Service costing ascertains the cost per unit of an intangible service, and where a single measure is inadequate, a composite cost unit combining two factors is used to express that cost fairly.

*(Full-marks tip: Define "composite" precisely as combining TWO measurable factors and give a two-factor example (tonne-km). Listing the cost unit against each service in a table secures the application marks.)*

### Q99. Ch: Service Costing — Toll Road / Vehicle-Kilometre (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** A toll operator manages a **50-kilometre** stretch of highway. The total annual cost to be recovered through toll is **₹5,00,00,000**. The estimated annual traffic is **20,00,000 vehicles**, each on average traversing the **full 50 km**. The operator uses the composite cost unit "cost per vehicle-kilometre". Compute (a) the total vehicle-kilometres per annum, (b) the toll rate per vehicle-kilometre, and (c) the toll payable by a vehicle travelling the full 50 km. (d) If a lighter class of vehicle is charged at **0.6 times** the standard vehicle-km rate, what toll would it pay for the full stretch?

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Total vehicle-kilometres per annum**
= Number of vehicles × Average distance per vehicle
= 20,00,000 vehicles × 50 km = **10,00,00,000 vehicle-km** (10 crore vehicle-km)

**(a)** Total vehicle-kilometres = **10,00,00,000 vehicle-km**.

**(b) Toll rate per vehicle-kilometre**
= Total annual cost ÷ Total vehicle-km = ₹5,00,00,000 ÷ 10,00,00,000 = **₹0.50 per vehicle-km**

**(c) Toll for a vehicle travelling the full 50 km (standard class)**
= ₹0.50 × 50 km = **₹25 per vehicle**

**(d) Toll for the lighter class (0.6 × standard rate)**
Rate = ₹0.50 × 0.6 = ₹0.30 per vehicle-km
Toll for 50 km = ₹0.30 × 50 = **₹15 per vehicle**

**Answer:** Total = **10,00,00,000 vehicle-km**; toll rate = **₹0.50 per vehicle-km**; standard vehicle toll for 50 km = **₹25**; lighter-class vehicle toll for 50 km = **₹15**.

*(Full-marks tip: The composite unit here combines number of vehicles AND distance — compute vehicle-km first, then divide cost by it. Note this differs from the passenger-car-unit approach: here the road length, not axle-load equivalence, drives the composite unit.)*

### Q100. Ch: Service Costing — Composite Cost Unit / Selection of Cost Unit (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** "In service costing the selection of an appropriate cost unit is critical, and often a composite unit is required." Explain, with reasons, why a composite cost unit is preferred in certain services, and state the appropriate composite/simple cost unit for: (i) goods transport, (ii) electricity generation, (iii) a toll road, and (iv) a hotel.

**Answer:**
A **cost unit** in service costing is the unit of service in terms of which cost is ascertained and controlled. A **simple cost unit** expresses cost by reference to a single factor (e.g., per kilowatt-hour, per room-day), whereas a **composite cost unit** combines **two factors** into one measure.

A composite unit is preferred wherever the cost of the service depends on **more than one variable simultaneously**, so that any single factor would misstate the cost. In goods transport, for example, the cost of carrying **1 tonne over 100 km** is not comparable to carrying **10 tonnes over 10 km**, even though the distance (or the load) considered alone might appear similar; a composite unit — the **tonne-kilometre** — combines both load and distance and therefore reflects the true extent of the service. The composite unit thus enables meaningful comparison of costs between periods and between operators, and supports fair pricing.

| Service | Appropriate cost unit | Type |
|---|---|---|
| (i) Goods transport | Tonne-kilometre | Composite (load × distance) |
| (ii) Electricity generation | Kilowatt-hour (kWh) | Simple |
| (iii) Toll road | Vehicle-kilometre (or per vehicle) | Composite (vehicle × distance) |
| (iv) Hotel | Room-day (or bed-night) | Composite (room × time) |

**Conclusion:** A composite cost unit is used where two factors together determine the service rendered; by combining both, it expresses the cost fairly and enables valid comparison and pricing, whereas a simple unit suffices only where a single factor governs the cost.
