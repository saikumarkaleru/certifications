### Q1. Ch: Material Cost — Perpetual vs Periodic Inventory System (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between the "Perpetual Inventory System" and the "Periodic Inventory System" of recording and verifying stores. Also state briefly how each system establishes the physical existence of stock.

**Answer:**
A **Perpetual Inventory System** is a system of records — maintained through the Bin Card and the Stores Ledger — that reflects the physical movement of stocks and their **current balance on a continuous, running basis** after every receipt and issue. It is invariably supported by a programme of **continuous stock-taking**, under which a few items are physically counted every day so that every item is verified several times a year without closing down the stores.

A **Periodic Inventory System** (also called the physical/annual inventory system) determines the quantity and value of stock only by an **actual physical count taken at the end of the accounting period**; consumption for the period is derived as *Opening Stock + Purchases − Closing Stock*, and no running balance is kept during the year.

The two systems are distinguished as under:

| Basis | Perpetual Inventory System | Periodic Inventory System |
|---|---|---|
| Recording | Continuous — Bin Card & Stores Ledger updated after every transaction | No continuous record; consumption found by difference |
| Stock balance | Available at any moment from the records | Known only at period-end after physical count |
| Physical verification | By continuous stock-taking spread over the year | By one physical count at period-end |
| Disruption of work | Production/stores not stopped for counting | Stores/production usually halted for the count |
| Detection of discrepancy | Discrepancies located promptly, item by item | Discrepancies detected late and in a lump |
| Loss/pilferage | Abnormal loss, obsolescence, theft revealed early | Concealed in the "consumption" figure |
| Cost of operation | Higher clerical cost but better control | Lower running cost, weaker control |

Under the perpetual system, physical existence is established because the **book balance shown by the Bin Card/Stores Ledger is compared with the physically counted balance** on the day that item falls due for continuous stock-taking, and any difference is investigated and adjusted. Under the periodic system, physical existence is established **only once** — by the year-end physical count that then becomes the closing stock.

**Conclusion:** The perpetual inventory system gives up-to-date balances and reliable, ongoing control through continuous stock-taking, whereas the periodic system merely values stock once a year by physical count and buries losses in consumption.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards the crisp linkage — perpetual = continuous *records* + continuous stock-taking, periodic = physical count by *difference*. Do not confuse "continuous stock-taking" (a verification programme) with "perpetual inventory" (a record system); losing that distinction is the common deduction.)*

### Q2. Ch: Material Cost — Normal & Abnormal Loss in Transit (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** A chemical was purchased by Zephyr Ltd. The following particulars relate to a consignment. Normal loss in transit due to evaporation/handling is estimated at **2%** of the quantity despatched and carries **no realisable value**. Determine the rate at which the good units received should be charged to stores and the treatment of any abnormal loss.

| Particulars | Amount |
|---|---|
| Quantity despatched by supplier | 5,000 units |
| Basic price | ₹200 per unit |
| Freight and insurance paid | ₹15,000 |
| Quantity actually received in stores | 4,880 units |

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Total delivered cost of the consignment**
= (5,000 × ₹200) + ₹15,000 = ₹10,00,000 + ₹15,000 = **₹10,15,000**

**WN-2: Analysis of loss in transit**
- Normal loss = 2% × 5,000 = **100 units** (no realisable value)
- Good units expected = 5,000 − 100 = **4,900 units**
- Units actually received = 4,880 units
- **Abnormal loss = 4,900 − 4,880 = 20 units**

**WN-3: Cost per good unit** (normal loss cost is absorbed by good units)
= Total delivered cost ÷ Good units expected
= ₹10,15,000 ÷ 4,900 = **₹207.14 per unit** (rounded)

**Statement Showing Valuation of Consignment**

| Particulars | Units | Rate (₹) | Value (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cost charged to Stores (good units received) | 4,880 | 207.14 | 10,10,857 |
| Abnormal loss (to Costing P&L A/c) | 20 | 207.14 | 4,143 |
| **Total delivered cost** | **4,900** | — | **10,15,000** |

*(Total tallies: 10,10,857 + 4,143 = ₹10,15,000, subject to rounding.)*

**Treatment:** The cost of **normal loss (100 units)** is not shown separately — it is absorbed by inflating the rate of the good units to ₹207.14. The **abnormal loss of 20 units (₹4,143)** is charged to the **Costing Profit & Loss Account**, not to the cost of production, because abnormal losses must not distort product cost.

**Answer:** Good units received are charged to stores at **₹207.14 per unit** (total ₹10,10,857); abnormal loss of **₹4,143** is written off to the Costing Profit & Loss Account.

*(Full-marks tip: Divide the delivered cost by the *expected* good output (units despatched − normal loss), NOT by units received. Charging normal loss to Costing P&L or valuing abnormal loss at basic price instead of the loaded rate are the two usual deductions.)*

### Q3. Ch: Material Cost — Recovery of Returnable Containers / Packing (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** A supplier delivers a raw chemical in returnable steel drums. From the data below, compute the cost per kg of the chemical to be charged to production, showing the treatment of the container charges.

| Particulars | Details |
|---|---|
| Quantity of chemical purchased | 2,000 kg @ ₹80 per kg |
| Number of drums used (100 kg each) | 20 drums |
| Deposit charged by supplier per drum | ₹150 |
| Refund allowed on drums returned within 30 days | ₹120 per drum |
| Drums returned in time | 18 drums |
| Drums retained/not returned by buyer | 2 drums |

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Basic cost of the chemical**
= 2,000 kg × ₹80 = **₹1,60,000**

**WN-2: Net cost of containers borne by the buyer**

| Particulars | Drums | ₹ per drum | ₹ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deposit charged on all drums | 20 | 150 | 3,000 |
| Less: Refund on drums returned in time | 18 | 120 | (2,160) |
| **Net container charge borne** | | | **840** |

This ₹840 is made up of — (i) non-refundable charge on 18 returned drums = 18 × (150 − 120) = ₹540, and (ii) full cost of 2 drums retained = 2 × 150 = ₹300; total ₹840. As the drums are used solely to bring in this material, the **net container cost is a part of the cost of material**.

**WN-3: Total cost and rate per kg**
= Basic cost + Net container charge = ₹1,60,000 + ₹840 = **₹1,60,840**
Cost per kg = ₹1,60,840 ÷ 2,000 kg = **₹80.42 per kg**

**Answer:** Cost of the chemical to be charged to production = **₹1,60,840, i.e., ₹80.42 per kg** (basic ₹80 + ₹0.42 net container recovery).

*(Full-marks tip: Only the *net irrecoverable* container charge (deposit less refund actually received) is added to material cost — never the gross deposit, because the refundable portion returns to the buyer. Treating the whole deposit as cost, or ignoring the fully-lost 2 retained drums, are the common errors.)*

### Q4. Ch: Material Cost — EOQ with Quantity Discount (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** From the following data of Helix Components Ltd., (i) compute the Economic Order Quantity, and (ii) advise whether the company should avail the bulk-purchase discount offered by the supplier.

| Particulars | Details |
|---|---|
| Annual consumption | 48,000 units |
| Purchase price (normal) | ₹40 per unit |
| Ordering cost per order | ₹200 |
| Carrying cost | 10% per annum of purchase price |
| Discount offered: 2% if a single order is 6,000 units or more | — |

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Economic Order Quantity (at normal price)**
Carrying cost per unit p.a. = 10% × ₹40 = ₹4
EOQ = √(2 × A × O ÷ C) = √(2 × 48,000 × 200 ÷ 4)
= √(1,92,00,000 ÷ 4) = √(48,00,000) = **2,191 units** (approx.)

**WN-2: Total annual cost at EOQ (order size 2,191 units, price ₹40)**

| Cost element | Computation | ₹ |
|---|---|---|
| Purchase cost | 48,000 × 40 | 19,20,000 |
| Ordering cost | (48,000 ÷ 2,191) × 200 | 4,382 |
| Carrying cost | (2,191 ÷ 2) × 4 | 4,382 |
| **Total** | | **19,28,764** |

*(At EOQ, ordering cost ≈ carrying cost, confirming the quantity.)*

**WN-3: Total annual cost if discount availed (order size 6,000 units, price ₹40 × 98% = ₹39.20)**
Carrying cost per unit = 10% × ₹39.20 = ₹3.92

| Cost element | Computation | ₹ |
|---|---|---|
| Purchase cost | 48,000 × 39.20 | 18,81,600 |
| Ordering cost | (48,000 ÷ 6,000) × 200 | 1,600 |
| Carrying cost | (6,000 ÷ 2) × 3.92 | 11,760 |
| **Total** | | **18,94,960** |

**WN-4: Comparison**

| Option | Total Annual Cost (₹) |
|---|---|
| Order at EOQ (2,191 units) | 19,28,764 |
| Order 6,000 units with 2% discount | 18,94,960 |
| **Net saving by availing discount** | **33,804** |

**Answer:** EOQ = **2,191 units**. However, availing the **2% quantity discount by ordering 6,000 units** lowers total annual cost by **₹33,804** (mainly through reduced purchase price and fewer orders), and is therefore recommended.

*(Full-marks tip: A discount decision must be judged on *total cost* (purchase + ordering + carrying), never on EOQ alone. The extra carrying cost of the larger lot must be weighed against the price saving; omitting purchase cost from the comparison is the classic mistake.)*

### Q5. Ch: Material Cost — Stores Ledger under Weighted Average (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** Prepare the Stores Ledger Account of Material 'X' for January 2026 under the **Weighted Average Price Method** from the transactions below. Returns to supplier are to be valued at the price at which the material was originally received.

| Date | Transaction |
|---|---|
| Jan 1 | Opening balance 500 units @ ₹20 |
| Jan 5 | Received 1,000 units @ ₹22 |
| Jan 10 | Issued 800 units |
| Jan 15 | Received 1,200 units @ ₹24 |
| Jan 18 | Returned to supplier 100 units (out of Jan 15 lot) |
| Jan 22 | Issued 900 units |
| Jan 28 | Received 500 units @ ₹25 |
| Jan 30 | Issued 600 units |

**Solution:**

**Stores Ledger Account of Material 'X' — Weighted Average Method**

| Date | Receipts (units / ₹) | Issues (units / rate / ₹) | Balance (units / ₹ / avg rate) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jan 1 | — | — | 500 / 10,000 / 20.000 |
| Jan 5 | 1,000 @ 22 = 22,000 | — | 1,500 / 32,000 / 21.333 |
| Jan 10 | — | 800 @ 21.333 = 17,067 | 700 / 14,933 / 21.333 |
| Jan 15 | 1,200 @ 24 = 28,800 | — | 1,900 / 43,733 / 23.018 |
| Jan 18 | (100) @ 24 = (2,400) *(return to supplier)* | — | 1,800 / 41,333 / 22.963 |
| Jan 22 | — | 900 @ 22.963 = 20,667 | 900 / 20,667 / 22.963 |
| Jan 28 | 500 @ 25 = 12,500 | — | 1,400 / 33,167 / 23.690 |
| Jan 30 | — | 600 @ 23.690 = 14,214 | 800 / 18,952 / 23.690 |

**WN-1: Average rate recomputation** — a fresh weighted-average rate is struck after **every receipt and after the return to supplier**, but NOT after an issue (issues do not change the average). E.g., after Jan 15: (14,933 + 28,800) ÷ 1,900 = ₹23.018.

**WN-2: Treatment of return to supplier (Jan 18)** — the 100 units returned belonged to the Jan 15 lot and are removed at their **original receipt price of ₹24** (₹2,400), after which the balance is re-averaged to ₹22.963.

**WN-3: Reconciliation (control check)**

| Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Total value received (10,000 + 22,000 + 28,800 + 12,500) | 73,300 |
| Less: Return to supplier | (2,400) |
| Net inflow | 70,900 |
| Total issues (17,067 + 20,667 + 14,214) | 51,948 |
| Closing stock (800 units) | 18,952 |
| Issues + Closing | **70,900** ✔ |

**Answer:** Total value of issues during January = **₹51,948**; closing stock = **800 units valued at ₹18,952** (average ₹23.69 per unit).

*(Full-marks tip: Under weighted average, re-strike the rate only on receipts and on returns-to-supplier — never on issues — and value the vendor return at the *purchase* price, not the current average. Carrying the average to 3 decimals and reconciling issues + closing = net inflow demonstrates arithmetical control the examiner looks for.)*

### Q6. Ch: Material Cost — Stock Discrepancies under Continuous Stock-Taking (Marks: 4) [Application]
**Question:** During continuous stock-taking under a perpetual inventory system, the storekeeper of Vanta Ltd. finds that for several items the physically counted quantity differs from the Bin Card balance. Explain how such shortages and surpluses of stores should be treated in cost accounts, distinguishing normal from abnormal discrepancies.

**Answer:**
Under a perpetual inventory system supported by continuous stock-taking, any difference between the **physical balance and the book (Bin Card / Stores Ledger) balance** is a **stock discrepancy** that must be investigated for its cause and then adjusted so that the records reflect the true physical quantity.

The treatment depends on the nature of the discrepancy:

| Type of discrepancy | Cause | Treatment in Cost Accounts |
|---|---|---|
| **Normal shortage** | Unavoidable — evaporation, shrinkage, breaking of bulk, weighing/handling losses | Absorbed as a part of material cost by charging it to the **cost of good material issued** (rate inflated / charged to factory overhead) |
| **Abnormal shortage** | Avoidable — pilferage, theft, careless handling, wrong postings | Charged to the **Costing Profit & Loss Account**; kept out of product cost |
| **Surplus (excess found)** | Over-recording of issues, under-recording of receipts, wrong postings | Credited back — value taken to **Costing Profit & Loss Account** (abnormal gain) after correcting the records |

The procedure is: (i) recount to confirm, (ii) investigate the cause, (iii) classify as normal or abnormal, (iv) authorise adjustment of the Bin Card/Stores Ledger so the book balance equals the physical balance, and (v) route the value to product cost (if normal) or to Costing P&L (if abnormal).

**Conclusion:** Normal discrepancies remain within cost of production, while abnormal discrepancies (whether shortage or surplus) are transferred to the Costing Profit & Loss Account so that product cost is not distorted.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner wants the normal-vs-abnormal split *and* the corrective step of adjusting the records to the physical count. Merely saying "charge to P&L" without the normal-loss-absorbed-in-cost treatment loses marks.)*

### Q7. Ch: Material Cost — Input–Output / Inventory Turnover Ratio (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** From the following data, compute the Inventory Turnover Ratio and the average number of days for which each material is held, and identify the slow-moving material.

| Particulars | Material P (₹) | Material Q (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| Opening stock | 20,000 | 15,000 |
| Closing stock | 30,000 | 25,000 |
| Materials consumed during the year | 1,50,000 | 40,000 |

*(Assume 365 days in the year.)*

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Average stock**
- Material P = (20,000 + 30,000) ÷ 2 = ₹25,000
- Material Q = (15,000 + 25,000) ÷ 2 = ₹20,000

**WN-2: Inventory Turnover Ratio** = Cost of materials consumed ÷ Average stock
- Material P = 1,50,000 ÷ 25,000 = **6 times**
- Material Q = 40,000 ÷ 20,000 = **2 times**

**WN-3: Average holding period (days)** = 365 ÷ Turnover Ratio
- Material P = 365 ÷ 6 = **60.83 days ≈ 61 days**
- Material Q = 365 ÷ 2 = **182.5 days ≈ 183 days**

**Statement of Inventory Turnover**

| Material | Turnover Ratio | Avg. holding period | Remark |
|---|---|---|---|
| P | 6 times | 61 days | Fast-moving |
| Q | 2 times | 183 days | **Slow-moving** |

**Answer:** Material **Q** is the slow-moving item — it turns over only **2 times** a year and is held for about **183 days**, against Material P which turns over **6 times** (held ~61 days). A **higher turnover ratio signifies faster consumption and lower blockage of funds**; the low ratio of Material Q warrants review for overstocking or obsolescence.

*(Full-marks tip: A *higher* turnover ratio = *faster* moving material, so the item with the LOWEST ratio is slow-moving — reversing this is the classic slip. Always use *consumption* (not purchases) over *average* stock.)*

### Q8. Ch: Material Cost — Stock Levels (Reorder, Minimum, Maximum, Danger) (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** From the following information relating to Component 'K', calculate the Re-order Level, Minimum Level, Maximum Level, Average Stock Level and Danger Level.

| Particulars | Details |
|---|---|
| Normal usage | 300 units per day |
| Minimum usage | 200 units per day |
| Maximum usage | 400 units per day |
| Re-order (lead) period | 10 to 15 days |
| Re-order Quantity | 3,000 units |
| Emergency lead time (for danger level) | 4 days |

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Re-order Level (ROL)** = Maximum usage × Maximum lead time
= 400 × 15 = **6,000 units**

**WN-2: Minimum Level** = ROL − (Normal usage × Average lead time)
Average lead time = (10 + 15) ÷ 2 = 12.5 days
= 6,000 − (300 × 12.5) = 6,000 − 3,750 = **2,250 units**

**WN-3: Maximum Level** = ROL + Re-order Quantity − (Minimum usage × Minimum lead time)
= 6,000 + 3,000 − (200 × 10) = 9,000 − 2,000 = **7,000 units**

**WN-4: Average Stock Level** = Minimum Level + ½ × Re-order Quantity
= 2,250 + ½ × 3,000 = 2,250 + 1,500 = **3,750 units**
*(OR = (Minimum + Maximum) ÷ 2 = (2,250 + 7,000) ÷ 2 = 4,625 units — either basis is acceptable if stated.)*

**WN-5: Danger Level** = Normal (average) usage × Emergency lead time
= 300 × 4 = **1,200 units**

**Statement of Stock Levels — Component 'K'**

| Level | Units |
|---|---|
| Re-order Level | 6,000 |
| Minimum Level | 2,250 |
| Maximum Level | 7,000 |
| Average Stock Level | 3,750 |
| Danger Level | 1,200 |

**Answer:** ROL = **6,000 units**, Minimum = **2,250 units**, Maximum = **7,000 units**, Average = **3,750 units**, Danger = **1,200 units**.

*(Full-marks tip: Use *maximum* usage × *maximum* lead for ROL, but *minimum* usage × *minimum* lead when computing the Maximum Level. Mixing up which usage/lead pairs with which formula is the single biggest source of lost marks; state the average-stock basis you adopt.)*

### Q9. Ch: Employee (Labour) Cost — Piece Rate vs Time-plus-Bonus with Output Data (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** In a factory the standard time allowed for a job is **30 minutes per unit** and the guaranteed time wage is **₹60 per hour**. In an 8-hour day a worker completed **20 units**. Compute the worker's earnings and the labour cost per unit under each of the following, and comment: (i) Time-rate (guaranteed day wage); (ii) Straight piece-rate; (iii) Halsey plan (50% of time saved); (iv) Rowan plan.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Basic data**
- Standard time for 20 units = 20 × 30 min = 600 min = **10 hours**
- Time actually taken = **8 hours**
- **Time saved = 10 − 8 = 2 hours**
- Piece rate per unit = ₹60 × (30/60) = **₹30 per unit**

**WN-2: Earnings under each system**

*(i) Time rate* = Hours worked × Rate = 8 × ₹60 = **₹480**

*(ii) Straight piece rate* = Units × Rate per unit = 20 × ₹30 = **₹600**
*(Check: Standard time 10 hrs × ₹60 = ₹600 — same as piece basis.)*

*(iii) Halsey* = (Hours worked × Rate) + 50% × Time saved × Rate
= 480 + (50% × 2 × 60) = 480 + 60 = **₹540**

*(iv) Rowan* = (Hours worked × Rate) + (Time saved ÷ Standard time) × Hours worked × Rate
= 480 + (2 ÷ 10) × 8 × 60 = 480 + 96 = **₹576**

**Statement Showing Comparative Earnings and Labour Cost per Unit**

| System | Earnings (₹) | Effective rate/hr (₹) | Labour cost/unit (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Time rate | 480 | 60.00 | 24.00 |
| Straight piece rate | 600 | 75.00 | 30.00 |
| Halsey (50%) | 540 | 67.50 | 27.00 |
| Rowan | 576 | 72.00 | 28.80 |

*(Effective rate/hr = Earnings ÷ 8 hrs; Labour cost/unit = Earnings ÷ 20 units.)*

**Comment:** The worker earns **most under straight piece-rate (₹600)** because the entire benefit of efficiency accrues to him; the employer's labour cost per unit is also highest (₹30). The **Rowan (₹576)** gives more to the worker than **Halsey (₹540)** at this efficiency (time saved = 20% of standard, i.e., below 50%). All three incentive schemes exceed the guaranteed time wage of ₹480, so the worker's minimum wage is protected.

**Answer:** Earnings — Time rate ₹480; Piece rate ₹600; Halsey ₹540; Rowan ₹576. Labour cost per unit — ₹24.00 / ₹30.00 / ₹27.00 / ₹28.80 respectively.

*(Full-marks tip: Compute *time saved* correctly (standard time for actual output − actual time) and show labour cost per unit for the comparison the question demands. Under Rowan the bonus fraction is time-saved ÷ *standard* time; using actual time in the denominator is the frequent error.)*

### Q10. Ch: Employee (Labour) Cost — Effect of Merger of DA into Basic Wages (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** Astra Ltd. proposes to **merge the Dearness Allowance (DA) into basic wages**. The company's provisions for gratuity and bonus are computed on **basic wages only**, whereas Provident Fund is on (Basic + DA). Using the monthly data of one worker below, show the effect of the merger on the company's total monthly labour cost.

| Particulars | Present (₹) | 
|---|---|
| Basic wages | 18,000 |
| Dearness Allowance | 12,000 |
| Employer's PF contribution | 12% of (Basic + DA) |
| Gratuity provision | 4.81% of Basic |
| Bonus provision | 8.33% of Basic |

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Labour cost BEFORE merger (Basic ₹18,000; DA ₹12,000)**

| Element | Computation | ₹ |
|---|---|---|
| Basic wages | — | 18,000 |
| Dearness Allowance | — | 12,000 |
| Employer PF | 12% × (18,000 + 12,000) | 3,600 |
| Gratuity | 4.81% × 18,000 | 865.80 |
| Bonus | 8.33% × 18,000 | 1,499.40 |
| **Total labour cost** | | **35,965.20** |

**WN-2: Labour cost AFTER merger (Basic ₹30,000; DA Nil)**
On merger the DA of ₹12,000 becomes a part of basic, so gratuity and bonus are now computed on ₹30,000; PF base (Basic + DA) is unchanged at ₹30,000.

| Element | Computation | ₹ |
|---|---|---|
| Basic wages (18,000 + 12,000) | — | 30,000 |
| Dearness Allowance | — | Nil |
| Employer PF | 12% × 30,000 | 3,600 |
| Gratuity | 4.81% × 30,000 | 1,443.00 |
| Bonus | 8.33% × 30,000 | 2,499.00 |
| **Total labour cost** | | **37,542.00** |

**WN-3: Effect of the merger**

| Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Total cost after merger | 37,542.00 |
| Total cost before merger | 35,965.20 |
| **Increase in monthly labour cost** | **1,576.80** |

The increase of ₹1,576.80 arises **entirely from gratuity and bonus** (their base rose by ₹12,000): additional gratuity = 4.81% × 12,000 = ₹577.20 and additional bonus = 8.33% × 12,000 = ₹999.60 (total ₹1,576.80). PF and take-home wages are unaffected because their base was already Basic + DA.

**Answer:** Merger of DA into basic **raises the monthly labour cost by ₹1,576.80 per worker** (from ₹35,965.20 to ₹37,542.00), caused by the enlarged base for gratuity and bonus.

*(Full-marks tip: The examiner rewards identifying *which* items are basic-linked and therefore rise on merger (gratuity, bonus) versus those already on Basic+DA (PF) that do not. State that direct wages/PF are unchanged — asserting PF also rises is the common error.)*

### Q11. Ch: Employee (Labour) Cost — Overtime: Customer's Request vs General Pressure (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** Explain how the **overtime premium** should be treated in cost accounts under different circumstances. In a month a factory paid 200 overtime hours (normal rate ₹50 per hour, overtime paid at double the normal rate); of these, 120 hours were worked at the **specific request of a customer** to expedite his order and 80 hours arose from **general pressure of work**. Compute the overtime premium and state its treatment.

**Answer / Solution:**
**Overtime premium** is the *excess* over the normal wage rate paid for hours worked beyond the normal working hours. The wage for the normal-hour equivalent is always a direct/normal labour cost; only the **premium element** needs special treatment, which depends on the *cause*:

| Circumstance | Treatment of overtime premium |
|---|---|
| At the **specific request of a customer** to complete his job early | Charged **directly to that job/work order** as direct wages |
| **General pressure of work / to meet output targets** | Treated as **factory (production) overhead** and recovered from all jobs |
| Due to **abnormal conditions** (e.g., flood, breakdown, management fault) | Charged to the **Costing Profit & Loss Account** |
| **Seasonal / regular** feature of the trade | Included in **factory overhead** by inflating the wage rate |

**WN-1: Overtime premium rate** = Overtime rate − Normal rate = (2 × ₹50) − ₹50 = **₹50 per hour**

**WN-2: Total overtime premium** = 200 hrs × ₹50 = **₹10,000**

**WN-3: Allocation of the premium**

| Cause | Hours | Premium (₹) | Charged to |
|---|---|---|---|
| Customer's specific request | 120 | 6,000 | The customer's job (direct) |
| General pressure of work | 80 | 4,000 | Factory overhead |
| **Total** | **200** | **10,000** | |

**Answer:** Total overtime premium = **₹10,000**; of this **₹6,000** (customer's request) is charged **directly to that job**, and **₹4,000** (general pressure) is treated as **factory overhead**. The normal-rate wages for all 200 hours (200 × ₹50 = ₹10,000) remain ordinary direct wages.

*(Full-marks tip: Treat only the *premium* specially, not the whole overtime payment. Correctly routing customer-requested overtime to the job and general overtime to overhead — with the abnormal case to Costing P&L — is what earns full marks.)*

### Q12. Ch: Employee (Labour) Cost — Labour Turnover Rates (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** From the following data of Nimbus Ltd. for the year, compute the labour turnover rate under (i) Separation method, (ii) Replacement method, and (iii) Flux method (on both bases).

| Particulars | Number |
|---|---|
| Number of workers at the beginning of the year | 4,800 |
| Number of workers at the end of the year | 5,200 |
| Workers who left / were discharged during the year | 80 |
| Workers engaged to replace those who left | 60 |
| Additional workers engaged for a new expansion | 340 |

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Average number of workers** = (4,800 + 5,200) ÷ 2 = **5,000 workers**

**WN-2: Accessions** = Replacements + New (expansion) recruits = 60 + 340 = **400 workers joined**

**WN-3: Labour turnover rates**

*(i) Separation method* = (Separations ÷ Average workers) × 100
= (80 ÷ 5,000) × 100 = **1.60%**

*(ii) Replacement method* = (Replacements ÷ Average workers) × 100
= (60 ÷ 5,000) × 100 = **1.20%**

*(iii) Flux method:*
- On (Separations + Replacements) basis = (80 + 60) ÷ 5,000 × 100 = (140 ÷ 5,000) × 100 = **2.80%**
- On (Separations + Accessions) basis = (80 + 400) ÷ 5,000 × 100 = (480 ÷ 5,000) × 100 = **9.60%**

**Statement of Labour Turnover**

| Method | Rate |
|---|---|
| Separation | 1.60% |
| Replacement | 1.20% |
| Flux (Sep + Repl) | 2.80% |
| Flux (Sep + Accessions) | 9.60% |

**Answer:** Separation = **1.60%**, Replacement = **1.20%**, Flux = **2.80%** (separations + replacements) or **9.60%** if all accessions (including expansion recruits) are counted.

*(Full-marks tip: The new workers taken for *expansion* are accessions but NOT replacements — so the replacement rate uses only the 60, while the flux rate on the accession basis includes all 400. Confusing expansion recruits with replacements distorts every rate.)*

### Q14. Ch: Employee (Labour) Cost — Treatment of Idle Time (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** What is meant by "Idle Time"? Distinguish between normal and abnormal idle time and explain their treatment in cost accounts.

**Answer:**
**Idle time** refers to the **time for which the workers are paid but no production or productive work is obtained** — i.e., the difference between the time paid for and the time actually booked on jobs. It is measured in labour hours and its wage cost must be accounted for.

Idle time is classified as under:

| Basis | Normal Idle Time | Abnormal Idle Time |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Inherent/unavoidable in the working situation | Avoidable; arises from causes that ought to be controlled |
| Examples | Time from gate to shop, tea/rest breaks, machine set-up, waiting for instructions/tools between jobs | Strikes, lock-outs, power failure, machine breakdown, shortage of material, poor planning |
| Controllability | Cannot ordinarily be eliminated | Can and should be controlled/eliminated |

**Treatment in cost accounts:**
- The **cost of normal idle time** is a legitimate part of the cost of production. It is treated either by (a) inflating the wage rate so that the productive hours absorb the idle-time wage, or (b) charging it to **factory overhead**.
- The **cost of abnormal idle time is not charged to production**; it is transferred to the **Costing Profit & Loss Account** so that it does not inflate and distort product cost.

**Conclusion:** Normal idle-time cost is absorbed into production (via inflated rate or factory overhead), whereas abnormal idle-time cost is written off to the Costing Profit & Loss Account.

*(Full-marks tip: Give at least two examples of each type and state *both* acceptable methods for normal idle time (inflated rate OR overhead). Charging abnormal idle time to production is the standard deduction.)*

### Q15. Ch: Employee (Labour) Cost — Taylor's Differential Piece Rate (Marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** Under Taylor's Differential Piece Rate System, the standard output is **8 units per hour** in an 8-hour day. The ordinary piece rate is **₹5 per unit**. Taylor's lower rate is **83%** and higher rate is **125%** of the ordinary piece rate. Calculate the daily earnings of Worker A who produces **50 units** and Worker B who produces **70 units** in a day, and state the effect of the scheme.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Standard daily output** = 8 units/hour × 8 hours = **64 units per day**

**WN-2: Differential piece rates**
- Lower rate = 83% × ₹5 = **₹4.15 per unit** (applied when output < standard)
- Higher rate = 125% × ₹5 = **₹6.25 per unit** (applied when output ≥ standard)

**WN-3: Determination of applicable rate**
- Worker A: 50 units < 64 (below standard) → lower rate ₹4.15
- Worker B: 70 units > 64 (above standard) → higher rate ₹6.25

**Statement Showing Daily Earnings — Taylor's Differential Piece Rate**

| Worker | Output (units) | Efficiency (Output ÷ 64) | Rate applied (₹) | Earnings (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 50 | 78.1% (below std) | 4.15 | 50 × 4.15 = **207.50** |
| B | 70 | 109.4% (above std) | 6.25 | 70 × 6.25 = **437.50** |

**Effect of the scheme:** Taylor's system is **highly penal to the inefficient and highly rewarding to the efficient** — there is no guaranteed time wage, so the below-standard Worker A is paid the low rate on *every* unit (only ₹207.50), while the above-standard Worker B is paid the high rate on *every* unit (₹437.50). The sharp gap is designed to drive out slow workers and reward fast ones.

**Answer:** Worker A earns **₹207.50** and Worker B earns **₹437.50** per day.

*(Full-marks tip: Under Taylor there is *no guaranteed minimum* and the selected rate applies to the *entire* output (not a slab split). Applying the higher rate only to the excess units, or blending rates, is the standard error.)*

### Q16. Ch: Employee (Labour) Cost — Effective Wage Rate with Normal Idle Time & Fringe Benefits (Marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** A worker is paid **₹120 per day of 8 hours**. Out of every 8-hour day, **40 minutes** is treated as normal idle time. Fringe benefits amount to **25% of wages**. In a day the worker produces **30 units**. Compute (i) the effective (inflated) labour hour rate and (ii) the labour cost per unit.

**Solution:**

**WN-1: Effective working hours per day**
Normal idle time = 40 min = 40/60 hour = 0.6667 hour
Effective productive hours = 8 − 0.6667 = **7.3333 hours**

**WN-2: Total labour cost per day (wages + fringe benefits)**
Fringe benefits = 25% × ₹120 = ₹30
Total daily labour cost = ₹120 + ₹30 = **₹150**

**WN-3: Effective labour hour rate**
= Total daily labour cost ÷ Effective productive hours
= ₹150 ÷ 7.3333 = **₹20.45 per productive hour**

| Labour cost per unit | ₹5.00 |

| (vi) Stores / material-handling service | Number of material requisitions or value/weight of material handled | Reflects the volume of service each department draws from stores |

**Analysis:** Job 501 is machining-intensive (a department whose overhead is far heavier — ₹20/hr against the blanket ₹12/hr). The blanket rate therefore *under-charges* the job by ₹214.40 − ₹144.00 = **₹70.40**, understating its cost and, if used for pricing, eroding the margin.

**Conclusion / Advice:** Where departments differ significantly in their overhead intensity and jobs do not pass uniformly through all of them, the firm should adopt **departmental overhead rates** in place of a single blanket rate to cost each job accurately.

*(Full-marks tip: don't stop at the two figures — the marks are in the advice that a blanket rate is safe only when all jobs spend broadly equal time in each department; here they do not, so departmental rates are warranted.)*

### Q24. Ch: Overheads – Absorption Costing — Methods of Disposal of Under- or Over-Absorbed Overhead (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the three methods available for the disposal of under- or over-absorbed overhead, indicating when each is appropriate.

**Answer:** When overhead is absorbed on a pre-determined rate, the amount absorbed rarely equals the actual overhead; the difference — under- or over-absorption — must be disposed of at the year end by one of the following three methods:

**(1) Writing off to the Costing Profit & Loss Account.** The whole balance is transferred to the Costing P&L Account. This is appropriate when the amount is *small*, or when it has arisen from *abnormal* reasons (e.g., abnormal idle time, strikes, breakdowns) that should not be allowed to distort product cost.

**(2) Use of a supplementary rate.** A supplementary overhead rate is worked out and applied to adjust the cost of units in cost of sales, finished-goods stock and work-in-progress. A *positive* (additive) supplementary rate corrects under-absorption; a *negative* rate corrects over-absorption. This is appropriate when the amount is *large* and has arisen from *normal* causes such as errors in estimating the rate, so that costs and stock values are restored to what they would have been on actual overhead.

**(3) Carrying over to the next accounting period.** The balance is carried forward in an Overhead Reserve/Suspense Account to be set off against the opposite balance expected next period. This is appropriate only where the business is *seasonal* or the under/over-absorption is due to normal cyclical fluctuations that will reverse — it is generally not favoured, as costs of one period should not be carried into another.

**Conclusion:** The choice turns on the *size* of the balance and *why* it arose — small or abnormal amounts are written off, large normal amounts are corrected by a supplementary rate, and only genuinely cyclical balances are carried forward.

*(Full-marks tip: pair each method with the circumstance in which it applies — the examiner is testing judgement, not merely the list. Note that abnormal balances are written off even if large.)*

### Q27. Ch: Activity-Based Costing — Cost Hierarchy Applied to a Distribution Business (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the four levels of the ABC cost hierarchy and give, for each level, an example of a distribution/logistics cost that would belong there.

**Answer:** Activity-Based Costing classifies activities (and their costs) into a *hierarchy* according to what causes the cost to be incurred, so that each cost is driven by an appropriate level of activity rather than by volume alone:

| Level | Cost is driven by | Distribution/logistics example |
|---|---|---|
| **Unit-level** | Each individual unit handled/moved | Cost of picking or packing per item; fuel per parcel delivered |
| **Batch-level** | Each batch, order or delivery run, regardless of the number of units in it | Order-processing cost per order; cost of a delivery trip; a warehouse set-up for a dispatch run |
| **Product/Customer-sustaining level** | Supporting a particular product line, route or customer, irrespective of the number of orders or units | Cost of maintaining a customer account, a dedicated route, or product-specific storage conditions |
| **Facility (organisation)-sustaining level** | The distribution operation as a whole | Warehouse rent, depreciation of the depot, general management of the logistics centre |

**Conclusion:** By recognising that batch- and product/customer-level costs vary with the *number of orders, deliveries and customers* — not with volume — ABC traces distribution overhead far more accurately than a single volume-based rate; facility-level costs, having no cause-and-effect driver, are apportioned or treated as period costs.

*(Full-marks tip: the batch-level idea — cost driven by the number of orders/deliveries rather than units — is the heart of ABC's advantage in distribution; illustrate every level with a logistics example, not a generic factory one.)*

### Q29. Ch: Activity-Based Costing — When ABC Gives Materially Different (and Better) Results (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** In what circumstances does Activity-Based Costing produce results that differ materially from — and are more reliable than — traditional volume-based absorption costing? When is a firm justified in adopting ABC?

**Answer:** ABC and traditional absorption costing give broadly similar answers in a simple, single-product-type operation; the two diverge materially only when the following conditions are present:

1. **Overheads form a large proportion of total cost** (Sec. of cost that is *not* directly variable with volume) — in modern, automated, service-oriented businesses, so the manner of charging overhead materially affects reported cost.
2. **Overheads are driven by factors other than volume** — set-ups, orders, inspections, deliveries and customer service. A volume-based rate (labour or machine hours) then *over-costs* high-volume, simple products/customers and *under-costs* low-volume, complex ones — the "product/customer cost cross-subsidy".
3. **The firm produces a diverse product/customer mix** — differing in batch sizes, complexity and the support activities each consumes; the greater this diversity, the greater the distortion under a single rate.
4. **The competitive environment demands accurate cost** for pricing, product-line and customer-retention decisions, so distortions carry a real cost.

A firm is justified in adopting ABC when these conditions hold **and** the *benefit* of more accurate costs exceeds the *cost* of designing and running the more elaborate system.

**Conclusion:** ABC is worth adopting where overheads are high, driven by non-volume factors, and the product/customer mix is diverse — precisely the setting in which a single volume-based rate seriously mis-states cost.

*(Full-marks tip: frame the answer around *diversity + non-volume drivers + high overhead*, and end with the cost-benefit test — that judgement is what distinguishes a top answer from a memorised list of "benefits of ABC".)*

### Q34. Ch: Cost Accounting Systems & Reconciliation — Pre-requisites and Advantages of Integrated Accounting (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** What is an Integrated Accounting System? State its essential pre-requisites and its advantages, and distinguish it briefly from a non-integrated system.

**Answer:** An **Integrated (or Integral) Accounting System** is one in which the cost accounts and the financial accounts are merged into a *single self-contained set of books*, so that both cost and financial information are obtained from one integrated ledger without maintaining two separate sets and without any need for reconciliation.

**Essential pre-requisites:**
1. A clear decision on the *degree of integration* — whether it is to cover the whole accounting or only up to prime/works cost.
2. A suitable, coded **chart of accounts** that classifies expenditure by nature (for financial reporting) and simultaneously by function/element (for cost ascertainment).
3. Agreement on the **treatment of items** such as the basis of stock valuation, provision for depreciation and the recovery of overheads, so that one figure serves both purposes.
4. **Perfect coordination** between the cost and financial staff and reliable, well-designed accounting records and supporting documents.

**Advantages:**
1. **No reconciliation** is necessary, since there is only one profit figure — that of the single ledger.
2. It **avoids duplication** of recording, saving clerical effort and cost.
3. It gives **complete and prompt information**, with cost and financial data cross-checked automatically.
4. There is a greater **accuracy** and coordination, as all data are drawn from one arithmetically self-balancing ledger.

**Distinction from a non-integrated system:**

| Basis | Integrated system | Non-integrated system |
|---|---|---|
| Set of books | One combined ledger | Separate cost and financial ledgers |
| Cost Ledger Control / GLA A/c | Not required (real accounts used) | Required to make the cost ledger self-balancing |
| Reconciliation | Not needed | Needed, as two profit figures arise |
| Clerical effort | Less (no duplication) | More (recording is duplicated) |

**Conclusion:** An integrated system unifies cost and financial accounting into one economical, reconciliation-free set of books; its success rests on a sound coded chart of accounts and agreed treatment of stock, depreciation and overheads.

*(Full-marks tip: bring out the two clinchers — "no General Ledger Adjustment Account" and "no reconciliation needed" — these are what separate an integrated from a non-integrated system. Listing the pre-requisites (coding, agreed treatments, coordination) earns the theory marks the distinction table alone would miss.)*

### Q49. Ch: Job & Contract Costing — Cost-Plus Contract & Profit under Different Bases (Marks: 6) [Theory]
**Question:** (a) Explain what is meant by a **"Cost-plus contract"** and state its advantages and disadvantages to the contractor and the contractee. (b) State the ICAI-recommended rules for determining the profit to be transferred to the Profit & Loss Account on an incomplete contract at various stages of completion.

**Answer:**
**(a) Cost-plus contract:** A cost-plus contract is one in which the contract price is not fixed at the outset but is ascertained by adding to the allowable/agreed cost of the work a stipulated amount or percentage of profit. Such contracts are used where it is not possible to estimate cost accurately in advance — for example, defence supplies, urgent work, or projects with volatile input prices. The costs to be reimbursed and the profit margin are defined in the agreement, and the contractee usually retains a right of cost audit.

**Advantages** — To the contractor: assured of recovery of cost plus a reasonable profit, so the risk of loss from price fluctuation is eliminated. To the contractee: the price is based on actual cost and is therefore, in principle, fair.

**Disadvantages** — To the contractee: the contractor has little incentive to control cost since a higher cost yields a higher absolute profit; the final price is not known in advance. To the contractor: profit is limited to the agreed margin and the accounts are subject to scrutiny/cost audit by the contractee.

**Distinction:**

| Basis | Fixed-price contract | Cost-plus contract |
|---|---|---|
| Price | Fixed at outset | Cost + agreed profit |
| Risk of cost rise | Borne by contractor | Borne by contractee |
| Cost control incentive | High | Low |
| Suitability | Cost estimable | Cost not estimable |

**(b) ICAI rules for profit on incomplete contracts:**
- **Completion less than 25%:** No profit is transferred to P&L; the entire notional profit is kept in reserve (prudence).
- **25% or more but less than 50%:** Profit to P&L = Notional Profit × ⅓ × (Cash received ÷ Work certified).
- **50% or more but less than 90%:** Profit to P&L = Notional Profit × ⅔ × (Cash received ÷ Work certified).
- **90% and above (near completion):** Use the estimated-total-profit method and transfer profit in the ratio of Work certified ÷ Contract price (or Cash received ÷ Contract price), being the portion earned.

**Conclusion:** A cost-plus contract shifts cost risk to the contractee but weakens cost discipline; and profit on incomplete contracts is recognised conservatively in rising fractions of notional/estimated profit as completion advances.

*(Full-marks tip: Give the exact fractions (⅓, ⅔) and the cash-received/work-certified adjustment, and the switch to estimated total profit at ≥90%. The examiner rewards the prudence rationale for the <25% no-profit rule.)*

### Q52. Ch: Process & Operation Costing — Inter-Process Profits (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** In some manufacturing concerns the output of one process is transferred to the next process not at cost but at a price showing a profit to the transferring process. Explain the purpose of charging such inter-process profit and state, with reasons, the difficulty it creates when valuing closing stock and preparing the final accounts.

**Answer:**
Where the output of one process is passed on to the next process at cost **plus a margin of profit**, the amount so added is called **inter-process profit**. It is a device of internal transfer pricing and does not represent profit earned from an outside party.

The **purpose** of charging inter-process profit is three-fold. First, it helps the management to **compare the cost of each process with the price at which a similar semi-finished product could be bought from or sold to the outside market**, so that the efficiency and economy of running each process internally can be judged (a "make or buy" appraisal at every stage). Secondly, it makes each process a **profit centre**, so that the performance of the person in charge of a process can be measured on a profit basis and not merely on a cost basis. Thirdly, it discloses whether it is more economical to **process the product further or to sell it** at an intermediate stage.

The **difficulty** it creates is that of **unrealised profit locked up in unsold closing stock**. Since profit can be recognised only when goods are sold to an outside party, the profit element loaded on the closing stock lying within the factory has **not been realised**. If such stock is carried in the Balance Sheet at transfer price (cost + profit), both the **stock value and the profit for the year would stand overstated**. Therefore a **"Provision for Unrealised Profit on Stock" (Stock Reserve)** must be created by debiting the Profit and Loss Account, so that stock appears in the Balance Sheet at cost to the concern and only the profit actually realised on goods sold outside is taken to the credit of the Profit and Loss Account.

**Conclusion:** Inter-process profit is charged for control and decision-making, but the unrealised profit contained in closing stock must be eliminated through a stock reserve so that neither stock nor profit is overstated.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner rewards the two ideas together — WHY profit is loaded (profit-centre/market comparison) AND the consequence (unrealised profit → stock reserve). Losing the "provision by debit to P&L" point is the common deduction.)*

### Q55. Ch: Process & Operation Costing — Nature & Applicability (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the essential characteristics of **process costing** and give any two situations in which it, rather than job costing, is the appropriate method of ascertaining cost.

**Answer:**
**Process costing** is a method of costing used to ascertain the cost of a product at each **process or stage of manufacture** where production is **continuous** and the output of one process becomes the input of the next. The cost per unit is obtained by **averaging** the total process cost over the number of units produced.

Its essential **characteristics** are as follows. (i) Production is **continuous and carried on in a sequence of distinct processes**, the finished output of one process forming the raw material of the succeeding process, the last process yielding the finished product. (ii) The products are **homogeneous and standardised**, so that one unit cannot be distinguished from another; hence cost is computed as an **average** and not order-wise. (iii) Costs are **accumulated process-wise** for a period (not job-wise), and a separate Process Account is maintained for each process. (iv) The existence of **normal and abnormal losses, work-in-progress, equivalent units and inter-process transfers** is a normal feature requiring specific treatment. (v) Some processes may produce **joint products and by-products** simultaneously.

**Two situations where process costing (not job costing) is appropriate:**

| Basis | Illustrative situation |
|---|---|
| Continuous mass production of identical units | Manufacture of chemicals, cement, sugar, paper, refined oil |
| Output flows through successive uniform operations | Textile spinning and weaving, steel rolling, food processing |

**Conclusion:** Process costing is used where production is continuous, output is uniform and cost has to be averaged process-wise — as against job costing, which suits distinct, customer-specific orders.

*(Full-marks tip: name at least two real industries. Merely defining process costing without the distinguishing features (continuous, homogeneous, average, sequential) loses half the marks.)*

### Q56. Ch: Process & Operation Costing — Operation Costing (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** What is **operation costing**? How does it differ from process costing? State the circumstances in which operation costing is more useful than process costing.

**Answer:**
**Operation costing** is a **refinement of process costing**. It is a method of costing applied where a product passes through a number of distinct **operations**, and the cost is ascertained **operation-wise** rather than merely process-wise. Under it, the cost of each operation is collected and divided by the number of units **processed at that operation** (not merely units completed) so that the cost per unit of every operation is available. It is especially concerned with the **rejections at each operation**, so that the cost of good output can be correctly loaded.

The distinction may be tabulated as follows:

| Basis | Process Costing | Operation Costing |
|---|---|---|
| Unit of cost collection | A whole process (which may contain several operations) | Each individual operation within a process |
| Degree of detail | Broader; cost per process | Finer; cost per operation |
| Treatment of rejections | Normal loss dealt with process-wise | Rejection rate applied operation-by-operation |
| Control | Process-level control | Tighter, operation-level control |

**Circumstances where operation costing is more useful:** where a product passes through a **large number of finely divisible operations** (e.g., manufacture of engineering components, cycles, toys, ready-made garments, hardware); where **each operation has its own rate of rejection/wastage** that must be separately monitored; and where management needs **operation-wise efficiency and cost control** rather than an averaged process figure.

**Conclusion:** Operation costing is process costing applied at the finer level of each operation, and is preferred where detailed, operation-wise cost control and rejection analysis are required.

*(Full-marks tip: stress that operation costing collects cost per operation and specifically tracks rejection at each operation — that is the point the examiner rewards over a generic "it is like process costing" answer.)*

### Q58. Ch: Joint Products & By-Products — Concepts & Methods (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between **joint products** and **by-products**. Also state the various methods available for apportioning joint costs among joint products.

**Answer:**
**Joint products** are two or more products of **more or less equal importance** (in terms of value) that are **produced simultaneously from the same raw material and process**, and are so related that no one product can be produced without the others (e.g., petrol, diesel and kerosene from crude oil). A **by-product** is a product of **relatively small value** that emerges **incidentally** in the course of manufacturing the main product (e.g., molasses in sugar manufacture, sawdust in a saw mill).

The distinction may be tabulated as follows:

| Basis | Joint Products | By-Products |
|---|---|---|
| Relative value | All of substantial and roughly equal value | Small/minor value compared with main product |
| Objective of production | Deliberately produced; each is an intended end | Emerges incidentally, not the main objective |
| Managerial attention | Each is a main product | Secondary; incidental |
| Cost treatment | Joint cost apportioned among them | Net realisable value usually credited to main product's cost |

**Methods of apportioning joint costs among joint products:**
1. **Physical unit (quantity) method** — joint cost apportioned in the ratio of physical output (weight/volume).
2. **Average unit cost method** — total joint cost ÷ total units, giving one average rate for all products.
3. **Market/sales value at split-off point method** — apportioned in the ratio of sales value at the point of separation.
4. **Net realisable value (reverse cost) method** — apportioned on the basis of final sales value less further processing and selling costs.
5. **Contribution margin method** — variable joint cost apportioned on units and fixed joint cost on the ratio of contribution margins.

**Conclusion:** The essential difference is one of **relative value and intention** — joint products are of major, comparable value produced deliberately, whereas by-products are of minor value produced incidentally.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner wants the "relative value / intention" test plus a named example of each, and a listed set of apportionment methods. A pure definition without the comparison table under-scores.)*

### Q59. Ch: Joint Products & By-Products — Contribution Margin Method (Marks: 8) [Problem]
**Question:** In a joint process, joint costs of ₹1,00,000 are incurred, of which ₹60,000 are variable and ₹40,000 are fixed. Two joint products A and B emerge. There is no further processing. The data are:

| Product | Units produced | Selling price per unit (₹) | Joint variable cost per unit already known to be traceable | 
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 4,000 | 33 | — |
| B | 6,000 | 18 | — |

Apportion the joint costs to A and B using the **Contribution Margin Method** and prepare a statement of total cost and profit for each product.

**Solution:**
Under the Contribution Margin Method the **variable joint cost** is apportioned on the basis of **units produced**, and the **fixed joint cost** is apportioned on the basis of the **contribution margin** earned by each product (Sales − Variable cost).

**WN-1: Apportion variable joint cost (₹60,000) on units produced (4,000 : 6,000 = 2 : 3).**
A = 60,000 × 4,000/10,000 = ₹24,000.
B = 60,000 × 6,000/10,000 = ₹36,000.

**WN-2: Compute contribution margin of each product.**

| Particulars | A (₹) | B (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| Sales (A: 4,000 × 33; B: 6,000 × 18) | 1,32,000 | 1,08,000 |
| Less: Variable joint cost (WN-1) | 24,000 | 36,000 |
| **Contribution margin** | **1,08,000** | **72,000** |

Contribution margin ratio A : B = 1,08,000 : 72,000 = **3 : 2**.

**WN-3: Apportion fixed joint cost (₹40,000) on contribution margin ratio (3 : 2).**
A = 40,000 × 3/5 = ₹24,000.
B = 40,000 × 2/5 = ₹16,000.

**Statement Showing Apportionment of Joint Cost, Total Cost and Profit**

| Particulars | A (₹) | B (₹) | Total (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Variable joint cost (WN-1) | 24,000 | 36,000 | 60,000 |
| Fixed joint cost (WN-3) | 24,000 | 16,000 | 40,000 |
| **Total joint cost apportioned** | **48,000** | **52,000** | **1,00,000** |
| Sales | 1,32,000 | 1,08,000 | 2,40,000 |
| **Profit (Sales − Total cost)** | **84,000** | **56,000** | **1,40,000** |

**Cross-check:** Total joint cost apportioned = 48,000 + 52,000 = ₹1,00,000, equal to the joint cost incurred. Total profit = 2,40,000 − 1,00,000 = ₹1,40,000. Reconciled.

**Answer:** Joint cost apportioned — **A = ₹48,000; B = ₹52,000**; profit — **A = ₹84,000; B = ₹56,000** (total profit ₹1,40,000).

*(Full-marks tip: keep the two stages separate — variable cost strictly on units, fixed cost strictly on contribution-margin ratio. Apportioning the whole ₹1,00,000 on one base is the error that costs most of the marks.)*

### Q60. Ch: Joint Products & By-Products — Further Processing Decision (Marks: 6) [Application]
**Question:** A joint product 'X' can be sold at the split-off point for ₹40 per unit; 5,000 units are available. **WN-1: Revenue if sold at split-off.** 5,000 units × ₹40 = ₹2,00,000.

**WN-2: Revenue if processed further.** 4,800 units × ₹55 = ₹2,64,000.

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Sales value after further processing (4,800 × ₹55) | 2,64,000 |
| Less: Sales value at split-off (5,000 × ₹40) | 2,00,000 |
| **Incremental revenue** | **64,000** |
| Less: Incremental (further) processing cost | 60,000 |
| **Net advantage of processing further** | **4,000** |

**Cross-check (total-approach):** Net realisation if processed = 2,64,000 − 60,000 = ₹2,04,000, against ₹2,00,000 if sold at split-off — a gain of ₹4,000, confirming the incremental result.

### Q61. Ch: Joint Products & By-Products — By-Product Accounting (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain briefly the methods commonly used for accounting for **by-products** in cost accounts.

**Answer:**
By-products, being of minor value, are not usually charged with a share of the joint cost; instead their **realisable value is set off against the cost of the main product**. The common methods are grouped under two heads.

**(A) Non-cost or sales value methods** — used where by-products are of very small value:
1. **Other income method:** the sales value of the by-product is simply credited to the **Profit and Loss Account** as miscellaneous income; no adjustment is made in the process account.
2. **Total sales less total cost method:** by-product sales are treated as a deduction from total cost of production (or added to sales) without a separate valuation.

**(B) Cost methods** — used where by-products are of appreciable value:
3. **Replacement/opportunity cost method:** where the by-product is used internally, it is valued at the price at which it would otherwise have to be purchased, and the process account is credited accordingly.
4. **Standard cost method:** the by-product is credited to the process at a pre-determined standard cost.
5. **Net realisable value (reverse cost) method:** from the final sale value of the by-product are deducted the estimated further processing cost, selling and distribution expenses and the desired profit margin; the resulting **net realisable value is credited to the main product's process account**, thereby reducing the cost of the main product.

**Conclusion:** The choice depends on the by-product's value — trivial by-products are credited as other income, while significant by-products are valued (usually by the net realisable value method) and set off against the main product cost.

*(Full-marks tip: naming both the "non-cost" group and the "cost" group with at least the net-realisable-value method explained fully secures the marks; listing only one method under-scores.)*

### Q62. Ch: Joint Products & By-Products — By-Product (Reverse Cost Method) (Marks: 6) [Problem]

### Q64. Ch: Joint Products & By-Products — Bases of Apportionment (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Compare the **physical unit method** and the **market (sales) value at split-off method** of apportioning joint costs. State the circumstances in which each is appropriate and the main limitation of each.

**Answer:**
Both are methods of apportioning the **joint (pre-separation) cost** among joint products; they differ in the base used.

Under the **physical unit method**, joint cost is apportioned in the ratio of the **physical measure of output** (weight, volume, number of units) at the split-off point. Under the **market value at split-off method**, joint cost is apportioned in the ratio of the **sales value of each product at the point of separation** (units × selling price at split-off).

| Basis | Physical Unit Method | Market Value at Split-off Method |
|---|---|---|
| Apportionment base | Physical quantity of output | Sales value at split-off |
| Underlying principle | Each unit bears equal cost | Cost borne in proportion to revenue-earning ability ("benefit-received") |
| Effect on product profitability | Products with low selling price may show a loss and high-priced ones a large profit | Yields a broadly uniform gross-margin percentage across products |
| Appropriate when | Products are measured in a common unit and have similar values | Products differ widely in value and a common physical unit may not exist |
| Main limitation | Ignores differences in value; may make a valuable product look artificially profitable and a cheap one loss-making | Requires a reliable market price at split-off, which may not be available if products are not saleable at that stage |

**Conclusion:** The physical unit method suits products of comparable value expressed in a common unit, whereas the market value method — which spreads cost according to revenue-earning capacity — is generally regarded as more equitable where product values differ significantly, provided split-off market prices are available.

*(Full-marks tip: the examiner rewards the "benefit-received / uniform gross margin" rationale of the market-value method and the "ignores value" limitation of the physical method. A table contrasting the two on base, appropriateness and limitation scores best.)*

### Q67. Ch: Process & Operation Costing — Stock Valuation under Inter-Process Profit (Marks: 5) [Application]
**Question:** At the year-end, a manufacturer using inter-process profits has the following stocks lying in the factory. Explain how each should be valued in the Balance Sheet, compute the total **provision for unrealised profit**, and state the amount at which stocks will appear in the Balance Sheet.

| Stock | Total value (transfer price) (₹) | Profit element included (₹) |
|---|---|---|
| Process I closing stock | 20,000 | Nil |
| Process II closing stock | 44,000 | 6,000 |
| Finished stock | 60,000 | 12,000 |

**Solution:**
Stock lying **within the factory** carries a profit that has **not yet been realised** through a sale to an outside party. In the Balance Sheet, stock must be shown at **cost to the concern**; therefore the profit element loaded on each stock has to be eliminated by creating a **Provision for Unrealised Profit (Stock Reserve)** through a debit to the Profit and Loss Account.

**WN-1: Provision for unrealised profit (= profit element in each stock).**

| Stock | Total value (₹) | Profit element (₹) | Cost element (₹) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Process I closing stock | 20,000 | Nil | 20,000 |
| Process II closing stock | 44,000 | 6,000 | 38,000 |
| Finished stock | 60,000 | 12,000 | 48,000 |
| **Total** | **1,24,000** | **18,000** | **1,06,000** |

**Provision for unrealised profit = ₹6,000 + ₹12,000 = ₹18,000.**

**Statement Showing Balance Sheet Value of Stocks**

| Particulars | Amount (₹) |
|---|---|
| Total value of stocks at transfer price | 1,24,000 |
| Less: Provision for unrealised profit (Stock Reserve) | 18,000 |
| **Value of stocks shown in the Balance Sheet (at cost)** | **1,06,000** |

**Explanation:** Process I stock carries no profit (its input is all at cost), so it needs no provision and appears at ₹20,000. Process II and finished stock carry an unrealised profit of ₹6,000 and ₹12,000 respectively, which is provided for. In the Profit and Loss Account, the ₹18,000 provision is debited (as an increase in stock reserve for the year), reducing the reported profit so that only profit realised on outside sales is recognised.

**Answer:** Provision for unrealised profit = **₹18,000**; stocks appear in the Balance Sheet at **₹1,06,000** (cost).

*(Full-marks tip: state clearly that stock is shown at cost and the profit element is removed via a stock reserve debited to P&L, and that Process I stock (all cost) needs no provision. The examiner penalises showing the ₹1,24,000 gross figure in the Balance Sheet.)*

**WN-2: Revised budgeted hours (for actual days)** = 24,000 × (23/24) = 23,000 hrs → revised budget FOH = 23 × 40,000 = ₹ 9,20,000

**WN-3: Variances**
- **FOH Cost (Total) Variance** = Absorbed − Actual = 9,20,000 − 9,85,000 = **₹ 65,000 (A)**

- **Volume Variance** = Absorbed − Budgeted = 9,20,000 − 9,60,000 = **₹ 40,000 (A)**

- **Capacity Variance** = SR × (AH − Revised Budgeted Hrs) = 40 × (22,500 − 23,000) = 40 × (−500) = **₹ 20,000 (A)**

### Q73. Ch: Marginal Costing — CVP with Target Profit & Margin of Safety (Marks: 10) [Problem]
**Question:** Konark Products manufactures a single product. The following relates to the current year:

| Particulars | Amount |
|---|---|
| Selling price per unit | ₹ 250 |
| Variable cost per unit | ₹ 150 |
| Fixed costs per annum | ₹ 24,00,000 |

You are required to compute, for the current structure: (i) P/V ratio, (ii) break-even point (units and ₹), (iii) margin of safety at current sales (units, ₹ and %), (iv) units and sales value to earn the target profit; and (v) advise whether the price-reduction plan should be adopted.

**WN-1: Contribution & P/V ratio (current)**

- P/V ratio = 100 ÷ 250 = **40%**

**(ii) Break-even point**
- BEP (units) = Fixed cost ÷ Contribution per unit = 24,00,000 ÷ 100 = **24,000 units**
- BEP (₹) = 24,00,000 ÷ 0.40 = **₹ 60,00,000**

**(iii) Margin of Safety (at 30,000 units)**
- Current sales value = 30,000 × 250 = ₹ 75,00,000
- MOS (units) = 30,000 − 24,000 = **6,000 units**
- MOS (₹) = 75,00,000 − 60,00,000 = **₹ 15,00,000**
- MOS (%) = 6,000 ÷ 30,000 = **20%**
- (Cross-check: current profit = MOS × contribution/unit = 6,000 × 100 = ₹ 6,00,000 = 30,000×100 − 24,00,000 ✓)

**(iv) Target-profit level (target ₹ 12,00,000)**
- Required units = (Fixed cost + Target profit) ÷ Contribution/unit = (24,00,000 + 12,00,000) ÷ 100 = 36,00,000 ÷ 100 = **36,000 units**
- Required sales value = 36,00,000 ÷ 0.40 = **₹ 90,00,000** (= 36,000 × 250 ✓)

| Particulars | Existing | Proposed |
|---|---|---|
| Selling price/unit | ₹ 250 | 250 × 0.92 = ₹ 230 |
| Variable cost/unit | ₹ 150 | ₹ 150 |
| Contribution/unit | ₹ 100 | ₹ 80 |
| Volume (units) | 30,000 | 30,000 × 1.25 = 37,500 |
| Total contribution | ₹ 30,00,000 | 37,500 × 80 = ₹ 30,00,000 |

| **Profit** | **₹ 6,00,000** | **₹ 3,00,000** |

### Q74. Ch: Marginal Costing — Accept/Reject Export Order at Below-Normal Price (Relevant Cost) (Marks: 10) [Application]
**Question:** Vindhya Cables Ltd. makes a product with an annual normal capacity of 1,20,000 units and is currently operating at 80% capacity, selling in the home market at ₹ 400 per unit. Its cost structure at present activity is:

| Cost element | ₹ per unit |
|---|---|
| Direct material | 120 |
| Direct labour | 80 |
| Variable factory overhead | 40 |
| Fixed factory overhead | 60 |
| Variable selling overhead (home) | 20 |
| Fixed selling & admin overhead | 30 |
| **Total cost** | **350** |

An overseas buyer offers to purchase **30,000 units at ₹ 290 per unit**, FOB. Accepting the order will need: (a) special export packing costing ₹ 15 per unit in place of the home variable selling overhead which will not be incurred; (b) a one-time export agent's fee (fixed) of ₹ 1,80,000; and (c) fixed factory overhead would rise by ₹ 2,40,000 owing to an additional supervisory shift. - Current output = 80% × 1,20,000 = 96,000 units; spare = 1,20,000 − 96,000 = 24,000 units
- Order = 30,000 units → exceeds spare by 6,000 units. Since home sales are 96,000 and capacity is fixed at 1,20,000, only 24,000 units can be made from spare capacity; the extra 6,000 units would displace home sales UNLESS extra capacity is arranged. As no extra capacity beyond 1,20,000 is available, 6,000 units of home sales (contribution) would be lost. (We assume the incremental supervisory shift given only raises fixed cost, not capacity.)

**WN-2: Relevant variable cost per export unit**
- Direct material 120 + Direct labour 80 + Variable factory OH 40 = ₹ 240
- Add export packing 15 (home variable selling ₹ 20 not incurred) = ₹ 255 relevant variable cost per export unit
- Fixed factory & fixed S&A overheads per unit are NOT relevant (already incurred), except the specific incremental fixed costs in (b) and (c).

**WN-3: Home contribution lost on displaced 6,000 units**
- Home contribution/unit = 400 − (120+80+40+20) = 400 − 260 = ₹ 140
- Lost contribution = 6,000 × 140 = ₹ 8,40,000

**WN-4: Incremental statement of accepting the order**

| Particulars | ₹ |
|---|---|
| Export revenue (30,000 × 290) | 87,00,000 |
| Less: Relevant variable cost (30,000 × 255) | (76,50,000) |
| Contribution from export order | 10,50,000 |
| Less: Contribution lost on displaced home sales (WN-3) | (8,40,000) |
| Less: Export agent's fee (specific fixed) | (1,80,000) |
| Less: Incremental fixed factory OH (supervisory shift) | (2,40,000) |
| **Net gain / (loss) from the order** | **(2,10,000)** |

**Decision:** The order yields a net loss of ₹ 2,10,000 and should **NOT be accepted** in these terms.

**WN-5: Minimum (break-even) price**
- The order breaks even when net gain = 0.
- Required contribution = displaced contribution + specific fixed = 8,40,000 + 1,80,000 + 2,40,000 = ₹ 12,60,000
- Required contribution per export unit = 12,60,000 ÷ 30,000 = ₹ 42
- Minimum price = relevant variable cost + required contribution = 255 + 42 = **₹ 297 per unit**

**Answer:** Accepting at ₹ 290 gives a net LOSS of ₹ 2,10,000 — reject. The order breaks even at ₹ 297 per unit; any price above ₹ 297 (with these fixed costs and displacement) makes it worthwhile.

*(Full-marks tip: relevant costing rewards separating incurred fixed cost (irrelevant) from incremental specific fixed cost (relevant) and, crucially, charging the opportunity cost of displaced home sales. Candidates who ignore the 6,000-unit displacement wrongly accept the order.)*

### Q75. Ch: Marginal Costing — Product Mix under a Scarce Resource (Limiting Factor) (Marks: 10) [Problem]
**Question:** Deccan Polymers makes three products — X, Y and Z — using a single grade of skilled labour which is limited to 60,000 hours per month. Market demand and per-unit data are given below. Fixed costs are ₹ 18,00,000 per month. Determine the profit-maximising product mix, the maximum monthly profit, and advise whether buying additional labour hours at ₹ 30 per hour above the normal ₹ 25 rate (for up to 10,000 extra hours) is worthwhile.

| Product | Units (demand) | Hrs/unit | Hrs used | Cumulative hrs |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| X (I) | 8,000 | 6 | 48,000 | 48,000 |
| Y (II) | balance | 10 | 12,000 | 60,000 |
| Z (III) | 0 | 12 | 0 | 60,000 |

- After X (48,000 hrs), remaining = 12,000 hrs → Y = 12,000 ÷ 10 = 1,200 units (demand 6,000, so constrained). Z gets nil.

| Product | Units | Contribution/unit ₹ | Total ₹ |
|---|---|---|---|
| X | 8,000 | 160 | 12,80,000 |
| Y | 1,200 | 210 | 2,52,000 |
| Z | 0 | 240 | 0 |

### Q88. Ch: Budgets & Budgetary Control — Fixed Budget vs Flexible Budget (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between a **Fixed Budget** and a **Flexible Budget**.

**Answer:** A **Fixed (or Static) Budget** is a budget which is designed to remain unchanged irrespective of the level of activity actually attained; it is drawn up for a single, assumed level of output and is not adjusted when actual volume differs. A **Flexible Budget** is a budget which, by recognising the difference in behaviour between fixed and variable costs, is designed to change in accordance with the level of activity actually attained, thereby giving budgeted figures for any volume of output.

| Basis | Fixed Budget | Flexible Budget |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Prepared for one fixed level of activity | Prepared for a range of activity levels |
| Cost classification | Costs are not segregated by behaviour | Costs are segregated into fixed, variable and semi-variable |
| Effect of volume change | Does not change with change in output | Recasts itself according to the actual output |
| Basis of comparison | Actual results compared with the single budget — often meaningless if volume differs | Actual results compared with a budget of the same volume — meaningful |
| Suitability | Where output can be accurately forecast and is stable | Where output fluctuates due to seasonal / market factors |
| Cost ascertainment | Difficult to ascertain cost correctly at other levels | Cost can be ascertained at any level of activity |

**Conclusion:** A flexible budget is a more realistic and useful tool for cost control because it provides a valid yardstick for comparison at the level of activity actually achieved, whereas a fixed budget serves only when actual activity coincides with the budgeted level.

*(Full-marks tip: Give at least four points of distinction in a table and open each budget with a proper definition. Merely writing "one changes and the other does not" without linking it to cost behaviour and to the validity of comparison loses marks.)*

### Q90. Ch: Budgets & Budgetary Control — Zero-Based Budgeting (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the concept of **Zero-Based Budgeting (ZBB)** and state its main **advantages**.

**Answer:** **Zero-Based Budgeting** is a method of budgeting whereby all activities are re-evaluated each time a budget is prepared, starting from a "zero base" — that is, every item of expenditure has to be **freshly justified as if it were being incurred for the first time**, rather than being carried forward from the previous period's budget with an incremental adjustment. Under ZBB the manager of each "decision unit" prepares "decision packages" describing each activity, its cost and its benefit, and these packages are then **ranked in order of priority** so that scarce resources are allocated to the activities yielding the greatest benefit.

The main steps are: (i) identification of decision units, (ii) development of decision packages, (iii) ranking of the packages, and (iv) allocation of resources according to the ranking.

**Advantages:**
1. It provides a **systematic questioning** of every activity and eliminates wasteful and obsolete expenditure that survives under incremental budgeting.
2. It ensures that resources are allocated on the basis of **cost-benefit and priority**, thereby directing funds to the most productive uses.
3. It **links expenditure to activities and objectives**, improving goal congruence and management involvement.
4. It is particularly useful for **discretionary and support costs** (e.g., administration, R&D, marketing) where a clear output-input relationship is absent.

**Conclusion:** ZBB improves the efficiency of resource allocation by forcing a fresh justification of every rupee spent, though it is time-consuming and demands considerable managerial effort.

### Q92. Ch: Budgets & Budgetary Control — Principal Budget Factor and Budget Manual (Marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain what is meant by the **Principal Budget Factor (Key Factor)** and a **Budget Manual**.

**Answer:** The **Principal Budget Factor**, also called the **Key Factor** or **Limiting Factor**, is that factor which, at a given time or over a period, **limits the activities of an undertaking** and around which the whole budgeting process must be built. The extent of its influence must first be assessed because it governs the preparation of all other budgets — for example, if sales demand is the limiting factor then the sales budget is prepared first and the production, material, labour and cash budgets are drawn up in accordance with it. The key factor may be **sales demand, availability of raw material, plant capacity, labour, or finance (cash)**, and it is not necessarily permanent — once removed by management action (say, by adding a shift or arranging more finance) another factor may become limiting.

A **Budget Manual** is a document (or booklet) which **standardises and sets out in writing the responsibilities of the persons engaged in, and the procedures, forms and records relating to, the preparation and use of budgets**. It typically contains a description of the objectives and organisation of the budgeting system, the duties and responsibilities of executives (including the budget officer and budget committee), the timetable for budget preparation, the specimen forms and reports to be used, the accounts codes, and the procedure for revising budgets.

**Conclusion:** Identifying the principal budget factor gives the budgeting process its correct starting point, while the budget manual gives it discipline, uniformity and clear allocation of responsibility.

*(Full-marks tip: Give a correct definition of the key factor **and** a worked example of the sequence it dictates (e.g., sales → production → material), plus at least three typical contents of the budget manual. Simply naming the two terms without explaining their role in the budgeting process loses marks.)*

### Q95. Ch: Service Costing — Features of Service Costing and Composite Cost Units (Marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** What is **Service (Operating) Costing**? State its distinctive features and explain the concept of a **composite cost unit**, giving suitable examples.

**Answer:** **Service Costing** (also called **Operating Costing**) is that method of costing which is used to ascertain the **cost of rendering a service** rather than manufacturing a product. It is applied by undertakings that provide services to the public (transport, electricity, hospitals, hotels, canteens) or by internal service departments that render services within an organisation (a power house, a captive canteen, an internal transport pool).

**Distinctive features of service costing:**
1. The **cost unit is a service**, not a tangible product, and is frequently a **composite unit** rather than a simple one.
2. The **investment in fixed assets is generally large** while the working-capital requirement is comparatively small.
3. Costs are usually classified into **fixed (standing) charges and variable (running/operating) charges**, and sometimes maintenance charges, to aid control.
4. The **total cost is divided by the number of service units** to obtain the cost per unit of service.

A **composite cost unit** is a cost unit obtained by **combining two parameters** so that it reflects both the quantum of service and the distance/period over which it is rendered. It is used where a single parameter would not fairly express the service.

| Undertaking | Simple unit | **Composite cost unit** |
|---|---|---|
| Goods transport | per tonne / per km | **tonne-kilometre** |
| Passenger transport | per passenger | **passenger-kilometre** |
| Hospital | per patient | **patient-day** |
| Hotel | per room | **room-day** |
| Electricity | — | **kilowatt-hour (kWh)** |

**Conclusion:** Service costing ascertains cost per unit of service by dividing total operating cost by the number of service units, and the choice of an appropriate (often composite) cost unit is central to producing a meaningful cost.

*(Full-marks tip: Define operating costing, give at least three genuine features, and present the composite cost units in a table with examples. Merely listing services without explaining why a composite (two-factor) unit is needed loses marks.)*
