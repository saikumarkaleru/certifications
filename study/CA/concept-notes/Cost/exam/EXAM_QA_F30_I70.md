# Cost & Management Accounting — Exam Q&A (30 Foundation + 70 Inter)

*100 questions in ICAI answer-writing pattern. Q1–Q30 are foundation-level (fundamentals across all chapters); Q31–Q100 are CA-Inter exam standard, chapter-wise. Each answer is written to score full marks, with a note on how the examiner awards marks. **Present problems with numbered Working Notes and proper formats; state the formula before you use it — examiners give step/format marks.***

## PART A — FOUNDATION LEVEL (Q1–Q30)

### Q1. Ch.1 Introduction — Cost, Costing, Cost Accounting, Cost Accountancy (marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between the terms **Cost, Costing, Cost Accounting and Cost Accountancy**.
**Model Answer:**
- **Cost** — the amount of expenditure (actual or notional) incurred on, or attributable to, a given thing (product, service or activity). It is measured in monetary terms.
- **Costing** — the *technique and process* of ascertaining costs. It is the set of rules and methods used to determine the cost of a product or service.
- **Cost Accounting** — the *formal system of accounting* for costs: recording, classifying, allocating and reporting costs, and ascertaining the profitability of activities. It begins where financial accounting ends.
- **Cost Accountancy** — the *broadest* term. It is the application of costing and cost-accounting principles, methods and techniques to the science, art and practice of cost control and the ascertainment of profitability, including presentation of information for managerial decision-making. It thus **includes** costing, cost accounting, budgetary control and cost audit.
**Conclusion:** Cost is the figure; costing is the technique; cost accounting is the system; cost accountancy is the whole discipline.
**> Full-marks tip:** Give **four separate headings** — examiners mark head-wise (1 mark each). A merged paragraph loses the distinction marks.

### Q2. Ch.1 — Cost Accounting vs Financial Accounting (marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** State any five points of distinction between **Cost Accounting** and **Financial Accounting**.
**Model Answer:**
| Basis | Financial Accounting | Cost Accounting |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Records transactions, shows overall profit & financial position | Ascertains cost per unit, controls cost, aids decisions |
| Users | External (shareholders, regulators, tax) | Internal (management) |
| Statutory | Mandatory (Companies Act) | Largely voluntary (cost records/audit for specified industries) |
| Nature of data | Historical, monetary only | Historical **and** estimated; monetary **and** non-monetary (units, hours) |
| Analysis | For the business as a whole | Product/process/department-wise |
| Stock valuation | At cost or NRV, whichever lower | At cost |
**> Full-marks tip:** A **table** with clear "basis" wins full marks fast; the examiner ticks each valid point. Aim for the exact number asked (five).

### Q3. Ch.1 — Cost Accounting vs Management Accounting (marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** How does **Management Accounting** differ from **Cost Accounting**?
**Model Answer:**
- **Scope:** Cost accounting is concerned mainly with cost ascertainment and control; management accounting is **wider** — it uses cost data plus financial data, statistics and other information for planning, decision-making and control.
- **Objective:** Cost accounting records and controls costs; management accounting **provides information to management** for policy formulation.
- **Data used:** Cost accounting uses cost data; management accounting draws on cost accounting, financial accounting, economics and statistics.
- **Principles:** Cost accounting follows definite costing principles; management accounting has **no rigid rules** — it is tailored to management's needs.
**Conclusion:** Cost accounting is a **part of** the wider field of management accounting.
**> Full-marks tip:** End with the "cost accounting is a subset of management accounting" line — it shows conceptual clarity.

### Q3b. Ch.1 — Cost Object, Cost Unit, Cost Centre (marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Define **Cost Object, Cost Unit and Cost Centre** with one example each.
**Model Answer:**
- **Cost Object** — anything for which a separate measurement of cost is desired (e.g., a product, service, project, customer). *Example:* a car in an automobile plant.
- **Cost Unit** — a **unit of product or service** in relation to which costs are ascertained or expressed. *Example:* per tonne (cement), per 1,000 bricks, per patient-day (hospital), per passenger-km (transport).
- **Cost Centre** — a **location, person, item of equipment or a group** for which costs are ascertained and used for cost control. Types: personal/impersonal; production/service. *Example:* the machining department.
**> Full-marks tip:** Always attach an **example** — examiners often allot ½–1 mark specifically for a correct cost unit example.

### Q4. Ch.1 — Classification of Costs (marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain the classification of costs on the basis of **(a) nature/element, (b) function, and (c) behaviour**.
**Model Answer:**
**(a) By nature/element:** Material, Labour and Expenses — each further split into **Direct** (traceable) and **Indirect** (not traceable; = overheads).
**(b) By function:** Production (manufacturing), Administration, Selling, Distribution and Research & Development costs.
**(c) By behaviour (variability):**
- **Fixed cost** — remains constant in total within a relevant range irrespective of output (e.g., rent). Per-unit fixed cost falls as output rises.
- **Variable cost** — varies in total in direct proportion to output (e.g., direct material); per-unit remains constant.
- **Semi-variable cost** — has both a fixed and a variable component (e.g., telephone, electricity).
**> Full-marks tip:** For behaviour, state **what stays constant — total or per unit** — for each type; that nuance earns the mark.

### Q5. Ch.1 — Elements of Cost / Components of Total Cost (marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the build-up of **Total Cost** from Prime Cost.
**Model Answer:**
- **Prime Cost** = Direct Material + Direct Labour + Direct Expenses.
- **Works/Factory Cost** = Prime Cost + Factory (works) overheads.
- **Cost of Production** = Works Cost + Administration overheads (relating to production).
- **Cost of Goods Sold** = Cost of Production ± Opening/Closing finished-goods stock.
- **Cost of Sales / Total Cost** = Cost of Goods Sold + Selling & Distribution overheads.
- **Sales** = Cost of Sales + Profit.
**> Full-marks tip:** Learn this **ladder** cold — it is the skeleton of every cost-sheet problem; write it as a vertical build-up, not prose.

### Q6. Ch.1 — Fixed, Variable, Semi-variable (marks: 3) [Theory]
**Question:** Give the behaviour of **total cost and per-unit cost** for fixed and variable costs as output increases.
**Model Answer:**
| Cost | Total cost | Per-unit cost |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed | Constant | **Decreases** |
| Variable | **Increases** proportionately | Constant |
| Semi-variable | Increases (less than proportionately) | Decreases |
**> Full-marks tip:** This 2×2 idea (total vs per-unit) is a favourite one-mark MCQ/short-note — memorise the table.

### Q7. Ch.1 — Direct vs Indirect Cost (marks: 3) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish **Direct** and **Indirect** costs with examples.
**Model Answer:**
- **Direct cost** — conveniently and wholly **traceable** to a cost object/unit (direct material, direct wages, direct expenses like hire of a special tool). Forms **Prime Cost**.
- **Indirect cost** — **not conveniently traceable** to a single cost unit; incurred for several cost objects (factory rent, supervisor's salary, indirect material). Together they are **overheads**.
**> Full-marks tip:** Note the link: **direct costs → prime cost; indirect costs → overheads.**

### Q8. Ch.14 — Relevant Cost, Sunk Cost, Opportunity Cost (marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain **Sunk cost, Opportunity cost and Relevant cost** for decision-making.
**Model Answer:**
- **Sunk cost** — a cost already incurred that **cannot be changed** by any present or future decision; it is **irrelevant** to decisions (e.g., cost of an already-purchased machine).
- **Opportunity cost** — the value of the **benefit foregone** by choosing one alternative over the next-best one (e.g., rent foregone by using own premises). It is a **relevant** cost though not recorded in books.
- **Relevant cost** — a **future cost that differs between alternatives**; only relevant costs (and revenues) should enter a decision.
**> Full-marks tip:** State explicitly "**sunk = irrelevant**, **opportunity = relevant**" — examiners test whether you can classify them, not just define.

### Q9. Ch.1 — Cost Control vs Cost Reduction (marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish between **Cost Control** and **Cost Reduction**.
**Model Answer:**
- **Cost Control** — keeping costs **within** predetermined targets/standards; it is a **preventive** exercise, achieved through standards and budgets. It does **not** question the standard itself.
- **Cost Reduction** — a **real and permanent reduction** in unit cost **without** impairing quality or suitability; it **challenges the standards** and seeks better methods. It is a **corrective, continuous** process.
| Basis | Cost Control | Cost Reduction |
|---|---|---|
| Aim | Achieve the target | Reduce the target itself |
| Nature | Preventive | Corrective, continuous |
| Standard | Accepted as such | Challenged for improvement |
**> Full-marks tip:** The line "cost control accepts standards, cost reduction challenges them" is the marks-earning distinction.

### Q10. Ch.2 Material — EOQ Concept (marks: 4) [Theory + Formula]
**Question:** What is **Economic Order Quantity (EOQ)**? State its formula and the assumption behind it.
**Model Answer:**
**EOQ** is the order size that **minimises the total of ordering cost and carrying cost** of inventory.
**Formula:** EOQ = √(2 × A × O / C), where A = annual demand (units), O = ordering cost per order, C = carrying cost per unit per annum.
At EOQ, **total ordering cost = total carrying cost**. Assumptions: demand and lead time are certain and constant; price is constant; no stock-outs.
**> Full-marks tip:** Always write the **formula with each symbol defined** before substituting; and state the equilibrium "ordering cost = carrying cost" — it earns a concept mark.

### Q11. Ch.2 — Stock Levels (marks: 5) [Theory + Formula]
**Question:** State the formulae for **Re-order Level, Minimum Level, Maximum Level and Danger Level**.
**Model Answer:**
- **Re-order Level (ROL)** = Maximum usage × Maximum lead time. (Alt.: Minimum level + (Avg usage × Avg lead time).)
- **Minimum Level** = ROL − (Average usage × Average lead time).
- **Maximum Level** = ROL + ROQ − (Minimum usage × Minimum lead time). *(ROQ = re-order quantity/EOQ.)*
- **Average Level** = (Minimum + Maximum) ÷ 2, or Minimum + ½ ROQ.
- **Danger Level** = Average usage × Lead time for emergency purchases.
**> Full-marks tip:** Note the pattern — **max×max for ROL and min×min for maximum level**. Mixing the usages up is the commonest error.

### Q12. Ch.2 — Pricing of Material Issues (marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain **FIFO, LIFO and Weighted Average** methods of pricing material issues, and the effect of each in times of **rising prices**.
**Model Answer:**
- **FIFO (First-In-First-Out)** — issues priced at the **oldest** cost; closing stock at latest (higher) cost. In rising prices → **lower issue cost, higher profit, higher closing stock**.
- **LIFO (Last-In-First-Out)** — issues at the **latest** cost; closing stock at old cost. In rising prices → **higher issue cost, lower profit**. (Not permitted under AS 2 for financial accounts.)
- **Weighted Average** — issues at the weighted-average cost of stock in hand, recomputed after each receipt. Smooths price fluctuations.
**> Full-marks tip:** State the **rising-price effect** on profit/closing stock — that is the usual sting in the question.

### Q13. Ch.3 Labour — Time Rate vs Piece Rate (marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish **Time Rate** and **Piece Rate** systems of wage payment; give one merit of each.
**Model Answer:**
- **Time Rate** — wages = hours worked × rate per hour; payment based on **time**, not output. *Merit:* quality is maintained; suits jobs where output is not measurable. *Demerit:* no incentive for speed.
- **Piece Rate** — wages = units produced × rate per unit; payment based on **output**. *Merit:* directly incentivises productivity. *Demerit:* quality may suffer; guaranteed minimum often needed.
**> Full-marks tip:** Add the word "**guaranteed day wage**" when discussing piece rate — examiners look for awareness that a minimum is usually assured.

### Q14. Ch.3 — Labour Turnover (marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Define **Labour Turnover** and state its methods of measurement.
**Model Answer:**
**Labour turnover** is the rate of change in the composition of the workforce during a period. Methods:
- **Separation method** = (Separations ÷ Average number of workers) × 100.
- **Replacement method** = (Replacements ÷ Average number of workers) × 100.
- **Flux method** = (Separations + Accessions/Replacements) ÷ Average number × 100.
**Average number of workers** = (Opening + Closing) ÷ 2.
**> Full-marks tip:** Write **all three formulae**; a numerical usually asks you to compute and compare them.

### Q15. Ch.3 — Idle Time & Overtime (marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** What is **Idle Time** and **Overtime**? How is the cost of each treated?
**Model Answer:**
- **Idle time** — time for which wages are paid but no production is obtained. **Normal idle time** (tea breaks, setting up) → treated as part of cost (loaded on to labour rate). **Abnormal idle time** (strikes, power failure) → charged to **Costing P&L**, not to product.
- **Overtime** — hours beyond normal working; paid at a premium. **Overtime premium** treatment: if due to customer's urgent order → charge to that job; if due to general pressure → treat as overhead; if abnormal → to Costing P&L.
**> Full-marks tip:** For both, the mark lies in **normal → product cost; abnormal → Costing P&L**.

### Q16. Ch.4 Overheads — Allocation, Apportionment, Absorption (marks: 5) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish **Allocation, Apportionment and Absorption** of overheads.
**Model Answer:**
- **Allocation** — charging the **whole** of an overhead item **directly** to a cost centre to which it wholly relates (e.g., a department's own supervisor salary).
- **Apportionment** — **distributing** a common overhead over several cost centres on an **equitable basis** (e.g., factory rent apportioned on floor area).
- **Absorption** — charging the overheads of a cost centre **on to the cost units** passing through it, using an **absorption (recovery) rate**.
**Sequence:** Allocation & apportionment (primary distribution) → re-apportionment of service centres (secondary distribution) → **absorption** into products.
**> Full-marks tip:** Give the **basis/example** for apportionment and state the **sequence** — both fetch marks.

### Q17. Ch.4 — Overhead Absorption Rate Methods (marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** List the methods of **absorbing production overheads** and state when the machine-hour rate is preferred.
**Model Answer:** Methods: **% of direct material, % of direct wages, % of prime cost, direct labour-hour rate, machine-hour rate, and rate per unit.**
The **machine-hour rate** is preferred where **production is largely machine-based** (capital-intensive), so overheads relate more to machine time than to labour.
**> Full-marks tip:** Machine-intensive → machine-hour rate; labour-intensive → labour-hour rate. State the reason (overheads follow the dominant resource).

### Q18. Ch.4 — Over/Under Absorption (marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** What is **over-absorption** and **under-absorption** of overheads and how is it disposed of?
**Model Answer:**
- **Under-absorption** — overheads absorbed (recovered) are **less** than actual overheads.
- **Over-absorption** — absorbed overheads **exceed** actual.
**Disposal:** (i) if due to **normal** reasons and amount small → transfer to **Costing P&L**; (ii) if due to **abnormal** reasons → to Costing P&L; (iii) if large and due to **wrong estimation** → adjust by a **supplementary rate** spread over cost of sales, finished goods and WIP.
**> Full-marks tip:** The examiner wants the **three disposal routes**; "supplementary rate" for large amounts is the key advanced point.

### Q19. Ch.5 — Activity-Based Costing (marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** What is **Activity-Based Costing (ABC)** and how does it differ from traditional absorption costing?
**Model Answer:**
**ABC** assigns overheads to products based on the **activities** that drive them, using **cost drivers**, rather than a single volume-based rate. Steps: identify activities → pool costs by activity → identify a **cost driver** for each → compute a **cost-driver rate** → absorb into products by driver usage.
**Difference:** traditional costing uses **volume-based** rates (labour/machine hours) and can distort costs when overheads are driven by complexity, not volume; ABC gives **more accurate** product costs, especially with diverse products.
**> Full-marks tip:** The words **"cost driver"** and **"activity cost pool"** must appear — they are the core of ABC.

### Q20. Ch.6 — Cost Sheet (marks: 3) [Theory]
**Question:** What is a **Cost Sheet** and what are its main purposes?
**Model Answer:** A **cost sheet** is a statement showing the **build-up of total cost** (prime cost → works cost → cost of production → cost of sales) and cost per unit for a period. Purposes: ascertain total and per-unit cost, **fix selling prices**, compare costs over periods, and aid cost control and quotations.
**> Full-marks tip:** Mention **cost per unit** and **price fixation** — those are the examiner's expected purposes.

### Q21. Ch.7 — Integrated vs Non-Integrated Accounts (marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish **Integrated** and **Non-Integrated** cost accounting systems.
**Model Answer:**
- **Non-integrated (cost ledger) system** — cost accounts are kept **separately** from financial accounts; a **Cost Ledger Control Account** completes double entry; results must be **reconciled** with financial accounts.
- **Integrated (integral) system** — **one set** of accounts serves both cost and financial purposes; no separate reconciliation is needed as there is a **single profit figure**.
**> Full-marks tip:** Key link — **non-integrated needs reconciliation; integrated avoids it.**

### Q22. Ch.7 — Reasons for Difference in Profit (Reconciliation) (marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State the reasons why profit shown by cost accounts differs from that shown by financial accounts.
**Model Answer:** (i) **Items in financial accounts only** — pure financial incomes (interest, dividend received, profit on sale of assets) and expenses (interest paid, loss on sale, donations, income tax); (ii) **Items in cost accounts only** — notional costs (notional rent, interest on capital); (iii) **Different valuation** of stock (cost uses one method, financial another); (iv) **Different treatment of overheads** (over/under absorption); (v) different depreciation methods.
**> Full-marks tip:** Group them as "financial-only", "cost-only", "different treatment" — reconciliation numericals are built on exactly these buckets.

### Q23. Ch.8 — Unit vs Batch Costing (marks: 3) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish **Unit (output) costing** and **Batch costing**; give an example of each.
**Model Answer:**
- **Unit/Output costing** — used where production is a **single, uniform product** in a continuous flow; cost per unit = total cost ÷ units (e.g., cement, bricks, steel).
- **Batch costing** — a **batch of identical items** is treated as one cost unit; cost per unit = batch cost ÷ units in batch (e.g., pharmaceuticals, biscuits, components). **Economic Batch Quantity** applies.
**> Full-marks tip:** Mention **EBQ** for batch costing — it parallels EOQ and is examinable.

### Q24. Ch.9 — Job vs Contract Costing (marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish **Job costing** and **Contract costing**.
**Model Answer:**
| Basis | Job Costing | Contract Costing |
|---|---|---|
| Size/duration | Small, short jobs | Large, long-duration |
| Location | Within the factory | At the **site** |
| Overheads | Significant factory overhead | Mostly **direct** (site) costs |
| Profit | On completion | **Notional/attributable** profit on incomplete contracts |
**> Full-marks tip:** The "**notional profit on incomplete contracts**" and "**site-based, mostly direct costs**" points distinguish contract costing.

### Q25. Ch.10 — Process Costing & Normal/Abnormal Loss (marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** What is **process costing**? Distinguish **normal loss, abnormal loss and abnormal gain**.
**Model Answer:** **Process costing** is used where production passes through **continuous processes** and output of one process is input to the next (e.g., chemicals, sugar). Cost is accumulated process-wise; **cost per unit = net process cost ÷ good units**.
- **Normal loss** — unavoidable, expected loss; its cost is **borne by good units**; scrap value credited to the process.
- **Abnormal loss** — loss **above** normal; valued at the **cost per good unit** and transferred to Costing P&L (not loaded on good units).
- **Abnormal gain** — actual loss **less** than normal; treated as the opposite of abnormal loss (debited to process, credited to Costing P&L).
**> Full-marks tip:** State that **abnormal loss/gain are valued at the same per-unit cost as good output** — this is the crux of the numerical.

### Q26. Ch.11 — Joint Products vs By-Products (marks: 3) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish **Joint Products** and **By-Products**.
**Model Answer:**
- **Joint products** — two or more products of **roughly equal importance** produced simultaneously from a common process (e.g., petrol and diesel from crude).
- **By-products** — products of **minor value** arising **incidentally** in the manufacture of the main product (e.g., molasses from sugar).
The distinction rests on **relative sales value / importance**.
**> Full-marks tip:** Mention the **basis is relative value**, and that joint costs are apportioned (sales value / physical unit method).

### Q27. Ch.12 — Service (Operating) Costing (marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** What is **Service/Operating costing** and what are **composite cost units**? Give examples.
**Model Answer:** **Operating (service) costing** ascertains the cost of rendering a **service** rather than making a product (transport, hospital, hotel, power). A **composite cost unit** combines two factors: e.g., **passenger-kilometre** (transport of passengers), **tonne-kilometre** (goods transport), **patient-day**, **kilowatt-hour**.
**> Full-marks tip:** Correctly naming the **composite cost unit** for the given service is worth a specific mark.

### Q28. Ch.13 — Standard Costing (marks: 3) [Theory]
**Question:** Define **Standard Cost** and **Standard Costing**, and state one advantage.
**Model Answer:** **Standard cost** is a **predetermined cost** computed in advance for a product/operation under specified conditions. **Standard costing** is the technique of **setting standards, comparing actuals with them, and analysing variances** to control cost. **Advantage:** it enables **management by exception** — attention is focused only on significant variances.
**> Full-marks tip:** Use the phrase "**management by exception**" — it is the textbook advantage examiners look for.

### Q29. Ch.14 — Marginal Costing: Contribution, P/V Ratio, BEP (marks: 5) [Theory + Formula]
**Question:** Define **Contribution, P/V ratio, Break-Even Point and Margin of Safety** with formulae.
**Model Answer:**
- **Contribution** = Sales − Variable cost = Fixed cost + Profit.
- **P/V ratio** = (Contribution ÷ Sales) × 100 = (Change in profit ÷ Change in sales) × 100.
- **Break-Even Point (units)** = Fixed cost ÷ Contribution per unit; **BEP (₹)** = Fixed cost ÷ P/V ratio.
- **Margin of Safety** = Actual sales − BEP sales = Profit ÷ P/V ratio.
**> Full-marks tip:** These four formulae are the backbone of marginal-costing numericals — reproduce them precisely; note **contribution = fixed cost + profit** as the linking identity.

### Q30. Ch.15 — Budget & Budgetary Control (marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Define a **Budget** and **Budgetary Control**; distinguish a **fixed** and a **flexible** budget.
**Model Answer:** A **budget** is a financial and/or quantitative plan for a defined future period, prepared and approved **before** the period. **Budgetary control** is the use of budgets to **control** operations — comparing actuals with budgets and acting on variances.
- **Fixed budget** — drawn for **one level of activity**; not adjusted if actual activity differs.
- **Flexible budget** — drawn to **flex with the actual level of activity** by separating fixed and variable costs; gives a meaningful comparison when output varies.
**> Full-marks tip:** State that a **flexible budget separates fixed and variable costs** — that is *why* it can flex, and the examiner rewards the reasoning.

## PART B — INTER LEVEL (Q31–Q100)

### Q31. Ch.2 Material — EOQ & Total Inventory Cost (marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** Annual demand 12,000 units; ordering cost ₹150 per order; purchase price ₹20 per unit; carrying cost 20% of unit price p.a. Compute **EOQ**, number of orders, and total inventory cost at EOQ.
**Model Answer:**
**WN-1 Carrying cost per unit** = 20% × ₹20 = **₹4 per unit p.a.**
**WN-2 EOQ** = √(2AO/C) = √(2 × 12,000 × 150 / 4) = √(36,00,000/... ) = √(9,00,000) = **3,000... ** → √(2×12,000×150/4) = √(36,00,000/4)=√9,00,000 = **948.7 ≈ 949 units**.
*(2×12,000×150 = 36,00,000; ÷4 = 9,00,000; √ = 948.68.)*
- **No. of orders** = 12,000 ÷ 949 ≈ **12.6 ≈ 13 orders**.
- **Ordering cost** = 12.65 × 150 ≈ ₹1,897; **Carrying cost** = (949/2) × 4 = ₹1,898 (equal, as expected at EOQ).
- **Total inventory (ordering + carrying) cost** ≈ ₹1,897 + ₹1,898 = **₹3,795**.
**> Full-marks tip:** Show that **ordering cost ≈ carrying cost** at EOQ — it proves your EOQ is right and earns the concept mark. Round units up.

### Q32. Ch.2 — Stock Levels (marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** Normal usage 100 units/week; min 50; max 150. Re-order quantity 600 units. Lead time 4 to 6 weeks. Compute **Re-order, Minimum, Maximum and Average** levels.
**Model Answer:**
**WN — usages & lead times:** Max usage 150, Min 50, Avg 100; Max LT 6, Min LT 4, Avg LT 5.
- **Re-order Level** = Max usage × Max LT = 150 × 6 = **900 units**.
- **Minimum Level** = ROL − (Avg usage × Avg LT) = 900 − (100 × 5) = **400 units**.
- **Maximum Level** = ROL + ROQ − (Min usage × Min LT) = 900 + 600 − (50 × 4) = 1,500 − 200 = **1,300 units**.
- **Average Level** = (Min + Max)/2 = (400 + 1,300)/2 = **850 units** (or Min + ½ROQ = 400 + 300 = 700 — state the formula used).
**> Full-marks tip:** Use **max×max for ROL** and **min×min inside Maximum level**; label each formula — mixing usages is the top error.

### Q33. Ch.2 — Store Ledger (FIFO) (marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** Opening 200 units @ ₹10. Receipts: 300 @ ₹12, 400 @ ₹13. Issues: 250, then 500. Value the **issues and closing stock under FIFO**.
**Model Answer:**
- **Issue 1 (250):** 200 @10 = 2,000 + 50 @12 = 600 → **₹2,600**.
- **Issue 2 (500):** 250 @12 = 3,000 + 250 @13 = 3,250 → **₹6,250**.
- **Closing stock** = 150 units @13 = **₹1,950**.
**Check:** total in = 2,000+3,600+5,200 = ₹10,800; out = 2,600+6,250 = 8,850; closing 1,950; 8,850+1,950 = **10,800 ✓**.
**> Full-marks tip:** Always run the **"receipts = issues + closing"** reconciliation — a tick from the examiner and it catches arithmetic slips.

### Q34. Ch.2 — Treatment of Normal & Abnormal Material Loss (marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** How are **normal and abnormal material losses/wastage** treated in cost accounts?
**Model Answer:** **Normal loss** (evaporation, shrinkage within tolerance) is **absorbed by good output** — the cost per good unit is inflated; any scrap realisation reduces material cost. **Abnormal loss** (theft, careless handling, breakage beyond normal) is **valued at cost and charged to the Costing P&L**, so that product cost is not distorted. **Waste** has no value; **scrap** has minor value credited to the process/job.
**> Full-marks tip:** Recurring theme — **normal → product; abnormal → Costing P&L.**

### Q35. Ch.3 Labour — Labour Turnover (marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** Opening workers 900, closing 1,100. During the year 40 workers left, 60 were discharged, and 150 were recruited (of whom 25 were to replace those who left/discharged, rest for expansion). Compute LTO by **Separation, Replacement and Flux** methods.
**Model Answer:**
**WN — Average workers** = (900 + 1,100)/2 = **1,000**. Separations = 40 + 60 = **100**; Replacements = **25**; Accessions (new for expansion) = 150 − 25 = 125.
- **Separation method** = 100/1,000 × 100 = **10%**.
- **Replacement method** = 25/1,000 × 100 = **2.5%**.
- **Flux method** = (Separations + Replacements + New recruits for expansion)/Avg × 100 = (100 + 150)/1,000 × 100 = **25%** *(flux = separations + accessions)*.
**> Full-marks tip:** Define each numerator clearly; the ICAI flux formula = **(separations + accessions) ÷ average × 100**. State the formula before plugging numbers.

### Q36. Ch.3 — Halsey & Rowan Incentive Plans (marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** Standard time 40 hours; actual time 30 hours; rate ₹50/hour. Compute earnings under **Halsey (50%)** and **Rowan** plans, and the effective hourly rate.
**Model Answer:**
**WN — Time saved** = 40 − 30 = **10 hours**. Time wages = 30 × 50 = ₹1,500.
- **Halsey:** Bonus = 50% × time saved × rate = 0.5 × 10 × 50 = ₹250. **Earnings = 1,500 + 250 = ₹1,750**; effective rate = 1,750/30 = **₹58.33/hr**.
- **Rowan:** Bonus = (Time saved / Std time) × time taken × rate = (10/40) × 30 × 50 = ₹375. **Earnings = 1,500 + 375 = ₹1,875**; effective rate = 1,875/30 = **₹62.50/hr**.
**> Full-marks tip:** Rowan bonus uses the **ratio (time saved / standard time)** as the cushion — state the formula. Rowan protects against over-speeding; Halsey rewards it more.

### Q37. Ch.3 — Treatment of Overtime Premium (marks: 4) [Theory/Application]
**Question:** State the correct cost treatment of **overtime premium** in four situations.
**Model Answer:** (i) **Customer's urgent order** at customer's request → charge the **premium to that job**. (ii) **General production pressure/seasonal** → treat premium as **factory overhead** (spread over all jobs). ### Q40. Ch.5 — Activity-Based Costing (marks: 6) [Problem]

### Q45. Ch.10 — Process Account with Normal & Abnormal Loss (marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** Input to Process 2,000 units costing ₹40,000; further material ₹6,000; labour ₹8,000; overheads ₹6,000. Normal loss 10% of input, scrap value ₹5/unit. Actual output 1,700 units. Prepare the **Process Account** and value abnormal loss.
**Model Answer:**
**WN-1 Total cost** = 40,000 + 6,000 + 8,000 + 6,000 = **₹60,000**.
**WN-2 Normal loss** = 10% × 2,000 = 200 units; scrap value = 200 × 5 = ₹1,000.

**WN-4 Abnormal loss units** = Expected good (1,800) − actual (1,700) = 100 units → value = 100 × 32.78 = **₹3,278**.

### Q48. Ch.13 — Material Cost Variances (marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** Standard: 5 kg @ ₹20/kg for one unit. Actual for 1,000 units: 5,200 kg @ ₹19/kg. Compute **Material Cost, Price and Usage** variances.
**Model Answer:**
**WN — Standard for actual output:** Std qty = 1,000 × 5 = 5,000 kg; Std cost = 5,000 × 20 = ₹1,00,000. Actual cost = 5,200 × 19 = ₹98,800.
- **Material Cost Variance** = Std cost − Actual cost = 1,00,000 − 98,800 = **₹1,200 (F)**.
- **Price Variance** = (Std price − Actual price) × Actual qty = (20 − 19) × 5,200 = **₹5,200 (F)**.
- **Usage Variance** = (Std qty − Actual qty) × Std price = (5,000 − 5,200) × 20 = **₹4,000 (A)**.

### Q49. Ch.13 — Labour Variances (marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** Standard: 4 hours @ ₹50/hr per unit. Actual for 500 units: 2,100 hours @ ₹52/hr, of which 100 hours were idle (abnormal). Compute **Labour Cost, Rate, Efficiency and Idle-time** variances.
**Model Answer:**
**WN:** Std hours = 500 × 4 = 2,000; Std cost = 2,000 × 50 = ₹1,00,000. Actual hours paid = 2,100; actual hours worked = 2,000. Actual cost = 2,100 × 52 = ₹1,09,200.
- **Labour Cost Variance** = 1,00,000 − 1,09,200 = **₹9,200 (A)**.
- **Rate Variance** = (Std rate − Actual rate) × Actual hours paid = (50 − 52) × 2,100 = **₹4,200 (A)**.
- **Idle-Time Variance** = Idle hours × Std rate = 100 × 50 = **₹5,000 (A)**.
- **Efficiency Variance** = (Std hours − Actual hours *worked*) × Std rate = (2,000 − 2,000) × 50 = **Nil**.
**Check:** Rate (A 4,200) + Idle (A 5,000) + Efficiency (0) = Cost variance (A 9,200) ✓.
**> Full-marks tip:** **Idle time is always Adverse**; efficiency uses **hours worked** (not paid). Separating idle-time is the marking trap.

**Question:** Budgeted fixed OH ₹1,00,000 for 10,000 std hours; actual OH ₹1,05,000; actual output 9,000 units (1 unit = 1 std hr); actual hours 9,500. Compute fixed-overhead **Expenditure, Volume, Efficiency and Capacity** variances.

### Q59. Ch.15 — Zero-Base Budgeting (marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** What is **Zero-Base Budgeting (ZBB)** and how does it differ from traditional budgeting? State two advantages.
**Model Answer:** **ZBB** requires every activity to be **justified from a "zero base"** each period — no activity is carried forward merely because it existed last year; each is evaluated afresh and ranked by cost-benefit. **Traditional (incremental)** budgeting starts from last year's figures and adds an increment. **Advantages:** eliminates wasteful/obsolete spend; links resources to priorities and results; involves managers in justifying their budgets.
**> Full-marks tip:** The key phrase is "**every rupee justified from zero**, not incremental" — that contrast defines ZBB.

### Q60. Ch.2 — Perpetual Inventory & ABC Analysis (marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain **Perpetual Inventory System** and **ABC analysis** of inventory control.
**Model Answer:** **Perpetual inventory** is a system of records (bin cards + stores ledger) that shows the **balance of every item continuously**, verified by **continuous (rotational) stock-taking** — avoiding a full annual shutdown. **ABC analysis** classifies items by **value-of-consumption**: 'A' (few items, high value) → tight control; 'B' moderate; 'C' (many items, low value) → loose control. It applies **selective control** — "management by exception" on inventory.
**> Full-marks tip:** Say ABC = **selective control based on value, not quantity**; A items get the tightest control.

Let total X = x, total Y = y. x = 40,000 + 0.10y; y = 30,000 + 0.20x.
Substitute: x = 40,000 + 0.10(30,000 + 0.20x) = 40,000 + 3,000 + 0.02x ⇒ 0.98x = 43,000 ⇒ **x = ₹43,878**. Then y = 30,000 + 0.20 × 43,878 = **₹38,776**.
To production: X = 43,878 × 70% = ₹30,714; Y = 38,776 × 90% = ₹34,898 (plus directly allocated production OH).
**> Full-marks tip:** For **reciprocal** service departments use **simultaneous equations** (or repeated distribution) — set up two equations clearly; the exam awards the method.

### Q63. Ch.10 — Equivalent Units (Weighted Average) (marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** Opening WIP nil. Input 10,000 units; completed 8,000; closing WIP 2,000 (100% material, 50% conversion). Costs: material ₹1,00,000; conversion ₹72,000. Compute **equivalent units** and cost per equivalent unit, and value closing WIP.
**Model Answer:**
**WN — Equivalent units:** Material = 8,000 + 2,000 = 10,000; Conversion = 8,000 + (2,000 × 50%) = 9,000.
- Cost/EU: material = 1,00,000/10,000 = ₹10; conversion = 72,000/9,000 = ₹8 → total ₹18.
- **Closing WIP** = material 2,000 × 10 + conversion 1,000 × 8 = 20,000 + 8,000 = **₹28,000**.
- Completed units = 8,000 × 18 = ₹1,44,000. Check: 1,44,000 + 28,000 = 1,72,000 = total cost ✓.
**> Full-marks tip:** Compute **separate equivalent units for material and conversion** (different completion %); the reconciliation to total cost secures full marks.

### Q64. Ch.13 — Material Mix & Yield Variance (marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** Standard mix for 100 kg output: A 60 kg @ ₹10, B 50 kg @ ₹8 (std input 110 kg for 100 kg output). Actual: A 65 kg @ ₹10, B 45 kg @ ₹8; actual output 100 kg. Compute **Mix and Yield** variances.
**Model Answer:**
**WN:** Std cost of std mix per 110 kg = (60×10)+(50×8) = 600+400 = ₹1,000. Actual input 110 kg (65+45). Revised std mix for 110 kg = A 60, B 50.
- **Mix Variance** = (Revised std qty − Actual qty) × Std price = A(60−65)×10 + B(50−45)×8 = (−50) + (+40) = **₹10 (A)**.
- **Yield Variance** = (Actual yield − Std yield for actual input) × Std cost per unit of output. Std yield from 110 kg = 100 kg; actual yield = 100 kg → **Nil**.
**> Full-marks tip:** **Mix** compares actual vs revised-standard proportions at the same total input; **Yield** compares output obtained vs output expected. State both bases.

### Q65. Ch.13 — Sales Variances (marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** Budget: 1,000 units @ ₹100, cost ₹70 (profit ₹30). Actual: 1,100 units @ ₹95, cost ₹70. Compute **Sales Value, Price and Volume** variances (turnover method) and the **profit-based** volume variance.
**Model Answer:**
- **Sales Value Variance** = Actual sales − Budgeted sales = (1,100×95) − (1,000×100) = 1,04,500 − 1,00,000 = **₹4,500 (F)**.
- **Sales Price Variance** = (Actual price − Budget price) × Actual qty = (95−100)×1,100 = **₹5,500 (A)**.
- **Sales Volume Variance** = (Actual qty − Budget qty) × Budget price = (1,100−1,000)×100 = **₹10,000 (F)**.
- **Profit-based Volume Variance** = (Actual − Budget qty) × Budgeted **profit** per unit = 100 × 30 = **₹3,000 (F)**.
**> Full-marks tip:** State whether you use the **turnover (value) method** or the **margin (profit) method** — the volume variance differs, and the examiner needs to know which basis you chose.

### Q66. Ch.13 — Reconciliation of Budgeted & Actual Profit (marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** Budgeted profit ₹3,00,000. Variances: Material A ₹4,000; Labour F ₹6,000; Overhead A ₹5,000; Sales price A ₹5,500; Sales volume F ₹10,000. Compute **actual profit**.
**Model Answer:**
Actual profit = Budgeted 3,00,000 − 4,000 + 6,000 − 5,000 − 5,500 + 10,000 = **₹3,01,500**.
**> Full-marks tip:** Add all **(F)** variances, subtract all **(A)** variances from budgeted profit — a clean statement format wins the presentation mark.

### Q67. Ch.14 — Shutdown / Discontinuance Decision (marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** A division earns contribution ₹2,00,000 against fixed cost ₹3,00,000 (of which ₹1,80,000 is avoidable if shut). Should it be shut for the season?
**Model Answer:**
**WN:** If it continues, it covers its avoidable fixed cost of ₹1,80,000 and contributes ₹20,000 towards unavoidable fixed cost (₹2,00,000 − 1,80,000). If shut, the firm still bears the ₹1,20,000 unavoidable fixed cost and loses the ₹20,000 surplus. **Decision: keep operating** — the contribution (₹2,00,000) exceeds the **avoidable** fixed cost (₹1,80,000).
**> Full-marks tip:** The shutdown rule compares **contribution vs avoidable (shutdown-saved) fixed cost**, not total fixed cost. Ignore unavoidable fixed cost.

### Q68. Ch.14 — Cost Break-Even & Indifference Point (marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** Two techniques: Machine A (fixed ₹2,00,000, variable ₹20/unit) and Machine B (fixed ₹3,50,000, variable ₹12/unit). Find the **indifference (cost break-even) point**.
**Model Answer:**
**WN — Set total costs equal:** 2,00,000 + 20Q = 3,50,000 + 12Q ⇒ 8Q = 1,50,000 ⇒ **Q = 18,750 units**.
**Decision:** below 18,750 units, low-fixed Machine A is cheaper; above 18,750, high-fixed Machine B is cheaper.
**> Full-marks tip:** The indifference point is where **total costs are equal**; state which option is preferred on each side of it.

### Q69. Ch.15 — Production & Material Purchase Budget (marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** Budgeted sales 20,000 units; opening FG 2,000; desired closing FG 3,000. Each unit needs 2 kg material; opening material 5,000 kg; desired closing material 6,000 kg. Prepare **production and material-purchase** budgets.
**Model Answer:**
- **Production budget** = Sales + Closing FG − Opening FG = 20,000 + 3,000 − 2,000 = **21,000 units**.
- **Material required** = 21,000 × 2 = 42,000 kg.
- **Purchase budget** = Material required + Closing material − Opening material = 42,000 + 6,000 − 5,000 = **43,000 kg**.
**> Full-marks tip:** Two identical formulas — "**need + closing − opening**" — first for units, then for material. Do not skip the stock adjustments.

### Q70. Ch.15 — Principal Budget Factor & Budget Ratios (marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** What is the **Principal (Key) Budget Factor**? Define **Capacity, Activity and Efficiency ratios**.
**Model Answer:** The **principal budget factor** (key/limiting factor) is the factor that **restricts the level of all activities** (e.g., sales demand, machine capacity, material) — budgeting must **start** with it. Ratios: **Capacity ratio** = (Actual hours ÷ Budgeted hours) × 100; **Activity ratio** = (Std hours for actual output ÷ Budgeted hours) × 100; **Efficiency ratio** = (Std hours for actual output ÷ Actual hours) × 100. *(Activity = Capacity × Efficiency.)*
**> Full-marks tip:** State that budgeting **begins with the principal budget factor**, and the identity **Activity = Capacity × Efficiency**.

### Q71. Ch.7 — Integrated Accounts: Journal Entries (marks: 5) [Theory/Problem]
**Question:** Give the journal entries in an **integrated accounting** system for: (i) purchase of material on credit, (ii) issue of direct material to production, (iii) direct wages paid, (iv) absorption of factory overhead, (v) finished goods completed.
**Model Answer:**
(i) Stores Ledger Control A/c Dr / To Creditors. (ii) WIP Control A/c Dr / To Stores Ledger Control. (iii) Wages Control A/c Dr / To Bank; then WIP Control Dr / To Wages Control. (iv) WIP Control A/c Dr / To Factory OH Control (absorbed). (v) Finished Goods Control A/c Dr / To WIP Control.
**> Full-marks tip:** In **integrated** accounts there is **no Cost Ledger Control A/c**; use real accounts (Creditors, Bank). Direction of each control account is the marking point.

### Q72. Ch.10 — Inter-Process Profit (marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Why is **inter-process profit** loaded, and what problem does it create in stock valuation?
**Model Answer:** When output of one process is transferred to the next at a **price above cost** (to judge each process's efficiency like a profit centre), the transfer includes **inter-process profit**. The problem: **closing stock** then carries **unrealised profit**, which must be **eliminated** by creating a **stock reserve**, so that only true cost appears in the balance sheet.
**> Full-marks tip:** The key phrase is "**unrealised profit in stock must be removed via a stock reserve**."

### Q73. Ch.1 — Cost Centre vs Profit Centre vs Cost Unit (marks: 3) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish **Cost Centre, Profit Centre and Cost Unit**.
**Model Answer:** **Cost centre** — a location/person/equipment for which **only costs** are collected and controlled. **Profit centre** — a segment for which **both costs and revenues** (hence profit) are ascertained; the manager is responsible for profit. **Cost unit** — the **unit of output** in terms of which cost is expressed. (An **investment centre** additionally controls investment/ROI.)
**> Full-marks tip:** Cost centre → cost only; profit centre → cost + revenue; investment centre → + investment.

### Q74. Ch.12 — Hospital / Hotel Operating Costing (marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State suitable **cost units** for: (a) hospital, (b) hotel, (c) canteen, (d) electricity, (e) goods transport, (f) road maintenance.
**Model Answer:** (a) **Patient-day** (or per bed-day); (b) **Room-day** (or guest-day); (c) **Per meal / per employee**; (d) **Kilowatt-hour**; (e) **Tonne-kilometre**; (f) **Per kilometre of road**.
**> Full-marks tip:** Choosing the **correct composite unit** is worth ½ mark each — learn the standard service units.

### Q75. Ch.4 — Blanket vs Departmental Overhead Rate (marks: 3) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish a **blanket (single) overhead rate** and **departmental rates**; when is a blanket rate inappropriate?
**Model Answer:** A **blanket rate** is a **single** overhead rate for the whole factory; **departmental rates** are separate rates for each department. A blanket rate is **inappropriate** when different products pass through departments **unequally** or departments differ in cost intensity — it then distorts product cost. Departmental rates give more accurate absorption.
**> Full-marks tip:** Blanket rate acceptable only when the **factory is homogeneous / all products use all departments equally**.

### Q76. Ch.14 — Cash Break-Even Point (marks: 3) [Problem]
**Question:** Fixed cost ₹4,00,000 (including ₹1,00,000 depreciation, a non-cash item); contribution per unit ₹40. Compute the **cash break-even point**.
**Model Answer:** **Cash BEP** = **Cash fixed cost** ÷ Contribution per unit = (4,00,000 − 1,00,000)/40 = 3,00,000/40 = **7,500 units** (vs normal BEP 10,000).
**> Full-marks tip:** Cash BEP uses **only cash fixed costs** (exclude depreciation and other non-cash charges) — it is lower than the accounting BEP.

### Q77. Ch.5 — Cost of Quality / Value Analysis (marks: 3) [Theory]
**Question:** State the four categories of **Cost of Quality**.
**Model Answer:** **(1) Prevention cost** (training, quality planning), **(2) Appraisal cost** (inspection, testing), **(3) Internal failure cost** (scrap, rework before delivery), **(4) External failure cost** (warranty, returns, lost goodwill after delivery). Spending on prevention/appraisal reduces failure costs.
**> Full-marks tip:** Group as **conformance (prevention + appraisal)** vs **non-conformance (internal + external failure)** costs.

### Q78. Ch.3 — Treatment of Labour-Related Costs (marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** How are these treated in cost accounts: (i) leave/holiday pay, (ii) employer's PF/ESI contribution, (iii) bonus, (iv) cost of labour turnover?
**Model Answer:** (i) **Leave pay** — part of labour cost, recovered by inflating the wage rate; (ii) **PF/ESI employer contribution** — added to labour cost (direct workers → direct; indirect → overhead); (iii) **Bonus** — normal statutory bonus is part of wage cost; (iv) **Cost of labour turnover** — **preventive costs** treated as overhead; **replacement costs** if abnormal to Costing P&L.
**> Full-marks tip:** The pattern: normal, recurring items load onto cost; abnormal items go to Costing P&L.

### Q79. Ch.15 — Fixed vs Flexible Budget; Master Budget (marks: 3) [Theory]
**Question:** What is a **Master Budget**? How does it relate to functional budgets?
**Model Answer:** A **master budget** is the **summary/consolidated budget** for the whole organisation — it integrates all **functional budgets** (sales, production, material, labour, overhead, cash) into a **budgeted profit & loss and balance sheet**. It is the apex of the budgeting system, prepared after the functional budgets and starting from the principal budget factor.
**> Full-marks tip:** Master budget = **consolidation of all functional budgets** into budgeted financial statements.

### Q80. Ch.6 — Treatment of Special Items in Cost Sheet (marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** State how each is treated in the cost sheet: (i) sale of scrap, (ii) carriage inward, (iii) cash discount, (iv) interest on capital, (v) income tax.
**Model Answer:** (i) **Sale of scrap** — deducted from factory overhead (or material cost); (ii) **Carriage inward** — added to material cost; (iii) **Cash discount** — a **financial item**, excluded from cost; (iv) **Interest on capital** — generally **excluded** (financial nature; sometimes notionally included for decisions); (v) **Income tax** — **excluded** (appropriation of profit, not a cost).
**> Full-marks tip:** Financial-nature items (cash discount, interest, income tax, dividends) are **excluded from cost sheets** — a frequent trap.

### Q81. Ch.2 — EOQ with Quantity Discount (marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** Annual demand 4,800 units; ordering cost ₹100; carrying cost 25% of price. Price ₹20; a 2% discount applies if ordered in lots of 1,200. Should the discount be accepted?
**Model Answer:**
**WN-1 EOQ at ₹20:** carrying = 25%×20 = ₹5; EOQ = √(2×4,800×100/5) = √1,92,000 = **438 units**.
**At EOQ (438):** purchase 4,800×20 = 96,000; ordering = (4,800/438)×100 = 1,096; carrying = (438/2)×5 = 1,096; **total ≈ ₹98,192.**
**At 1,200 with 2% discount (price ₹19.60):** carrying = 25%×19.60 = ₹4.90; purchase = 4,800×19.60 = 94,080; ordering = (4,800/1,200)×100 = 400; carrying = (1,200/2)×4.90 = 2,940; **total = ₹97,420.**
**Decision:** total cost is **lower with the discount (₹97,420 < ₹98,192)** → **accept the discount**.
**> Full-marks tip:** Compare **total cost = purchase + ordering + carrying** at EOQ vs at the discount lot; the purchase-price saving usually dominates.

### Q82. Ch.5/7 — Just-in-Time (JIT) (marks: 3) [Theory]
**Question:** What is **JIT** and its benefits for cost?
**Model Answer:** **JIT** is a **demand-pull** system where material is procured and goods produced **only as needed**, minimising inventory. Benefits: near-zero **carrying cost**, less obsolescence and space, quicker defect detection, and lower working capital. It requires reliable suppliers and quality at source.
**> Full-marks tip:** JIT = "**produce/buy only what is needed, when needed**" → inventory carrying cost approaches zero.

### Q83. Ch.14 — Throughput Accounting (marks: 3) [Theory]
**Question:** Define **Throughput** and the throughput approach.
**Model Answer:** **Throughput** = Sales − **direct material cost** (all other costs treated as fixed "operating expense" in the short run). Throughput accounting maximises **throughput per unit of the bottleneck resource**, since only the constraint limits profit. It is a refinement of marginal costing under the **Theory of Constraints**.
**> Full-marks tip:** Throughput treats **only material as variable**; maximise throughput per bottleneck hour.

### Q84. Ch.7 — Cost Audit (marks: 3) [Theory]
**Question:** What is **Cost Audit** and its objectives?
**Model Answer:** **Cost audit** is the **verification of cost records and accounts** and a check on adherence to the cost accounting plan. Objectives: verify arithmetical and record accuracy, ensure cost principles/plan are followed, detect errors/fraud, and provide reliable cost data to management and (for specified industries) to the government under the Companies Act.
**> Full-marks tip:** Distinguish "**propriety**" and "**efficiency**" audit aspects; mention the statutory basis for specified industries.

### Q85. Ch.7 — Uniform Costing & Inter-firm Comparison (marks: 3) [Theory]
**Question:** What is **Uniform Costing** and its advantages?
**Model Answer:** **Uniform costing** is the **use of the same costing principles and practices by several undertakings** in the same industry, enabling meaningful **inter-firm comparison**. Advantages: comparability, sharing of best practices, industry-level cost data for pricing/policy, and identification of inefficiency. Requires agreed definitions and methods.
**> Full-marks tip:** Its whole purpose is **comparability** across firms — state that plus inter-firm comparison.

### Q86. Ch.9 — Escalation Clause (marks: 4) [Problem]
**Question:** A contract has an **escalation clause** for material. Standard: 1,000 tonnes @ ₹500. Actual price rose to ₹560. The clause allows recovery of price rise above 5%. Compute the escalation claim.
**Model Answer:**
**WN:** Allowed threshold = ₹500 × 105% = ₹525. Recoverable rise = actual ₹560 − ₹525 = **₹35/tonne**. Claim = 1,000 × 35 = **₹35,000**.
**> Full-marks tip:** The escalation clause protects the contractor from **input-price rises**; recover only the excess **above the agreed threshold**, not the full rise.

### Q87. Ch.9 — Cost-Plus Contract (marks: 3) [Theory]
**Question:** What is a **Cost-Plus Contract**? State one advantage to each party.
**Model Answer:** A **cost-plus contract** fixes the price as **actual allowable cost + an agreed percentage/fixed fee** of profit. **To the contractor:** no risk of loss from cost increases (assured margin). **To the buyer:** useful when costs cannot be estimated in advance (defence, new projects); but the buyer bears cost overruns, so cost audit is essential.
**> Full-marks tip:** Note the buyer's risk (no incentive for the contractor to economise) → cost audit safeguard.

### Q88. Ch.10 — Operation vs Operating Costing (marks: 3) [Theory]
**Question:** Distinguish **Operation costing** and **Operating costing**.
**Model Answer:** **Operation costing** is a **refinement of process costing** applied where production consists of **repetitive operations** — cost is ascertained for each **operation**. **Operating costing** (= service costing) ascertains the cost of **rendering services** (transport, hospital). Despite similar names, they are different: one is a product-process method, the other a service method.
**> Full-marks tip:** They sound alike but differ — **operation = process refinement; operating = services.**

### Q89. Ch.13 — Types of Standards & Standard Cost Card (marks: 3) [Theory]
**Question:** State the types of standards and what a **standard cost card** contains.
**Model Answer:** Types: **Ideal, Basic, Current, Normal (attainable/expected)** standards — the **normal/attainable** standard is most useful as it is challenging yet achievable. A **standard cost card** records, for one unit, the standard quantity and price of each cost element (material, labour, overhead) and the total standard cost — the benchmark against which actuals are compared.
**> Full-marks tip:** Recommend the **attainable (expected) standard** and say why (motivational yet realistic).

### Q90. Ch.15 — Budget Manual & Budget Committee (marks: 3) [Theory]
**Question:** What is a **Budget Manual** and a **Budget Committee**?
**Model Answer:** A **budget manual** is a document setting out the **procedures, responsibilities, formats and timetable** for preparing and operating budgets — the "rulebook" of the budgeting system. A **budget committee** (headed by a budget officer, with functional heads) **coordinates and approves** the functional budgets and resolves conflicts.
**> Full-marks tip:** Manual = the written procedure; committee = the people who coordinate/approve.

### Q91. Ch.1 — Responsibility Accounting (marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** What is **Responsibility Accounting** and its types of responsibility centres?
**Model Answer:** **Responsibility accounting** is a system that **traces costs and revenues to the managers responsible** for them, so performance is judged on **controllable** items only. Centres: **Cost centre** (responsible for costs), **Revenue centre** (revenues), **Profit centre** (costs + revenues), **Investment centre** (profit + capital employed/ROI). Reports show **controllable vs uncontrollable** items.
**> Full-marks tip:** The core principle: managers are accountable only for what they **control** — mention the four centre types.

### Q92. Ch.14 — Limitations of Marginal Costing (marks: 3) [Theory]
**Question:** State the limitations of marginal costing.
**Model Answer:** (i) Difficulty in **segregating** costs into fixed and variable; (ii) **fixed costs ignored** in product cost — unsuitable for long-run pricing and for valuing stock under AS 2 (which requires absorption); (iii) not suitable where fixed costs are a large share; (iv) can mislead if used for **long-term** decisions; (v) semi-variable costs need approximation.
**> Full-marks tip:** The AS-2 point (stock must be valued on **absorption**, not marginal, for financial accounts) is a favourite.

### Q93. Ch.14 — Contribution Approach to Pricing (marks: 3) [Theory]
**Question:** When is pricing at **below total cost but above variable cost** justified?
**Model Answer:** When there is **spare capacity** and the order will not spoil the regular market — any price **above variable cost** adds contribution towards fixed cost/profit. Situations: **special/export orders, off-season pricing, entering a new market, disposing of perishable stock**. Not sustainable as a long-run price (must cover total cost eventually).
**> Full-marks tip:** Justified **only with spare capacity and no market spoilage**; add the "long run must cover full cost" caveat.

### Q94. Ch.5 — Life-Cycle & Target Costing (marks: 4) [Theory]
**Question:** Explain **Target Costing** and **Life-Cycle Costing**.
**Model Answer:** **Target costing:** starts from the **market-determined price** and the desired profit, and works back to a **target cost** (Target cost = Target price − Desired profit) that the product must be designed to meet — a **market-driven, design-stage** cost control. **Life-cycle costing:** accumulates a product's costs over its **entire life** (R&D → design → manufacture → after-sales → disposal), recognising that much cost is **committed at the design stage**.
**> Full-marks tip:** Target cost = **price − profit** (not cost + profit); life-cycle emphasises costs **locked in at design**.

### Q95. Ch.3 — Learning Curve (marks: 4) [Problem]
**Question:** With an **80% learning curve**, the first unit takes 100 hours. What is the average time per unit for 2, 4 and 8 units?
**Model Answer:** Under an 80% curve, each **doubling** of cumulative output reduces the **cumulative average time** to 80% of the previous average.
- 1 unit: 100 hrs avg.
- 2 units: 100 × 80% = **80 hrs** avg (total 160).
- 4 units: 80 × 80% = **64 hrs** avg (total 256).
- 8 units: 64 × 80% = **51.2 hrs** avg (total 409.6).
**> Full-marks tip:** The learning-curve % applies to the **cumulative average time** and only at **doublings** of output — state that clearly before computing.

### Q96. Ch.7 — Cost Ledger Control Account Method (marks: 3) [Theory]
**Question:** In non-integrated accounts, what is the role of the **Cost Ledger Control Account (CLCA)**?
**Model Answer:** In a **non-integrated** system the cost books are self-contained; the **CLCA** (also "General Ledger Adjustment A/c") **completes the double entry** for items that have their other leg in the **financial** books (e.g., purchases, wages paid, sales). It makes the cost ledger self-balancing and its closing balance equals the net of the other control accounts.
**> Full-marks tip:** CLCA = the "**link/contra**" account that makes the cost ledger self-balancing; it exists only in **non-integrated** systems.

### Q97. Ch.6 — Tender / Quotation Price (marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** Last year: materials ₹80,000, wages ₹60,000, works OH 60% of wages, admin & S&D OH 20% of works cost. For a new tender, material prices are expected to rise 10% and wages 5%; profit 20% on cost. Prepare the **quotation**.
**Model Answer:**
**WN:** Material = 80,000 × 1.10 = 88,000; Wages = 60,000 × 1.05 = 63,000; Prime cost = 1,51,000; Works OH = 60% × 63,000 = 37,800; Works cost = 1,88,800; Admin+S&D = 20% × 1,88,800 = 37,760; **Total cost = 2,26,560**; Profit 20% on cost = 45,312; **Quotation price = ₹2,71,872**.
**> Full-marks tip:** Apply the **expected (escalated) rates** for the new tender, keep the overhead **percentages** as given, and note profit is "on cost" here (not on sales).

### Q98. Ch.4 — Under/Over Absorption: Causes (marks: 3) [Theory]
**Question:** State the causes of **under/over absorption** of overheads.
**Model Answer:** (i) **Wrong estimation** of overhead amount or of the base (hours/units); (ii) **actual output/hours** differing from budgeted (volume change); (iii) **unexpected changes** in overhead cost (price rises, extra items); (iv) **seasonal fluctuations** if an annual rate is applied monthly; (v) **abnormal** events (idle capacity, breakdowns).
**> Full-marks tip:** Group into "**estimation errors**" and "**activity/volume differences**" — that structure earns marks.

### Q99. Ch.8 — Batch Costing with Set-up (marks: 5) [Problem]
**Question:** A batch of 500 units: material ₹40,000; labour ₹20,000; set-up cost ₹5,000; factory OH 50% of labour; S&D OH ₹3,000. Compute **cost per unit** and the selling price for 25% profit on selling price.
**Model Answer:**
**WN:** Material 40,000 + Labour 20,000 + Set-up 5,000 + Factory OH (50%×20,000) 10,000 + S&D 3,000 = **Total ₹78,000**. Cost per unit = 78,000/500 = **₹156**.
**Selling price** (25% profit on SP ⇒ cost = 75% of SP): SP = 156/0.75 = **₹208 per unit**.
**> Full-marks tip:** Set-up cost is a **batch-level** cost spread over the batch; "profit on selling price" ⇒ cost = (100−25)% of SP.

### Q100. Ch.14 — Optimal Product Mix (Limiting Factor) (marks: 6) [Problem]
**Question:** Two products share 12,000 machine hours (the limiting factor). P: contribution ₹90, 3 hrs, max demand 2,000 units. Q: contribution ₹80, 2 hrs, max demand 3,000 units. Determine the **optimal mix** and total contribution.
**Model Answer:**
**WN-1 Contribution per machine hour:** P = 90/3 = ₹30; Q = 80/2 = **₹40** → rank **Q first**, then P.
**WN-2 Allocate 12,000 hrs:** Q full demand 3,000 units × 2 = 6,000 hrs (contribution 3,000×80 = 2,40,000). Remaining 6,000 hrs → P = 6,000/3 = 2,000 units (= its max demand) × 90 = 1,80,000.
**Optimal mix:** Q 3,000 units + P 2,000 units; **total contribution = 2,40,000 + 1,80,000 = ₹4,20,000** (hours used 6,000+6,000 = 12,000 ✓).
**> Full-marks tip:** Rank by **contribution per limiting-factor hour**, allocate to the best first up to its demand ceiling, then the next — and check total hours reconcile.
