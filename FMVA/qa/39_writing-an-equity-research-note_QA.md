# Q&A — Writing an Equity Research Note

A practice bank for Chapter 39. Work each question before reading the answer. The chapter's governing sentence runs through every problem: **the note is the product; the model is the proof.** Section B numbers are reproducible by hand.

## Section A — Concept-Check

**A1. What is an equity research note, and how does it differ from a model?**

A note is a one-to-ten-page written document that recommends an action (buy, sell, or hold), states a price target, and argues in plain English why the market is mispricing the stock. A model is a machine that generates a number; the note is the argument for why that number matters. Nobody reads models. The note is the deliverable; the model is the appendix.

**A2. State the single question every note must answer.**

"Why is the market wrong?" A stock price already embeds the consensus view. If your model agrees with consensus, you have nothing to say and there is no trade. A note exists only because you hold a *variant perception* — you believe something the market does not, or weigh known facts differently. Everything that does not support or defend that variant view is filler. This variant view — also called the edge or differentiated view — is the entire reason the note exists.

**A3. Why is a note described as "an argument, not a report"?**

A report describes; an argument persuades. The note's job is to move a skeptical, time-poor reader to take an action by presenting a *falsifiable* thesis backed by evidence, with risks honestly stated. A report that merely narrates the company earns no decision. Investment committees vote on recommendations, not spreadsheets — so the deliverable must be built to persuade.

**A4. What is the inverted pyramid, and why does research writing use it?**

The inverted pyramid puts the conclusion first, supporting arguments second, and detail last — the opposite of the school essay. Research readers consume in layers (header, then thesis, then section headers, then full text), and each layer must leave a reader who stops there with the correct takeaway. Burying the recommendation behind a build-up ("Founded in 1987…") fails every reader who does not reach the end.

**A5. List the standard sections of a note in order.**

(1) Header block — rating, target, upside, key stats; (2) Thesis — the variant view in 2–4 sentences; (3) Business overview; (4) Expanded thesis — the pillars; (5) Valuation; (6) Catalysts; (7) Risks and mitigants; (8) Financial summary. Conclusion first, support second, detail last.

**A6. What belongs in the header block, and what is the single most-read number?**

Company and ticker, rating, current price and date, 12-month target, implied upside/downside, and a compact key-stats strip (market cap, EV, shares, 2–3 headline multiples). The single most-read figure is the **implied upside** — target versus current price as a percentage. It must be readable in three seconds.

**A7. Give the four-part shape of a strong thesis paragraph.**

(1) What the market believes / the mispricing; (2) what you believe instead, with the key number; (3) the mechanism that closes the gap and roughly when; (4) the rating and upside. It must be falsifiable — it should tell the reader exactly what to watch and what would prove you wrong.

**A8. What is the structure of a single thesis pillar?**

Claim → evidence → why the market misses it → quantified impact. The claim is a bolded topic sentence (one line); the evidence is data or model output; the market-miss explains why it is not already priced; the quantified impact ties it to revenue, margin, or the multiple in the model. Three pillars is the sweet spot — one is fragile, five means you have not prioritized.

**A9. Why does an honest risks section make a buy recommendation *stronger*?**

Credibility is the analyst's only currency. A note with no risks reads as naive — the reader mentally supplies the omitted risks and trusts you less. Naming the things that could break your thesis, quantifying the downside, and explaining why you take the bet anyway signals genuine stress-testing. Credibility compounds: honest risk sections get you believed on the next call.

**A10. What are catalysts, and why does a note need them?**

Catalysts are events that force the market to converge on your view, each with rough timing (e.g., prints showing the mix cross 50%, a plant commissioning, a guidance raise). A cheap stock can stay cheap forever; catalysts show you think about *time*, not just value, and that the thesis can pay off inside a holding period. List 2–4, each with a *what* and a *when*.

**A11. Explain the layering / skim structure.**

A note is read in five layers: header (3 seconds), thesis (30 seconds), section headers plus bold pillar sentences (2 minutes), full note (10 minutes), and the attached model. Each layer must be self-sufficient — a reader who stops anywhere leaves with the correct takeaway. Bolding pillar topic sentences lets the Layer-3 skimmer read a coherent argument from headers alone.

**A12. Why is clarity of writing treated as a proxy for clarity of thought?**

It is genuinely hard to write a crisp thesis about a business you do not understand — muddled writing exposes muddled thinking. Conversely, a clean, well-sequenced note lets a reader infer a clean, well-sequenced mind. That inference is what gets you the interview. The note is a *signal*, and its clarity is the payload.

## Section B — Applied / Build Problems

Every number below is reproducible by hand. Prove each before reading on.

**B1. Compute implied upside for the header.** Current price ₹1,240; 12-month target ₹1,560. What upside do you print, and to what precision?

Upside = (1560 − 1240) / 1240 = 320 / 1240 = 0.2581 = **+26%**. Round to a whole percent — printing "+25.81%" implies false precision you cannot forecast to. The header shows **+26%**.

**B2. Decompose the target into thesis-driven pieces.** The ₹320 gap between target (₹1,560) and price (₹1,240) is stated to be ~₹250 from multiple re-rating and the remainder from a higher forecast. How much is the forecast piece, and what share of the gap is each?

Forecast piece = 320 − 250 = **₹70**. Re-rating share = 250 / 320 = **78%**; forecast share = 70 / 320 = **22%**. Both flow from the single mix thesis, so the valuation reconciles to the thesis — the key credibility test.

**B3. Build the segment mix evidence for Pillar 1.** Specialty is 45% of EBITDA at a 31% margin; commodity is 55% at a 12% margin. Compute today's blended margin. Then compute it when the mix shifts to 60% specialty / 40% commodity (margins unchanged). What is the expansion in basis points?

Today: 0.45 × 31% + 0.55 × 12% = 13.95% + 6.60% = **20.55%**.
At 60/40: 0.60 × 31% + 0.40 × 12% = 18.60% + 4.80% = **23.40%**.
Expansion = 23.40% − 20.55% = 2.85 pts = **285 bps**. (The chapter's "~400bps" also loads revenue growth and operating leverage on top of pure mix; pure mix alone gives ~285bps — useful to know when challenged.)

**B4. Cross-check the DCF with a multiple.** FY2 EBITDA is ₹5,000 cr; you apply a re-rated 9x EV/EBITDA. Net debt is ₹3,000 cr; shares 27.8 cr. Compute the implied equity value per share.

EV = 9 × 5,000 = ₹45,000 cr. Equity value = 45,000 − 3,000 = ₹42,000 cr. Per share = 42,000 / 27.8 = **₹1,510/share** — a clean cross-check on the ₹1,560 DCF. Lesson: always sanity-check that the per-share reconciles to the intended magnitude before you print it.

**B5. Build the target-price bridge.** DCF enterprise value ₹43,400 cr; net debt ₹3,000 cr; shares 26 cr. Walk EV → equity → per share.

Equity value = 43,400 − 3,000 = ₹40,400 cr. Per share = 40,400 / 26 = **₹1,554 ≈ ₹1,560**. The clean sequence: EV → less net debt → equity value → ÷ shares → target price. One line, no detours.

**B6. Quantify the bear case for the risks section.** Base target ₹1,560. If the specialty mix plateaus at 45%, the margin bridge loses ~250bps and the target falls to ₹1,340. What is the residual upside at the bear-case target, and why still disclose a positive number?

Bear upside = (1340 − 1240) / 1240 = **+8%**. Disclosing it shows the thesis is asymmetric — even if the variant view largely fails, the downside is limited and still slightly positive, which strengthens the risk-adjusted case rather than weakening it.

**B7. Build the football-field ranges.** Methods and ₹/share ranges: DCF 1,490–1,630; EV/EBITDA comps 1,420–1,580; P/E comps 1,380–1,540; SOTP 1,510–1,690. Current price 1,240. In what band do all four methods overlap, and what is the note's takeaway?

Overlap = highest low to lowest high = max(1490, 1420, 1380, 1510) to min(1630, 1580, 1540, 1690) = **1,510 to 1,540**. Every method clears the ₹1,240 spot; the tight overlap at ₹1,510–1,540 is the defensible value conclusion — the visual argument for the Buy.

**B8. Excel mechanics for the football field.** You have Low and High per method. What two helper columns do you plot, and how do you make the bar "float"?

Plot **Base = Low** (invisible) and **Length = High − Low** (visible) as a stacked horizontal bar. Set the Base series fill to No Fill; the Length series then floats from each Low to each High. Example: DCF Base = 1,490, Length = 140. Add a vertical reference line at the current price (1,240) so the gap is instantly visible.

**B9. Write a compliant thesis from raw model output.** Model: stock at 6x forward EV/EBITDA; specialty 45% of EBITDA at 31% margin; mix reaches 60% by FY3; two contracted expansions; peers at 9–12x; target ₹1,560 vs ₹1,240 (+26%). Draft a 2–4 sentence thesis using the four-part shape.

> *"BSCL trades at 6x EV/EBITDA, priced as a cyclical commodity producer. We think this ignores a structural mix shift: specialty, now 45% of EBITDA at 31% margins, should exceed 60% by FY3 as two contracted-volume expansions ramp — lifting blended margins and warranting a re-rating toward 9x. As the mix becomes undeniable in quarterly prints, we initiate at BUY, target ₹1,560, +26%."*

It names consensus (6x, commodity), the disagreement with a number (45%→60%), the mechanism and timing (contracted ramps, quarterly prints), and lands on rating and upside.

**B10. Build the one-page financial summary spine.** From these figures, state what six lines you print and confirm one reconciliation: FY2 revenue ₹22,000 cr, EBITDA ₹5,000 cr, EBIT ₹4,000 cr, net income ₹2,600 cr, shares 26 cr. Compute EPS and the EBITDA margin.

Print: Revenue, EBITDA, EBIT, Net income, EPS, and Free cash flow (plus 2–3 ratios). EPS = 2,600 / 26 = **₹100**. EBITDA margin = 5,000 / 22,000 = **22.7%**. These let a reader sanity-check the story on one page; the full workbook is the attachment.

## Section C — Interview-Style Questions

**C1. "Pitch me a stock in 90 seconds."**

Model answer: Deliver the note out loud — thesis, two or three pillars, valuation, one risk. "I'm long BSCL, ₹1,240, target ₹1,560, 26% upside. The market prices it as a cyclical commodity chemicals name at 6x EV/EBITDA. That's wrong: specialty is already 45% of EBITDA at 31% margins versus 12% for commodity, and two *contracted* expansions take it past 60% by FY3 — the sell-side still models flat mix. That mix shift lifts blended margins and should re-rate the multiple toward the 9x specialty peers trade at; ~₹250 of my ₹320 upside is that re-rating. Catalyst: mix crossing 50% in the next two prints. Main risk: the ramp stalling at 45% — but Plant 2 is 60% pre-booked, and even that bear case gives ₹1,340, still +8%." That *is* the note spoken — if it doesn't flow in 90 seconds it isn't tight enough written.

**C2. "Why should I read your note instead of your model?"**

Model answer: A model is a machine for producing a number, not an argument. You have forty names and twenty minutes; you won't reverse-engineer my assumptions from cell references. The note tells you in one paragraph what I believe, why the market disagrees, and what the trade is — and lets you trace any claim down to the model. The model proves I can build; the note proves I understand what the numbers mean.

**C3. "Your DCF says the stock is worth more than the price. Isn't that just optimistic assumptions?"**

Model answer: That is exactly the discipline the note forces, which is why I decompose the upside. The ₹320 gap isn't a black box: ~₹250 is the re-rating from 6x to the 9x specialty peers command, and ~₹70 is my higher forecast — both flow from one thing, the mix crossing 60%. If I couldn't attribute the gap to a specific, checkable thesis, you'd be right to assume I'd just dialed up growth and dialed down WACC. The reconciliation is the test, and I run it before printing a target.

**C4. "What would make you wrong?"**

Model answer: The cleanest kill is the specialty mix plateauing at 45% instead of reaching 60% — that removes the margin bridge and most of the re-rating, taking me to a bear case of ₹1,340. I take the bet anyway because Plant 2's volumes are already 60% contracted. Secondary risks are an input-cost spike and project delay. The bear case is still +8% — the idea is asymmetric, which is why I'm comfortable being wrong on the details.

**C5. "How do you find a variant perception in the first place?"**

Model answer: I compare my forecast to what the price implies. The stock trades at a commodity 6x, so the market is extrapolating the down-cycle and treating the whole business as cyclical. My segment work says the blended business already earns specialty-like economics and the mix is still shifting. That gap between what the price assumes and what the segment data shows *is* the variant perception. If I find no genuine disagreement, the stock is fairly valued — I move on rather than fabricate an edge.

**C6. "Walk me through how you'd structure a research note if you were initiating coverage."**

Model answer: Header (rating, price, target, upside, key multiples, readable in three seconds); thesis in 2–4 sentences (consensus, variant view with a number, mechanism and timing, rating); a lean overview carrying only context the thesis needs; the body of three pillars, each claim → evidence → market-miss → quantified impact; valuation (primary method, key-drivers table, target bridge, sensitivity, reconciliation to the thesis); catalysts with timing; risks with quantified bear cases and mitigants; and a one-page financial summary. I write the thesis last, move it to the top, then cut 20% of the words.

**C7. "Isn't listing risks just giving the bear case ammunition?"**

Model answer: The opposite. A note with no risks reads as naive — you'll supply the omitted risks yourself and trust me less for hiding them. Naming the things that could break my thesis, quantifying each downside, and defending the bet anyway is what stress-testing looks like on paper, and it front-runs your objections. Credibility is the only thing an analyst really sells, and honest risk disclosure is how you build it.

## Section D — Common-Error Spotting

Each item contains a mistake from the chapter's traps. Identify it and give the fix.

**D1.** A note reads: "Our DCF gives ₹1,560, which is above the current price of ₹1,240, so we rate the stock Buy."

Error: **No thesis, just a number** (Trap 1). The note never says *why the market is wrong* — it just reports that a spreadsheet produced a bigger number. Fix: state the variant perception and reconcile the upside to it. A DCF output is not an argument.

**D2.** Pages 1–3 of a note cover the company's founding in 1987, its full product catalog, and a plant-by-plant history; the actual analysis starts on page 4.

Error: **The overview eats the note** (Trap 2), and the lede is buried (Trap 8). Fix: cut the overview to two or three paragraphs carrying only context the thesis uses, and put the recommendation and thesis first.

**D3.** A thesis states: "The company is well-positioned with strong fundamentals and is poised for growth going forward, representing a compelling opportunity."

Error: **Hedged, promotional, vague language** (Trap 3). Not one phrase contains a specific, checkable claim. Fix: replace with numbers and mechanism — "specialty crosses 60% of EBITDA by FY3, lifting blended margins ~400bps." Ban "well-positioned," "poised for," "compelling," "going forward."

**D4.** The risks section reads, in full: "A global recession could hurt earnings."

Error: **Fake / trivial risks** (Trap 4). This is a generic disclaimer, not the risk that would break *this* thesis. Fix: name the thesis-specific risk (mix plateaus at 45%), quantify the bear case (₹1,340), and give a mitigant.

**D5.** The header prints a 12-month target of **₹1,563.47**.

Error: **False precision** (Trap 5). Two-decimal precision implies you can forecast to the paisa; you cannot. Fix: round to ₹1,560 (or show a ₹1,500–1,620 range) and present sensitivity. Unjustified precision destroys credibility faster than an honest range does.

**D6.** A note claims 26% upside but, when asked, the analyst cannot say which parts of the thesis create the gap.

Error: **Valuation does not reconcile to the thesis** (Trap 6). The upside is probably just optimistic inputs. Fix: decompose the gap — e.g., ₹250 re-rating + ₹70 forecast — each traceable to the mix thesis. If it won't decompose, the assumptions, not the market, are the problem.

**D7.** A note argues its bull case forcefully but never mentions or addresses the obvious bear counterargument that the down-cycle is structural, not temporary.

Error: **Writing for a reader who already agrees** (Trap 7). Fix: name the strongest opposing view and rebut it inside the note — show why the specialty mix, being contracted and margin-accretive, is not just cyclical noise. You write for a skeptic who owns the other side.
